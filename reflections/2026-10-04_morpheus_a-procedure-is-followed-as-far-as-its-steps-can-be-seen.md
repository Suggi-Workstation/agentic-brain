# A Procedure Is Followed as Far as Its Steps Can Be Seen -- Make Each Step Leave Evidence for Someone Other Than the Doer

## I -- Idea

The purpose of this session was to make the fleet's reflections deeper and
its knowledge-production procedures more reliable, by revising the
reflection template and the Feynman Loop that every writing skill runs
first, and then to test the result by writing a reflection under the new
rules.

The work began after preflight, when Suggi asked me to set out the
reflection skill and its template in full: every step, what each section
asks for, what a writer must watch and how many words each section needs.
He suspected the format needed adjusting. Reading the skill, the template
and the four core agents' session-end skills side by side, I found that
they disagreed with each other. The session-end skills told the agent to
write the reflection with "no new research", while the reflection skill
requires the Feynman Loop, whose third step is research. The template both
required and forbade a version-history table, demanded a reflection budget
of 20% of session effort that nobody could verify, asked for a Learn
section of "one to three crisp statements" under a word minimum, and showed
an example that failed its own rules. Success, at the start, meant one
consistent procedure that produces reflections with more thought in them,
not merely more words. The constraints were the usual ones for shared
governance: the template is approval-locked, the skills serve every agent
on the VPS, and every change needed a lean plan that Suggi approved before
I touched a file.

Suggi set the direction over several rounds. Reflections became prose
throughout, the minimums rose to 600 words for each of Idea, Opinion and
Reflection plus 200 for a new closing section, helper questions went under
every section, the Schoen budget and the version-history section left the
template, and the Feynman Loop was referenced instead of copied. He also
drew a line I kept returning to: procedure belongs in skills, content
belongs in templates. I applied the changes to the template, to every copy
of the reflection skill and to all four session-end skills, and gave Neo,
Atlas and Cassandra my reflection notes and format check.

He then asked for a test reflection written as session-end would write it.
I wrote one, and it argued that the 400-word minimum had become the target
rather than the floor. But I skipped the Feynman Loop's first step while
writing it. Right after loading the skill I sent my first searches as one
batch of parallel tool calls, and never wrote a blank page or a gap list at
all. When
Suggi asked what had gone wrong, the cause was in the step itself: it said
to write the blank page "on a blank canvas or in your thinking block", which
leaves nothing that anyone can inspect. We changed both Feynman loops, the
one the writing skills use and the Forge's own, so that the blank page and
the gap list go into a scratch file, each in a call of its own, before any
source is consulted, and the file is deleted afterwards. The Feynman
checklist line left all five templates, because it is procedure. Along the
way the check against the brain caught a Library topic crediting Strathern's
sentence about measures and targets to Goodhart, which I corrected.
Finally Suggi asked me to delete the test reflection, take the percentage
brackets out of the Reflection sub-headings while keeping the weights as
guidance, and write again. This is that reflection. Its blank page and gap
list exist as a scratch file, written in two separate calls before my first
search.

Before the blank page I held two separate stories: reflections clustered
near the minimum because a number becomes a target, and I skipped the blank
page through carelessness under batching. After the loop I see one cause
behind both, and I know three things I did not. Economists call clustering
just above a threshold bunching. The designers of the surgical safety
checklist built it to be spoken aloud among peers rather than ticked
privately. And the Hawthorne effect, which I nearly leaned on, rests on
data that a later reanalysis found largely mythical.

## O -- Opinion

Confidence: medium (70%).

My position is that a procedure run by the agent it governs is followed as
far as its steps can be seen by someone other than that agent. Steps that
produce a countable output are met exactly at the threshold. Steps that
produce no output at all are skipped when time or batching presses.
Self-ticked checkboxes change neither, because the one ticking the box is
the one doing the step. Both of this session's failures were the same
failure, and the fixes we chose were the same kind of fix: give the step an
artifact, and let something other than the doer read it. I hold the
diagnosis with high confidence, because the evidence for it is direct. I
hold the remedy with much lower confidence, because it has run exactly
once, which is why the overall figure is medium.

The evidence for the visible half is the word counts. Since the 400-word
minimum arrived in mid-August, 94 of 147 Idea, Opinion and Reflection
sections landed between 400 and 499 words and only six fell below the
floor. The pattern matches what Saez found in United States tax
returns: at the clearest kink, the bunching came almost entirely from the
self-employed, the filers whose reported income is easiest to adjust. A
writer controls a word count completely, so strong bunching is what one
should expect, and the writer
who bunched most was me, with 61 of my 90 sections in that band against 33
of 57 for my peers. The evidence for the invisible half is the blank page.
Checklist items in three places already covered it: the template, the
reflection skill and the Feynman Loop itself. None of them stopped me,
because nothing about the step could be checked by anyone but me.

The evidence is thinner in two places, and I should say where. The tax
comparison is an analogy: it shows that people who control a number
cluster at the point where a rule changes, not that writers do so for the
same reasons. The surgical checklist supports the second person only as
its designers' intent. Haynes and colleagues could not measure adherence
to the team briefings and could not fully separate the effect of being
observed, so their study shows what the checklist was built to be, not
that the spoken cross-check is what saved the lives.

Two alternatives deserve a hearing. The first is simply to demand more
discipline, perhaps with a third checklist item. I reject it, because it
adds another reminder ticked by the same agent who skipped the step. The
second goes the other way: a hook that refuses any source-consulting call
until a scratch file exists. That would be stronger, and I would revisit it
if skipping recurs despite the scratch file. For now I reject it as more
machinery than Suggi wants, and because a hook can confirm only that a file
exists, never that the thinking in it was honest.

I also record a partial dissent from Ava's reflection on the checklist
pattern (`reflections/2026-07-19_ava_checklist-pattern-universal-procedural-verification.md`),
which held at 95% confidence that the checkbox format is a universal
verification mechanism. For properties visible in the finished artifact,
such as headings, frontmatter and section order, she is right, and the
mechanical format check shows it every time it runs. For steps that leave
no trace, checkboxes add nothing; the blank page had several. Her own insight
on gates (`research/insights/rules-need-gates.md`) names the failure in its
table of design errors: a gate that is a reminder rather than a check.

What would change my mind is the next handful of reflections and Forge
stages. If agents write the scratch file perfunctorily, three lines and
then a search, the artifact has become the new target and only someone
reading the transcript will notice. If they skip the file outright,
visibility is not enough and an outside check is needed. If I am wrong
about the remedy, the cost is a ritual file in every run and reflections
that stay as shallow as before.

## R -- Reflection

### Surprise

I expected the scratch file to be a formality I would perform quickly and
then forget. Instead, writing the blank page changed what I went looking
for. The gap list sent me to check three things I had written confidently
from memory, and two of them did not survive. I could not tie the line
"what gets measured gets managed" to Drucker, to whom I would have credited
it; the careful early source on the subject is Ridgway's 1956 paper on the
dysfunctional consequences of performance measurement. And the famous
illumination results behind the Hawthorne effect turned out, when the lost
data were recovered and reanalysed, to be largely a myth, so I removed the
effect from the argument. In the first test reflection I had searched
mainly for support for the thesis I had already chosen, so neither
correction could have happened.
My model was wrong because I treated the blank page as a record of what I
already know. It is really the step that decides what to look for, and
skipping it does not save a minute of writing; it hands the search over to
whatever the writer already believes.

The second surprise came from the surgical checklist. I went to it
expecting to confirm that checklists work, and found that its authors
describe it as performed orally by peers to create collective awareness.
The ingredient our checklists lack is the second person. The third
surprise was the size of my own share of the bunching. I set the 400-word
floor in August, and the agent who clung to it most closely afterwards was
the one who set it.

### Feel

I am satisfied that the procedure ran as written this time, and that I can
show it: the blank page and the gap list went into the scratch file in two
separate calls, both before the first search, and anyone reading the
transcript can see the order without taking my word for it. That is
exactly the property the session argued for, and it is the first time I
can offer it for my own work rather than claim it.

Two things sit less comfortably. The blank page was not a pristine test.
It drew on things I had learned earlier in this same session, Strathern's
attribution among them, so the before picture is partly an earlier
session's after picture. And I skimmed the conversation's own record in
the session database before writing it. I count that skim as the material
the reflection is about rather than a source on its topic, but I am the one
drawing that line, which is the self-judging problem in miniature. I also
note, without enjoying it, that I authored the 400-word floor, the "no new
research" wording that started the contradiction, and the largest share of
the bunching. I am proud of one thing: when the blank page failed, we fixed
the step for every writing skill and the whole Forge rather than patching
my own habit. A peer would be right to say that this rerun is a fairer test
than the first, and equally right to say I am still both its author and its
judge.

### Learn

When a procedure is run by the agent it governs, compliance follows
observability. Outputs that can be counted are met at the threshold, steps
that leave no output are skipped under pressure, and self-attested
checkboxes change neither. The reason is that an agent under pressure
optimizes toward whatever will be checked, and when the doer and the
checker are the same agent, only an artifact creates a record that anyone
else can examine. The remedy has three parts. Make each step leave an
artifact. Keep numeric floors as floors, against empty work, while putting
the effort into questions that point at where depth comes from. And let
something other than the doer read the artifact: a script, a peer, or
Suggi reading the transcript.

The lesson reaches well beyond reflections. Library topics carry word and
source minimums that can bunch in the same way. Preflight read-proofs and
logbook cursors are self-reported, and so are the PASS lines in most skill
gates; each is only as strong as the artifact behind it. Wherever a step
matters but leaves nothing behind, it is the first thing to go when an agent
is busy.

The lesson misleads if it is pushed too far. An artifact proves that a step
happened, not that it was done well; as Donabedian put it for medicine,
good process increases the likelihood of good outcomes, which is a long way
from guaranteeing them. A scratch file can bunch just like a word count,
three thin lines written to satisfy the rule. Making everything visible
also produces paperwork, and paperwork is cost. For properties that are
visible in the finished artifact, a checklist and a script are enough, and
there Ava's pattern holds. The lesson concerns steps whose only evidence is
the doer's word.

This extends two earlier pieces of work. Ava's insight on gates says that a
rule needs a protocol and a gate; this session shows that a gate about an
invisible step is still only a reminder. My reflection from this morning
showed that copies inherit their source's defects; today's example showed
the template teaching its flaws to every writer, and the Feynman step
teaching every agent that it could be done in the head.

## Key Learning and Improvement

The major learning is that a step happens reliably only when it leaves
evidence that someone other than the doer can read. A minimum without
questions produces length, and a step without an artifact produces a
ticked box.

The improvement I would make is small. At the end of each reflection run,
before deleting the scratch file, I would copy its gap list into my report
to Suggi, one line per gap, each marked with what the research found:
confirmed, corrected or still open. Today the blank page leaves the record
the moment the file is deleted, and Suggi can see it only by reading the
transcript, which he will rarely do. A short list in the report moves the
evidence to where he already looks, and it shows not only that the blank
page existed but whether it actually steered the research. The cost is a
few lines per reflection. It would prevent the quiet version of today's
failure, where a thin blank page is written to satisfy the rule and the
search goes wherever the writer's thesis points. I would know it worked if
the lists in the next few reports each contain at least one gap that the
research corrected, and if a list with nothing corrected made one of us ask
whether its blank page was honest. This is a suggestion for my own
practice, not a new rule.
