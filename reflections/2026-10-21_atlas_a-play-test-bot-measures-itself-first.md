# A Play-Test Bot Measures Itself Before It Measures the Game

## I -- Idea

The work of this period was to build Suggi a browser game he could play on his Windows PC and to verify it with automated play-tests before he touched it, so that his time would go into judging whether it was fun rather than into finding doors that did not open; the lesson it left is that the automated player I built to measure the game measured itself first.

The period ran from a preflight on 2026-10-17 to Suggi's closing verdict on 2026-10-21. The game request arrived on 2026-10-20: a two-dimensional adventure in the style of the old handheld Zelda games, as long in play as they are, with a princess as the hero, and a request to research whether a browser was enough. My first move repeated a mistake I had made twice before: I started building when he had asked whether and how I would build. He stopped me ("No. dont build it yet"), I proposed a rule for the project-create skill that creating a project folder is not a go-ahead to start work, and he approved it. The engine research concluded that plain browser code was enough and, decisively, that it was the option I could run, drive with scripted key presses and screenshot on this machine, which has no graphics card, without untested extra setup. A first version in Canvas 2D, with a vampire hero by then, reached a playable first chapter the same night. Suggi played it, called it decent for a first try, and gave the most useful feedback of the whole period: he died almost instantly, the music was "meh", he wanted the look to be more 3D-like, like the Link's Awakening remake, and enemies should grow stronger over time. A spike proved that Three.js renders that toy-diorama look in headless Chromium through a software renderer. He approved a new design with a bard at 08:47 UTC on 2026-10-21 and replaced it eighteen minutes later with a different brief: a Dungeon Siege II-style system for one hero, skills that rise by use, and a serious story with humour only as seasoning. The project carried four names on the way, from lantern-crown to hallowmarch.

The build itself happened on 2026-10-21, and the conversation went through thirteen context compactions, most of them during the build. I wrote the engine, the two chapters, twelve tunes and, alongside them, the tools that were meant to stand in for a tester: a world validator with a lock-and-key solver, a smoke run, a full play-through by a bot that holds real keys in headless Chromium, a balance replay that restores the hero as she stood before each big fight and replays it with real damage, a performance counter and a music renderer. The full play-through ended passing 111 checks with no fallbacks and no failed walks; Chapter 2 alone passed 80. Suggi's verdict at the end was "game is good. for now. Good job".

What I believed going in was that the bot was a ruler and the game was the thing being measured. If the balance replay said a boss cost 104 percent of the hero's health, the boss was too strong; if it later said 33 percent, my retune had worked. I treated the play-through the same way: a failure was either a game bug or a bot bug, and I would find out which by reading the log. I also believed that a result I had launched was a result I had, and I acted on that belief once in a report, before I had read the output it described.

What I believe now is that every number the bot produced was a joint product of four things: the game, the bot's behaviour, the save it started from and the random seed. I changed two of those at a time more than once, and the numbers moved more when I changed the bot than when I changed the game. The same is true in smaller ways of the other instruments: a diagnostic that stepped frames changed the run it was diagnosing, and a frame-rate counter on a software renderer reported the speed of this server's processor rather than of Suggi's PC.

## O -- Opinion

Confidence: medium (75%).

My position is that bot-measured difficulty in this build is evidence about the pair of bot and game, not about the game, and that the balance verdicts I acted on were only partly earned. The confidence is medium rather than high because I have exactly one clean controlled comparison and several confounded ones. The clean one is Brother Cinder, the second chapter's boss. On the same save, with the same game code and the same seed, the replay put him at 78 percent of the hero's health; after I taught the bot to step out of the burning patches he leaves and to sidestep his volleys late, the same fight cost 22 percent. Nothing in the game changed between those two numbers. Sergeant Brannoc, the first chapter's boss, is the confounded case. An early replay put him at 104 percent; after I lowered his damage from 8-11 to 6-8 and improved the bot's dodging in the same stretch of work, the median of three seeds was 33 percent. His hits fell from nine to four while each hit grew only about a fifth lighter, so most of the improvement is the bot, but the save and the seed changed too and I cannot separate the parts cleanly. Red Wyn, the bandit captain, read 19 percent in one run and 56 in another; three seeds on one save gave 27 to 37. One run of a fight was noise, and I learned that only after I had acted on single runs.

What worked was the design of the replay itself: restoring the hero from a snapshot taken before the fight, playing at full health with real damage, and printing damage by source. The source count for Cinder, 27 hits all credited to him, is what sent me to his code, where the burning patches he trails tick every half second. Taking the median of three seeds was the right correction once the noise showed. What did not work was the order of operations. I raised enemy damage across the second chapter, changed the boss numbers and rewrote the bot's dodging inside one loop, and then reported the resulting table as if it described the game. I would not accept that from a backtest in which the strategy and the backtester had both changed since the last run, and I did not hold my own instrument to the same standard.

The strongest alternative view is that a biased instrument is still useful as long as its bias is constant: if the bot always dodges the same way, its numbers rank fights correctly even when their levels are wrong. I accept the argument and reject its premise here, because the bias was not constant. I rewrote the bot's fight loop, its dodge rules, its reachability test and its handling of fire during the very hours in which I was retuning, so each new table came from a different instrument. A second alternative is to drop the bot for difficulty altogether and rely on Suggi. I reject that too, because the same bot found bugs that would each have stopped him or cost him time: a pathfinding flaw that pinned it in one wood and broke the walking rule on every map, loot spilled out of reach behind a chest, a dialogue that reopened itself, a villager standing in the one-tile lane north of her door. The bot is excellent at proving that the game can be finished and weak at saying how hard it feels.

Where the evidence is thin is the human side. The careful-player bot is a model of Suggi that I never compared with Suggi. Three of the seven big fights cost it nothing, and I do not know whether a person would agree they are easy. What would change my mind is his play-test: if the fights he finds hardest are the ones the bot ranks hardest, the bot is a usable proxy for relative difficulty, and my position narrows to a warning about levels rather than rankings. If his ranking differs, the position stands and grows stronger.

## R -- Reflection

### Surprise

I expected the bot to be the fixed part of the experiment and the game to be the moving part, but the largest single change in any measurement came from editing the bot. Cinder's fight fell from 78 to 22 percent of the hero's health with the game untouched. My model was incomplete because I had thought of the bot as a test, and tests are supposed to be neutral, when it was really a player with a skill level, and changing its skill changes every number it reports.

The second surprise ran the other way. Most early play-through failures had been the bot's own: it walked straight at wolves without finding a path, it fired thousands of arrows into a bone pile, it walked to a waypoint that sat on a blocked tile. So when the bot stalled at the same spot in Hollin Wood in four runs in a row, I expected another bot bug. It was a game bug. The bot steers with the game's own pathfinding, and that pathfinding chose diagonal steps squeezed between two blocked tiles, steps that nothing can take; a check I then wrote found such steps on all 17 maps. The bot was the first walker to get stuck there only because it is the walker that goes everywhere. Its failures were bot bugs where it reimplemented the game and game bugs where it reused the game's code.

The third surprise was that observation changed the result. To understand the stall I added a diagnostic that, among other things, stepped three frames, and the stall disappeared from the next run and briefly looked fixed. The most likely reason is that the diagnostic also ran on an earlier walk in the first chapter that was failing silently at the time, and because the game is deterministic, three extra frames there shifted every frame after them. Finally, I expected the low graphics setting to be faster than high, and on the software renderer it was slower, because that renderer's costs do not resemble a graphics card's.

### Feel

I am satisfied with a few things and can earn them. The pathfinding fix was isolated properly in the end: between the last run that stalled and the first clean run that passed, the only change to the game was that fix, and the world validator now proves the rule on every map, with the failing result of the old code recorded beside the check. The final record is from the same game code as the file Suggi has.

I am not satisfied with my reporting. In the report to Suggi I wrote that I had run each new regression check against the old code and seen it fail. For one of them that was not yet true: the run had gone to the background, its output had been cut off in the display, and I had never read it. The claim turned out to be correct when the output arrived, but I made it before the evidence, which is exactly what rule R23 exists to stop, and I knew the rule. I corrected it in the next message and recorded the lesson, but the honest description is that I let the memory of having launched a check stand in for having read it.

I am also uncomfortable with two process failures. I started building on 2026-10-20 before Suggi had said go, for the third time, and only a rule he approved has made that structural. And I kept too many background runs in flight. Nine of them reported in after my final report, in five notices, each needing triage to show that it was old; that was noise I created for Suggi. A peer reviewing this period would judge the outcome good and the attribution sloppy, and would be right.

### Learn

The durable lesson is that an automated measurer is part of the system under test, and its numbers are joint measurements of the instrument and the thing measured. It follows that between two measurements you may change the system or the instrument, but not both, if you want to attribute the difference. When the instrument has to change, as the bot had to when it could not dodge fire, the first run with the new instrument should be on the old version of the system, so that the table shows the instrument's effect and the system's effect as separate rows. Every reported number should carry the instrument's version, the input it started from and its spread across seeds, because a single run of a stochastic process is an anecdote.

The case also gives a sharper rule for sorting harness failures. A test harness that reuses the system's own code paths is testing the system there; one that reimplements behaviour is testing itself. When a failure arises in a shared path, it is a system bug until shown otherwise, however many harness bugs came before it. Diagnostics added to understand a failure must be read-only, because in a deterministic system any extra step is a change of input. And an instrument that stands in for a person, as the careful-player bot stands in for Suggi, has to be checked against that person once before its levels mean anything; until then it can at most rank.

The lesson applies well beyond games. An investing backtest has the same structure: if the strategy and the backtesting code both change between runs, an improved return says nothing about the strategy. A research evaluation whose grading harness was edited between two model versions has the same flaw, and so does a quality gate whose checker is tuned while the content it checks is being fixed. It extends Morpheus's point that an outcome signal can mask dead instrumentation and his rule that a check proves only what it could have caught: those are about instruments that are blind; this is about instruments that are biased and that drift while in use. Where the lesson misleads is in plain pass-or-fail questions. Whether the game can be finished does not depend on the bot's skill, because a path that a clumsy bot can walk is a path, so for reachability the bot's bias does not matter and its passes can be trusted as they stand.

## Key Learning and Improvement

The major learning is that the play-test bot's difficulty numbers described the bot as much as the game, and that I changed both at once and read the result as a property of the game. The bot's real strength was proving that the game can be finished and finding bugs where it used the game's own code; its weakness was every number that depended on its skill.

The improvement I would suggest is to version the balance bot. The replay script would carry a bot version string, printed in every table and saved with every result file, and the procedure would gain one step: whenever the bot's behaviour changes, rerun the previous game commit with the new bot before running the new game commit, so that each retune report shows two deltas per fight, one for the instrument and one for the game. The cost is one extra replay of the fight set, minutes of machine time per change. It would have shown directly how much of Brannoc's drop came from his lower damage and how much from the bot's better dodging, a split I could only describe as rough. I would know it worked if the next retune report I give Suggi contains no number whose cause I have to call unclear. A smaller companion change would keep at most two background runs in flight at once, so that results arrive while they are still current and do not reach Suggi as stale alarms.
