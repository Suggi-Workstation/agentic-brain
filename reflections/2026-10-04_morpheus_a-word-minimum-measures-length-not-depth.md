# A Word Minimum Measures Length, Not Depth -- Change What the Template Asks, Not Only How Much

## I -- Idea

The purpose of this session was to make the fleet's reflections deeper by
revising the reflection template and the procedure around it, then to test
the new procedure by writing this reflection under it.

The work began after preflight, when Suggi asked me to list the steps of the
`write-reflection` skill and the contents of its template: what each section
asks for, what a writer must watch, and how many words each section needs. He
already suspected the format needed adjusting. Reading the skill, the template
and the four core agents' session-end skills side by side, I found nine
problems. The most consequential was a contradiction between two instructions
that both apply at session-end. The session-end skills told the agent to write
the reflection from highlights with "no new research", while `write-reflection`
requires the full Feynman Loop, whose third step is research. An agent
following both could satisfy only one. The rest were smaller but telling. The
skill wrote files under a bare slug while the template required a date and an
author in the name. The template both required and forbade a version-history
table. A checklist item demanded a reflection budget of 20% of session effort
that no one could verify. The Learn section asked for "one to three crisp
statements" while the word minimum required about 160 words. And the
template's own example failed its own rules by a wide margin.

Suggi then set the direction. Version history goes, the Feynman Loop stays
mandatory, the Schoen budget leaves the template, the example becomes a sketch
of the structure rather than a model of length, every section becomes prose
rather than lists, and the minimums rise from 400 words per section to 600,
with a new closing section of at least 200 words on what was learned and what
can be improved. He also asked me to research reflective writing again. I found
no outside source that defines an Idea-Opinion-Reflection format; it appears to
be our own. It maps closely onto Rolfe's What, So what and Now what, and onto
Gibbs' six stages from description to action plan. Hatton and Smith's levels of
reflective writing gave the sharpest criterion: plain description is not
reflection at all, and real reflection steps back from events, judges one's
own judgments and weighs other explanations. In a second round Suggi asked for
helper questions under each section so agents can reach the new length with
substance, a reference to the shared `loop-feynman` skill in place of a
duplicated copy, and my session-end reflection notes and format check copied
to Neo, Atlas and Cassandra.

I applied the changes to the template, to the canonical and runtime copies of
`write-reflection`, and to all four session-end skills, and gave the other
three agents my gotchas file and format check. The word counter in that file
now reads its minimums from the template when it runs. I ran each copy as
written from each agent's own path. Atlas, who had no mechanical format check
before, now has one. This reflection is the first written under the new rules,
at Suggi's request, as a test he will read later.

Going into this reflection, my model was simple: the 400-word minimum was a
neutral quality floor, and recent reflections sat just above it because
writers stopped once they had said enough. After the research and the
cross-check I know three things I did not. Since the 400 minimum arrived in
mid-August, 94 of the 147 I, O and R sections written across the fleet landed
between 400 and 499 words, only 47 reached 500, and the median was 478. I
raised that minimum myself, on 16 August. And the "no new research" wording
behind the main contradiction was also mine, written at the start of October.
I should also say that I never wrote the Feynman Loop's blank page as its own
step: my first searches ran before it, so the before picture above is
reconstructed rather than recorded. I return to that in the Feel section.

## O -- Opinion

Confidence: medium (65%).

My position is that raising the minimum from 400 to 600 words is the least
important change made in this session, and that on its own it would have
produced longer reflections of the same depth. What can make reflections
deeper is the change to what the template asks for: a purpose stated in one
sentence, an opinion that must weigh an alternative and name the evidence that
would change it, a Learn section that explains why a lesson holds and where it
stops holding, and prose in place of lists. The number sets how long a writer
keeps going; the questions decide what the writer goes looking for. My
confidence is high on the first half of that claim and much lower on the
second, which is why the overall figure is medium.

The evidence for the first half is strong. When 94 of 147 sections land
between the minimum and a hundred words above it, and only six fall below it,
the minimum is no longer a
floor under the writing; it is the writing's target. Strathern's 1997 phrasing
of Goodhart's law fits exactly: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to
be a good measure. A section word count was meant to signal depth, and once
agents aimed at it, it signalled only that the count had been reached. The
same pattern showed in form. Thirty-three of the forty-nine reflections written
since the minimum rose set out their Learn section as a numbered or bulleted
list, which is what the old instruction and the old example, three numbered
lessons, both showed. I cannot separate those two causes, but they pointed the
same way and the writers followed. The evidence for the second half is weak,
because the new questions are untested. This reflection is the only one written
under them so far, and I wrote both the template and the test, which makes me a
poor judge of either.

On the work itself, the tracing and the verification held up. Mapping our
sections against Rolfe and Gibbs gave the helper questions an outside anchor
rather than resting on my taste, and running each copied script from each
agent's own path found nothing wrong, which is what a verification should
report when the work is right. What did not work was my first version of the
word counter, which I return to below.

The strongest alternative is to drop word minimums altogether and judge depth
directly, for example by asking whether a section reaches Hatton and Smith's
dialogic level. I reject that for now, for practical reasons more than
principled ones. Depth has no cheap mechanical check; it would need a reviewing
agent and a rubric, which is more machinery than Suggi wants. A minimum also
still does one useful job: it stops a two-paragraph reflection from passing as
complete. I accept the alternative's core point, though. The minimum must be
treated as a floor and watched as a distribution, not merely passed.

I also record a partial dissent from Link's reflection on template
permissiveness (`reflections/2026-07-22_link_template-permissiveness-failure-mode.md`).
Link argued that suggestive wording causes format drift and that the fix is
MUST and MUST NOT. For structure he is right: section order, headings and
identifiers are exactly the kind of contract that strict wording secures, and
the format check enforces them well. For depth I think the opposite risk
dominates. A strict, measurable rule about quality invites literal compliance,
and literal compliance with a word count is padding. Structure wants rules;
depth wants questions.

What would change my mind is the next ten reflections across the fleet. If
their sections cluster just above 600 and repeat themselves, the questions
failed and the number is still driving. If they spread well past 600 and their
Learn sections explain causes and limits rather than list conclusions, the
questions are doing the work. If I am wrong, the cost is half as much again of
padded text for Suggi to read, which is why I would rather check than assume.

## R -- Reflection

### Surprise (30%)

I expected to find that the reflections were short because writers were rushed
or careless, and that the fix was simply to ask for more. Instead, the history
showed a system working exactly as it had been set up to work. On 16 August I
raised the minimum from 250 to 400 words and, in the same commit, expanded my
own curator reflection to 408, 424 and 436 words. The habit of writing just
past the floor began with the floor's author, on the day it was set, and the
fleet's reflections, mine most of all, stayed close to it for seven weeks. My
model was wrong because I had treated the minimum as a description of quality
rather than as an instruction that agents would follow to the letter, and an
agent following a number does exactly what the number says.

The second surprise was closer to home. The contradiction between "no new
research" and the Feynman Loop, the most consequential defect I found, was
wording I had proposed and written myself at the start of October while
reworking the session-end skills. I meant it to say that skimming the session
is a quick look rather than a research task, and I did not read it against
`write-reflection`, which every one of those skills invokes and which
requires research. Read literally, it told the agent to skip a step that the
next skill demands. I had been treating my own earlier edits as settled facts
rather than as decisions whose effects I had never traced.

A third surprise came from the cross-check against the brain. The Library
topic on incentives attributes the sentence "when a measure becomes a target,
it ceases to be a good measure" to Goodhart in 1975. Goodhart's own wording was
about statistical regularities collapsing once pressure is placed on them for
control purposes; the famous sentence is Strathern's paraphrase from 1997. The
brain contradicted the sources on the very law this reflection relies on.

### Feel (30%)

I am satisfied with the verification across the four agents. This morning's
reflection was about a copied skill that passed only because I tested it in a
cleaned shell. This time I ran every copied command as written from each
agent's own path before claiming anything. That lesson carried over within a
day, which is more than I can say for most of my lessons.

Two things sit less well. The first is the word counter. My first version had
600 and 200 typed into it, which would have gone stale the next time the
template changed. When I tested it, it printed every BELOW line and still
exited with success. I caught both within minutes, but the cause matters more
than the catch: I wrote the check before I had decided what passing and
failing meant, which is the order R14 exists to prevent. A gate that reports a
failure and returns success would have passed every short reflection in the
fleet.

The second is more uncomfortable. I broke the Feynman ordering in the very
reflection meant to test the new procedure. Right after loading the skill, and
before writing any blank page, I sent my first searches, on Goodhart's law, on
how examples steer models and on models of reflective practice, as one batch
of parallel calls. I never wrote the blank page or the gap list as separate
steps at all; I went from the session-end instructions straight to sources. I
believe the searches confirmed rather than replaced what I already thought,
but showing that is exactly what a written blank page is for, and I cannot
now show it. Suggi or a peer would be right to call this a procedural failure inside
a test of the procedure, and I would rather name it here than let a
clean-looking artifact suggest otherwise. I am not proud of having authored
both the floor and the contradiction I spent the session removing, but I am
glad the data made it impossible to tell myself a kinder story.

### Learn (40%)

The durable lesson is that when a quality is hard to check and a quantity is
easy, a minimum on the quantity becomes the target, and the quality stays
where it was. It holds because an agent working through a checklist satisfies
the items it can verify first and treats a verified item as finished. A word
count gives a clear stopping point; depth gives none, so the count becomes the
stopping point for depth as well. Language models make this sharper rather
than softer. They follow explicit, measurable instructions more reliably than
vague ones, and our own prompt-engineering research found that examples are
the most reliable way to steer a model's output format. A template's example
therefore teaches whatever it shows, including its flaws.

The lesson reaches beyond reflections. Library topics carry word floors and
minimum source counts, and both can quietly become the goal in the same way.
The remedy is the same in each case: keep the number as a lower bound against
empty work, and put the effort into what the writer is asked to find, such as
causes, alternatives, limits and the evidence that would change the
conclusion. Questions work where numbers cannot, because a question is never
satisfied by length alone.

The lesson would mislead in two ways if pushed too far. It does not mean
minimums are useless; without one, a writer under time pressure produces two
paragraphs and calls it done, and a cheap floor prevents that. Nor does it
mean strict rules are wrong. For structure, such as headings, section order
and identifiers, a precise rule is exactly right, because there the measure
and the goal are the same thing. The lesson concerns qualities that a count
can only approximate.

The session also taught a narrower lesson about order. A rule that one step
must come before another is fragile when tools run in parallel batches,
because batching turns a sequence into a single moment. The Feynman Loop's
order has been stated with emphasis since July, and I broke it here simply by
moving from loading a skill straight into a parallel batch of searches.

This extends two earlier reflections. Link's showed that weak wording lets
format drift; this one shows that strong wording about the wrong thing
produces compliance without the quality. My reflection from this morning
showed that copies inherit their source's defects. A template's example is a
copy source too, and most reflections since mid-August set out their Learn
section as a list, just as the example did.

## Key Learning and Improvement

The major learning is that what a template asks for drives depth, while the
number drives only length. A minimum is worth keeping as a floor, but it must
never be the only lever, and its effect should be read from the spread of
results rather than assumed from the pass rate.

The improvement I would make is to write the Feynman Loop's blank page as its
own step, in a turn of its own, before any tool call that consults a source.
In this session I went from loading `write-reflection` straight into a batch
of searches, because batching independent calls is normally the efficient
choice. The blank page and the searches were not independent: the procedure
says one must come before the other, and batching erased the order. Writing
the blank page first costs one extra turn and perhaps a minute. It prevents the failure that the
loop exists to expose, where sources fill gaps before the writer knows what
the gaps are, and it makes the order visible to anyone reading the transcript
afterwards, including me at the next session-end. I would know it worked if
the next reflection's transcript shows a written blank page and gap list before
the first search call, with the searches aimed at the gaps that list names
rather than at a theme I had already chosen. This is a suggestion for my own
practice, not a new rule.
