# A Source File I Write Is a Claim, Not a Source

## I -- Idea

This run set out to review Prosus's results for the half-year to 30 September 2026, revalue the company, and carry the new value into every file that depends on it, so that Suggi, who holds the Amsterdam shares, would find a current and checked view after the results instead of the one I published on 10 November. It was a scheduled job, one of the one-time results jobs Suggi asked me to keep for each holding's reporting date, and it ran without anyone present: preflight, the review and revaluation, a learnings capture, this closure and a short report to Bot Chat.

The context matters for what follows. The 10 November valuation put Prosus at Bear, Base and Bull values of 38, 61 and 86 euros a share against a price of 37.34, built as a sum of parts: the Tencent stake at my own estimate of Tencent's value, the other assets at a discount to the company's published marks, less net debt and ten years of head-office costs. The Tencent job that should have run on 18 November, after Tencent's third quarter, had failed on a weekly provider limit, as had the Alibaba job the next day, so this run also had to bring Tencent's third quarter into the Tencent estimate, since that estimate supplies most of the Prosus value. Between the two dates the market moved hard: Prosus closed at 30.79 euros on 24 November and Tencent at 341.60 Hong Kong dollars, both about 18% lower than on 9 November.

The steps were the ones the job prompt and the company-research skill lay down. I first reproduced the published valuation from the old task script found in the Trash and asserted that it returned 61.15, 85.72 and 37.93, so that any change could be traced to new inputs rather than to a changed method. I then fetched the interim statements, the results release, the company's asset summary dated 25 November, the distribution notice, Tencent's third-quarter release, the price histories and the ECB's ninety-day reference rates. I rebuilt the Tencent estimate on nine months of non-IFRS profit at an annual rate, kept the growth and multiple cases, and rebuilt the Prosus sum of parts on the new asset summary and share count. I rewrote the Investing Hub file through the gate tools with thirteen seeded errors run ahead of the commit, and all thirteen were caught. I then updated the Prosus and Tencent knowledge notes with a section each on the period, the off-book note's citation, the holding line in the private portfolio file and the daily brief's value markers.

The finding on the company is short. The half confirmed both halves of the thesis I wrote in November. Prosus kept buying its own shares, US$2.5 billion in the half, at about 78% of the market value of the Tencent shares each Prosus share represents, so each sale of Tencent to fund the buyback left the remaining owners with more Tencent per share, about 0.984 shares on 20 November against 0.978 at the end of September. At the same time the food-delivery businesses took the cash the profitable businesses made: iFood's adjusted EBITDA fell from US$184 million to US$28 million against new competitors, and the group's free cash flow without the Tencent dividend was an outflow of US$339 million. Tencent's third quarter showed its core still growing while the new AI products cost more operating profit than in the second quarter, and capital expenditure rose about sixfold from a year earlier. The new values are 38, 62 and 87 euros, almost unchanged, while the price fell 18%.

What I believed going in was that the risk in such a run sat in the judgment: the growth cases, the weights on unlisted marks, the treatment of AI spending. What I believe now is that the most dangerous error of the run was in a file I created myself in the first hour, the exchange-rate table, and that no gate downstream of it could have found it, because every gate treated that file as a source.

## O -- Opinion

Confidence: medium (70%) in the valuation and the verdict; high (85%) in the process lesson.

My position on Prosus is that the verdict should stay where it was: undervalued relative to Base, with medium confidence, and a holding whose return depends less on what Tencent is worth than on whether management keeps converting the discount into Tencent per share. The half supports that view on both sides. The buyback did what it was supposed to do, and the food-delivery spending did what the contrary case feared. Neither changes the arithmetic much, because the Tencent stake is so much larger than the businesses that consumed cash. The price fell 18% in a fortnight while my values barely moved, and the reason is visible in the record: the Tencent price fell by the same proportion, so Prosus traded as a proxy for Tencent's price, not for anyone's estimate of Tencent's value. At 30.79 euros the price sits below my Bear value and below the market value of the Tencent shares alone after all debt and capitalized costs, about 35 euros. I would not call that a margin of safety, and the Hub file does not, because the values rest on an earnings proxy for Tencent and because a holding-company discount closes only through a distribution or a buyback that Naspers controls.

The confidence is medium and not high for a reason the third quarter made sharper. My Tencent estimate takes non-IFRS profit as owner earnings, while Tencent's free cash flow for the quarter was negative 69.1 billion renminbi. The estimate is honest about this in its label, but the gap between the two measures is now large enough that a reader could mistake a stable value for a stable business. The alternative approach, valuing Tencent on free cash flow, would give a far lower number today and treat a capital programme as a permanent loss. I reject it for the Base case because the spending is a choice whose returns are unknown rather than known to be zero, and I keep it as the Bear case's charge of one year of spending earning half its cost. What would change my mind is a second and third quarter in which the new AI products keep widening their losses while capital spending stays at the third-quarter rate; then the Bear case becomes the central one.

On the process, my position is firmer. The run went well where the gates were built for it and nearly went badly where they were not. The ledger caught two sensitivity figures I had typed into the draft before computing them, 70 and 56 euros where the computed values were 65 and 59, and three rounding slips. The knowledge-note gate caught a rebuild that silently deleted three sections of the Tencent note because I had cut to the wrong heading. Those are the gates doing their job. The exchange-rate error was different in kind. I parsed the ECB's XML with a regular expression whose lookahead let one day's block supply the rates for every date, so the table I saved as my FX source showed the same rates for 9, 17, 20 and 24 November. With those rates the Base value came out at 57.86 euros instead of 61.93. Every check I ran afterwards would have passed it: the ledger recomputed from the same inputs, the figure check would have found the same numbers in the same saved file, and the seeds tested the report against that file. I caught it only because I printed four dates side by side to bind them and saw identical rows.

An alternative view is that this is an ordinary bug, fixed and done, and that writing it up as a lesson overstates it. I disagree because of where it sat. A bug in a calculation is caught by a second calculation. A bug in the file that every later check uses as ground truth is invisible to all of them, and the only defence is to check that file against the raw by a different route before anything is built on it.

## R -- Reflection

### Surprise

I expected the decisive uncertainty of the run to be Tencent: how much the third quarter would move the estimate, and whether the AI losses would force a change of method. Instead the Tencent estimate moved little, from 508 to 498 Hong Kong dollars at Base, and the largest single swing of the whole run came from a parsing step I had treated as clerical. The regex had looked right and printed plausible numbers: an exchange rate of 1.1645 dollars to the euro looked like an ordinary rate, and nothing in the output looked wrong until I put dates next to each other. My model of risk ranked tasks by how much judgment they need, and the parse needed none, so I gave it no check at all.

The second surprise was how cleanly the price and the value separated. I had expected the price fall since 9 November to reflect something in the results or a widening of the discount. The record shows instead that Prosus and Tencent fell by almost the same 18%, while the published net asset value per share and my Base value stayed close to where they were. The discount to the published net asset value went from about 39% to about 42%. The market repriced Tencent, and Prosus followed it, which is what a holding company's price does in the short run whatever its owners believe about the parts.

A smaller surprise was the gate catching my typed sensitivities. I knew the skill tells me to compute before drafting, and I believed I had: the two numbers I typed were plausible guesses written while the model was still open in another window, and they were wrong by five euros. The ledger existed precisely for this and it worked, which I note because the same habit produced the FX error, one step earlier, where no ledger was watching.

### Feel

I am satisfied with the review itself. It answers the questions the job asked: the segments, the cash flow, the buyback price against the value of the Tencent shares, the discount, the acquisitions, the capital repayment and its election window. Every figure in the Hub file and both notes passed a scripted check, and forty seeded errors across the three files were all caught before their commits. The off-book table now values each unlisted business from its own profit or a transaction and shows that only about five euros a share of the other assets are supported by a price or current profit, against nine counted at Base, which is the honest shape of the problem.

I am uncomfortable that the FX error got as far as a draft valuation. It did not reach a commit, but it reached the numbers I was writing, and if I had not printed those four dates together it would have reached the Hub, the notes, the portfolio line and the brief's markers, all consistent with each other and all wrong by about seven percent. That is the worst kind of error for Suggi, because the consistency across files would have made it look checked. I also typed figures before computing them, which the skill forbids and which the ledger caught; the pattern, plausible numbers written ahead of their record, is the same one my October reflections describe, and it recurs because typing a plausible number is faster than printing one.

I hesitated over one decision and think the choice was right. The Tencent job had failed, and the prompt allowed the Tencent note to change only if its range moved. The range did move, so I added a short third-quarter section and the new values to the Tencent note rather than leaving it at the old estimate while the Prosus file used the new one. Leaving two notes on two different Tencent values would have been the kind of stale cross-reference Suggi checks for.

### Learn

The durable lesson is that a file I derive and then cite is a claim, not a source, and it must be checked against the raw by a route that does not share its method before anything is built on it. Every gate I have reads saved files as ground truth: the ledger binds phrases to them, the figure check matches numbers against them, and the seeds test the report against them. Those checks protect the step from saved file to report. They say nothing about the step from raw download to saved file when I perform that step myself, because the same mistake that wrote the file is then the reference every later check consults. A wrong derived file therefore produces a set of outputs that agree with each other perfectly, and agreement among outputs is exactly what my gates are designed to reward.

The reason this holds is structural. A check detects an error only when it compares two things that could differ. A recomputation from the same inputs, a match against the same file, or a seed planted downstream of the file all compare the error with itself. The useful comparison is with the raw through an independent path: here, reading each date's block by locating the date in the raw text, rather than by the pattern that produced the table, and failing if rates repeat across dates. That is what the new `ecb.py` in the company-research gate tools does, and it reproduces the old 9 November table exactly while rejecting a seeded file in which every date carries the same rates.

The lesson applies wherever I transform a source before citing it: a PDF table flattened to text, a two-column filing whose columns interleave, a spreadsheet saved as text, a price page cut to one row. In this same run the interim statements' two-column layout interleaved sentences in the layout text, and I produced a column-ordered copy to quote from; that copy too is a derived file, and its safeguard was that every quote had to match it verbatim and every figure had to match the quote, which tests the copy against itself. The safer form is the one the FX parser now has: a second extraction route and an assertion that both agree on the values that matter.

It would mislead to read this as distrust of every saved file. A file fetched and saved byte for byte is a source; the risk begins where my code reshapes it. It would also mislead to think the fix closes the class. It closes one instance with a tool, and the class stays open wherever I write a parser in a hurry. The practical rule is to give every derived input file one check against the raw by a different method, and to put that check in the task's gate rather than in my attention.

## Key Learning and Improvement

The major learning is that a gate certifies the path from its reference to its output, and when I write the reference myself the gate certifies nothing about the reference. In this run the exchange-rate table I generated was wrong in a way that made every later number consistent and wrong, and the checks that caught other errors could not see it, because they all read that table as truth.

The improvement I would suggest is to make the check of derived inputs part of the gate script rather than a habit. For exchange rates it now is: `ecb.py` reads each date twice by different routes and fails on repeated rates, and the company-research skill points to it instead of to the raw XML. The next step I would propose is a small `derived_inputs` stage at the top of each task's gate, run before the ledger: for each file under `src/` that is not a byte-for-byte download, one assertion that a decisive value in it matches the raw by an independent read, for example the price row against the page's own table, the rate row against the XML block, and a column-ordered filing text against the layout text for three figures. The cost is a few lines per task and a few seconds per run. It would have caught this run's error before any valuation was drafted. I would know it worked if, over the next ten results jobs, every derived file carries such an assertion and no valuation is ever revised because an input table was wrong.
