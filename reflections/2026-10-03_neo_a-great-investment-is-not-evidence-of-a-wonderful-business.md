# A Great Investment Is Not Evidence of a Wonderful Business

## I -- Idea

A famous investor's large gain is not evidence that the business was wonderful, so a company profile must answer the investment question and the business question separately, and the business question must start from no.

Since this morning's closure, the work has been nine company research cycles on famous value-investing cases and a change to how company files are written. After the first three, American Express, Teledyne and Valeant, Suggi raised the section word floors and asked for a new section. My first heading asked why a company was a value-investing case. He pointed out that most companies are ordinary, then asked for the question Buffett and Munger would ask, answered critically. The section became "Is It a Wonderful Business?", placed after Simple Evaluation with a floor of 200 words. It starts from no and tests four traits, each shown, absent or unproven: a moat that protects returns, high returns on capital, little need for new capital or reinvestment at similar returns, and able, owner-minded management. Price, a famous owner or a cheap purchase does not count.

Six cycles ran under that rule, two for each investor Suggi named: See's Candies and The Washington Post for Buffett, BYD and East West Bancorp for Li Lu, Progressive and Fastenal for Sequoia. The verdicts varied. See's is wonderful in Buffett's narrow sense, but it is a cash generator that stopped growing after 2007. Progressive is a qualified yes, because its advantage is pricing skill that must be re-earned every year in a cyclical industry. Fastenal was wonderful while it could open stores at high returns and is now a very good business with a shorter runway. The Washington Post was wonderful in 1973 and is ordinary today. BYD and East West are not. Every file passed a strict quote check against saved filings and letters, and two general notes gained cases from this round: when-to-sell from the Washington Post and reinvestment-economics from Fastenal.

Before the cycles, my picture was that the signature holdings of Buffett, Li Lu and Sequoia were mostly wonderful businesses bought at sensible prices, with the investor's return as the proof. After them, each return broke into different parts. BYD's came from a low price, an exceptional founder and a market that grew several hundredfold; its net margin never exceeded 5.2% from 2020 to 2025, and its operating cash flow leaned on paying suppliers late. East West was a good bank bought during the 2023 panic at roughly 1.3 times book; its return on equity was negative in 2008 and 2.37% in 2009. Much of Berkshire's Washington Post gain came from Katharine Graham's repurchases at bargain prices, and the franchise behind it was gone by 2012.

The cross-check against the Brain found one conflict. The See's case study, `library/case-studies/berkshire-sees-candies-acquisition.md`, gives roughly 10 times after-tax earnings for the 1972 purchase; the letters' own figures give about 12. The company file records the discrepancy; the Brain file, written by another author, is unchanged.

## O -- Opinion

Confidence: medium (70%). The six cases point the same way, but they were chosen for fame, which selects for winners, and the verdicts are my judgments from filings rather than outcomes later events have tested. I may have been too harsh on East West, whose 2023 test was real evidence, or too generous to Progressive, whose moat is a skill rather than a structure.

My position is that the wonderful-business question should be answered independently of the investment record, and that its default must be no. An owner's return has three separable sources: the price paid, the operator and market growth, and the economics of the business. Only the third is what Buffett and Munger mean by wonderful. When a profile lets the first two vouch for the third, it teaches the reader to follow famous names instead of economics. BYD shows this most clearly. Li Lu bought an under-capitalized company run by an engineer of rare ability, at a price he described as giving a margin of safety, and sales then grew several hundredfold. None of that requires a moat, and BYD's own annual report describes price wars squeezing margins. The right lesson from BYD is about backing an operator at a low price, not about recognizing a wonderful business.

The second part of my position concerns method. I think the heading change mattered more than the word floors. A floor makes a section longer; it does not make the judgment harder. Twice a Risks section came in short, and each time I added a real risk the filings supported, Kaplan's UK visa licences and East West's funding cost, so the floor produced substance only because more evidence existed. The question framing sets the default the writer argues from. It is my speculation, not something I tested by writing each file both ways, that a heading asking why a company is a good case would have produced a success story for BYD.

The third part is that the strict quote check was the gate that actually improved accuracy, because building a quote forced me back to the source for every sentence. Three details I had supplied from memory failed there. Fastenal's chief executive had already announced his departure. BYD's origin as a battery maker was not in the sources I held. And my first draft implied that East West's lower costs gave it better returns than JPMorgan Chase, which its 2025 figures do not show. The word validator could not have caught any of these. This confirms what I told Suggi when he asked about depth: word counts measure length, and depth comes from sourcing.

I would change my mind if a larger and less fame-selected sample showed that celebrated owners' best results usually did coincide with wonderful businesses, so that the separation added little in practice.

## R -- Reflection

### Surprise (30%)

I expected the signature holdings of these three investors mostly to pass the wonderful-business test, with BYD the likely exception. Instead two of six failed outright, one passed only for its past, and the cleanest pass, See's, had stopped growing: its 2011 sales were below 2007, and its average pre-tax profit for 2008 to 2014 was below the 2007 level. The best business in the sample was the one that could not use more capital.

I also expected the strict quote check to catch formatting faults, such as citation placement and mismatched source numbers. It did catch those, twice for a sentence with too many citations. But its larger effect was on facts. The details that failed while I built its quotes were plausible things I had written from memory, and plausibility had carried them into the draft.

### Feel (30%)

I am satisfied that the verdicts were critical. Saying no to BYD and East West, both chosen by an investor I respect, was the honest reading of the filings, and I did not soften it to fit the owner's reputation.

I am less satisfied with how I drafted. I wrote first and verified afterwards, so three remembered details reached a draft before the evidence check removed them. The over-cited sentence error happened twice, in See's and then in East West. The first time I split the offending sentence and moved on, which fixed the symptom; only at this closure did I change the drafting rule in the capture skill so the class is prevented. Two Risks sections also missed their floor on the first pass, which suggests I was writing toward the section's shape rather than toward the evidence. Each gap was filled with a sourced risk, not padding, but a second pass should not have been needed.

### Learn (40%)

1. When a profile asks whether a business is wonderful, split the owner's return into price paid, operator and market growth, and business economics, and answer only from the last. A famous owner, a large gain or a cheap purchase is evidence about the investment, not the business.
2. A moat has a date. Test durability by stating when and why a trait was lost: the Washington Post's franchise weakening was named in 1991 and the newspaper was losing money by 2012. Test the reinvestment runway with unit counts, not margins: Fastenal's stores grew from 897 to 2,369 between 2000 and 2009, then openings slowed to a trickle while operating margins stayed near 20%.
3. Write only what a saved source says, and pair each sentence with its quote at the time of writing, with no more than three citations per sentence. That turns the evidence check from an audit after the fact into a constraint on drafting, so remembered details fail before they enter a file.

## One Actionable Change

I would build each company file's evidence spec sentence by sentence while drafting, putting the start and end needle beside each claim as it is written. A remembered detail or an over-cited sentence would then fail at the moment it is written rather than after the whole draft exists. This is offered as a suggestion; the capture skill now carries the narrower rule about citations per sentence and checking details against saved text.
