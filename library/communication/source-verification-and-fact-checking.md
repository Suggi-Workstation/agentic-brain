# Source Verification and Fact-Checking -- Accuracy Requires a Traceable Method Before Publication

Source verification is the disciplined process of testing whether each publishable claim is supported by identifiable, relevant, and sufficiently independent evidence. It cannot certify absolute truth, but a repeatable workflow can show what was checked, what the available evidence supports, what remains uncertain, and how errors will be corrected [4][5][6][7].

## Background

Verification and fact-checking are related but not identical practices. Verification is the broader activity of establishing the reliability of information while reporting, researching, or editing. Fact-checking is a specific application: a claim is isolated, evidence is gathered, and the claim is compared with that evidence. Professional fact-checkers often examine statements that are already public, while editorial fact-checkers test a draft before publication. The timing changes the immediate task, but not the core questions: What exactly is being asserted? Where did the information originate? What evidence could support or contradict it? How independent are the confirming sources? What conclusion does the evidence justify [3][4][5]?

Accuracy has long been treated as a defining journalistic obligation, yet the methods used to attain it have not always been standardized. Shapiro, Brin, Bedard-Brule, and Mychajlowycz interviewed 28 Canadian newspaper journalists about the verification of one completed story each. The journalists described source triangulation, primary documents, observation, and other practices, but the researchers found substantial variation and pragmatic compromise. Concrete details such as names and numbers often received more checking than other factual statements, while some attributed statements rested on a single person's word. The study therefore distinguished a strong professional commitment to verification from consistent methodological discipline [1].

Digital publishing increased both the available evidence and the number of ways evidence can be stripped of context. A copied quotation may lose the question that prompted it. A statistic may travel from a data table to a press release, from the release to a news report, and from the report to a social post. A genuine photograph may acquire a false date or caption. An authentic document may be incomplete, superseded, or falsely described. The Verification Handbook and the Berkeley Protocol respond to this environment by treating verification as a combination of source assessment, provenance tracing, content analysis, technical inspection, and external corroboration rather than as a single tool or intuition [9][10][11].

Professional standards increasingly make the method visible. The International Fact-Checking Network requires signatories to identify significant evidence, prefer suitable primary sources, check key elements against more than one named source when possible, explain methodology, present evidence on both sides of a claim, and maintain an open corrections policy [5]. Full Fact similarly states that it links to primary sources wherever possible, aims for at least two sources for the central claim unless only one relevant source exists, contacts the claimant when necessary, and has another researcher review each article before publication [4]. These rules shift verification from an individual reputation to a process that a reader or editor can inspect.

Major news organizations express the same principle in different forms. The Associated Press requires reporters using other people's material to judge its factual and contextual accuracy, credits underlying sources, restricts anonymous sourcing, and labels factual corrections as corrections [6]. The BBC states that information, documents, and digital material must be checked to achieve due accuracy; if material cannot be verified, the output should usually say so and attribute it. The BBC also places due accuracy above speed in news and current affairs and requires serious errors to be corrected quickly, clearly, and appropriately [7]. AFP's editorial standards require accurate context, transparent sourcing, skepticism toward claims supplied by newsmakers, verification of visual material, and prompt, transparent correction of errors [8].

These standards do not imply that every claim can be settled. Some statements are opinions, predictions, value judgments, or claims so vague that no observable evidence could decide them. Some checkable claims remain unresolved because records are missing, sources conflict, or the relevant event cannot be reconstructed. A responsible workflow therefore needs conclusions such as supported, contradicted, misleading, and insufficient evidence rather than forcing every question into true or false. The uncertainty must attach to a specified claim and reason: missing data, imprecise measurement, disputed interpretation, source dependence, or a changing evidence base [11][12].

The practical aim is not maximal skepticism toward everything. It is calibrated scrutiny. The strongest checks go to claims that are consequential, surprising, central to the publication, difficult to reverse, or vulnerable to distortion. Names, dates, quotations, calculations, captions, and links may be mechanically simple but reputationally costly when wrong. Causal explanations and broad generalizations need a different form of checking because agreement on individual facts does not prove that the facts warrant the stated conclusion. Verification is therefore both granular and structural: each component must be supportable, and the components must add up to an accurate overall representation [4][6][7][17].

## Core Concepts

### Define the Claim Before Searching

A check begins with a proposition precise enough for evidence to bear on it. The sentence "the policy failed" is not yet a useful checking unit because failure could mean cost overrun, missed deadlines, no measurable effect, or political rejection. A verifiable version identifies the policy, outcome measure, population, place, and period. Statistics, dates, identities, quotations, document contents, and observable events are usually more directly checkable than motives, moral judgments, or forecasts [4][5].

Complex sentences should be decomposed into atomic claims. If a sentence says a program was the first of its kind, cost less than its predecessor, and improved outcomes by 20 percent, those are three checks with different evidence requirements. Nieminen and Sankari's analysis of PolitiFact shows why this matters: assigning one verdict to several propositions can conceal that one part is supported, another disputed, and another false [14]. Claim decomposition also exposes hidden premises. A percentage increase may be mathematically correct while the selected baseline makes the conclusion misleading. Full Fact explicitly checks underlying assumptions because accurate components can be assembled into an inaccurate implication [4].

The checker should preserve the original wording and context before paraphrasing it. Record who made the claim, when, where, to which audience, and in response to what. A qualification such as "among surveyed respondents" cannot be dropped when the claim is rewritten as a statement about a whole population. The initial record is the control against which later interpretation is tested [4][5].

### Trace Evidence Upstream

Source tracing moves from a circulating assertion toward the earliest accessible evidence. A news article quoting a press release is not independent confirmation of the study described in that release. A cluster of websites repeating one wire report is one evidentiary line, not many. The upstream source may be an official data table, statute, court filing, research article, recording, photograph, database, direct witness, or original document. Primary evidence is not automatically correct, but it reduces the transformations between the evidence and the claim and allows the checker to inspect context directly [4][5][9].

A useful evidence chain records at least four layers: the claim being checked, the immediate source that supplied it, the upstream evidence on which that source relied, and the passage or field that supports the claim. This chain should also record version, date, page, table, timestamp, and access route where relevant. The chain makes two common errors visible. A source error occurs when the citation points to the wrong item or cannot be located. A content error occurs when the cited item exists but does not support the assertion attributed to it. Mogull's review of quotation accuracy in medical research distinguishes these error types and shows that citation presence alone does not establish citation fidelity [16].

Primary and secondary sources answer different questions. A primary record may establish what a government agency reported, what a speaker said, or what a study found. A careful secondary source may explain limitations, compare interpretations, or identify evidence the primary publisher omitted. The strongest workflow uses the primary item for the direct proposition and qualified secondary analysis for context. If only secondary evidence is available, the publication should identify that limitation instead of presenting a repetition chain as direct observation [4][5].

### Evaluate the Source Laterally

Source evaluation should not depend only on a site's appearance or its own description of itself. Wineburg and McGrew call the expert alternative lateral reading: leave the page, open other sources, identify who controls or funds the publisher, and examine what credible independent sources say about it [2]. This method separates the evaluation of a source from the evaluation of a particular claim. A normally reliable institution can make an error, while a partisan or unfamiliar source can link to a genuine document. Source reputation changes the prior level of scrutiny; it does not replace claim-level evidence.

The check should examine access to the relevant facts, expertise, incentives, record of correction, and transparency. A witness may have direct access but a limited field of view. A company may know its own sales figures but have an incentive to present them selectively. A domain expert may interpret research well but lack first-hand knowledge of the specific event. An anonymous source may possess necessary information, yet anonymity prevents readers from independently evaluating identity and motive. AP and IFCN standards therefore treat transparency, relevant interests, and exceptional anonymous sourcing as part of the evidence assessment [5][6].

Authority is claim-specific. A central bank is authoritative for the figures it publishes, not for every causal interpretation of the economy. An original poster can establish that an account published a statement, but not that the statement is true. A document's letterhead can indicate origin, but not authenticity by itself. Lateral evaluation asks both "Who is this?" and "Why should this source know this particular thing?" [2][9].

### Corroborate Through Independent Paths

Corroboration means testing a claim through evidence that does not merely inherit the same error. Two reports based on one spokesperson are one path. Two eyewitnesses who coordinated their accounts may also be dependent. Independence is therefore more important than raw source count. Full Fact and the IFCN generally seek at least two sources for central claims while recognizing that a unique record may sometimes be the only relevant source [4][5]. The purpose of a second source is not ritual agreement; it is a different route by which the claim could fail or survive.

Triangulation can combine evidence types. A claimed event might be tested against a direct recording, contemporaneous public records, and an independent witness. A claimed location might be tested against landmarks, maps, shadows, weather, and other images. A statistical claim might be tested by reproducing the calculation, comparing an official table, and asking a subject-matter expert whether the denominator and comparison are appropriate. Convergence across methods is usually stronger than repetition within one method [9][10][11].

Contradiction should not be averaged away. When credible sources disagree, identify the disputed element, compare their definitions and time frames, and determine whether they are measuring the same thing. A revised dataset may supersede an early estimate. Two casualty counts may use different inclusion rules. Two studies may concern different populations. The conclusion should reflect the narrowest proposition actually supported after those differences are resolved, or it should remain unsettled [4][7][12].

### Verify Quotations as Both Words and Claims

A quotation check has two layers. First, did the speaker use these words? Second, does the quotation accurately represent what the speaker meant in context? The strongest record is an original audio or video file or an official transcript checked against the full exchange. The checker should compare wording, speaker identity, timestamp, surrounding question, omitted clauses, editing, and any translation. A clip can be authentic and still misleading if its beginning or end changes the meaning [8][9][10].

Exact quotation does not transfer the truth of the quoted assertion. AFP's standards make this distinction explicit by requiring journalists to challenge facts supplied by a quoted newsmaker rather than treating correct attribution as verification [8]. If a source says that spending doubled, the recording can prove the source said it; the relevant accounts are still needed to test the number. A publication should distinguish "X said Y" from "Y is true."

Pre-publication review should not let a source rewrite history. The Association of Health Care Journalists' practitioner guidance recommends checking recordings and transcripts and, where needed, paraphrasing the factual substance to the source rather than offering the quotation for cosmetic revision [18]. Material edits for clarity should not create words, rearrange meaning, or conceal context. If no original record is available, the publication should disclose the basis of the quotation and lower confidence accordingly.

### Check Documents, Data, and Calculations

A document must be checked for origin, completeness, date, version, and relation to the claim. Find the issuing body, compare the file with the publisher's repository, inspect signatures or docket identifiers where applicable, and determine whether a later version exists. A genuine draft does not establish final policy. A real invoice does not establish that the goods were delivered. Authentication answers whether the item is what it purports to be; interpretation answers what the item actually proves [9].

Numerical checking requires reconstructing the operation, not merely matching a displayed number. Identify the numerator, denominator, unit, population, period, inflation basis, seasonal adjustment, sample, uncertainty interval, and revision status. Recalculate percentages and totals from the underlying table where possible. Compare like with like: stocks with stocks, flows with flows, nominal with nominal, and the same time windows. Inspect whether a chart starts from a truncated axis or whether categories changed between periods. AP standards require access to underlying data for graphics and prohibit visual distortion of the data [6].

Research claims require attention to study design. A paper can support an association without supporting causation, or a finding in one sample without justifying a universal claim. Read the methods, result being cited, limitations, and any correction or retraction. Trace review articles and press releases back to the underlying studies. Mogull's review is a warning against citation laundering: even scholarly articles can repeat or transform earlier assertions incorrectly [16].

### Verify Images, Video, and Audio as Content in Context

Visual verification separates source, file, and depicted content. The Berkeley Protocol recommends examining these dimensions collectively for consistency [9]. The Verification Handbook operationalizes the task by identifying the originator, corroborating location and time, confirming that the image depicts what its label claims, and obtaining permission where required [10][11]. No single test settles all four questions.

Start by preserving the original or best available file and working on copies. Record the URL, account, upload time, caption, and acquisition method. Reverse image search or keyframe search can locate earlier appearances and expose an old image paired with a new event. Metadata can supply device, timestamp, or location clues, but social platforms often strip it and metadata can be edited. Absence of metadata is therefore not proof of manipulation, and presence of plausible metadata is not proof of truth [9][11].

Content checks use landmarks, street signs, language, architecture, terrain, weather, shadows, uniforms, license plates, and other visible features. These clues should be compared with maps, satellite imagery, weather records, or independent footage. Video adds sequence and audio checks: inspect keyframes, discontinuities, synchronization, and whether the soundtrack belongs to the event. Synthetic-media detectors may be useful leads, but a detector score should not stand alone as a verdict.

Content credentials can strengthen provenance without deciding truth. The C2PA specification binds assertions about an asset's history to the asset through cryptographic mechanisms and can reveal recorded edits or origin information. The specification expressly avoids judging whether the provenance assertions are good, bad, or true; it verifies association, structure, and tamper evidence within its trust model [17]. A credential can show a traceable production history while the photographed claim remains false, staged, or miscaptioned.

### Maintain an Evidence Ledger and Independent Review

A defensible workflow records each claim, its status, supporting and contradicting evidence, source dependencies, unresolved questions, and the exact text that will be published. The record may be a marked manuscript, table, or issue tracker. Its purpose is to prevent a checked fact from becoming unchecked during revision and to let another reviewer reproduce the reasoning. Full Fact requires a second researcher to review material before publication; the IFCN requires enough source detail for readers to replicate the work [4][5].

The reviewer should not merely confirm that links open. Review must test whether the evidence entails the wording, whether counterevidence was searched for, whether source independence was overstated, and whether qualifications survived editing. A useful adversarial question is: What evidence would make this sentence false or materially misleading? If the answer cannot be stated, the claim may be too vague to verify.

### Match the Conclusion to the Evidence

Verdicts should report evidence state, not rhetorical confidence. "Supported" means the specified evidence adequately supports the specified proposition. "Contradicted" means reliable evidence conflicts with it. "Misleading" means some elements may be accurate while context, comparison, or implication creates a materially false impression. "Insufficient evidence" means the search did not establish either support or contradiction. "Unverifiable" should be reserved for claims that cannot be tested with accessible evidence, not used as a synonym for false [4][5][12].

Uncertainty labels should identify why and how much is unknown. Numerical ranges are preferable when a defensible interval exists. Verbal labels should be defined because terms such as likely or possible are interpreted differently. The uncertainty may concern a number, the reliability of a source, the completeness of a record, or the strength of an inference. Van der Bles and colleagues distinguish direct uncertainty about a fact or number from indirect uncertainty about the underlying evidence or model [12].

### Correct the Record, Not Just the File

Correction is part of verification because publication creates a continuing duty to maintain an accurate record. A correction should state what was wrong, give the correct information, identify when the change was made, and be placed where users of the original can see it. AP rejects euphemisms for factual corrections, while BBC guidance requires online amendments to acknowledge the error and make the change clear [6][7]. IFCN standards add that organizations should have a visible policy and seek to expose readers of the original to the corrected version [5].

Corrections differ from silent copy edits and updates. A typo that does not change meaning may be fixed without implying a factual reversal. A factual error needs a labeled correction. New information that arrived after publication may require an update, with the chronology preserved. If an error changes the central conclusion, the rating, headline, derivative posts, syndication partners, and archived versions may all need attention. The correction mechanism should follow the error through the same distribution paths that carried it.

## Evidence

### Verification Practice Is Valued but Uneven

Shapiro and colleagues used post hoc qualitative interviews with 28 Canadian journalists, each reconstructing how one newspaper story was verified. Participants described methods resembling social-scientific practice, including source triangulation, primary documents, data analysis, and observation. The researchers nevertheless found wide variation in strategy and in the amount of scrutiny applied to different facts. Names, numbers, and other concrete details often received special attention, while attributed statements could pass on one source's account. The study's method depends on retrospective self-report, but its central finding is important: declaring verification a professional norm does not itself produce a uniform protocol [1].

That finding supports procedural controls such as claim inventories, evidence ledgers, and independent review. These controls do not guarantee correctness. They do make omissions visible and reduce reliance on a reporter's unaided memory about what was checked. This is a synthesis of the gap Shapiro and colleagues observed and the transparency requirements in Full Fact and IFCN practice [1][4][5].

### Lateral Reading Outperformed Surface Inspection

Wineburg and McGrew compared 10 professional fact-checkers, 10 doctoral historians, and 25 Stanford undergraduates in think-aloud evaluations of live websites. The tasks concerned social and political topics and focused on how participants reached credibility judgments. Historians and students often stayed within the site and attended to features such as logos, domain names, references, and presentation. Fact-checkers instead left the site quickly, opened new tabs, and investigated the organization from outside sources. The fact-checkers reached more warranted conclusions and generally did so faster [2].

The result does not show that fact-checkers are infallible or that page content should never be read closely. It demonstrates that polished presentation is weak evidence of institutional credibility and that external investigation can efficiently reveal ownership, sponsorship, and reputation. Lateral reading is therefore an early triage method: establish what the source is before spending substantial time interpreting what it says [2].

### Professional Fact-Checking Is a Multi-Stage, Manual Pipeline

Micallef, Armacost, Memon, and Patil conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 professional fact-checkers from 19 countries. Participants described five recurring stages: selecting a claim; contextualizing and analyzing it; consulting data and domain experts; writing the result and choosing a rating; and disseminating the report. The study also found that work was spread across poorly integrated tools, that participants were overwhelmed by the volume of candidate claims, and that manual labor limited scale [3].

These findings support two operational conclusions. First, verification is not reducible to evidence retrieval: claim selection, interpretation, editorial judgment, explanation, and distribution remain separate tasks. Second, tools should preserve provenance and handoffs across the pipeline rather than automate only a verdict. Because the evidence comes from practitioner interviews, it describes reported work practices rather than direct observation of every check; the authors identify the limits of self-report [3].

### Claim Structure and Rating Scales Create Their Own Errors

Nieminen and Sankari evaluated a random sample of 858 PolitiFact checks against 24 criteria drawn from fact-checking scholarship and the IFCN code. PolitiFact performed well on many criteria, but 279 checks, or 33 percent of the sample, combined multiple propositions under one rating. The researchers argued that a single rating can obscure the truth status of each component and recommended separating claims or reformulating the checked content as one proposition [14]. The evidence directly supports claim decomposition as a quality control.

Markowitz and colleagues compared PolitiFact with The Washington Post Fact Checker and then cross-checked a random sample against Snopes and FactCheck.org. In the PolitiFact-Washington Post comparison, 116 of 513 PolitiFact statements, or 22.6 percent, did not appear in the Washington Post database. Where both organizations checked claims, their bottom-line judgments about attributed veracity showed nearly complete agreement, while the severity scales were only moderately correlated. The study therefore located disagreement more in selection and scaling than in the basic determination for shared cases [15].

Together, these studies show that a verdict is partly a product of claim boundaries, selection rules, and scale design. A careful publication should expose the proposition and evidence, not ask a label to carry the entire explanation. Different ratings may reflect different wording, timing, or comparison sets rather than diametrically opposed evidence [14][15].

### Citation Presence Does Not Guarantee Citation Accuracy

Mogull reviewed 47 articles about quotation and citation errors in medical research and analyzed the designs of the underlying studies. Earlier reviews had often reported error rates of roughly 20 to 25 percent, but Mogull found that inconsistent denominators and sampling methods made those headline estimates misleading. After restricting and recalculating the more representative evidence, the estimated quotation-error rate was 14.5 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 10.5 to 18.6 percent [16].

The precise estimate applies to the reviewed medical literature and should not be generalized mechanically to all publishing. The broader implication is methodological: a citation can be correctly formatted while the cited assertion misstates, oversimplifies, or exceeds the source. Verification must compare the sentence with the exact source passage, not treat a bibliography as proof. Mogull's own recalculation also illustrates why definitions and denominators must be checked before repeating a statistic [16].

### Digital Items Require Source, File, and Content Analysis

The Berkeley Protocol is a professional methodology rather than a controlled experiment. It was developed jointly by the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights and the Human Rights Center at the University of California, Berkeley for digital open-source investigations. Its verification framework separates source analysis, technical analysis of the item or file, and content analysis, then tests those dimensions together for consistency. It also treats external corroboration, preservation, documentation, security, and work on copies rather than originals as part of the method [9].

The Verification Handbook translates similar principles into newsroom practice. Its image workflow asks the checker to establish the originator, corroborate location and approximate time, confirm what the image depicts, and address permission. It notes that social platforms often strip metadata, so metadata is one clue rather than a mandatory proof. These institutional methods converge on a central point: reverse search, metadata, visual clues, or source testimony can each fail, but their independent convergence can justify a stronger conclusion [10][11].

C2PA addresses a narrower technical problem. Its Content Credentials standard can bind signed provenance assertions and recorded transformations to a digital asset and detect tampering within the credential system. The specification explicitly does not decide whether the assertions are true or whether the depicted event occurred as claimed [17]. This boundary is evidence against treating provenance technology or synthetic-media detection as a complete fact-check.

### Communicating Uncertainty Need Not Destroy Trust

Van der Bles and colleagues first reviewed how uncertainty about facts, numbers, and scientific claims is expressed across domains. They distinguished the object, source, level, magnitude, expression, and format of uncertainty, and emphasized that empirical evidence on audience effects was still limited [12]. That framework discourages a generic "uncertain" label: the communicator should specify what is unknown and why.

A later program by van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman, and Spiegelhalter tested uncertainty communication in four online experiments and one BBC News field experiment, with 5,780 participants in total. Communicating uncertainty increased perceived uncertainty, as intended. The studies found only a small overall reduction in trust in the numbers or source, concentrated mainly in lengthy verbal uncertainty statements; numerical ranges generally did not produce the same trust reduction [13].

The evidence does not mean every uncertainty statement is harmless or that a numeric range is always available. It supports a narrower conclusion: transparent uncertainty is compatible with public trust, and format matters. For quantitative claims, a justified interval can convey limits more precisely than vague hedging. For qualitative claims, the publication should name the missing evidence or disputed inference rather than use an undefined confidence word [12][13].

## Implications

### For Writers and Researchers: Build Verification Into Drafting

Writers should treat verification as part of composition, not a final ritual. The claim inventory begins while evidence is gathered: every number, name, date, quotation, superlative, causal statement, and consequential characterization receives a source or an explicit note that it is analysis. Keeping the upstream source beside the draft prevents the common failure in which a writer remembers a conclusion but loses the evidence or qualification that justified it [4][5][16].

A practical synthesis of the reviewed standards is an eight-step workflow. First, freeze the original claim and context. Second, divide it into checkable propositions. Third, trace each proposition to the earliest accessible evidence. Fourth, evaluate the source laterally and disclose relevant interests. Fifth, seek an independent evidentiary path and record contradiction as well as support. Sixth, reproduce calculations and inspect quotations, documents, and media in their original context. Seventh, assign a conclusion no stronger than the evidence and label specific uncertainty. Eighth, obtain independent review and preserve the record needed for correction [2][4][5][9].

The workflow changes writing choices. If a strong statement depends on one ambiguous source, the remedy may be to narrow the sentence rather than search indefinitely for confirmation. If the evidence supports correlation but not causation, the verb should change. If a range is defensible but a point estimate is not, publish the range. If the only evidence is a claimant's own press release, attribute the claim rather than adopt it as fact. Verification therefore improves prose by forcing the grammar to reflect the evidence state.

### For Editors and Publishers: Make Accuracy a System Property

An organization cannot rely on individual conscientiousness alone. Shapiro's interviews show that professionals can strongly value verification while applying it unevenly [1]. Editors can reduce this variation with explicit claim categories, source requirements, review roles, and escalation thresholds. High-risk claims include allegations about identifiable people, health and safety information, large financial or statistical assertions, material based on anonymous sources, and content likely to propagate rapidly. Routine details still need checking, but high-risk claims require documented independent review.

The evidence ledger should survive publication. It can support complaints, later updates, syndication corrections, and periodic audits. Each record should show the claim wording, evidence used, source dependencies, reviewer, publication state, and unresolved caveats. A change-control process should confirm that verified wording was not altered after the check. The AHCJ guidance to review all versions captures a general systems lesson: a correct edit that is not propagated to the published version is operationally equivalent to no correction [18].

The corrections policy should define material error, minor edit, clarification, update, retraction, and change of conclusion. It should specify placement, timing, ownership, and downstream notification. AP, BBC, and IFCN standards all reject the idea that quietly replacing text fully repairs a factual error [5][6][7]. Corrections are more credible when the audience can see both the nature of the mistake and the corrected record. A visible correction also creates internal learning data: repeated error classes can be traced to weak steps in the workflow.

### For Scientific, Technical, and Financial Communication: Check the Inference

Specialized communication fails when correct numbers are attached to incorrect interpretations. A technical checker should inspect units, definitions, denominator choice, sample, model assumptions, revision policy, uncertainty interval, and domain limits. A scientific abstract may state an association while a press release implies causation. A financial comparison may mix a quarterly flow with a year-end stock. A health percentage may be relative risk without absolute baseline. These are not copy errors; they are failures to preserve the conditions under which a claim is true [4][6][16].

Domain expertise is therefore necessary but insufficient. The subject expert can test whether the evidence has been interpreted correctly; an editor can test whether the publication communicates that interpretation without hidden assumptions. Independence matters because authors are often too close to their own argument to notice that a caveat has disappeared. Full Fact's second-researcher review and the IFCN requirement to present evidence both for and against a claim provide models for separating production from verification [4][5].

Uncertainty should be treated as information, not embarrassment. Van der Bles and colleagues provide evidence that numerical uncertainty can be communicated without a substantial loss of trust [13]. The implication is not to invent precision. It is to publish a range when the analysis supports one, to describe evidence quality when it does not, and to separate unknown from unknowable. Readers need to know whether uncertainty comes from sampling, measurement, model choice, missing records, source conflict, or future events [12][13].

### For Visual and Breaking-News Work: Slow the Claim, Not Necessarily the Entire Report

Breaking news creates a false choice between speed and accuracy. BBC standards resolve the priority by placing due accuracy above speed, while allowing attribution and explicit non-verification when material cannot yet be confirmed [7]. A newsroom can publish what is known without adopting the strongest circulating claim. It can say that a video was posted by a specified account and appears to show an event while separately stating that location, time, or authorship has not been verified.

Visual teams should preserve original files, record acquisition, search for earlier instances, contact the uploader when safe, and test place and time through independent clues [9][10][11]. Metadata and credentials can shorten the search, but neither proves the depicted narrative. The C2PA boundary is especially important: tamper-evident provenance can validate an asset's recorded history without validating the truth of its caption [17]. Editors should therefore separate questions about manipulation, origin, and meaning in both the investigation and the published label.

When evidence is incomplete, the update path should be designed before publication. A provisional report should state what would change its status. If later evidence resolves the date or location, the update should preserve the chronology. If the initial claim was materially wrong, the publication needs a correction, not merely a fresh timestamp. This approach makes uncertainty reversible without making error invisible.

### For Fact-Check Labels: Explain the Proposition and the Scale

Labels are compression devices, not substitutes for evidence. Nieminen and Sankari show that complex propositions can be flattened into one rating, while Markowitz and colleagues show that organizations may agree on basic veracity yet differ in which claims they select and how they scale severity [14][15]. A reader should be able to see the exact proposition, decision rule, key supporting and contradicting evidence, and date of the assessment.

The safest scale separates at least four states: supported, contradicted, misleading through context or implication, and insufficient evidence. Organizations may use more categories, but every category needs a public definition. A label should not imply intent unless there is evidence of intent. False is a judgment about a proposition; lie is also a claim about the speaker's knowledge and purpose. The evidence required for the second claim is different and usually stronger [14].

A conclusion should be time-bounded when the evidence can change. "Unsupported as of the review date" is not the same as "disproved." A later correction to a dataset can legitimately change a verdict, but the revision should preserve the earlier evidence state and explain why the conclusion changed. Transparent versioning prevents a moving knowledge base from appearing as arbitrary editorial inconsistency.

### For Automation and AI: Preserve Human Judgment and Provenance

Automation can discover duplicate claims, extract text, compare images, generate transcripts, recalculate figures, and monitor updates. Micallef and colleagues show why integrated tools are attractive: professional fact-checking is manual, fragmented, and difficult to scale [3]. Yet their five-stage pipeline also identifies work that cannot be collapsed safely into one confidence score. Selecting the proposition, judging source independence, resolving conflicting definitions, assessing harm, and writing a fair conclusion require accountable judgment.

Automated outputs should therefore be treated as leads. A reverse-image match needs contextual inspection. A transcript needs comparison with audio. A language model's citation needs opening and passage-level checking. A synthetic-media detector needs corroboration. A content credential verifies bounded provenance assertions, not the truth of the world depicted [17]. The system should log tool version, query, input, output, and human decision so another reviewer can reconstruct how automation affected the conclusion.

The worst automation failure is plausible fabrication entering the evidence chain without a visible boundary. Prevent it by requiring every consequential sentence to terminate in retrievable evidence or an explicit analysis label. If a tool cannot expose its evidence, it may help prioritize work but should not decide publication. This is a synthesis of the transparency requirements in the IFCN code, the documented pipeline in Micallef and colleagues, and the bounded trust model in C2PA [3][5][17].

### For Readers: Inspect the Method, Not Only the Verdict

A reader evaluating a fact-check should ask whether the claim was stated precisely, whether primary evidence is linked, whether corroborating sources are independent, whether counterevidence is addressed, whether uncertainty is specific, and whether corrections are visible. These questions do not require the reader to repeat the entire investigation. They test whether the publisher has made replication possible in principle [5].

The method also protects against false balance. Equal numbers of sources do not create equal evidence, and opposing quotations do not settle an empirical question. Verification weighs access, method, independence, and relevance. At the same time, confidence should not outrun the record. A transparent "insufficient evidence" is more accurate than a decisive label constructed from absence, and a narrow supported claim is more useful than a broad assertion whose components were never separately tested.

The resulting standard is demanding but simple: publish the strongest statement that survives traceable evidence, independent challenge, contextual inspection, and explicit uncertainty. Correct it visibly when better evidence shows that it failed. That standard turns accuracy from an aspiration into a repeatable communication practice [4][5][6][7].
