# Public Speaking Works When Design and Delivery Serve Audience Understanding and Action

Public speaking is the deliberate design and live delivery of a spoken message for a particular audience, purpose, setting, and time limit. Its quality is therefore better judged by what the audience can understand, remember, trust, question, and appropriately do than by the speaker's charisma or the sophistication of the presentation software [1][3][12].

## Background

Public speaking predates presentation software by millennia, but its enduring problem has not changed: one person must help many listeners construct a sufficiently similar understanding from a message that disappears as it is spoken. Classical rhetoric supplied an early production system through invention, arrangement, style, memory, and delivery. Modern communication education retains that whole-system view. The National Communication Association's standards define communication through speaker, listener, message, medium, feedback, and noise; they also treat audience analysis, message organization, supporting material, introductions, conclusions, delivery, and anxiety management as related competencies rather than independent tricks [1]. Presentation craft is therefore narrower than rhetoric as a general theory of persuasion, but broader than slide design. It coordinates purpose, content, structure, evidence, language, visuals, voice, body, setting, interaction, and revision.

The spoken channel creates constraints that written communication does not. A reader can stop, reread, compare distant paragraphs, inspect a citation, or choose a different pace. A live listener usually receives each phrase once while also interpreting the speaker, visuals, room, and other audience members. The presenter's structure must consequently be audible: the opening establishes purpose and relevance, the body advances a small set of related claims, transitions state how those claims connect, and the close consolidates the intended takeaway. NCA standards accordingly require speakers to analyze audiences, adapt messages and behaviors, organize messages so listeners can understand, and adjust during presentation in response to feedback [1]. The author's synthesis is that a talk is not an essay read aloud. It is a time-bound path through evidence whose route must remain visible while the audience is walking it.

Visual projection added a second information stream. That stream can externalize a diagram, comparison, image, quotation, process, or quantitative relationship that speech alone would describe poorly. It can also compete with the speaker for attention. Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning is based on separate but capacity-limited processing of words and pictures and on the learner's active work of selecting, organizing, and integrating information. The third edition of Multimedia Learning reviews 15 design principles grounded in more than 200 experimental studies [2]. Relevant principles include coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial and temporal contiguity, segmenting, and modality. Their shared implication is not that slides must be sparse as a matter of taste. It is that each displayed element should perform identifiable explanatory work and should appear where and when the audience needs it [2].

Empirical work also weakened the assumption that ordinary slide practice is self-correcting. Kosslyn and colleagues translated findings about perception, working memory, comprehension, and long-term memory into eight cognitive communication principles. In a stratified sample of 140 web-posted slide decks, four judges found violations across academic, business, government, and other categories; even the least frequently violated principle appeared in at least one quarter of the sampled decks. Separate studies found that viewers noticed and disliked recurring violations but were not consistently able to diagnose them [4]. The result matters because a presenter cannot rely on software defaults, familiarity, or visual polish as proof of communicative quality.

A parallel literature on speaking anxiety distinguishes discomfort from incompetence. Anxiety can consume attention, increase disfluency, encourage avoidance, and make preparation harder, but eliminating all arousal is neither a realistic nor a necessary precondition for a useful talk. A 2019 meta-analysis of 30 randomized controlled trials involving 1,355 participants found that psychological interventions reduced fear of public speaking, with an overall effect of Hedges' g = 0.74 across 62 interventions; technology-assisted delivery was not less effective than face-to-face delivery in the reviewed evidence [6]. NCA standards likewise treat anxiety as a normal communication problem that can be managed through strategies and graduated participation [1]. The practical shift is from waiting to feel fearless toward building controlled exposure, preparation, and recovery into rehearsal.

The setting has also diversified. Face-to-face presentations share a physical room; online presentations use a synchronous virtual room; hybrid presentations require the speaker to serve physically present and remote audiences at once; asynchronous presentations are recorded and cannot respond to the original audience in real time. Ruth and colleagues' research overview emphasizes that hybrid delivery places additional demands on speaker attention and technical capture because remote participants must receive room interaction through cameras and microphones. Online eye contact is indirect: looking at the camera produces the audience's experience of gaze, while looking at faces on screen helps the speaker read limited feedback [11]. These formats change the control surface, not the core obligation. The speaker must still design for comprehension, feedback, inclusion, and appropriate action.

The field has accumulated abundant practitioner advice but less direct experimentation than its confidence sometimes suggests. Blome, Sondermann, and Augustin reviewed 91 expert-opinion articles on medical research presentations and found strong agreement around 29 recommendations, including simple slides, audience adaptation, rehearsal, not reading the talk, and eye contact. They also noted that empirical and experimental evidence was scarce and that some familiar prescriptions, including rigid text limits, were not directly validated by the evidence they reviewed [5]. This boundary is important. Presentation practice should use robust cognitive and communication principles, test them against the actual audience and task, and avoid turning convenient rules of thumb into universal laws.

## Core Concepts

### Define success as an audience change

A presentation should begin with an intended audience outcome, not with a pile of material. Informative talks may aim for a defensible mental model, persuasive talks for a warranted judgment, instructional talks for a repeatable procedure, and decision talks for a choice made from explicit alternatives and evidence. NCA distinguishes functions such as informing, influencing, relating, imagining, and appreciating, and requires communicators to choose strategies appropriate to context and goal [1]. The author's synthesis is a compact specification: after the talk, what should this audience understand, remember, believe with what confidence, or be able to do? Content that does not support that change is a candidate for removal, a handout, or follow-up.

The outcome also supplies evaluation criteria. Applause, laughter, visual polish, or speaker confidence can indicate engagement, but none proves comprehension or transfer. Better measures follow the purpose: a one-sentence paraphrase tests the central claim; a worked example tests application; a decision or commitment tests action; questions reveal unresolved assumptions; delayed recall tests retention; and a later behavior or artifact tests transfer. Blome and colleagues proposed comprehension and retention, attitude change, interest, and presenter reputation as distinct outcomes, which should not be collapsed into one impression of effectiveness [5]. A talk can entertain without teaching, persuade without informing, or teach without creating action. The speaker should decide which outcomes matter before choosing methods.

### Analyze the audience without stereotyping it

Audience analysis asks what listeners already know, need, value, expect, fear, dispute, and can reasonably do in the setting. It also asks about language, accessibility, authority, incentives, voluntary or compulsory attendance, and the consequences of error. NCA standards make audience analysis and adaptation explicit speaking competencies [1]. Kosslyn and colleagues add a cognitive reason: unfamiliar symbols, conventions, terminology, and formats require effortful retrieval from long-term memory and may not be understood at all if the relevant prior knowledge is absent [4].

Demographic categories may suggest questions, but they are weak substitutes for evidence about the actual people. A job title does not prove technical knowledge; age does not prove digital fluency; nationality does not establish one cultural code; and organizational rank does not reveal the listener's decision criteria. The author's synthesis is to gather audience evidence through the host, prior artifacts, representative interviews, registration questions, an opening poll, or early diagnostic questions. Then design a lowest-assumption path while offering optional depth. This approach adapts the explanation without patronizing listeners or manipulating their identities.

A presentation can serve several audiences, but it should name the primary one. A technical briefing may simultaneously address operators who need a procedure, managers who need a risk decision, and specialists who will audit assumptions. One undifferentiated stream usually overloads novices while boring experts. Layering is more defensible: state the decision and core model in shared language, expose the evidence needed for trust, move specialist derivations to backup material, and reserve time for differentiated questions. In hybrid settings, the remote audience is not merely an observer category; it has different access to room sound, visual detail, turn-taking, and side conversation [10][11].

### Build a claim path, not a topic inventory

A topic label names an area; a presentation needs a sequence of claims. The central claim should answer the audience's governing question. Each main point should then perform a distinct function: define the problem, establish evidence, compare alternatives, explain a mechanism, address an objection, or specify action. The structure can be chronological, causal, problem-solution, comparative, spatial, or procedural, but its logic should be recoverable from the transitions. NCA standards require an effective introduction, body, and conclusion and the use of support appropriate to purpose [1].

The opening has four jobs. It earns attention with relevance rather than spectacle; identifies the subject and scope; states the governing claim or question; and previews the route. An anecdote, case, image, demonstration, question, or statistic is useful only if it creates a clean bridge to that route. The close reverses the process: it restates the claim in light of the evidence, compresses the main reasons, identifies limitations where material, and states the next decision or action. Kosslyn and colleagues found that a missing crisp ending was a frequent structural violation in their sample [4]. The author's synthesis is that beginnings create a prediction about what the talk will deliver, while endings settle whether it did.

Signposting makes structure audible. Phrases such as "the first constraint," "the evidence changes here," "this example tests the mechanism," and "the remaining uncertainty is" tell listeners both location and relation. A transition should not merely announce the next slide; it should explain why the next idea follows. Mayer's signaling principle supports cues that direct attention to organization and essential material, while NCA standards require message organization and adjustment for understanding [1][2]. A useful test is whether a listener who cannot see the slides can still reconstruct the argument's parts and connections.

Concrete examples carry abstract claims into a form the audience can inspect. One well-chosen case can define a category, reveal a mechanism, or demonstrate a decision rule; several varied cases can expose boundary conditions. Examples should not silently replace population evidence. A vivid anecdote can make a claim memorable while remaining unrepresentative. The speaker should label its role: illustration, counterexample, case evidence, or proof of possibility. Where statistics matter, translate them into the relevant denominator, period, baseline, and comparison while keeping the original measure available for audit.

### Design slides as synchronized evidence

A slide should answer the question, "What can the audience understand better by seeing this now?" Suitable answers include a spatial relation, trend, comparison, sequence, image detail, exact quotation, formula, map, or short set of alternatives. If speech can convey the material more clearly, the slide may be unnecessary. Mayer's multimedia principles support combining words with relevant graphics while removing extraneous material, signaling structure, placing related items near each other, synchronizing narration with the relevant visual, and avoiding redundant on-screen prose that competes with spoken words [2].

A strong slide title can state the claim rather than name the topic. "Failures rose after the maintenance interval doubled" tells the audience how to read a chart; "Failure data" does not. Garner and Alley operationalized this logic in the assertion-evidence approach: each slide carries a sentence assertion supported primarily by visual evidence. In their comparison involving 110 engineering students, assertion-evidence slides produced better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger delayed recall than common PowerPoint defaults [3]. The result came from a technical educational presentation, so it does not prove that every slide in every genre needs the same form. It does support explicit claims paired with relevant evidence.

Density should be governed by processing demand, viewing conditions, and purpose rather than by a universal word count. A short quotation may need exact text. A legal definition may need to remain visible. A data table may be appropriate for expert audit but poor for a timed explanation. Blome and colleagues found consensus for simple, readable slides but also found that common numerical limits were expert rules rather than securely established thresholds [5]. The author's synthesis is to run a distance-and-time test: can the farthest or smallest-screen participant identify the intended evidence during the time available, without choosing between reading paragraphs and listening to the speaker?

Visuals need narration that directs rather than duplicates. Name the graphic, orient the audience to axes or parts, point to the relevant pattern, interpret that pattern, and state its relation to the claim. Decorative images impose attention costs without explanatory return. Animations are justified when they reveal sequence, change, assembly, or causality; ornamental movement can distract or create false emphasis. Kosslyn and colleagues found that viewers were annoyed by presenters who displayed dense text while speaking, read slides word for word, or addressed the screen rather than the audience [4]. The design objective is coordinated attention: at any moment, the audience should know whether to look, listen, compare, decide, or ask.

### Deliver meaning through voice, body, and presence

Delivery is an information channel, not a layer of personality added after content. Articulation supports intelligibility; volume supports access; pace controls processing time; pauses mark boundaries and emphasis; intonation distinguishes assertion, contrast, uncertainty, and completion. The safest practical rule is variability governed by meaning rather than theatrical display. Blome and colleagues found frequent expert advice to avoid monotony, avoid excessive speed, face the audience, and show appropriate energy, while also warning that much presentation advice remained opinion-based [5]. W3C guidance independently recommends clear speech and a pace that participants and interpreters can follow [10].

Gesture can externalize shape, direction, size, grouping, or change. Kelly and colleagues experimentally presented speech with congruent or incongruent gestures and object-directed actions. Incongruent visual information produced more errors and slower responses, with gesture more tightly integrated with accompanying speech than object action in parts of the study [8]. The implication is bounded: meaningful gestures can participate in comprehension, but contradictory or purposeless movement can interfere. A speaker should therefore rehearse gestures that locate or model information while eliminating repeated fidgeting that communicates no relation.

Eye contact is best understood as an allocation of attention. In a 1976 experiment, 144 college students heard versions of the same informative speech that varied eye contact, posture, and vocal inflection. Eye contact was associated with higher comprehension and credibility; posture had little effect, and vocal-inflection differences did not significantly change comprehension in that design [9]. The result is old, used one educational population, and does not justify continuous staring. It supports distributing visible attention across listeners rather than reading from a screen. Online, the physical target changes: camera gaze creates the appearance of eye contact, while screen gaze gathers feedback, so the presenter must alternate deliberately [11].

Authenticity does not mean lack of design. A conversational delivery can be highly prepared because the speaker has rehearsed the route, examples, transitions, visuals, and time rather than memorized every sentence. Exact wording is appropriate for a quotation, safety instruction, legal statement, or opening and close that must be precise. The rest benefits from a speaking outline whose prompts preserve sequence while allowing responsive language. The author's synthesis is that preparation should reduce cognitive competition: fewer resources spent recalling the next point leave more capacity for observing the audience and adapting.

### Rehearse the system, not only the script

Rehearsal should occur aloud, at full size, against the actual time limit, with the visuals and controls that will be used. Silent review cannot reveal breath, articulation, transition, timing, gesture, or whether a chart can be explained before attention disperses. Choi, Honeycutt, and Bodie found across two studies that planned imagined interactions and multimodal rehearsal could reduce disfluencies and improve self-evaluated performance; participants without planning showed longer disfluency durations, and combined training conditions produced favorable fluency outcomes [7]. The result supports structured rehearsal while not proving that imagery replaces live practice.

A useful rehearsal sequence separates failure classes. First, run a content pass: is every claim supported, scoped, and understandable? Second, run a route pass: can the opening, transitions, and close be delivered without searching? Third, run a visual pass: does every element appear at the moment it is needed? Fourth, run a delivery pass: record voice, gaze, posture, and gesture. Fifth, run an audience pass with a representative listener who must paraphrase the main claim and identify confusion. Sixth, run a failure pass: disconnected network, missing font, dead video, failed microphone, absent clicker, shortened slot, or hostile question. The sequence is the author's synthesis of preparation, multimedia, anxiety, and accessibility evidence [1][2][6][7][10].

### Treat anxiety as a trainable response

Anxiety management has three distinct targets: the interpretation of arousal, the skills required by the task, and exposure to the feared situation. A speaker may need cognitive work on catastrophic predictions, practice on structure and delivery, and repeated speaking experiences that allow new learning. Ebrahimi and colleagues' meta-analysis found benefits across psychological interventions and found stronger self-report effects than physiological or behavioral effects, so feeling better and performing differently should be measured separately [6]. The author's synthesis is that speakers should not infer failure from a fast pulse, nor infer competence from calmness alone.

Preparation should be graduated and specific. Begin with a short explanation to one supportive listener, then record it, deliver it to a small group, add questions, move to the actual room or platform, and simulate the full event. Each repetition should have a narrow target and feedback criterion. If anxiety is severe, persistent, disabling, or part of broader social anxiety, professional assessment is more appropriate than generic speaking advice. The communication skill is not a substitute for clinical care, and the clinical literature should not be generalized to every nervous presenter [6].

### Use interaction and questions as feedback

Questions are not an appendix to the talk; they test what the audience understood, doubted, or needs next. The speaker should specify whether questions are invited during sections or held for a closing period, because the choice affects flow and access. During an answer, listen without composing a defense, restate the question when audibility or scope requires it, answer the core issue first, show the supporting basis, and stop. If the evidence is unknown, say so and identify what would be needed to answer. NCA treats feedback as information that should alter messages, goals, and interaction [1].

Preparation for questions should map claims to vulnerabilities. For each central claim, list its strongest evidence, principal limitation, plausible counterargument, implementation risk, and the slide or source that answers it. This is different from scripting every response. It creates retrieval routes. In hybrid events, a facilitator or explicit turn-taking method may be needed so remote questions are not structurally subordinate to room questions; W3C guidance also emphasizes accessible materials, captions or transcripts where needed, and inclusive participation across remote, in-person, and hybrid settings [10].

## Evidence

### Assertion-evidence slides improved technical learning

Garner and Alley compared two versions of the same technical presentation for 110 engineering students. One version used common PowerPoint defaults, including topic-style headings and conventional text structures; the other used sentence assertions supported by visual evidence and incorporated six multimedia-learning principles. Essay responses showed better comprehension and fewer misconceptions in the assertion-evidence condition. Participants also reported lower cognitive load, and the group showed stronger delayed recall [3]. The design is important because the spoken presentation was held constant while slide structure changed, making slide design a plausible cause of the learning differences. Its boundary is equally important: one technical educational context cannot establish one universal slide grammar for ceremonial, legal, artistic, or highly interactive talks.

### Common slide failures were widespread and hard to diagnose

Kosslyn, Kievit, Russell, and Shephard conducted three related studies. Study 1 used a random stratified web sample of 140 PowerPoint slide decks and four judges who coded 137 rules derived from eight psychological principles. Violations occurred across fields rather than concentrating in one profession, and even the least violated principle appeared in at least one quarter of decks. Study 2 surveyed experiences of presentation flaws and found that respondents noticed and were annoyed by problems associated with several principles. Study 3 showed that observers were not highly accurate at recognizing which psychological rule a slide violated, even when they sensed a problem [4]. The evidence supports explicit design training and review. It does not show that a deck's coded violations caused a measured decline in audience learning in every case, and the web-posted sample may differ from decks never published online.

### Psychological interventions reduced public-speaking fear

Ebrahimi, Pallesen, Kenter, and Nordgreen conducted a random-effects meta-analysis of 30 randomized controlled trials with 1,355 participants and 62 intervention conditions. The pooled effect on fear of public speaking was Hedges' g = 0.74 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.61 to 0.87. Effects remained against active and passive controls, technology-assisted delivery performed comparably to traditional face-to-face delivery, and follow-up effects were larger in the reported synthesis. Self-report outcomes were larger than physiological and behavioral outcomes [6]. This supports cognitive, behavioral, exposure-based, and technology-assisted help rather than resignation. It also requires measurement discipline: reduced reported fear is not identical to improved speech quality, and the variation across interventions prevents one routine from being declared best for every speaker.

### Structured mental rehearsal affected fluency

Choi, Honeycutt, and Bodie examined imagined interactions and rehearsal in two studies of speaking performance. They coded silent and vocalized pauses and compared training and imagery conditions. Public-speaking apprehension was associated with disfluency, while strategic planning through imagined interactions reduced some adverse effects. Participants who did not plan had longer disfluency durations, and combined imagined-interaction training with multimodal imagery produced favorable fluency and self-evaluation results [7]. The study supports rehearsing not only words but also the anticipated audience, setting, sequence, and response. Because mental rehearsal is an indirect simulation and self-evaluation is not independent audience judgment, it should complement rather than replace full aloud practice and external feedback.

### Gesture and speech were processed as coordinated information

Kelly, Healey, Ozyurek, and Holler used multimodal videos in which auditory words accompanied either gestures or actions on objects. Participants related audio or visual information to primes, and the experiment varied whether speech and visual information were congruent. Incongruent pairings produced more errors and longer response times than congruent pairings. Gesture showed tighter integration with speech than object action in parts of the design, even though actions could be easier to identify [8]. This is evidence against treating the speaker's hands as irrelevant decoration. It supports semantically congruent gesture, especially when a hand movement depicts the relation being explained. It does not support maximizing gesture frequency, and it warns that a movement inconsistent with the spoken claim can create processing conflict.

### Eye contact affected comprehension and credibility in one live-speech experiment

Beebe placed 144 college students into groups that heard the same informative speech under combinations of constant or absent eye contact, formal or casual posture, and varied or limited vocal inflection. Participants then completed comprehension testing and credibility ratings. Eye contact was associated with better comprehension and credibility. Posture had little effect on either outcome, and vocal inflection did not significantly affect comprehension in this experiment [9]. The study challenges the common tendency to treat every delivery recommendation as equally consequential. Its age, student sample, categorical manipulations, and live setting limit generalization. The defensible conclusion is that visible audience orientation can matter; it is not that continuous eye contact or exaggerated vocal variety guarantees learning.

### Expert consensus was substantial but not equivalent to experimental proof

Blome, Sondermann, and Augustin systematically reviewed 91 expert-opinion articles about medical research presentations. They extracted 679 recommendations, with 29 appearing in at least 20 percent of articles. The five most frequent were to keep slides simple, adapt to the audience, rehearse, avoid reading the talk, and make eye contact. The authors found little direct contradiction except over light versus dark slide backgrounds [5]. This convergence identifies durable practitioner concerns and helps prioritize training. The review also found scarce experimental evidence and noted that student studies did not consistently validate some concise-slide prescriptions. Consensus is therefore a source of hypotheses and operating defaults, not a warrant for rigid formulas.

### Presentation medium changed evaluations more clearly than learning

Moulton, Turkay, and Kosslyn randomized presenters to create PowerPoint, Prezi, or oral-only presentations and evaluated the resulting talks across two experiments. Audiences rated Prezi talks as more organized, engaging, persuasive, and effective, while PowerPoint and oral talks were rated comparably. The researchers found some differences in judgments about the presented business scenario but no corresponding evidence that the formats changed memory or comprehension [12]. A published correction clarified the funder's role: Prezi funded the work but did not control design, analysis, publication, or manuscript preparation. The result separates experiential appeal from learning and supports a format-neutral rule: software can shape perception, but the speaker still must test understanding rather than infer it from novelty or audience ratings.

### Accessibility guidance converges with cognitive design

W3C's Web Accessibility Initiative provides official guidance for in-person, remote, and hybrid meetings and presentations. It recommends an overview and review of key points, consistent slide design, clear content, limited slide text, accessible materials, captions or transcripts where needed, verbal description of meaningful visuals, clear pacing, and a visible, well-lit speaker. It also notes that screen-shared content may not be accessible to assistive technology and that some participants cannot see, hear, move, speak, or process information in the expected way [10]. This is institutional guidance rather than a controlled trial, but it converges with multimedia research on signaling, redundancy, and cognitive load [2]. Accessibility is thus not a separate compliance layer; many accessible practices improve the clarity and recoverability of the message for the entire audience.

## Implications

### For speakers: design backward from the final test

The speaker should begin with the evidence of success that will exist after the talk. If the audience must choose, define the decision, options, criteria, and uncertainty. If it must learn a process, define the performance it should reproduce. If it must understand a model, define the explanation a listener should be able to give without the slides. This backward specification constrains content, examples, visuals, and interaction. It also prevents the common failure of trying to compress everything the speaker knows into the available time. The author's synthesis is that selection is the central ethical act of presentation design: omitted context can mislead, but indiscriminate inclusion can make every point equally inaccessible [1][2][4].

A practical preparation document can contain six lines: audience, purpose, one-sentence claim, three supporting moves, principal limitation, and requested action. Expand only after those lines are coherent. For each main move, pair a claim with the evidence needed to accept it and an example needed to understand it. For each slide, specify whether the audience should look, listen, compare, calculate, imagine, or decide. For the close, write the sentence the audience should still be able to state the next day. These are the author's operational deductions from audience analysis, assertion-evidence design, and outcome-based evaluation [1][3][5].

Time is a design constraint rather than a delivery accident. Rehearse to a shorter internal limit than the official slot so transitions, equipment delays, laughter, interpretation, and questions do not consume the conclusion. If the run is long, remove claims or move evidence to backup; do not merely accelerate. W3C guidance warns that fast speech can exclude participants and interpreters [10]. A complete short path with optional depth is more useful than an unfinished long path.

### For educators and coaches: train observable components

Presentation competence should be decomposed into audience analysis, argument and structure, evidence use, language, visual design, delivery, interaction, time control, accessibility, and reflection. A single global score such as "confidence" hides which component failed and encourages style imitation. NCA's standards already model component competencies, while research distinguishes learning, persuasion, engagement, anxiety, fluency, and credibility as different outcomes [1][5][6][7]. Assessment should likewise use separate criteria and weight them according to the assignment's purpose.

Feedback should be behavioral and audience-linked. "Be more engaging" is not an executable instruction. "State the decision by the end of the opening," "pause after the three alternatives," "replace the topic title with the chart's claim," and "look at the camera for the request" identify an action and intended effect. Video review can reveal gaze, pacing, gesture, and slide timing, but the speaker should also receive listener evidence: what was the claim, where was the route lost, which visual helped, and what question remains? The worst coaching outcome is a polished speaker who cannot tell whether the audience understood.

Anxiety training should combine graded exposure, cognitive preparation, skills practice, and measurement appropriate to the problem. The meta-analytic evidence supports psychological interventions while showing that self-report, behavior, and physiology can move differently [6]. Coaches should avoid diagnosing ordinary nerves as a disorder and avoid treating severe impairment as a mere failure to practice. Referral pathways, voluntary exposure, and privacy matter, especially when recorded performance or virtual reality is used.

### For organizations: make presentation quality a system property

Organizations should specify the audience, decision, time, format, accessibility requirements, and ownership of questions before a speaker builds a deck. Templates can encode legible typography, contrast, source fields, claim-oriented titles, and accessible reading order, but they should not force decorative branding or one content pattern on every message. Kosslyn's findings show that familiar defaults can preserve widespread errors, while Garner and Alley show that structure can improve learning [3][4]. Template governance should therefore be tested through representative talks rather than accepted because the slides look consistent.

Review should separate factual validation from communication validation. A subject-matter expert checks claims, evidence, uncertainty, and omissions. A representative audience member checks language, route, pace, and relevance. An accessibility check tests reading order, alternatives, captions, contrast, meaningful link text, and whether key visuals are described aloud. A technical run checks room, screen, microphone, camera, network, media, fonts, and backups. No single reviewer can reliably substitute for all four perspectives [4][10].

Recorded presentations need version control and correction. A live qualification can disappear when a clip is edited; a changed chart can leave the narration wrong; captions can alter technical terms; and a slide deck can circulate without the spoken explanation that made it accurate. The author's synthesis is to publish the recording, accessible slides or transcript, sources, date, and correction path as one governed artifact. The communication remains auditable after the room has emptied.

### For high-stakes technical and scientific talks: expose the evidence chain

High-stakes audiences need more than confidence and simplification. Each consequential claim should reveal the source, population, period, unit, uncertainty, and boundary needed to interpret it. Visuals should preserve axes, denominators, comparison groups, and missing data rather than beautify them away. The presenter can explain one pattern at a time while keeping full evidence in backup or linked material. The assertion-evidence model is especially useful when the assertion remains faithful to the graphic and when the graphic contains the information required to challenge it [3].

Demonstrations should have a fallback. A live system can reveal process and credibility, but failure can consume the talk. Record a short verified version, preserve still images of key states, and prepare the conclusion that the demonstration was meant to support. Questions should be anticipated at the level of assumptions and limitations, not only extra detail. Saying "I do not know" protects trust when the alternative is improvising a fact. A useful answer distinguishes what the evidence establishes, what the speaker infers, and what would change the conclusion.

### For remote and hybrid delivery: design two rooms explicitly

Online speakers should distinguish camera gaze from feedback gaze. Use camera gaze for openings, central claims, transitions, and direct requests; use screen gaze to inspect reactions, chat, and raised hands. Place the audience window near the camera where possible, make the speaker visible and well lit, and test audio before refining video. The research overview by Ruth and colleagues treats online and hybrid performance as settings with different perceptual constraints, while W3C requires accessible platforms, materials, captions or interpretation where needed, and explicit inclusion practices [10][11].

Hybrid events contain two unequal audiences unless facilitation corrects the asymmetry. Room laughter, questions, demonstrations, and pointing may never reach remote participants. Use microphones for every contribution, repeat or restate room questions, describe visible events, expose shared artifacts in accessible formats, and assign someone to monitor chat and remote turns. The presenter's content should survive a small screen and variable bandwidth. These are not merely technical conveniences; they determine whether the remote audience receives the same argument and opportunity to respond [10][11].

### For audiences: evaluate the message separately from the performance

Audiences should distinguish four questions. Was the claim clear? Was the evidence sufficient and relevant? Did the structure support understanding? Did the delivery or medium alter credibility without altering evidence? Moulton and colleagues found that a presentation medium could improve audience evaluations without improving measured comprehension or memory [12]. Kosslyn and colleagues found that observers often noticed slide problems without identifying the violated principle [4]. The practical implication is to record both reaction and reason: "I was persuaded" is not yet an account of which premise, evidence, or experience produced the judgment.

Questioners can improve the evidence state by asking for the claim's scope, denominator, comparison, uncertainty, or strongest counterevidence. They can request a definition, example, source, or next step without converting the question period into a competing speech. The speaker's answer should update the shared model; the audience's role is not passive reception but disciplined feedback. This is consistent with NCA's transactional model in which feedback changes the message and interaction [1].

### A reversible presentation workflow

The author's synthesis of the reviewed evidence is a ten-step workflow. First, define the audience and outcome. Second, state one governing claim or question. Third, select a small number of necessary supporting moves. Fourth, map each move to evidence, example, limitation, and action. Fifth, write an opening that creates relevance and a close that resolves the route. Sixth, build only visuals that make a relation easier to see. Seventh, verify sources, accessibility, timing, and technical conditions. Eighth, rehearse aloud through content, route, visual, delivery, audience, and failure passes. Ninth, deliver while monitoring feedback and adapting without abandoning the claim path. Tenth, measure the intended audience outcome and revise the system rather than the speaker's persona [1][2][3][6][7][10].

This workflow is reversible because costly commitments occur late. A claim path can be tested before a deck exists; paper sketches can be rejected before design work; a rehearsal can reveal overload before the event; and backup media can contain technical failure. The worst avoidable outcome is not visible nervousness. It is a confident, polished presentation that leaves the audience with an inaccurate model or no usable next step. Public speaking succeeds when preparation, evidence, visuals, delivery, and interaction jointly reduce that risk.
