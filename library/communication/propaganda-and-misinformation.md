# Propaganda and Misinformation -- How Information Is Weaponized to Shape Belief and Behavior

Propaganda and misinformation are not accidents of the information age --
they are systematic techniques for shaping belief by distorting,
suppressing, or fabricating information. Propaganda is the deliberate,
orchestrated attempt to influence public opinion through mass
communication, a practice industrialized in the twentieth century and
digitally supercharged in the twenty-first. Misinformation is its broader
shadow: false or misleading information spread regardless of intent,
distinguished from disinformation (deliberately deceptive) and
malinformation (true information weaponized out of context). Together,
these techniques exploit cognitive vulnerabilities that make humans
susceptible to falsehoods -- and their reach has expanded dramatically
with social media platforms whose engagement-maximizing algorithms
amplify emotionally charged, novel, and identity-reinforcing content
regardless of its truth value.

## Background

Systematic propaganda emerged from the convergence of mass media, modern
psychology, and total war. World War I saw the first large-scale state
propaganda campaigns, with governments on all sides producing posters,
films, and news reports designed to demonize enemies, mobilize populations,
and maintain morale. The U.S. Committee on Public Information, led by
George Creel, demonstrated how a modern state could manufacture consensus
through coordinated media control. A young Edward Bernays served on the
Creel Committee and emerged from the war with a revelation: if propaganda
could be used to make the world "safe for democracy" during wartime, it
could be used to manage populations during peacetime.

Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew, fused psychoanalytic theory with mass
communication to create the modern public relations industry. His 1928
book Propaganda made the argument explicit: "The conscious and intelligent
manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an
important element in democratic society." Bernays engineered campaigns
that linked products to unconscious desires -- convincing women to smoke
by framing cigarettes as "torches of freedom" (a deployment of feminist
symbolism to sell an addictive product), or staging celebrity-endorsed
breakfasts to increase bacon consumption. His techniques were
systematically adopted by corporations and governments worldwide. In the
1930s, Bernays' methods found their most sinister application when Joseph
Goebbels, the Nazi Minister of Propaganda, adapted Bernays' writings to
build the propaganda apparatus of the Third Reich. Goebbels understood
propaganda as total: every medium -- radio, film, newspapers, posters,
rallies, even architecture -- became an instrument for manufacturing
the "Fuhrer cult" and demonizing Jews, communists, and other scapegoats.

The Soviet Union developed its own parallel tradition under the label
"agitprop" (agitation and propaganda), which treated communication as a
revolutionary weapon. Soviet propaganda theory distinguished between
agitation (short-term, emotional appeals to mobilize action) and
propaganda (long-term, ideological education to reshape worldview). This
tradition informed Cold War information warfare and persists in
contemporary Russian disinformation strategies, including the "firehose
of falsehood" model documented by the RAND Corporation: a high-volume,
multi-channel barrage of contradictory narratives designed not to
persuade but to exhaust the capacity for critical evaluation and create
the impression that truth itself is unknowable.

The digital revolution transformed the propaganda landscape in three
fundamental ways. First, it democratized production: anyone with a
smartphone can create and distribute content that reaches millions.
Second, it individualized targeting: algorithmic systems enable
micro-targeted propaganda calibrated to individual psychological
profiles, a capability that Cambridge Analytica exploited in the 2016
elections. Third, it automated amplification: bot networks, coordinated
inauthentic behavior, and algorithmic recommendation systems can
manufacture the appearance of grassroots consensus (astroturfing) at a
scale and speed that was impossible in the broadcast era.

## Core Concepts

### The Propaganda Triad: Technique, Medium, and Psychology

Effective propaganda operates at three levels simultaneously. At the
technique level, it employs specific rhetorical and psychological
devices. At the medium level, it exploits the structural properties of
communication channels. At the psychology level, it leverages cognitive
biases and emotional vulnerabilities that make humans susceptible.

**Technique level.** The Institute for Propaganda Analysis, founded in
1937, identified seven basic propaganda devices that remain relevant:
name-calling (attaching negative labels to people or ideas), glittering
generalities (associating concepts with vague, positive-sounding words),
transfer (borrowing authority from respected symbols), testimonial
(citing respected or disrespected people), plain folks (posing as a
regular person), bandwagon (claiming "everyone is doing it"), and card
stacking (selective presentation of facts). Modern propaganda adds
techniques such as whataboutism (deflecting criticism by pointing to an
opponent's perceived hypocrisy), flooding (overwhelming information
environments with content to make verification impossible), and
decontextualization (presenting factual information in a misleading
context).

**Medium level.** McLuhan's insight that "the medium is the message" is
directly applicable. Propagandists exploit the structural properties of
different media: the emotional immediacy of video, the virality
mechanics of social platforms, the perceived authority of legacy news
formatting, the intimacy of messaging apps. Facebook's algorithm
amplifies content that elicits strong emotional reactions (particularly
anger), Twitter's retweet mechanic favors concise, provocative
statements over nuanced argument, and WhatsApp's encrypted group chats
create high-trust enclaves where misinformation circulates with minimal
external correction.

**Psychology level.** Propaganda works because human cognition is not a
dispassionate truth-evaluating engine. Confirmation bias makes people
accept information that aligns with existing beliefs and reject contrary
evidence. The illusory truth effect means that repeated exposure to a
claim increases its perceived truthfulness, even when the claim is known
to be false. The Dunning-Kruger effect makes people overestimate their
ability to distinguish truth from falsehood. Emotional arousal --
particularly fear, anger, and moral outrage -- suppresses analytical
reasoning and increases sharing behavior. These vulnerabilities are not
bugs in human cognition; they are features of a brain optimized for
social cohesion and rapid threat detection rather than statistical
accuracy.

### The Taxonomy of Information Disorder

Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (2017), in a framework adopted by
the Council of Europe, proposed a taxonomy that distinguishes three
types of information disorder based on the relationship between falsity
and intent:

**Misinformation** is false information shared without intent to harm.
A person sharing an inaccurate health remedy because they genuinely
believe it is helpful is spreading misinformation. The defining feature
is the absence of malicious intent -- the sharer believes the content
is true or helpful.

**Disinformation** is false information deliberately created and
disseminated to deceive, manipulate, or cause harm. State-sponsored
election interference campaigns, fabricated news designed to look like
legitimate journalism, and coordinated smear campaigns are
disinformation. Intent to deceive is the defining feature.

**Malinformation** is genuine information shared with intent to cause
harm, typically by moving private or selectively true information into
the public sphere out of context. Leaking a politician's private
correspondence or publishing someone's medical history without consent
falls into this category. The information is true; the harm comes from
the context and intent of its release.

This taxonomy matters because each category demands different responses.
Misinformation calls for education and correction. Disinformation calls
for exposure and accountability. Malinformation calls for ethical
judgment about privacy, context, and proportionality.

### The Virality Mechanics of Falsehood

A landmark 2018 study by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral published in Science
analyzed approximately 126,000 rumor cascades on Twitter from 2006 to
2017 and found that falsehoods spread "farther, faster, deeper, and more
broadly than the truth in all categories of information." False news
stories were 70% more likely to be retweeted than true stories, and the
truth took approximately six times as long as falsehood to reach 1,500
people.

The researchers tested the obvious hypothesis that bots were responsible
for the differential and found the opposite: humans, not bots, were the
primary drivers of false news spread. The explanation lay in the
psychological properties of false content. False news was more novel
(statistically surprising compared to what users had seen before) and
elicited stronger emotional responses -- particularly fear, disgust, and
surprise. Novelty captures attention, and emotional arousal drives
sharing. The truth, by contrast, tends to be less novel and more
emotionally restrained -- exactly the properties that make it less viral
in engagement-maximizing algorithmic environments.

### The Propaganda Model of Media

Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's Manufacturing Consent (1988) proposed
a structural analysis of how propaganda operates not through overt state
control (as in authoritarian regimes) but through five "filters" that
shape media output in market democracies: (1) ownership concentration
and profit orientation, (2) advertising as the primary revenue source,
(3) reliance on official sources (government, corporate PR), (4) flak
(disciplining of journalists who deviate from acceptable narratives),
and (5) anti-communism (or its modern equivalent, the "common enemy"
narrative). The Herman-Chomsky model argues that propaganda in
democratic societies is not a departure from journalistic norms but an
emergent property of institutional structure -- a systemic bias produced
by market incentives rather than individual intention.

### The Inoculation Theory and Prebunking

The most empirically supported counter-strategy to misinformation draws
on an analogy with vaccination. William McGuire's inoculation theory
(1964) proposed that exposing people to a weakened dose of a persuasive
argument, along with a refutation, could build psychological resistance
against future persuasion attempts -- just as a vaccine exposes the
immune system to a weakened pathogen to build resistance against the
real infection.

Modern "prebunking" interventions apply this principle to misinformation
at scale. Rather than fact-checking each individual falsehood after it
spreads (a reactive strategy that cannot keep pace with production),
prebunking teaches people to recognize the manipulative techniques
common across misinformation campaigns: emotional manipulation, false
dichotomies, scapegoating, incoherence, and impersonation of legitimate
sources. Games like Bad News and Go Viral, developed by researchers at
Cambridge University's Social Decision-Making Lab, have been shown to
improve participants' ability to identify manipulation techniques across
multiple controlled studies. Google's Jigsaw unit deployed prebunking
video campaigns across several European countries in 2022, reaching
millions of users with short animations explaining common manipulation
tactics. The key insight is that technique-focused prebunking is
scalable in a way that content-specific fact-checking is not: teaching
someone to recognize emotional manipulation equips them against novel
misinformation that fact-checkers have not yet encountered.

## Evidence

The evidence base for understanding propaganda and misinformation draws
on multiple disciplines and methodological approaches.

The historical record provides the most dramatic case studies. The Nazi
propaganda apparatus demonstrated what total media control could
achieve: the systematic dehumanization of Jews, Roma, and other groups
was not a spontaneous social phenomenon but an engineered communication
campaign sustained over more than a decade across every available
medium. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum's documentation shows how
Goebbels' Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda systematically
extended state control over newspapers (the Editor's Law of 1933),
radio (the production of the inexpensive Volksempfanger "people's
receiver"), film, and public spectacle. The propaganda was not subtle --
it was relentless, repetitive, and emotionally calibrated, precisely
following the techniques Bernays had outlined.

The 2016 U.S. presidential election catalyzed a wave of empirical
research on digitally-mediated information operations. The Senate
Intelligence Committee's investigation documented how the Russian
Internet Research Agency (IRA) created thousands of fake social media
accounts that impersonated American activists, organized real-world
events, and targeted divisive content at specific demographic segments.
The operation reached an estimated 126 million Americans on Facebook
alone. Subsequent research by the Oxford Internet Institute documented
organized social media manipulation in at least 70 countries as of 2020,
with government agencies, political parties, and private contractors
all employing computational propaganda techniques.

The Vosoughi et al. (2018) Science study provided the most rigorous
quantification of misinformation's structural advantage over truth in
social media environments. The study's finding that falsehoods spread
farther, faster, and deeper than truth across all categories (politics,
urban legends, business, science, terrorism, and natural disasters) is
the most robust empirical demonstration that the medium-level properties
of social platforms systematically favor false content.

The psychological mechanisms behind susceptibility to misinformation
have been extensively documented. A 2024 APA consensus statement
synthesizing research identified key cognitive factors: confirmation
bias, the illusory truth effect, source confusion (remembering
information but forgetting whether the source was reliable), and the
tendency for emotional arousal to suppress analytical reasoning.
Pennycook and Rand's research demonstrated that people who score higher
on the Cognitive Reflection Test (a measure of analytical thinking
disposition) are better at distinguishing true from false headlines,
suggesting that susceptibility to misinformation is partly a failure
of reflective processing rather than motivated reasoning alone.

Prebunking has demonstrated effectiveness across multiple large-scale
trials. Roozenbeek et al. (2022) conducted five randomized controlled
experiments with a total of nearly 30,000 participants, finding that
watching short inoculation videos improved participants' ability to
identify manipulation techniques, increased their ability to distinguish
trustworthy from untrustworthy content, and improved the quality of
their sharing decisions. A 2024 EU Joint Research Centre study published
in Nature confirmed that both prebunking and debunking significantly
reduced agreement with false claims about climate change and COVID-19
vaccines, as well as the likelihood of sharing misinformation.

## Implications

For media consumers, the implication is uncomfortable but clear: the
default information environment is adversarial. Cognitive vulnerabilities
that served humans well in small-group environments become liabilities
in algorithmically-mediated information ecosystems designed to maximize
engagement rather than accuracy. The most effective defense is not
trying to evaluate every claim individually -- a cognitively exhausting
and ultimately impossible strategy -- but developing meta-cognitive
habits: pausing before sharing emotionally charged content, checking
sources before trusting, recognizing when a piece of content is designed
to provoke rather than inform, and maintaining awareness of one's own
confirmation bias.

For communicators and content creators, the propaganda framework
provides both a toolkit and a warning. The same techniques that make
propaganda effective -- emotional engagement, narrative simplicity,
repetition, social proof -- can be used ethically in persuasion and
education. The ethical line is not technique but intent and transparency.
Bernays' career illustrates the ambiguity: many of his techniques (the
"hearty breakfast" campaign, soap-sculpture contests for children) were
benign by the standards of the time, but the same psychological
architecture could be, and was, put to genocidal purposes. Communicators
who understand propaganda techniques bear a special responsibility: the
burden of knowing that the tools of persuasion are also the tools of
manipulation.

For platform governance and policy, the evidence suggests that
individual-level interventions (debunking, prebunking, literacy
training) are necessary but insufficient. The structural properties of
engagement-maximizing algorithms systematically favor emotionally
charged and novel content -- the very properties that characterize
misinformation. System-level interventions -- algorithmic transparency,
platform liability reform, investment in authoritative information
ecosystems, and measures to reduce the virality of unverified content
-- address the medium rather than merely treating symptoms. The APA
consensus noted that system-level interventions may be more effective
than individual-level approaches but raise more complex questions about
free expression.

For democratic resilience, the implications are existential. Democratic
theory assumes a minimally informed citizenry capable of evaluating
competing claims and making reasoned choices. A communication
environment in which falsehoods structurally outperform truth, in which
adversarial states can cheaply target citizenries with personalized
disinformation, and in which trust in institutions has been strategically
eroded undermines the cognitive preconditions of democratic
self-governance. Addressing this challenge requires extending the
concept of national security to include informational resilience: the
capacity of a population to maintain shared factual reference points
in the face of systematic manipulation.

For education, propaganda studies suggest a curriculum that goes beyond
traditional media literacy. Rather than merely teaching students to
evaluate sources (a skill that adversarial environments can game),
education should inoculate: expose students to the manipulative
techniques themselves, have them create their own propaganda to
understand how it works, and train the meta-cognitive pause that
interrupts the reflex to share emotionally arousing content.

## Counter-Strategies and Their Limits

Current counter-misinformation interventions fall into four categories,
each with distinct mechanisms and limitations.

Debunking -- fact-checking false claims after they spread -- is the most
visible approach. It works in controlled settings: detailed corrections
that explain why a claim is false and provide an alternative factual
account reduce belief in misinformation. However, debunking faces the
"continued influence effect": corrected misinformation often continues
to influence reasoning even after people acknowledge the correction.
Debunking is also inherently reactive and cannot scale to match the
volume of misinformation production. Furthermore, people who are most
predisposed to believe misinformation tend to avoid correction.

Prebunking -- preemptive exposure to weakened manipulation techniques
-- addresses debunking's scalability problem by targeting the technique
rather than the specific content. As detailed above, the evidence base
is strong, but open questions remain about durability (do inoculation
effects persist?), cross-cultural generalizability, and whether
technique-focused prebunking works against the most emotionally
compelling misinformation.

Literacy interventions -- media literacy, digital literacy, news
literacy -- aim to build general critical evaluation skills. These
interventions are difficult to evaluate because they vary enormously in
content, duration, and quality. The APA notes that literacy training
may be most effective when combined with other approaches rather than
deployed in isolation.

Nudging -- small environmental changes to steer behavior -- includes
interventions such as prompting users to consider accuracy before
sharing content. Pennycook et al. demonstrated that simply asking people
to rate the accuracy of a single non-political headline before deciding
what to share significantly improved the veracity of their subsequent
sharing decisions. Nudges are cheap to implement at scale but address
only individual behavior, not the structural incentives of the
platforms.

No single approach is sufficient. The emerging expert consensus favors
a layered defense: system-level changes to reduce the virality of
unverified content, combined with individual-level inoculation and
literacy training, supported by targeted debunking of the most
consequential false claims.
