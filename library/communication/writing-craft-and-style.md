# Writing Craft and Style -- Why Clear Prose Is a Thinking Technology, Not a Decoration

Writing craft is the set of repeatable techniques -- sentence structure, word choice, rhythm, cohesion, and revision -- that turn thought into prose a reader can understand on the first pass. The craft tradition, from Strunk and White's The Elements of Style to Steven Pinker's The Sense of Style, makes one recurring claim: clarity is not decoration but a cognitive technology, because prose that matches how readers actually process language transfers ideas faster, more accurately, and with more credibility than prose that fights the reader's working memory. This topic distills the core principles of clear writing, the experimental and institutional evidence behind them, and what they mean for anyone -- agents and knowledge workers above all -- who thinks by writing.

## Background

The modern craft tradition has a precise starting point. William Strunk Jr., an English professor at Cornell, privately printed a slim handbook for his own classes in 1918, and it was published commercially in 1920 as The Elements of Style. The book organizes its advice into eight elementary rules of usage, ten elementary principles of composition, a few matters of form, and lists of commonly misused and misspelled words. Its most famous commandments are "Omit needless words" and "Use the active voice." Strunk taught at Cornell from 1899 to 1937, and one of his students was E. B. White, who studied under him in 1919. White later revised and expanded the handbook for Macmillan in 1959, and it has never gone out of print since. In a 1957 New Yorker column White wrote that, under the remembered sting of Strunk's "kindly lash," he had been "trying to omit needless words since 1919." Stephen King, in his own book On Writing, would later call The Elements of Style the book every aspiring writer should read.

A second pillar of the tradition is George Orwell's 1946 essay "Politics and the English Language," published in Horizon. Orwell argued that political prose had decayed into vagueness and empty convention, and he catalogued its pathologies: dying metaphors, verbal false limbs, pretentious diction, and meaningless words. He insisted the decline was reversible through discipline and offered six rules: never use a metaphor you are used to seeing in print; never use a long word where a short one will do; if it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out; never use the passive where you can use the active; never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent; and break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. Orwell's essay turned style into an ethical question: language that hides its meaning enables thought that does not think.

The 1970s and 1980s turned the craft into a teachable discipline. William Zinsser's On Writing Well, first published in 1976, became the standard nonfiction craft book, organized around the claim that clutter is the disease of American writing and that the secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components. Zinsser's central doctrines -- that rewriting is the essence of writing, that there is no good writing only good rewriting, and that every word that does not do useful work should be cut -- remain the default syllabus for the craft. Joseph M. Williams's Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace (1981) took a different route: instead of a list of prohibitions, Williams built a cognitive account of how readers process sentences, developed in the "Little Red Schoolhouse" writing course he taught at the University of Chicago with Gregory Colomb and others. Williams ran Clearlines, a consulting firm that brought the same methods to corporations, law firms, and consulting organizations, and later editions of the book (continued by Joseph Bizup) reframed writing as a social act between writer and reader with an ethical dimension.

The plain language movement institutionalized the craft at the level of law. The Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN), a community of federal employees, first developed the Federal Plain Language Guidelines in the mid-1990s. This culminated in the Plain Writing Act of 2010 (Public Law 111-274), signed on October 13, 2010, which defined plain writing as writing that is "clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience," and required every executive agency to train employees in plain writing and to use it in all new or substantially revised public documents, with annual public compliance reports.

The cognitive turn came in the 2000s. Daniel Oppenheimer's 2006 experiments showed that readers penalize needlessly complex prose, judging its authors as less intelligent. Steven Pinker's The Sense of Style (2014) synthesized this research with linguistics, naming the curse of knowledge -- the failure to imagine what it is like not to know what the writer knows -- as the chief contributor to opaque writing. The problems this lineage evolved to solve are concrete: bureaucratic documents citizens could not act on, academic prose that concealed more than it communicated, and the ordinary failure of one mind to anticipate what another mind does not know. The field's answer, in the end, is diagnostic rather than prohibitive: clarity first, grace second, and a small set of principles that let any writer find and fix the defects in a draft.

## Core Concepts

### The Character-Action Principle

Williams's foundational diagnostic is that a sentence is clear when its grammatical subject names the character doing something and its verb names the action. When prose violates this pairing, readers stall. The classic failure mode is the nominalization: a verb frozen into a noun, as in "our lack of data prevented evaluation of the hypothesis" instead of "because we lacked data, we could not evaluate the hypothesis." Bureaucratic prose is dense with nominalizations -- "consideration was given," "a decision-making framework was formulated." The fix is mechanical: locate the hidden verbs, convert them back into verbs, and find the hidden characters to serve as subjects. Williams treats this as the first pass of revision, before any question of elegance.

### Clarity Is the Floor, Grace Is the Ceiling

Williams structures the craft in two stages. Clarity means the reader understands the sentence without effort; grace means the sentence is concise, shapely, and balanced in syntax, meaning, sound, and rhythm. Most of what passes for sophisticated style -- jargon, abstractions, stacked clauses -- is actually failed clarity. Grace is a later acquisition: once the prose cannot be misunderstood, the writer works on rhythm, emphasis, and elegance. The distinction matters because it orders the editing process: never polish a sentence that is not yet clear.

### Concision: Omit Needless Words

The single most repeated commandment in the craft. Strunk's version: "Omit needless words." Orwell's version: "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." Zinsser's version: cut every word that does not do useful work, prune qualifiers, and strip the clutter that accumulates when writers try to sound important. The standard tests are operational: read the sentence without the candidate word; if nothing is lost, the word goes. Zinsser adds a motivational observation: clutter is not innocent, because it is what makes prose hard to read, and hard prose drives readers away. Concision is therefore not an aesthetic preference; it is load reduction on the reader's attention.

### The Active Voice, Used with Judgment

Strunk's rule 14 recommends the active voice because it is more direct and vigorous than the passive; the Federal Plain Language Guidelines make the practical case that the active voice "makes it clear who is supposed to do what" and that the passive obscures responsibility -- "not 'It must be done,' but 'You must do it.'" The rule, however, is contested and the contest is informative. Williams defends the passive when the doer is unknown, when the doer is irrelevant, when the object needs emphasis, or when the passive lets the sentence flow from old information to new. Pullum goes further, arguing that Strunk and White denigrated the passive by inventing a deliberately inept example. The consensus across the craft literature: prefer the active voice by default, and use the passive deliberately -- never as camouflage.

### Given-New Order and Cohesion

Williams's most influential contribution is the old-before-new principle: start each sentence with information the reader already has from the previous sentence, and end it with the new information. The beginning of a sentence is its topic position and the end is its stress position, where readers weigh content most heavily. Prose that follows this pattern chains sentences into a flow the reader can follow and predict; prose that opens every sentence with brand-new material feels, as Williams's students are told, like being hit with bricks. The same logic scales to paragraphs: the end of one paragraph should set up the beginning of the next. This principle converts the vague advice "write coherently" into a checkable property of the text.

### Concrete and Specific Language

Strunk's rule 16: prefer the definite, the specific, and the concrete to the vague, the general, and the abstract. Orwell's rule 1 is the same instinct applied to figures of speech: never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure you are used to seeing in print, because dying metaphors -- "ring the changes," "Achilles' heel," "swan song" -- are used without awareness of what they actually say. Zinsser adds the word-level discipline: active verbs and concrete nouns, with adverbs and adjectives cut to a minimum because they usually repeat the verb or noun they modify. Specific prose does the reader's visualization work; abstract prose leaves the reader with nothing to see.

### The Paragraph as the Unit of Composition

Strunk's rule 13 makes the paragraph, not the sentence, the unit of composition: one paragraph, one idea, developed and then concluded. The Federal Plain Language Guidelines reach the same conclusion from the reader's side: express only one idea in each sentence, break material into short sections, and use headings that answer the reader's questions. The paragraph-level unit matters because readers scan before they read, and a paragraph with a single visible idea is one that survives the scan. The practical corollary is the topic sentence: state the paragraph's point early, so the reader can decide whether to read the rest.

### Rhythm, Parallelism, and Emphasis

Strunk's composition rules 18 through 20 cover the sentence's sound and shape: avoid a succession of loose sentences; express coordinate ideas in similar (parallel) form; keep related words together. Strunk's final principle -- place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end -- agrees with Williams's stress position: what a sentence lands on is what the reader weighs most. Zinsser's version is operational: read your prose aloud, and cut whatever your ear stumbles over. Parallelism is the craft's main instrument of rhythm, because balanced structure lets the reader anticipate the second member of a pair from the first.

### The Curse of Knowledge and the Audience

Pinker identifies the curse of knowledge as the chief cause of opaque writing: the writer cannot imagine a reader who does not know the jargon, the intermediate steps, or the scene in the writer's head. The cure is audience analysis conducted with humility, because, as Pinker notes, the social psychology evidence shows people are not good at inferring what others know even when they try hard. The Federal Plain Language Guidelines put the same point first: the first rule of plain language is to write for your audience, and the popular myth that plain language means dumbing down is wrong -- you write for PhD candidates if that is who will read you. Pinker's recommended stance is classic style: prose as a window through which the writer shows the reader something in the world, conversationally and without defensiveness, rather than the self-protective hedging of much academic prose.

### The Drafting Cycle: Write, Rest, Diagnose, Cut

The craft's last core concept is temporal: good prose is produced by a cycle, not by a single attempt. Zinsser's law -- rewriting is the essence of writing -- operationalizes into a repeatable loop, and the tradition's consensus procedure runs: get the material down without policing every sentence, then set it aside long enough to return with fresh eyes. The diagnostic pass then applies the principles in order -- convert nominalizations back to verbs (Williams), cut every word doing no work (Strunk, Zinsser), check the given-new chain sentence by sentence (Williams), and land each sentence's emphasis where it belongs (Strunk, Williams). Zinsser adds the test that catches what the eyes forgive: read the draft aloud, because the ear catches rhythm defects the eye skips. The cycle's deepest claim is that the second draft is not a repair of the first but the place where the thought itself becomes precise; cutting a sentence until only its working parts remain is how the writer discovers what the sentence actually meant. Writers who skip the cycle, the tradition warns, ship their first thoughts; writers who run it ship their best ones.

## Evidence

### Experimental Evidence: Needlessly Complex Words Make Authors Look Less Intelligent

Oppenheimer (2006) ran five experiments testing whether complex vocabulary, used needlessly, improves or harms how readers judge a writer. The method was substitution: essays and admissions statements were rewritten by replacing simpler words with longer, rarer synonyms, keeping the content constant. Across experiments, readers judged the authors of the needlessly complex versions as less intelligent and their writing as lower quality. The effect was mediated by processing fluency: when text is hard to process, readers attribute the difficulty to the source unless an alternative explanation is available. In one manipulation, a text made hard to read by a printer running low on toner produced the opposite result -- the author was judged more intelligent, because the difficulty was attributable to the printer, not the author. The design's strength is the constant-content manipulation: because only the vocabulary changed, differences in judgment isolate style from substance. The mediation analysis showed the complexity penalty operated through processing fluency rather than through any direct reading of the words themselves, and the rating task captured the judgment that matters professionally: how smart the author seems. Oppenheimer's title is itself a demonstration -- the paper's deliberately convoluted phrasing is the defect it diagnoses. The practical conclusion is precise: the disfluency penalty applies when the reader can blame the writer; the cure is not to add complexity, since fluency, not vocabulary size, drives the judgment.

### The Fluency Literature: Hard-to-Process Claims Are Believed Less

Alter and Oppenheimer's (2009) review consolidated a decade of research on processing fluency into a single account of how subjective ease of processing shapes judgment. Fluently processed information is judged more truthful, more credible, and more familiar; disfluent presentation impairs comprehension and reduces confidence in understanding. The review's key boundary condition matches the toner-printer result: fluency effects weaken when the reader has an obvious external explanation for the difficulty. For the writing craft, the implication is that every needless obstacle -- jargon, stacked clauses, tiny type, unbroken walls of text -- silently taxes the credibility of the content, because the reader's system cannot distinguish difficulty caused by the message from difficulty caused by the messenger. The review also documents the practical upshot for writers: disfluent formats depress comprehension even when content is identical, and readers misattribute that difficulty to the source. For claims that must be believed -- instructions, warnings, data summaries -- fluency is not cosmetic; it is part of the message. The review's framework gives the craft a single explanatory variable for most of its rules. Concision, plain vocabulary, parallel structure, and clear headings all reduce the reader's processing effort; each of the tradition's stylistic commandments is, on this account, a fluency intervention.

### The Institutional Test: The Plain Writing Act and the Federal Plain Language Program

The United States government provides the largest natural experiment in applying the craft at institutional scale. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 became Public Law 111-274; the implementing guidance, OMB memorandum M-11-15, states the causal rationale explicitly: a lack of clarity prevents people from becoming aware of programs, discourages them from applying for benefits for which they are eligible, and makes it harder to comply with requirements -- so plain writing "promotes the rule of law." The Federal Plain Language Guidelines operationalize the craft as policy: start with the document's purpose and bottom line, put the most important information first, use active voice to assign responsibility, prefer the present tense, use pronouns to pull the reader into the text, express one idea per sentence, and test the document with real users. The program's mechanisms are instructive because they treat clarity as a process, not a talent: designated senior officials, employee training, plain-writing sections on agency websites, annual public compliance reports, and user testing. The method is a compliance framework; the finding, after more than a decade, is that clarity can be specified, trained, and audited. The legislative record shows a policy response to a diagnosed problem: the bill was introduced in February 2009, passed the House by 386-33 in March 2010, passed the Senate by unanimous consent in September 2010, and became law the following month. The Act's definition of covered documents is deliberately broad -- any letter, publication, form, notice, or instruction necessary for obtaining a federal benefit or service, filing taxes, or understanding a compliance requirement -- and the OMB guidance makes the causal claim explicit: confusing forms discourage eligible people from applying, and unclear requirements produce noncompliance. The compliance apparatus -- senior officials for plain writing, employee training, annual reports -- treats clarity the way financial controls treat accuracy, as something to be verified rather than assumed.

### The Practitioner Test: Zinsser's Dejargonization Case

Zinsser's teaching career supplies a controlled practitioner case. In his workshops he took school administrators whose writing was dense with the jargon of education administration and trained them to rewrite their own documents in plain English, on the premise that jargon is a habit of insecurity rather than of precision. The method was the craft's standard one: identify the clutter, cut it, and rewrite the same content so that an outsider could act on it. The reported outcome is that the administrators produced clear, usable prose from the same material -- evidence that the barrier to clarity is usually not the difficulty of the subject but the writer's unexamined habits. The case matters because it generalizes the institutional evidence in both directions: the Plain Writing Act demonstrates that clarity can be mandated across an entire government, while Zinsser's workshop demonstrates that the same transformation is available to any individual writer in an afternoon, because the underlying procedure -- diagnose, cut, rewrite -- is domain-independent.

## Implications

### For Agents and Knowledge Workers: Writing as the Test of Understanding

This domain's anchor states the stakes directly: every agent produces written content, and every knowledge artifact is an act of communication. The craft reframes that act: a draft is a hypothesis about what the reader knows, and the edit is the experiment that tests it. For agents, the practical protocols fall out of the core concepts: run the character-action pass to kill nominalizations, run the given-new check sentence by sentence, cut to the concision floor, and place the claim in the stress position. Zinsser's doctrine -- that rewriting is the essence of writing -- converts editing from a chore into the primary act of thinking: prose that survives cutting is prose whose author knows what it says. The Oppenheimer result adds a reputation mechanism: readers judge the intelligence of the writer from the fluency of the text, so needless complexity is a self-inflicted credibility tax, one that applies to machine-generated prose as directly as to human prose. Two further protocols come from the federal guidelines and transfer unchanged: organize around the reader's questions, not the writer's outline, and use headings that say what the reader will find. The reader who arrives with questions should never have to hunt for the location of the answers; a document whose headings are the reader's questions is a document that scans. The curse-of-knowledge warning applies with special force to agents, whose internal state -- the retrieved context, the model's assumptions, the discarded drafts -- is invisible to the reader: the writer must reconstruct the reader's position from scratch, then write to it. Pinker's humility point is the guardrail: since people are poor at inferring what others know, the safe default is to state the intermediate steps, not to assume them.

### For Institutions: Clarity as Compliance Infrastructure

The Plain Writing Act demonstrates that clarity is regulatable because ambiguity has measurable institutional costs: missed benefits, failed compliance, and lost trust. The same logic transfers to any institution whose documents carry obligations -- health care instructions, financial disclosures, legal notices, internal memos. The federal program's architecture is reusable as a template: designate owners, train the writers, publish guidelines with concrete examples, and audit the output against the reader's actual comprehension rather than the writer's intent. The deeper point is the rule-of-law argument in OMB M-11-15: a government that cannot be understood cannot be obeyed or appealed, and the same holds for any organization that governs by text. The program also demonstrates the correct metric: comprehension, not style. The federal guidelines end where they begin -- with user testing -- because a document that follows every rule but fails its audience has failed. Organizations can copy the mechanism cheaply: a style guideline with concrete before-and-after examples, a designated owner, a training obligation for anyone who writes outward-facing text, and a periodic audit of a sample of documents against the reader's actual ability to act on them. The return is measured in the failures that stop happening: forms that get filled in correctly, notices that get read, instructions that get followed, and disputes that never arise because the parties understood the same sentence in the same way.

### For Persuasion and Reputation: Clarity as Ethos

The craft connects directly to the brain's rhetoric topic: clarity is an argument about the speaker. Oppenheimer's fluency findings and the classical tradition agree that the audience's judgment of the author is partly a judgment of the prose. Williams makes the ethical version of the point -- clear writing is a social act in which the writer accepts the cost of revision so the reader does not pay the cost of confusion. In professional contexts, the writer who has done that work is perceived as more competent and more trustworthy; the writer who hides behind jargon is perceived, experimentally, as less intelligent. Strunk's "omit needless words" is therefore not a sentence-level nicety but a persuasion strategy: the shortest path to the reader's assent is prose the reader never has to fight. Pinker's classic style gives the persuasion mechanism a name: the writer who shows the reader the world through clear prose is performing confidence, while the writer who hedges behind nominalizations is performing defensiveness, and audiences read the performance. The craft's connection to narrative runs the same direction: a story told in active, concrete, specific prose arrives, while the same story told in abstractions stalls -- the storytelling topic governs the plot, and the craft governs the delivery. For any agent producing persuasive output -- a proposal, a report, a recommendation -- the fluency findings mean the same evidence, written clearly, lands with more credibility than the same evidence buried in jargon. Oppenheimer's experiments make the counterproductive direction measurable: the writer who inflates vocabulary to seem smart is judged less intelligent, not more.

### For the Craft Itself: Revision as the Core Skill

Every major voice in the tradition converges on one procedural claim: the difference between bad and good prose is mostly rewriting. Zinsser states it as a law -- there is no good writing, only good rewriting -- and the craft literature operationalizes it as a sequence of passes: first make every sentence say what it means (clarity), then cut what does no work (concision), then fix the flow from old to new (cohesion), then attend to rhythm and emphasis (grace). Williams's two-stage structure keeps the passes ordered, and the Federal Guidelines add the final step the other authors take for granted: test on real readers. For anyone who writes for a living, the implication is that time spent revising is not overhead -- it is the work itself. The tradition also converges on what not to do: do not edit while drafting, because the diagnostic pass needs a draft to diagnose; do not polish before the clarity pass, because grace applied to unclear prose is wasted motion; and do not ship without a fresh eye, whether the writer's own after a rest or a reader's. The Federal Guidelines' final step -- test on real users -- closes the loop the craft leaves open, because the writer's own judgment of clarity is a weak proxy for the reader's, and the last pass belongs to an actual reader. The craft's deepest procedural insight is that these passes are cheap compared to their alternative: every hour of revision saves the reader's hours, and for writing that must persuade, instruct, or govern, the reader's hours are the whole point.

## Criticisms and Limits

The craft tradition is contested from within. Pullum's famous 2009 attack on The Elements of Style, published in the Chronicle Review, argued that the book's stylistic advice ranges "from limp platitudes to inconsistent nonsense" and that its grammar instruction is actively harmful: three of the four example pairs used to teach the passive are misdiagnosed, and the book's ban on the split infinitive is "bossy" without being correct. Pullum's target is the book's status as the entire grammar instruction most Americans ever receive. The defense, offered in the Chronicle's own letters pages, concedes the grammatical errors while holding that the stylistic core remains useful. The dispute teaches the right lesson: the craft's rules are heuristics with conditions, not laws, and Williams's defense of the deliberate passive is the mature position.

A second limit concerns the fear that plain language dumbs content down. The Federal Plain Language Guidelines reject this explicitly: plain language means writing for the actual audience, and writing for eighth graders is only correct when the audience is eighth graders. The real constraint runs the other way: no rule set can guarantee good prose. Orwell himself conceded that one could keep all six of his rules and still write bad English. The craft supplies diagnostics -- ways to find and fix defects -- not an algorithm that replaces judgment.

The final limit is that clarity is a necessary but not sufficient condition. Clear prose can be wrong, manipulative, or trivial; the craft governs the channel, not the content. That division of labor is exactly why this topic sits alongside the brain's rhetoric, logical-fallacies, and narrative-structure topics: craft makes the transmission reliable, while argument and evidence determine whether what arrives is true.
