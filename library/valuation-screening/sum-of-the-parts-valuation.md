# Sum-of-the-Parts Valuation -- Estimating Businesses Whose Pieces Are Worth More Than the Whole

Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation estimates a multi-business company's equity value by valuing economically distinct components with methods suited to each component, then reconciling those values through the parent balance sheet. Its apparent precision is justified only when segment earnings are normalized, standalone costs and shared liabilities are recognized, and taxes, dis-synergies, and separation costs are not mistaken for free value.

## Background

SOTP addresses a basic measurement problem: consolidated financial statements can combine businesses with different growth, margins, reinvestment needs, risks, and appropriate valuation conventions. Applying one multiple or one undifferentiated discounted cash flow (DCF) model to such a company can hide those differences. A segment-level valuation instead treats each operating business, material investment, and nonoperating asset as a distinct claim, values each claim on a consistent enterprise-value or equity-value basis, and then bridges the total to the value attributable to common shareholders. Damodaran's valuation materials illustrate both relative-valuation and DCF versions of this procedure and explicitly value corporate expenses apart from operating divisions ([1]).

The method is broader than a breakup calculation. It can be used even when no sale, spin-off, or liquidation is expected because the objective is first to estimate economic value, not to predict a transaction. Chlomou and Demirakos describe SOTP as a widely used practitioner framework for firms whose segments have different profitability and growth characteristics; their study also shows that analysts commonly treat it as a dominant or preferred valuation model ([2]). The author's synthesis is that a valid SOTP answers, "What are the consolidated claims worth if their distinct economics are modeled correctly?" A realizable-breakup analysis asks an additional question: "What net proceeds could owners receive after operational separation, tax, timing, and transaction frictions?" The second question cannot be answered by merely adding the first set of values.

Modern segment disclosures provide a starting map but not a ready-made set of standalone companies. IFRS 8 identifies operating segments through the information regularly reviewed by the chief operating decision maker and requires separate reporting when revenue, profit or loss, or assets meet specified 10 percent thresholds, subject to its other aggregation and coverage rules ([3]). U.S. GAAP Topic 280 uses a management approach to segment reporting, and FASB's 2023 update added disclosures about significant segment expenses while retaining that framework ([4]). SEC guidance likewise emphasizes compliance with segment-disclosure requirements and the relationship between internal reporting and external disclosure ([5]). These rules are designed to provide decision-useful reporting; they do not assert that every reportable segment has the systems, management, contracts, capital structure, or cost base of an independent enterprise.

This distinction became important in the empirical literature on diversified firms. Berger and Ofek estimated segment values using multiples from single-segment firms and reported that diversified firms traded at an average discount relative to imputed component values ([6]). That finding encouraged a simple interpretation: a conglomerate must be worth less than separable pure plays. Later research showed that the inference is sensitive to self-selection, the quality of segment definitions, benchmark construction, target characteristics, and the treatment of debt. Campa and Kedia found that controlling for the endogenous decision to diversify reduced the measured discount and sometimes turned it into a premium ([7]). Graham, Lemmon, and Wolf found that half or more of the decline in measured excess value around diversification in their samples arose because acquirers bought already-discounted businesses ([8]). Villalonga obtained a discount from conventional segment data but a premium when the same broad sample was decomposed with establishment-level business data ([9]). Mansi and Reeb showed that leverage and the use of book debt in excess-value calculations could also bias the apparent equity discount ([10]).

Those studies do not prove that conglomerate costs are imaginary. Berger and Ofek associated larger discounts with value-damaging patterns such as overinvestment and cross-subsidization, while subsequent papers identify both costs and benefits of internal capital markets ([6] [8]). The studies instead establish a narrower discipline: the gap between quoted market value and an analyst's imputed sum is not, by itself, evidence that shareholders can capture that gap through a breakup. Component definitions, comparable-company selection, accounting measurements, financing claims, and counterfactual standalone performance all affect the result ([6] [7] [8] [9] [10]).

Practical divestitures add another layer. McKinsey defines value created for a seller as price received minus value forgone minus separation costs; value forgone includes the business as currently operated and synergies with the parent. It also documents stranded parent costs in shared services and general overhead, some of which can persist for years after a disposal ([11]). Therefore, a parent allocation is not automatically a removable expense, and an unallocated expense is not automatically irrelevant. The analyst must estimate which functions travel with a segment, which must be rebuilt by the buyer or spun entity, which remain at the parent, and which can genuinely disappear.

The intellectual development of SOTP therefore runs from a useful arithmetic decomposition to a counterfactual operating model. Valuation texts now treat valuation by parts as a distinct technique alongside DCF, multiples, taxes, nonoperating items, and the enterprise-to-equity bridge ([12]). Academic evidence warns that reported segments and pure-play benchmarks can mismeasure the pieces ([6] [7] [8] [9] [10]). Accounting standards explain what management reports, not what separation would cost ([3] [4] [5]). The resulting method is most reliable when it does not begin with the desired conclusion that the pieces are worth more, but with a reconciliation that could also show no discount, a justified discount, or a whole worth more than its separable parts.

## Core Concepts

### Define the valuation perimeter before choosing a multiple

The first decision is what constitutes a value-bearing part. Reportable operating segments are an obvious starting point, but IFRS 8 and Topic 280 permit aggregation and reflect internal management reporting rather than a universal economic taxonomy ([3] [4]). Chlomou and Demirakos found cases in which analysts used more SOTP components than the issuer reported under IFRS 8 ([2]). Villalonga showed why decomposition matters: conventional segment data can group multiple activities, classify similar operations differently across firms, and change even without a corresponding operating change ([9]).

A useful perimeter separates components when at least one of four features differs materially: cash-flow economics, risk and cost of capital, appropriate peer group or valuation method, or ownership claim. Thus, a regulated utility, a cyclical manufacturer, a high-growth software unit, and a minority stake may require four different treatments even if management reports only two segments. Conversely, two reporting units need not be valued separately when they share customers, assets, economics, and a common peer set. The author's synthesis is that segmentation should be no finer than the available evidence can support: splitting a company into many rows without credible standalone data creates more assumptions, not more information.

The perimeter must also distinguish operating assets from nonoperating claims. Consolidated cash, equity investments, unconsolidated affiliates, surplus property, tax assets, and financial subsidiaries may not belong inside the operating multiple applied to an industrial segment. Debt, leases, pensions, preferred stock, noncontrolling interests, employee options, environmental provisions, and other debt-like claims may sit outside reported segment profit but still rank ahead of common equity. Valuation references treat the move from enterprise value to value per share and the treatment of nonoperating items as separate, necessary stages ([12]).

### Reconstruct standalone economics rather than copying segment profit

Reported segment measures commonly reflect management's internal view and can differ in included expenses, transfer prices, depreciation, stock compensation, restructuring charges, and capital allocation. IFRS 8 requires disclosure of the measure of segment profit or loss reported to the chief operating decision maker and reconciliations to consolidated amounts; it does not require every issuer to present a standardized standalone income statement, balance sheet, and cash-flow statement for each segment ([3]). FASB's segment-expense update improves expense visibility but remains anchored to the management approach ([4]).

A standalone normalization therefore starts with a three-column bridge: reported segment results, valuation adjustments, and pro forma standalone results. Intersegment revenue and cost must cancel on consolidation, so each segment should be valued using external economics unless a transfer would survive separation under an arm's-length contract. Centrally purchased services should be assigned according to their economic use, not according to an arbitrary reporting allocation. Expenses excluded from the segment measure but required to operate independently - finance, tax, audit, public-company reporting, insurance, information technology, treasury, legal, human resources, and governance - must be added somewhere. Conversely, duplicative coordination expenses that can credibly be removed should not be capitalized forever. McKinsey's divestiture analysis distinguishes transferable costs, redundant costs, and stranded parent costs, which is the relevant operating distinction ([11]).

Normalization also concerns the denominator. EBITDA must be consistent with the enterprise value being estimated: leases, pension service costs, stock compensation, capitalized development, and unusual gains or losses should be treated consistently between the subject and peers. Revenue multiples require comparable gross-versus-net presentation and similar unit economics. Book-value or earnings methods for financial businesses require capital, credit losses, and regulatory constraints that cannot be represented by an industrial EV/EBITDA multiple. DCF requires segment-level revenue, margins, taxes, reinvestment, and working capital rather than a mechanical allocation of consolidated free cash flow. These are applications of the consistency principles used in multiples, DCF, and cost-of-capital analysis ([1] [12]).

Cyclicality deserves explicit treatment. A peak commodity margin multiplied by a mid-cycle peer multiple can capitalize two optimistic assumptions at once. A trough result can make a durable asset look worthless. The appropriate base is normalized revenue, margin, earnings, or cash flow that reflects a defensible cycle, while the selected multiple should be measured at a compatible point in the cycle. The same discipline applies to businesses undergoing restructuring: cost savings should enter only to the extent that their implementation costs, probability, and timing are modeled.

### Match each component to its own valuation method

SOTP is a framework, not a single formula. Damodaran demonstrates both relative and DCF valuation by division and assigns different costs of capital and operating assumptions to businesses with different risk and economics ([1]). Chlomou and Demirakos found that analysts in their sample most often implemented SOTP with EBITDA multiples, but they did not find that SOTP significantly outperformed a full-company DCF in their setting ([2]). The implication is not that one method is universally superior; it is that component methods must be selected and cross-checked rather than treated as interchangeable labels.

For an operating segment valued on an enterprise basis, an enterprise multiple should be applied to a matching operating statistic. EV/EBITDA may be useful when depreciation patterns and capital intensity are reasonably comparable, but EV/EBIT, EV/sales, or a DCF may be more informative when they are not. Asset-based value may be appropriate for separable property, investment portfolios, natural-resource reserves, or businesses whose earning power does not adequately represent asset value. An equity method such as price-to-book, residual income, or capitalized earnings may be needed for regulated financial companies because debt is an operating input rather than merely a financing claim. The underlying valuation literature treats method choice as contingent on economics and available information ([1] [12]).

Comparable-company selection is often the largest hidden judgment. A pure play should match the segment's activity, geography, scale, cyclicality, growth, margin, capital intensity, accounting, and control characteristics closely enough that its multiple carries information. Berger and Ofek's method used industry-matched single-segment firms to impute component values, but later studies showed that the benchmark firms and conglomerate units can differ systematically ([6] [7] [8] [9]). A median multiple does not remove this selection problem. The analyst should disclose the peer set, period, metric definition, and reason for any premium or discount, then triangulate with DCF or another method where possible.

### Preserve the enterprise-to-equity bridge

The central identity is conceptually simple:

`Equity value = sum of attributable component values + nonoperating assets - parent and debt-like claims - value leakage.`

The difficult part is ensuring that every row uses the same value basis. If a segment is valued with EV/EBITDA or unlevered DCF, the result is enterprise value; debt must not already be embedded in that segment value. If a listed stake is entered at its equity market value, its investee debt is already reflected in the quoted equity claim and should not be subtracted again at the parent. A partially owned consolidated subsidiary requires a consistent treatment of its full enterprise value and noncontrolling interest, or only the parent's attributable equity value. Mixing the two is a common source of double counting. Standard valuation frameworks separate operating enterprise value, nonoperating assets, financing claims, and common equity for this reason ([12]).

Common parent debt should ordinarily be subtracted once in the final bridge. Allocating it to segments can be useful for estimating segment costs of capital or a pro forma separation capital structure, but that analytical allocation does not create additional debt. Segment-specific project debt may travel with an asset, while parent guarantees or cross-defaults may make legal allocation different from economic attribution. Mansi and Reeb's evidence that debt measurement can alter the apparent diversification discount is a warning that financing conventions can drive the result ([10]). The model should reconcile gross debt, cash, leases, pensions, preferred stock, noncontrolling interests, and other claims to the consolidated balance sheet.

Corporate overhead should also appear once. One approach allocates necessary standalone costs into each component and separately values the irreducible parent center. Another leaves segment results before corporate allocations and capitalizes the entire corporate cost at the center. Either can work if complete, but combining standalone costs in the segments with the full historical corporate expense at the center double counts expense. Damodaran's United Technologies example explicitly deducts a value for corporate expenses from the sum of divisional values ([1]). McKinsey's stranded-cost analysis explains why the historical allocation cannot simply be assumed to vanish on separation ([11]).

### Separate intrinsic value, market discount, and realizable proceeds

A "conglomerate discount" can denote at least three different quantities. First, a market discount is the observed gap between the parent's traded value and an imputed SOTP. Second, an intrinsic discount is a reduction for persistent economic burdens such as inefficient capital allocation, unrecoverable overhead, governance costs, opacity, or tax leakage. Third, a transaction discount reflects the net cost and uncertainty of converting component estimates into cash or independently traded securities. Empirical studies show that the observed first quantity is sensitive to benchmark and measurement choices ([6] [7] [8] [9] [10]); divestiture evidence shows that the third quantity can include lost synergies, separation spending, and stranded costs ([11]).

A blanket holding-company percentage is therefore not a law of valuation. If taxes, overhead, illiquidity, control rights, governance, and realization delay have been modeled explicitly, applying an additional generic discount may count the same burden twice. If those burdens cannot be modeled separately, a discount may serve as a scenario assumption, but its basis should be stated and varied. The author's synthesis is to treat the discount as an output whenever possible: compare reconciled intrinsic value with market value, then explain the gap by testable costs and expectations rather than inserting the gap into the calculation.

Tax deserves its own schedule. Selling an appreciated asset may create entity-level or shareholder-level tax; distributing a stake can have a different result; a spin-off may depend on jurisdiction-specific qualification; trapped cash and tax attributes may not be freely transferable. Because these outcomes depend on legal structure and facts, an intrinsic going-concern SOTP should not pretend that one transaction path is certain. It should show at least a no-separation case, a tax-efficient separation case, and a taxable-sale case when realization is part of the thesis. McKinsey's definition of divestiture value explicitly includes separation costs and value forgone, reinforcing the need to distinguish gross component value from net proceeds ([11]).

### Make uncertainty visible at the component and bridge levels

A single target value hides where the thesis can fail. Each material component should have a low, base, and high case for the operating statistic and the valuation parameter. The bridge should separately vary corporate-cost removal, tax leakage, transaction cost, net debt, pension or lease claims, and realization timing. Sensitivity is especially important when one small but high-multiple segment contributes a large share of estimated upside or when a listed stake is volatile.

Dependencies matter. A recession can lower cyclical segment earnings, peer multiples, asset-sale proceeds, and parent cash simultaneously. A breakup can eliminate a discount while also destroying purchasing, funding, brand, tax, or customer synergies. Treating every favorable case as independent creates an implausible upper bound. The author's synthesis is to use coherent scenarios before probabilistic aggregation: define operating and realization states that preserve causal relationships, then test the SOTP against a consolidated DCF, an observed market bridge, and a downside asset or earnings-power case. Reverse valuation can then identify what segment performance, cost removal, or transaction outcome the current price already implies.

## Evidence

### Practitioner implementation is common but not automatically more accurate

Chlomou and Demirakos conducted structured content analysis of 265 equity-research reports from 33 brokerage houses covering 140 UK firms. They found that analysts typically used EBITDA multiples in SOTP, often treated SOTP as the dominant or preferred model, and in some circumstances identified more components than the issuers' IFRS 8 reportable segments. They also found no evidence in their tests that SOTP significantly outperformed a full-company DCF ([2]). The method and sample make this unusually direct evidence about actual analyst practice rather than a textbook prescription.

The study supports two conclusions and rejects a third. First, SOTP is genuinely used where segment economics differ. Second, analysts do not have to accept accounting segments as the final valuation perimeter. It does not support the inference that more components necessarily produce more accurate target prices. Chlomou and Demirakos discuss the possibility that additional segmentation can support optimistic targets, so transparency about component choice and reconciliation is part of model control ([2]).

Damodaran's United Technologies teaching case provides a complementary worked example. It values divisions using sector-specific relative multiples and separate DCF assumptions, then deducts the capitalized value of unallocated corporate expenses. The relative SOTP, DCF SOTP, consolidated DCF, and market enterprise value differ, demonstrating that method choice and corporate-cost treatment affect the estimated gap ([1]). This is a pedagogical case rather than an out-of-sample performance study, but it makes the accounting architecture observable.

### The original discount result is economically important but not self-proving

Berger and Ofek used segment-level sales, assets, and industry multiples to compare diversified U.S. firms with imputed values based on single-segment firms. They reported an average value loss of roughly 13 to 15 percent and linked larger losses to overinvestment and cross-subsidization, with some offsetting tax and financing benefits ([6]). The paper established a durable empirical benchmark: market value can be lower than the estimated sum of segment values.

That design, however, requires the benchmark pure plays to represent the conglomerate's divisions. Graham, Lemmon, and Wolf studied 286 acquisitions with identifiable acquirers and targets, plus a separate sample of firms increasing their reported segment count. In the acquisition sample, target firms were already discounted before purchase, and adding them mechanically explained most of the acquirers' measured decline in excess value; in their broader segment-increase analysis, pre-existing target discounts explained about half of the observed decline. They also found no significant excess-value decline when the segment increase reflected internal growth or a reporting change rather than an acquisition ([8]). Their conclusion is not that diversification never destroys value, but that typical standalones can be a biased counterfactual.

Campa and Kedia addressed another counterfactual problem: firms choose whether to diversify. Using Compustat segment data from 1978 through 1996 and three methods to control for endogeneity, they found evidence of self-selection. The measured diversification discount fell in every approach and sometimes became a premium after controlling for the diversification decision ([7]). For SOTP, the implication is direct: a segment should not receive a pristine peer multiple merely because a peer is focused. Differences in quality, opportunity set, scale, and prior performance must be reflected in normalized fundamentals or the multiple.

### Segment definition can reverse the sign of the result

Villalonga merged Compustat with the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Information Tracking Series, which identifies establishments and permits more consistently defined business units. Her common sample contained 12,708 firm-year observations from 1989 through 1996. Conventional segment data produced an average diversified-firm excess value of -0.18, while business-unit data for the same broad sample produced an average premium of 0.28; the result remained positive through multiple robustness tests ([9]).

The study identifies concrete segment-data problems: reported segments can aggregate several activities, similar activities can be classified differently across firms, and firms can change segment reporting without changing their real operations. It does not establish that every diversified firm deserves a premium. It shows that the estimated discount can be an artifact of how the pieces and industry benchmarks are defined ([9]). This is exactly the model risk faced by an analyst who copies issuer segments into an SOTP without testing economic comparability.

### Financing conventions and implementation frictions change equity value

Mansi and Reeb examined whether risk reduction and debt measurement explain part of the diversification discount. They found that shareholder losses were related to leverage, that all-equity firms did not exhibit the same discount, and that using book debt in excess-value calculations created downward bias for diversified firms; their overall results made diversification insignificantly related to excess firm value ([10]). The evidence reinforces the enterprise-to-equity rule: an SOTP can appear to create or destroy common equity through the mechanics of debt measurement even when operating asset estimates are unchanged.

Accounting standards add evidence about the limits of source data. IFRS 8 defines segments from internal review by the chief operating decision maker and applies quantitative reporting thresholds; FASB's current Topic 280 project retained the management approach while requiring more significant-expense information ([3] [4]). SEC enforcement guidance emphasizes that external segment reporting must correspond appropriately to internal information ([5]). These sources improve confidence that disclosed segments reflect management's decision structure, but they do not supply all pro forma standalone costs, assets, liabilities, or cash flows. The missing data must be estimated and disclosed as model assumptions.

Finally, transaction practice explains why gross SOTP is not net realizable value. McKinsey's divestiture framework subtracts the value of the business forgone, lost synergies, separation costs, and stranded costs from proceeds. Its discussion identifies shared information technology, procurement, marketing, investor relations, legal, compliance, and other parent functions as potential sources of stranded cost and notes that recovery can take time ([11]). These are not reasons to abandon SOTP. They are evidence that the final bridge must contain operating and realization frictions rather than a single unexplained haircut.

Taken together, the evidence does not deliver a universal conglomerate premium or discount. It establishes that reported market gaps are conditional on unit definition, peer validity, self-selection, financing measurement, corporate-cost treatment, and realizability ([2] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] [11]). The author's synthesis is that SOTP is best viewed as an auditable hypothesis generator: it identifies where value may reside and which assumptions must be true, but the arithmetic alone does not prove that the value can be captured.

## Implications

### For investors and analysts: build a reconciliation, not a target-price collage

A decision-useful SOTP should begin with an ownership and accounting map. List each operating unit, unconsolidated investment, listed stake, nonoperating asset, parent liability, noncontrolling interest, and material contingent claim. For every row, state ownership percentage, accounting treatment, value basis, currency, valuation date, and method. Reconcile revenue, EBITDA or EBIT, assets, cash, and debt to consolidated disclosures before applying valuation assumptions. This process follows from the segment-definition and enterprise-to-equity problems documented in the standards and literature ([3] [4] [6] [8] [9] [10] [12]).

The next table should bridge reported to standalone economics. It should identify intersegment eliminations, parent allocations, omitted central services, one-time costs, stock compensation, lease treatment, working capital, capital expenditure, and taxes. Each adjustment needs a rationale and an owner: the segment, the residual parent, or a realization schedule. McKinsey's distinction among transferred, removable, and stranded costs provides a practical classification ([11]). Where disclosure is insufficient, the model should show a range rather than conceal the gap inside a multiple.

Each component valuation should then disclose the operating statistic, peer set or cash-flow forecast, selected multiple or discount rate, ownership adjustment, and resulting enterprise or equity value. The comparable set should be screened for economics, not merely industry label. A DCF cross-check is particularly valuable when a selected multiple implies margins, growth, or returns unlike the segment's history. Damodaran's parallel use of relative and DCF component values demonstrates this triangulation ([1]); Chlomou and Demirakos' performance result cautions against assuming that the SOTP label itself adds accuracy ([2]).

The parent bridge should be mechanically reviewable. The sum of operating enterprise values plus nonoperating assets should reconcile to total enterprise value; debt-like claims, noncontrolling interests, and other senior claims should then reconcile to common equity. Listed investments entered at equity value should not have investee debt subtracted again. Consolidated subsidiary enterprise values should not be counted without the corresponding noncontrolling interest. Common debt and corporate expense should each be deducted once. These controls implement the value-basis distinctions in mainstream valuation frameworks ([12]) and respond to the financing sensitivity documented by Mansi and Reeb ([10]).

### For value investors: separate cheapness from a realizable catalyst

A market capitalization below a modeled SOTP can indicate mispricing, but it can also indicate that the model overvalues weak segments, ignores corporate burdens, or assumes realization that owners cannot compel. The diversification literature shows that benchmark choice and pre-existing business quality explain part of apparent discounts ([7] [8] [9]). Therefore, a margin of safety should be measured against a conservative, reconciled case rather than against the most favorable pure-play multiples.

A separate catalyst analysis should ask who can act, what action is legally and operationally available, when value could be realized, and what leaks before owners receive it. Potential actions include better disclosure, cost restructuring, capital reallocation, partial asset sales, subsidiary listings, spin-offs, or full separation. The value consequence of each path differs because control premiums, taxes, minority rights, debt covenants, lost synergies, and stranded costs differ ([11]). The author's synthesis is that "sum of values" and "path to owners" should be separate schedules; combining them makes an attractive intrinsic estimate look like a guaranteed near-term return.

Management quality matters through capital allocation, not as an arbitrary percentage discount. A parent that reinvests cash from a mature unit into low-return projects can destroy value even if current segment EBITDA is correctly measured. A parent that supplies scarce capital, shared distribution, risk capacity, or operational capability can make the whole worth more. Berger and Ofek document patterns consistent with costs in some diversified firms, while Graham, Lemmon, and Wolf and Campa and Kedia show why those costs should not be generalized to every firm ([6] [7] [8]). An investor should therefore examine segment returns, incremental investment, intersegment funding, acquisition history, and disposal discipline alongside the valuation.

### For boards and corporate strategists: use SOTP as a resource-allocation diagnostic

SOTP can reveal that businesses face different investor expectations, capital costs, and reinvestment opportunities. A board can compare each component's value under current ownership with its value under alternative ownership after accounting for synergies and separation costs. Damodaran's component-specific DCF framework provides the valuation architecture ([1]), while McKinsey's divestiture identity supplies the net-value test ([11]). A high gross bid does not create value if it merely compensates for a business whose current value and synergies are being surrendered.

The same model can identify organizational work required before any transaction. If shared systems, contracts, brands, people, data, or financing cannot be separated, their cost and timing belong in the decision. If parent overhead remains after a disposal, the board needs a removal plan rather than a pro rata allocation. If a segment lacks audited standalone statements or a credible management team, its pure-play multiple may describe a future state that requires investment. Treating these issues explicitly turns SOTP from an activist slogan into a capital-allocation model ([11]).

Boards should also be cautious about interpreting the public-market discount as proof of managerial failure. Campa and Kedia, Graham and coauthors, Villalonga, and Mansi and Reeb each identify mechanisms by which measured discounts can shrink, disappear, or reverse without assuming a value-creating breakup ([7] [8] [9] [10]). The right response is not to dismiss the market gap, but to decompose it: operating underperformance, disclosure opacity, leverage, governance, corporate cost, taxes, illiquidity, or genuine synergy. Each explanation implies a different remedy.

### For model governance: make the thesis falsifiable

A robust SOTP records conditions that would invalidate it. Examples include segment margins that cannot be reconciled to consolidated profit, peers whose economics diverge from the subject, a corporate-cost estimate that omits standalone duplication, debt or minority claims that do not reconcile, tax assumptions without a feasible transaction path, or upside that disappears under mid-cycle earnings. These tests follow directly from the measurement failures documented across segment-reporting and diversification studies ([2] [7] [8] [9] [10]).

Sensitivity should distinguish operating uncertainty from realization uncertainty. The operating matrix varies revenue, normalized margins, reinvestment, cost of capital, and peer multiples. The realization matrix varies tax, stranded cost, separation spending, lost synergies, timing, and probability. A coherent downside case should allow adverse variables to move together rather than assuming diversification benefits disappear while every segment receives a peak multiple. Reverse analysis should compare the current enterprise value with the values implied for the components after fixing the most observable claims, then ask which assumptions the residual requires.

The model should also carry dates and evidence quality. Market prices, share counts, net debt, and foreign-exchange rates should share a valuation date. Forecasts should state their source and scenario. Peer multiples should use compatible fiscal periods. Segment data should identify whether they come from audited notes, management reporting, or analyst allocation. Academic evidence on reported segments demonstrates that classification itself is a source of error, so provenance is part of valuation control rather than clerical detail ([9]).

The ultimate implication is asymmetric. SOTP is especially useful when heterogeneity makes consolidated valuation misleading, yet the same heterogeneity makes component estimates difficult. More rows can expose value, but they also create more opportunities to mix bases, choose optimistic peers, omit liabilities, and capitalize removable costs inconsistently. The author's synthesis is that the strongest SOTP is not the one with the highest total. It is the one whose perimeter, standalone adjustments, method choices, parent bridge, and realization frictions remain intelligible under a skeptical review and whose conclusion can survive a consolidated DCF and downside cross-check.
