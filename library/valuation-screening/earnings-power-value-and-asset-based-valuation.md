# Earnings Power Value and Asset-Based Valuation -- Anchoring Intrinsic Value in What a Company Already Earns and Owns

Earnings Power Value (EPV) and asset-based valuation are two families of
intrinsic-value methods that deliberately avoid the growth forecasts on
which discounted cash flow (DCF) models depend. EPV, popularized by
Bruce Greenwald, capitalizes a company's normalized current earnings at
its cost of capital to estimate what the business is worth if it simply
persists unchanged; asset-based valuation estimates what the underlying
assets would fetch if reproduced from scratch or liquidated. Together
they anchor value in what a company already earns and already owns
rather than what it might become, making them the conservative half of
the valuation toolbox and the natural complement to growth-oriented DCF.

## Background

The intellectual lineage of asset-based and earnings-power valuation
runs directly through Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, whose Security
Analysis (1934) established the discipline of buying securities below
their demonstrable value. Graham's central obsession was the gap between
what a business is worth and what the market asks for it. His most
defensive methods measured worth not by projected profits but by the
assets already on the balance sheet: a company whose market price fell
below its net current assets, after subtracting every liability, was
cheap by construction, because the buyer was acquiring the working
capital at a discount and receiving the fixed assets and any future
earnings for nothing. This net-net approach treated the balance sheet as
a floor that required no forecasting at all.

Graham's framework flourished in the post-Depression decades, when
depressed markets made such bargains abundant. Graham-Newman, the
partnership Graham and Jerome Newman ran from 1936 to 1956, compounded
at roughly 20 percent a year using exactly this kind of quantitative
asset discipline. His students carried the method forward: Walter
Schloss ran a pure net-net strategy from 1956 to 2002, and Warren
Buffett built the early years of his partnership on the same "cigar
butt" stocks -- companies so cheap that even one last puff of value was
worth having.

The second half of the twentieth century shifted the center of gravity
away from assets and toward growth. Philip Fisher's qualitative growth
investing, the dividend discount models of John Burr Williams and Myron
Gordon, and the institutionalization of discounted cash flow analysis in
the 1970s and 1980s all directed attention to the future stream of cash
a business might produce. The balance sheet receded into the background,
treated as a minor input to a forecast rather than an anchor in its own
right. By the 1990s the growth-oriented DCF had become the default
language of valuation on Wall Street.

Seth Klarman's Margin of Safety (1991) pushed back, reasserting
liquidation value as the conservative benchmark against which a purchase
price should be measured. Klarman argued that an investor who buys at a
price that could be recovered from the assets alone, even in a forced
sale, has minimized the downside before the upside is ever considered.
The margin of safety, in his telling, is not a vague preference but an
arithmetic gap between price and the asset value an acquirer or
liquidator could realistically extract.

Bruce Greenwald synthesized these strands in Value Investing: From
Graham to Buffett and Beyond (2001, with Jude Kahn, Paul Sonkin, and
Michael van Biema). Greenwald, a professor at Columbia Business School,
organized intrinsic value into three layers: the reproduction value of
the assets, the value of current earnings power, and the value of
growth. His key contribution was to make each layer explicit and to show
that earnings power value -- the capitalized value of normalized current
earnings with no growth assumed -- could be computed from data that is
already in hand, without the fragile exercise of predicting the future.
This restored the balance sheet and the current income statement to the
center of a rigorous valuation framework at a time when most of the
industry had abandoned them.

## Core Concepts

### Earnings Power Value -- The Framework and Formula

Earnings Power Value answers a single, deliberately modest question:
what is the company worth if its current earnings simply persist forever,
with no growth and no decline? The answer is found by capitalizing
normalized earnings at the cost of capital. In its simplest form, EPV
equals adjusted earnings divided by the cost of capital, where adjusted
earnings are normalized operating profit after tax and the cost of
capital is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). A company
generating 100 million in sustainable after-tax operating earnings, with
a 10 percent cost of capital, has an EPV of 1 billion.

The power of the method lies in what it refuses to do. It makes no
forecast of revenue growth, no assumption about margin expansion, and no
guess about what the business will look like in ten years. It simply
capitalizes the earnings that already exist. Greenwald framed EPV as the
value of the firm "as currently constituted" -- the standing business,
separate from any future investment or competitive advantage it might
develop. This makes EPV both conservative and robust: it is far less
sensitive to the discount rate and to forecasting error than a DCF, and
it cannot be inflated by an optimistic terminal value, which in a
typical DCF routinely accounts for more than half of the result.

### Normalized Earnings and Maintenance Capex

The single most important analytical task in EPV is computing the
"normalized" earnings figure, because a naive current-year number is
usually misleading. Normalization has three parts. First, strip out
one-time and non-recurring items -- restructuring charges, asset sales,
litigation settlements -- so that the earnings figure represents the
repeatable core of the business. Second, adjust for the business cycle:
for a cyclical company, a single year's peak or trough earnings are not
sustainable, so the analyst uses a multi-year average or a mid-cycle
estimate. Third, and most technically important, replace depreciation
with maintenance capital expenditure. A business must reinvest to keep
its plant, equipment, and working capital in working order, but that
reinvestment does not add to earnings power; only spending above the
maintenance level funds growth. EPV therefore adds back the full
depreciation charge but subtracts the maintenance capex required to
sustain current operations, capitalizing only the earnings that could
theoretically be paid out without shrinking the business. The cost of
capital itself -- the denominator -- is not a growth-adjusted figure but
the plain opportunity cost of funds for a business of comparable risk,
typically estimated through the CAPM and WACC machinery.

### The Three-Tier Value Hierarchy

Greenwald's framework decomposes intrinsic value into three additive
layers. The first is asset value: what the firm's assets would cost to
reproduce at today's prices. The second is earnings power value: the
capitalized value of current normalized earnings, which captures the
franchise the firm already possesses (its brand, customer relationships,
and competitive position) as reflected in its current profitability. The
third is the value of growth: the additional value created by future
investments, which exists only if those investments earn returns above
the cost of capital. A firm that earns exactly its cost of capital is
worth no more than its EPV no matter how fast it grows, because each new
dollar of growth merely returns the cost of the capital required to fund
it. A firm that earns below its cost of capital destroys value with
every dollar of growth. Only a firm with a durable competitive advantage
-- a franchise, in Greenwald's language -- turns growth into value.

### Reproduction Value, Liquidation Value, and Book Value

Asset-based valuation is not a single number but a family of three
related measures, and the differences among them matter. Book value is
the accountant's figure, recorded at historical cost less depreciation,
and it can be wildly out of line with economic reality -- too low for
assets that have appreciated, too high for assets that are functionally
obsolete. Reproduction value asks a different question: what would it
cost a competitor to replicate this firm's assets from scratch at
current prices? Greenwald preferred reproduction value as the starting
point of his hierarchy because it estimates what a rational entrant
would have to spend to compete, and therefore whether the firm's
earnings reflect a genuine franchise or merely a temporary position.
Liquidation value is the most conservative of the three: the net amount
that could be realized by selling the assets off, either in an orderly
sale or under forced conditions. Liquidation value is typically lower
than fair market value because a compelled seller with a short exposure
window must accept discounts, especially on illiquid assets such as real
estate or specialized equipment. Klarman's insistence on liquidation
value as the benchmark rests on exactly this point: it is the value an
investor could actually extract if everything went wrong, and therefore
the truest measure of downside protection.

### Net-Net Working Capital

Graham's net-net working capital (NCAV) screen is the most extreme form
of asset-based valuation, and it deserves separate treatment because it
is the purest expression of the "no forecasting" philosophy. A stock
qualifies as a net-net when its market capitalization falls below its
net current asset value, defined as current assets (cash, receivables,
inventory) minus all liabilities, both current and long-term. In other
words, the market prices the entire company at less than the liquidation
value of its current assets alone, after every creditor has been paid.
Graham typically demanded a price no higher than two-thirds of NCAV to
provide a margin of safety. At such a price the investor is effectively
buying working capital at a discount and receiving all fixed assets and
all future earnings for free. The method is almost unusable for
asset-light businesses -- a software company with no inventory and no
receivables can never be a net-net -- which is precisely the point: it
is a tool for the asset-heavy, the distressed, and the ignored.

### The Value of Growth and Franchise Value

The conceptual capstone of the framework is the separation of growth
from value. Greenwald's most important analytical lesson is that growth
is not intrinsically valuable; it is only valuable to the extent that
the firm can invest new capital at returns above its cost of capital. A
business with no competitive advantage -- one whose economics are
perfectly contestable -- earns its cost of capital, and for such a
business growth adds nothing. A business with a durable advantage earns
above its cost of capital, and for that business growth is enormously
valuable. This reframes the entire valuation exercise: the analyst's
first job is not to forecast growth but to determine whether the firm
possesses a franchise, because the answer changes whether growth should
be valued at all. EPV is the tool that isolates the answer, by valuing
the firm as if it will never grow and thereby exposing how much of the
market price is already betting on a competitive advantage that may not
exist.

## Choosing Between the Three Approaches

The three valuation families -- asset-based, EPV, and DCF -- are not
rivals but tools for different situations, and the mark of a competent
analyst is knowing which one the facts demand. Asset-based valuation is
the tool of last resort and of first resort simultaneously: it is the
right lens when a business is distressed, asset-heavy, or in secular
decline, because in those cases the going-concern earnings are either
negative or unreliable, and the floor of asset value is the only
dependable anchor. Banks, insurers, real estate companies, natural
resource firms, and shipping companies all lend themselves to
asset-based analysis because their value is dominated by identifiable,
marketable assets. EPV is the tool for mature, stable businesses whose
current earnings are a reliable guide to the future but whose growth is
uncertain or modest -- a consumer staple, a utility, a cyclical at
mid-cycle. It produces a conservative value that requires no growth
assumption and is therefore robust to the analyst's optimism. DCF is the
tool for genuine compounders: businesses with a durable franchise where
the value genuinely does lie in the future, and where the analyst is
willing to make, and defend, an explicit growth forecast. The common
error is to use DCF everywhere, because DCF is the only one of the three
that can silently inflate value by smuggling an over-optimistic terminal
value into the denominator of every other judgment. The disciplined
analyst triangulates: compute the reproduction value as the floor, the
EPV as the standing value, and the DCF only if and when a defensible
growth story exists, then reconcile the three against each other.

## Evidence

### Oppenheimer (1986) and the NCAV Anomaly

The most direct empirical test of pure asset-based valuation comes from
Henry Oppenheimer's 1986 study of Graham's net current asset value
strategy, published in the Financial Analysts Journal. Oppenheimer
tested a simple rule -- buy stocks trading at or below their net current
asset value, hold for a fixed period, then sell -- across U.S. equities
from 1970 to 1983. The strategy returned roughly 29 percent annualized
against approximately 12 percent for the broad market over the same
window. The excess return was not explained by market beta, suggesting
that the anomaly was not compensation for higher systematic risk but a
genuine mispricing: institutional investors structurally cannot deploy
meaningful capital into the tiny, illiquid, distressed companies that
dominate the net-net universe, leaving them systematically underpriced
for the individual investor willing to buy them. Subsequent replications
extending into the 2000s and 2010s found the effect persisted, with
geometric returns in the mid-to-high twenties and excess returns
unexplained by the CAPM, the Fama-French three-factor model, or the
Carhart four-factor model.

### The Book-to-Market (Value) Premium

The academic foundation for asset- and earnings-based conservatism is
the value premium documented by Eugene Fama and Kenneth French in their
1992 study of the cross-section of expected stock returns. Sorting U.S.
stocks by book-to-market ratio -- the inverse of the price-to-book
multiple that anchors asset-based valuation -- they found that the
cheapest quintile of stocks outperformed the most expensive by roughly
four to five percent annually over the 1963 to 1990 sample. The finding
was subsequently replicated across thirteen international markets, and
its economic magnitude made it a first-order challenge to the
single-factor capital asset pricing model. The value premium is the
empirical expression of the same idea that motivates EPV and asset-based
valuation: the market systematically underprices what a company already
has and already earns, relative to what it might one day grow into.

### Practitioner Records

The long-run records of the investors who actually practiced these
methods are among the most persuasive evidence, precisely because they
span decades and multiple market regimes. Graham-Newman compounded at
roughly 20 percent annually from 1936 to 1956 with a strategy built on
asset-based screens. Walter Schloss, who worked for Graham and then ran
his own partnership from 1956 to 2002, compounded at roughly 16 percent
annually for over four decades by buying stocks below net working
capital, holding fifty to one hundred positions, and selling at book
value -- with no management meetings, no industry research, and no cash
flow models at all. Warren Buffett's partnership years (1956-1969)
returned roughly 30 percent annually, much of it from the same
concentrated net-net "cigar butt" investments. The consistency of these
records across different operators and different eras is evidence that
the asset-based approach exploits a durable structural inefficiency
rather than a period-specific lucky streak.

### Why Growth Forecasts Fail

The indirect evidence for EPV and asset-based methods is the documented
fragility of the alternative. In a conventional DCF, the terminal value
-- the value of everything after the explicit forecast period -- is
routinely the largest single component of the result, often exceeding
half, because the analyst assumes the business keeps growing forever at
a rate chosen largely by convention. That terminal value is acutely
sensitive to the assumed growth rate and discount rate, so a
small, defensible-sounding change in either can move the answer by tens
of percent. EPV and asset-based valuation sidestep this problem
entirely: EPV assumes zero growth and therefore has no terminal growth
rate to argue about, and asset-based valuation makes no forecast of the
future at all. Greenwald's framework supplies the theoretical reason
this matters, by showing that growth only creates value when returns
exceed the cost of capital -- a condition the typical DCF assumes by
default but rarely proves. The author's assessment is that the
over-optimism embedded in growth forecasts is the single largest source
of valuation error, and that EPV and asset-based methods are best
understood as a systematic defense against it.

### A Convergent Picture

Taken together, these four streams of evidence point in the same
direction. The empirical record shows that buying below asset value, as
in Oppenheimer's net-net study, and below earnings value, as in the
Fama-French book-to-market premium, has produced persistent and
economically large excess returns across nearly a century, across
multiple operators, and across international markets. The practitioner
records demonstrate that these results were not merely academic
artifacts but were actually capturable by real investors trading real
portfolios, in spite of the friction of wide spreads and illiquidity in
the small, distressed names where the bargains concentrate. And the
theoretical work of Greenwald explains why the pattern should be
expected to persist rather than to be arbitraged away: the value of
growth is real only when returns exceed the cost of capital, yet the
market on average prices growth as if that condition were always met.
The author's assessment is that asset-based and earnings-power valuation
are not a niche technique but the expression of a deeper truth about
markets -- that demonstrable, existing value is systematically
underpriced relative to speculative, projected value -- and that an
investor who ignores these anchors surrenders the strongest and most
replicable edge the record has to offer.

## Implications

### For the Practicing Investor

The practical consequence of this framework is a disciplined division of
labor among valuation tools. An investor analyzing a stable, mature
business should compute its EPV first, because the result is a
conservative value that assumes the business merely persists, and any
price above EPV is an explicit bet on growth that must be justified
separately. An investor facing a distressed or asset-heavy company
should compute reproduction value and liquidation value, and treat the
latter as the floor beneath which the downside is largely removed. Only
after these conservative anchors are in place should a DCF be attempted,
and only for a business with a genuinely defensible franchise. This
ordering operationalizes the margin of safety: it forces the analyst to
confront how much of the price is already paying for an unproven future
before deciding whether the gamble is worth taking. A stock that trades
above its EPV by a wide margin is not necessarily a bad buy, but it is a
buy whose thesis rests entirely on growth -- and the investor who has
not computed EPV does not actually know how large that growth bet is.

### For Deep-Value and Distressed Investing

Asset-based valuation is the analytical backbone of deep-value and
distressed investing, where earnings are absent, negative, or
meaningless and the balance sheet is the only source of information. In
liquidation, reorganization, and special-situation analysis, the
question is never "what could this earn" but "what could these assets
fetch," and the analyst's job is to value each asset class separately --
cash at full value, receivables at a haircut, inventory at forced-sale
prices, real estate at appraised value -- and sum them against the
capital structure. The net-net screen is the systematic, diversified
version of this logic for public equities. The lesson for the modern
investor is that these methods are not relics: the net-net anomaly has
persisted for nearly a century precisely because the institutional
capital that prices most of the market cannot, by mandate, buy the tiny
and distressed companies where the mispricing lives.

### Against Overconfidence and Growth Optimism

The behavioral value of EPV and asset-based valuation is that they are
engineered to counteract the specific cognitive failure that DCF
amplifies: overconfidence in one's ability to forecast the future. A
DCF invites the analyst to project revenue, margins, and growth decades
out, and every one of those projections is an opportunity for optimism
to compound silently. EPV, by assuming zero growth, removes the forecast
entirely; asset-based valuation removes even the earnings. In this
sense these methods are not merely alternative calculations but
anti-overconfidence technologies, in the same family as reference class
forecasting and premortems. The investor who habitually asks "what is
this worth if it never grows, and what would its assets fetch if it
failed" is forced to confront the possibility that the future will not
cooperate -- which is, in practice, the question that separates the
investor who survives a downturn from the one who does not.

### For Corporate Managers and Capital Allocators

The framework also disciplines the internal allocation of capital, not
just the purchase of securities. A manager evaluating whether to expand
should ask the same question EPV asks of the stock market: does this
investment earn more than the cost of the capital it consumes? The
three-tier hierarchy translates directly into a capital-allocation test,
because a division that earns less than its cost of capital is
destroying value with every dollar of growth, and the disciplined
response is to shrink it, sell it, or return its capital to shareholders
rather than reinvest. Greenwald's franchise concept is as much a
strategy tool as a valuation tool: it tells a firm whether its
competitive position actually justifies reinvestment, and it warns
management against mistaking mere scale for genuine advantage. A company
that applies the same conservative lens to its own projects that a value
investor applies to a stock -- demanding that current economics, not
hoped-for growth, justify the price -- is far less likely to destroy
value through acquisition, over-expansion, or the pursuit of growth for
its own sake. Read this way, the balance sheet and the income statement
become instruments of humility for managers and capital allocators, not
merely documents for investors, and the EPV framework becomes a
disciplining question that every executive ought to be able to answer
about every line of business: what is this worth if it never grows, and
would its assets still justify its existence if it failed?

### Connection to the Wider Brain

This topic sits at the junction of several domains in the brain. It is
the quantitative counterpart to the philosophy in value-investing, which
argues why valuation matters at all; the cost-of-capital topic supplies
the denominator that EPV capitalizes against; and the multiples topic
supplies the price-to-book and price-to-earnings building blocks that
asset-based screens assemble. It also connects to the behavioral
literature on overconfidence and anchoring, because EPV and asset-based
valuation are best understood as institutionalized humility in a field
whose dominant tool systematically invites hubris.
