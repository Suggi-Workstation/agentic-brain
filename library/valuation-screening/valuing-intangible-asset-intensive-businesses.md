# Intangible-Asset-Intensive Businesses Are Valued by Reconstructing Investment, Not Inventing Assets

Businesses built through research, software, brands, data, customer relationships, and organizational capability cannot be valued reliably from reported earnings and book capital alone because accounting recognition often separates current expenditure from the future benefits it may create.[1][2][4] A defensible valuation reconstructs only supportable intangible investment, applies explicit useful lives and failure assumptions, and reconciles the adjustments through earnings, reinvestment, returns on capital, DCF, and multiples without treating estimated capital as an independently appraised asset.[4][6][7]

## Background

An economic asset is a resource expected to contribute to future benefits, but a recognized accounting intangible is a narrower category. IAS 38 defines an intangible asset as an identifiable non-monetary asset without physical substance and requires identifiability through separability or contractual or legal rights.[1] The standard lists software, licences, trademarks, patents, films, copyrights, and import quotas as examples, while acquired goodwill is governed separately by IFRS 3.[1] Economic analysis is broader because the productive stock may include data, operating routines, customer acquisition systems, worker know-how, and organization-specific practices even when those resources cannot be sold separately or controlled completely.[8][10]

The distinction matters because many expenditures that resemble investment economically are expenses in financial reporting. Under IAS 38, research expenditure is expensed, development expenditure is capitalized only after specified criteria are met, and internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists, and similar items are not recognized.[1] The IFRS Foundation explains that internally generated cost is often difficult to distinguish from the cost of maintaining or enhancing the entity's operations or goodwill.[1] Under US GAAP, FASB's historical guidance generally expenses R&D, recognizes acquired identifiable intangibles, capitalizes acquired in-process R&D separately from goodwill, and applies specialized rules to internal-use and external-use software.[2]

Software demonstrates why a single label does not establish comparability. FASB's 2023 history states that costs for software to be sold are expensed before technological feasibility and capitalized between technological feasibility and product release, while internal-use software historically capitalized application-development-stage costs.[2] Accounting Standards Update 2025-06 removes the prescriptive internal-use project stages and, when effective, requires capitalization after management has authorized and committed funding and completion and intended use are probable; it is effective for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2027, with early adoption permitted.[3] In a 2026 valuation, the analyst must therefore identify the reporting framework, adoption status, software purpose, and policy rather than assume that all software spending received the same treatment.[2][3]

Acquisition accounting creates a second asymmetry. Internally built knowledge and organization capital commonly pass through R&D or SG&A, whereas acquired identifiable assets and goodwill enter the balance sheet through purchase-price allocation.[2][6][7] Peters and Taylor report that the mean firm in their study purchased 19 percent of its measured intangible capital externally and the median purchased 3 percent, leaving most of their estimated stock absent from the reported balance sheet.[6] Their estimate is model-based rather than an accounting fact, but it shows why organic builders and serial acquirers can report different assets, earnings, and returns despite similar underlying activities.[6]

Intangibles also differ economically from machines and buildings. Crouzet, Eberly, Eisfeldt, and Papanikolaou describe intangible capital as information stored in software, documents, databases, designs, or people.[10] Its use can be partly non-rival because the same algorithm, brand, or process can operate in multiple locations, creating scale and scope; its benefits can also be difficult to exclude because competitors or employees may copy, transfer, or appropriate part of them.[10] Organization capital can be embodied in key talent, so shareholders may not own all of the cash-flow rights that the capability produces.[8][10] These characteristics affect value through scalability, ownership, durability, and risk, not merely through whether an expenditure is visible on the balance sheet.[8][10]

Macroeconomic work established that the omission is economically large. Corrado, Hulten, and Sichel classify intangible investment into computerized information, innovative property, and economic competencies, including software, R&D, brand-related activity, worker capability, and organizational resources.[9] Their US estimates found intangible business investment of about $1 trillion in 1999, roughly comparable with tangible investment at that time, and their growth-accounting analysis found that recognizing intangible capital materially changed measured capital deepening and labor-productivity growth.[9] These aggregate estimates do not provide the value of any company, but they show why physical capital expenditure is an incomplete measure of reinvestment.[9]

The valuation problem is not solved by adding a plug called "intangibles." Damodaran's reconstruction begins with historical expenditure, an estimated economic life, an amortization schedule, and matched changes to earnings and invested capital.[4] Peters and Taylor use a perpetual-inventory method for accumulated R&D and a selected fraction of SG&A, while Ewens, Peters, and Wang estimate capitalization parameters from acquisition prices and find substantial industry variation.[6][7] Every approach depends on assumptions about which spending is investment, how quickly it decays, and whether the firm can capture the resulting benefits.[6][7][10]

The author's synthesis is that the correct objective is accounting consistency for valuation, not replacement of audited financial statements. The analyst asks what spending created a supportable future-benefit stock, what portion merely ran the current period, and how the classification changes normalized profit, reinvestment, and capital. The answer should remain an analytical schedule whose assumptions can be removed or stressed; it should not be represented as recognized accounting, separable fair value, or evidence of a competitive advantage unless independent facts establish those conclusions.[1][4][7]

## Core Concepts

### Start with expenditure, not a market-value plug

The most defensible reconstruction begins with observable spending. For knowledge capital, the usual starting point is disclosed R&D. For software, the analyst reconciles total development spending with the amount already capitalized and the policy governing the boundary between expense and asset.[2][3] For brand, customer, data, and organization capital, SG&A may contain candidate investment, but it also contains recurring selling, administration, rent, payroll, professional fees, and other current-period costs.[4][6][7] The analyst must therefore identify a supportable investment fraction rather than capitalize the whole line.

A reconstructed intangible stock can be expressed as a perpetual-inventory schedule:

`K_t = (1 - delta) * K_(t-1) + I_t`

Here `I_t` is the identified intangible investment during period `t`, `delta` is the economic amortization or depreciation rate, and `K_t` is the surviving cost-based stock.[6][7] A straight-line vintage schedule is an equivalent practical representation when the analyst assumes a fixed useful life: each historical expenditure retains only the fraction not yet amortized.[4] The schedule estimates unconsumed historical cost under stated assumptions. It does not estimate fair value and does not prove that the expenditures succeeded.[4][7]

The starting stock matters when only a short history is available. Peters and Taylor reconstruct pre-Compustat R&D and SG&A from founding dates and early spending because assigning a zero opening stock can understate established firms.[6] A company analysis can instead use a truncated stock and disclose the omission, obtain older filings, or model a bounded opening range. The author's assessment is that an explicit incomplete stock is preferable to a precise but unverifiable opening balance.

### Separate the investment fraction from the useful life

Two assumptions govern most reconstructions. The investment fraction answers how much current spending is intended to create benefits beyond the period. The useful life or depreciation rate answers how long those benefits survive. These assumptions should not be combined into one unexplained capitalization percentage because they affect earnings, reinvestment, and invested capital differently.[4][6][7]

R&D is a visible line, but not every research dollar becomes a productive asset. Failed projects are part of the cost of creating the successful portfolio, so a portfolio-level stock may include expected failures through a faster depreciation rate rather than deleting failed projects only after they become visible.[7] Ewens, Peters, and Wang estimate an average 33 percent annual R&D depreciation rate from their acquisition-based model and emphasize that the rate includes successful and failed projects.[7] Peters and Taylor instead use BEA industry rates ranging from 10 percent for pharmaceuticals to 40 percent for computers and peripherals, with 15 percent for uncovered industries.[6] The difference is evidence that useful life is uncertain, not permission to choose whichever rate produces the preferred value.[6][7]

SG&A requires an additional investment-fraction decision. Peters and Taylor capitalize 30 percent of SG&A after separating R&D and use a 20 percent depreciation rate, while acknowledging the proxy's measurement limits.[6] Ewens, Peters, and Wang estimate an average investment fraction of 27 percent but find a range from 19 percent in consumer industries to 49 percent in healthcare in their model.[7] Damodaran's Coca-Cola illustration assumes that two-thirds of SG&A is selling and advertising and half of that amount builds the brand, but he presents the case as an analytical illustration with data and life limitations, not a universal coefficient.[4] The author's synthesis is that industry research supplies priors; company disclosures and operating evidence must determine the selected range.

### Match earnings, reinvestment, and capital

Capitalization is a reclassification, not a free earnings add-back. For a selected expenditure `I_t` and current amortization `A_t`, the analytical operating-income adjustment is:

`Adjusted operating income = reported operating income + I_t - A_t`

The analytical invested-capital adjustment adds the surviving stock `K_t` to the corresponding operating capital, subject to tax and claim consistency.[4] The same current-period amount `I_t - A_t` is also added to reinvestment. Because adjusted after-tax operating profit and adjusted reinvestment move together before tax refinements, free cash flow should not rise merely because an expense is relabeled as investment.[4] A model that adds R&D back to earnings but omits the reconstructed investment manufactures cash flow.

Return on invested capital must use matched periods and definitions. The numerator is adjusted after-tax operating profit; the denominator is beginning or average adjusted operating capital. Damodaran's Amgen illustration increases both income and capital and therefore reduces the reported return rather than automatically improving it.[4] This is the expected control: when a large omitted capital stock is restored, an apparently extraordinary return may become lower but more economically interpretable.[4]

A write-off or obsolescence event also needs two-sided treatment. If a research platform, software system, customer cohort, or brand campaign ceases to produce benefits sooner than expected, the remaining analytical stock should be impaired and future amortization should change. The loss should not be erased by adding current spending back while leaving the failed stock in capital. The author's synthesis is that useful-life review is the intangible equivalent of testing whether a physical asset still supports production.

### Distinguish maintenance from growth investment

Intangible spending has maintenance and growth components even when both appear in one expense line. Maintenance preserves current revenue, product relevance, data quality, brand salience, customer retention, security, regulatory compliance, or operating capability. Growth spending adds new products, markets, customers, functions, or capacity. Damodaran's treatment requires reinvestment to remain consistent with growth and return on capital; Peters and Taylor likewise treat R&D and part of SG&A as investment rather than assuming that asset-light firms grow without capital.[4][6]

The split is not directly observable. A mature software company may need recurring engineering merely to maintain compatibility, security, and service quality. A consumer brand may need advertising to offset forgetting and competition before any additional demand is created. A data asset may require continuing collection, cleaning, permissions, governance, and infrastructure to remain usable. These examples are the author's synthesis of the maintenance principle; the amount must be estimated from company-specific product releases, retention, cohort behavior, management disclosure, and spending outcomes rather than from the asset label alone.[4][10]

One diagnostic is a steady-state test. Ask what level of spending is required for current revenue, margin, customer activity, and product capability to remain approximately unchanged. That amount belongs in maintenance economics even if accounting calls it R&D or SG&A. Spending above the estimated maintenance level may be growth investment only when there is evidence of incremental output or option value. A residual assumption that every increase is growth is circular because it defines spending as productive before observing its effect.

### Use unit economics as evidence, not as an asset balance

Unit economics help connect aggregate expenditure to future benefits. Relevant evidence can include customer acquisition cost, gross profit by cohort, retention, churn, expansion, contract duration, implementation cost, support burden, developer productivity, release cadence, conversion, pricing, and contribution margin. The choice depends on the business model. These measures are not interchangeable, and accounting disclosure may not supply enough history to reconstruct them.

A cohort can support capitalization when the analyst can trace current acquisition or development spending to multi-period gross profit after service and retention costs. The asset life should reflect observed survival or benefit decay, not the contractual maximum or management's desired payback. If recent cohorts retain worse, acquisition cost rises, or service burden increases, the implied stock should not preserve old economics automatically. The author's synthesis is that cohort evidence is strongest when definitions remain constant, cash receipts reconcile with reported revenue, and gross profit includes the continuing cost of serving the cohort.

For software and research portfolios, project-level probabilities and milestone evidence can supplement cohort data. A pipeline with identified projects, technical stages, addressable users, expected launch dates, and abandonment rules is more supportable than a single R&D multiple. Yet the portfolio remains uncertain: a technically successful product can fail commercially, and a failed project can create reusable knowledge. The model should therefore use scenarios or expected-value assumptions rather than capitalize only visible successes after the fact.[7][10]

### Treat different intangible categories according to their economics

R&D and patents have identifiable project or portfolio spending, but their lives depend on technical cycles, approval periods, legal protection, competing innovation, and commercial adoption.[4][6][7] Software may scale at low marginal distribution cost, but code can require continuous maintenance and become obsolete through architecture, security, platform, or customer changes.[3][10] A brand can persist through repeated customer experience and communication, but advertising expense contains both current selling and longer-lived investment.[1][4]

Data is valuable only through lawful access, quality, relevance, integration, analysis, and a product or decision process that converts it into cash flow. Crouzet and coauthors explain that the economic value lies in information rather than the storage medium and that limited excludability affects capture.[10] The author's synthesis is that database size is therefore not a valuation method: duplicated, stale, biased, restricted, or nonexclusive records can increase storage cost without increasing owner cash flow.

Customer relationships require attention to attrition, concentration, switching behavior, service obligations, and acquisition cost. Internally generated customer lists may be absent under IAS 38 even when acquired customer relationships are recognized in a transaction.[1][2] Organization capital is still less separable because it may reside in routines, teams, incentives, and key talent.[8][10] Eisfeldt and Papanikolaou's model treats key employees as sharing the cash flows and documents different risk characteristics for firms with high estimated organization capital.[8] The valuation implication is to forecast compensation, retention, and transfer risk rather than assign all organizational output to shareholders.

### Reconcile DCF instead of adding a separate intangible value

A DCF already values all assets that produce the forecast cash flows, whether recognized or unrecognized. Capitalizing R&D or SG&A changes the analytical decomposition of operating profit and reinvestment, but it should not create an additional value line if the same future cash flows are already forecast.[4] The principal benefits are better measurement of normalized margins, invested capital, reinvestment rates, and incremental returns. Double counting occurs when an analyst values forecast cash flow and then adds the reconstructed intangible stock as a separate asset.

The adjusted DCF should satisfy four controls. First, historical margins and returns should be restated using the same capitalization policy used to forecast them. Second, growth should require adjusted reinvestment, including maintenance and growth intangible spending. Third, the terminal state should include the continuing intangible investment needed to sustain terminal revenue and competitive position. Fourth, the forecast return on incremental adjusted capital should fade or persist according to evidence about competition, ownership, and scalability rather than according to the accounting treatment.[4][10]

The reconstructed stock is most useful as a diagnostic denominator. If the DCF assumes rapid growth with little adjusted investment, the model may be treating intangible growth as free. If it assumes high adjusted returns indefinitely, the analyst should identify the property rights, switching costs, network effects, legal protection, or organization-specific capability that preserves them. That is a separate competitive-advantage inquiry. Capitalizing expenditure measures resource commitment; it does not establish a moat.

### Reconcile multiples on a common accounting basis

Raw P/E, EV/EBIT, EV/EBITDA, price-to-book, and return-on-capital comparisons can be distorted when peers expense different amounts of economic investment or have different acquisition histories.[5][6][7] A fast-growing organic builder can report lower current earnings and a smaller asset base than an acquirer that records purchased intangibles. A peer set can therefore appear to reward the acquirer or penalize the builder without reflecting better economics.

The comparison can be repaired in two ways. The stronger method restates peers and the target under one capitalization convention, producing adjusted earnings, amortization, invested capital, and multiples. When disclosure is insufficient, the analyst should preserve reported accounting, stratify peers by R&D and SG&A intensity, acquisition history, growth, margins, and capitalized software, and use a wider range. The author's synthesis is that a partially adjusted target compared with unadjusted peers is often worse than a transparent reported-basis comparison.

Book and residual-income models require special caution. Adding a reconstructed stock to book capital can improve comparability, but the same adjustment must alter amortization and future earnings.[4][5] The residual-income topic already explains that an unsupported intangible asset becomes an invented starting anchor. For multiples, the adjusted denominator should be paired with a price or enterprise value whose claims and nonoperating items are otherwise consistent. No adjustment repairs a poor peer set or a sector-wide pricing error.

### Define a falsifiable adjustment policy

A supportable policy states the expenditure lines, investment fraction, history, opening stock, useful life, amortization shape, tax treatment, impairment rule, maintenance split, and missing-data treatment. It reports both adjusted and reported results. It also states what evidence would shorten the life, reduce the fraction, or eliminate the adjustment.

The worst failure is to infer an asset from the gap between market value and book value. That gap can contain expected rents, growth options, measurement differences, nonoperating assets, financing effects, market error, or overvaluation.[7][10] Solving backward from price to an intangible plug makes the asset unfalsifiable. The author's synthesis is that expenditure reconstruction may explain part of the gap; the unexplained residual should remain unexplained rather than be renamed.

## Evidence

### Accounting standards document recognition asymmetry

IAS 38 supplies direct institutional evidence rather than a valuation estimate. Its method is a recognition framework based on identifiability, probable benefits, and reliable measurement. It expenses research, capitalizes qualifying development, excludes internally generated brands and customer lists, amortizes finite-life assets, and tests indefinite-life assets annually for impairment.[1] The evidence establishes that absence from the balance sheet is not evidence of economic absence, but it also explains why recognition is constrained: future benefit, control, separability, and cost measurement can be difficult to demonstrate.[1]

FASB's 2023 review documents a different but related structure. The source traces immediate R&D expensing, specialized capitalization rules for software, recognition of acquired identifiable intangibles, expense treatment for many internally developed intangibles, and capitalization of acquired in-process R&D.[2] Stakeholder feedback cited in the presentation identified missing information and differences between purchased and internally generated assets.[2] ASU 2025-06 then changes the internal-use software recognition threshold prospectively from its effective date, illustrating that even within one reporting system the timing of capitalization can change.[3] These sources support policy normalization before cross-company comparison; they do not tell the analyst what an unrecorded asset is worth.

### Lev and Sougiannis test whether reconstructed R&D carries information

Lev and Sougiannis estimate R&D capital for a large sample of public companies, adjust reported earnings and book values, and test whether the reconstructed measures are associated with market valuation.[5] Their peer-reviewed 1996 study reports that the estimates are statistically reliable and economically meaningful, that adjusted earnings and book values are value-relevant, and that R&D capital is associated with subsequent stock returns.[5] The return result admits at least two explanations identified by the authors: mispricing or compensation for risk.[5] The study supports disciplined capitalization as informative, not the claim that every research dollar has equal value or that the reconstructed cost stock equals fair value.

### Peters and Taylor test a total-capital version of Tobin's q

Peters and Taylor construct knowledge capital by accumulating historical R&D and organization capital by accumulating 30 percent of SG&A, using industry R&D depreciation rates and a 20 percent organization-capital depreciation rate.[6] They add reported purchased intangibles and compare a total-q measure, market value divided by physical plus intangible capital, with conventional q measures.[6] In their panel tests, total q explains physical and intangible investment with within-firm R-squared values of 21 percent and 28 percent and total investment with 33 percent.[6] They also report that the macro investment-q regression rises from 4 percent explanatory power under the conventional specification to 61 percent when intangible capital is included.[6]

The method improves measurement but remains parameter-dependent. Peters and Taylor acknowledge difficulty measuring the SG&A investment component and test alternatives.[6] Their results show that omitted intangibles can impair capital and investment-opportunity measures; they do not validate one fixed 30 percent fraction for every firm. The study is especially useful as a warning against comparing market value with a book denominator that systematically omits the investment responsible for future output.[6]

### Ewens, Peters, and Wang estimate parameters from transactions

Ewens, Peters, and Wang use more than 1,500 public-company acquisitions from 1996 through 2017, matching purchase-price allocations for identifiable intangibles and goodwill with ten years of target R&D and SG&A.[7] They supplement the acquisition sample with 479 bankruptcies to reduce success-selection bias and estimate industry-level capitalization parameters.[7] Their revised NBER working paper reports average R&D depreciation of 33 percent, an average SG&A investment fraction of 27 percent, and SG&A fractions ranging from 19 percent in consumer industries to 49 percent in healthcare.[7]

They validate the estimates against market enterprise values, an adjusted HML factor, key-talent risk language, brand rankings, and patent values.[7] The adjusted capital measures improve cross-sectional explanatory power for enterprise value in every year from 1986 through 2016, with significance after 1995, and their intangible-adjusted HML construction has a reported 87 percent higher Sharpe ratio than the book-only construction.[7] The study also reports better sorting of brand rankings and association with patent values.[7] Because acquisition goodwill can contain synergies or overpayment and the document is a working paper rather than a final peer-reviewed article, the results should calibrate ranges rather than be imported as company facts.[7]

### Eisfeldt and Papanikolaou show that organization capital changes risk ownership

Eisfeldt and Papanikolaou estimate organization capital by accumulating SG&A with a perpetual-inventory method and rank firms relative to industry peers.[8] They link the measure to productivity, managerial quality, information-technology spending, key-personnel risk disclosures, and asset returns.[8] Their Journal of Finance study reports that firms with more organization capital have average returns 4.6 percent higher than firms with less and that a high-minus-low organization-capital portfolio returns about 4.7 percent per year.[8]

Their structural explanation is not that organization capital is a free asset. It is partly embodied in key talent, whose outside option affects the share of cash flow reaching shareholders.[8] This evidence supports explicit labor-retention and appropriation risk in valuation. It also limits a simple SG&A capitalization schedule: a cost stock attributed to the company can overstate owner value when employees or other parties retain part of the economic claim.[8][10]

### Aggregate and conceptual evidence explain why category matters

Corrado, Hulten, and Sichel build a broad national-accounting taxonomy and incorporate omitted intangible investment into a standard sources-of-growth framework.[9] Their estimates find a large omitted investment and capital stock and a material change in the measured sources of productivity growth.[9] The method operates at the economy level and cannot allocate value to a security, but it demonstrates that software alone is a narrow proxy and that innovative property and economic competencies are economically consequential.[9]

Crouzet and coauthors synthesize evidence around non-rival use and limited excludability.[10] Their framework explains why software, data, brands, and methods can scale across uses while remaining vulnerable to imitation, employee departure, or incomplete ownership.[10] These properties clarify why a large cost stock need not produce owner value and why a modest historical cost can support substantial value when the intangible scales and remains excludable. The author's synthesis is that expenditure reconstruction measures the input; competitive and ownership analysis determines the cash-flow output.

### Practitioner examples show the mechanics and their limits

Damodaran capitalizes historical R&D for Amgen, selected advertising for Coca-Cola, and recruiting and training for an illustrative consulting company by assigning useful lives, computing unamortized stocks, and adjusting income and capital.[4] In the Amgen example, the R&D adjustment raises operating earnings but raises invested capital enough to reduce the calculated return on capital.[4] In the Coca-Cola example, assumptions about the share of SG&A attributable to advertising and brand building drive the estimated brand stock.[4]

These cases demonstrate symmetric bookkeeping and sensitivity; they are not empirical proof of the assumed useful lives or investment fractions. Damodaran explicitly warns that SG&A or advertising should be capitalized only when evidence supports benefits across multiple periods.[4] The cases therefore support an auditable procedure and an inversion: if the analyst cannot defend the fraction, life, and future benefit, leaving the spending expensed is more reliable than creating a false asset.

## Implications

### For investors: reconstruct before comparing quality

An investor should begin with a reported-to-adjusted bridge over enough years to cover the longest assumed life. The bridge should show R&D, capitalized and expensed software, candidate SG&A categories, acquisitions, recognized intangibles, goodwill, amortization, impairments, and disposal effects.[1][2][3] The analyst then selects ranges for investment fractions and lives, calculates surviving cost stocks, and reports adjusted operating profit, reinvestment, invested capital, and returns beside the reported measures.[4][6][7]

The adjusted return on capital should be interpreted as a diagnostic, not a ranking produced by one formula. A lower adjusted return can reveal that reported capital omitted years of investment. A higher adjusted profit can reveal that current expense exceeds amortization during a build-out. Neither result proves competitive advantage. The investor still needs evidence that customers pay, products remain relevant, employees stay, data can be used lawfully, and competitors cannot reproduce the benefits cheaply.[8][10]

Organic and acquired growth should be compared on one basis. For an organic builder, reconstruct supportable internal investment. For an acquirer, retain the full economic capital committed, including recognized intangibles and goodwill, unless the objective is explicitly different. Do not remove acquisition goodwill from the denominator after leaving acquired earnings in the numerator, and do not capitalize internal spending while also adding an independent brand or data appraisal that reflects the same future cash flows. The author's synthesis is that the most useful comparison is return on all capital required to create or buy the operating capability.

### For DCF: make growth pay for intangible reinvestment

A DCF should forecast reported operating items and analytical reclassifications separately. Revenue, margins, and tax are forecast on the operating business; the intangible schedule then divides relevant spending into expense and investment and calculates amortization. Adjusted NOPAT, reinvestment, and free cash flow must reconcile to the reported-basis cash flow.[4] If capitalization changes DCF value by itself, the model likely omitted a matching reinvestment item, tax effect, or terminal obligation.

The explicit forecast should connect spending to operating evidence. R&D can be linked to pipeline stages, launch probabilities, product revenue, and useful lives. Software can be linked to releases, user growth, reliability, security, and replacement cycles. Brand and customer investment can be linked to cohorts, retention, pricing, distribution, and contribution profit. Organization capital can be linked to productivity, process transfer, employee retention, and compensation. These links are the author's recommended evidence map; unavailable disclosure should widen the range rather than be replaced with precision.

Terminal value requires continuing intangible maintenance. A company cannot preserve mature revenue, margins, brand strength, software capability, and data relevance while intangible investment falls to zero unless the analyst can explain why the assets do not decay. Conversely, terminal spending need not equal the current high-growth level. The model should estimate mature maintenance from benefit decay and competitive requirements, then tie terminal growth to adjusted incremental return on capital.[4][10]

Reverse DCF provides a useful check. Starting from price, solve for the revenue growth, mature margin, intangible reinvestment rate, useful life, and return on adjusted capital required. Compare those assumptions with historical cohorts and peer economics. Do not solve for a market-value intangible stock and call it evidence; price is the proposition being tested.

### For multiples and screening: normalize before declaring cheapness

A screen based on P/E, EV/EBITDA, or P/B can misclassify intangible-intensive firms. Current R&D or customer investment can depress earnings, while expensing prior investment can make the balance sheet and invested-capital denominator small.[5][6] Capitalized software and acquisition accounting can create additional differences among peers.[2][3] Raw low or high multiples therefore need an accounting-intensity explanation before they become valuation signals.

A practical screen can report R&D-to-sales, SG&A-to-sales, acquisition intensity, recognized intangibles-to-assets, goodwill, capitalized software, adjusted operating margin, adjusted return on capital, and price relative to both reported and adjusted earnings or capital. The variables are research flags, not buy rules. Large differences between reported and adjusted results identify cases where the investment thesis depends heavily on life and investment-fraction assumptions.

Comparable-company analysis should use the same convention for target and peers. When data permit, apply one schedule to all. When they do not, separate organic from acquisition-heavy peers, distinguish reporting frameworks and software policies, and emphasize revenue, gross profit, cohort, and cash-flow measures that require fewer unsupported adjustments. A peer median built from inconsistent denominators transfers accounting noise into the valuation.[2][3][6]

### For management and boards: improve disclosure without overstating certainty

Management can make valuation more reliable without placing speculative fair values on internally generated assets. Useful disclosure includes total R&D, major program categories, capitalized and expensed software, amortization, project stage, significant abandonment, customer acquisition definitions, retention by cohort, data-governance constraints, and the relation between intangible spending and operating outcomes. FASB stakeholder feedback identified the information gap, and ASU 2025-06 expands the relevance of disclosed capitalization policy for internal-use software.[2][3]

Boards should review intangible programs as capital allocation while preserving uncertainty. Approval records should state purpose, milestones, funding, expected life, maintenance obligations, abandonment conditions, and the operating measure that will show whether benefits arrived. Post-audits should compare the original case with outcomes. The author's synthesis is that the control objective is not to maximize capitalized cost; it is to prevent persistent spending from escaping return analysis merely because it is expensed.

Compensation design should recognize that reported earnings can penalize current investment while adjusted metrics can be gamed by broad capitalization. A balanced review should use cash flow, milestones, cohort outcomes, and multi-year adjusted returns. Organization capital also creates key-person risk and shared claims with employees, so compensation and retention costs are part of the economics rather than deductions from an asset that belongs entirely to shareholders.[8]

### For model reviewers: use a consistency and skepticism checklist

First, verify the perimeter. Identify whether the schedule includes R&D, software, advertising, customer acquisition, data, training, or broader SG&A, and prevent overlap. Second, verify history and opening stock. Third, test the investment fraction against disclosures and operating outcomes. Fourth, test useful lives against technical cycles, customer attrition, legal terms, employee turnover, and obsolescence. Fifth, match amortization, earnings, reinvestment, taxes, and capital.[1][4][6][7]

Sixth, reconcile acquisitions. Purchased intangibles and goodwill should not disappear merely to improve return metrics. Seventh, reconcile DCF. Reclassification should not create cash flow, and terminal maintenance should remain. Eighth, reconcile multiples across peers. Ninth, separate cost stock from fair value. Tenth, state falsifiers and sensitivity ranges. The author's assessment is that a model fails when changing the assumed life or SG&A fraction materially changes the conclusion and the analyst cannot provide evidence for the selected point.

The worst-case error is false precision that converts a weak business into an apparently high-return compounder. It can arise by adding current R&D back without amortization, capitalizing all SG&A, assigning long lives to obsolete assets, omitting maintenance, removing acquired goodwill, or counting the reconstructed stock again beside DCF value. The corresponding defense is symmetry, explicit assumptions, scenario ranges, and a rule that unexplained market-to-book value remains a residual rather than an invented asset.[4][7][10]

### A bounded valuation workflow

A defensible workflow has nine steps. Define the reporting framework and valuation date. Reconcile reported intangible accounting. Classify candidate spending by economic purpose. Estimate an investment-fraction range. Estimate useful lives or depreciation rates by category. Build vintage schedules and matched earnings and capital adjustments. Separate maintenance from growth. Reconcile DCF and multiples. Finally, present reported, adjusted, and downside cases with the evidence that would change each assumption.[1][2][3][4][6][7]

The workflow does not produce a universal intangible value. It improves the internal consistency of valuation inputs and exposes how much of the conclusion depends on unreported capital. The author's synthesis is that its strongest output is not the reconstructed balance: it is the ability to ask whether growth has been paid for, whether returns survive a complete denominator, and whether owner cash flows justify the market price.
