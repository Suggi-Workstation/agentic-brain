# Cyclical Companies Are Valued by Sustainable Mid-Cycle Economics, Not Current Earnings

A cyclical company's current profit can be a poor guide to value because volume, price, utilization, margins, working capital, and financing conditions move together across the cycle [1][2]. A defensible valuation reconstructs sustainable mid-cycle earnings and cash flow, tests more than one cycle path, and applies DCF or multiples to operating and capital-structure assumptions that describe the same economic state [1][2][3][4]. This does not require predicting the next macroeconomic turn; it requires preventing one peak or trough year from being capitalized as if it were permanent.

## Background

A cyclical company is one whose earnings rise and fall materially with recurring changes in demand, supply, capacity utilization, input prices, or commodity prices. Steel, mining, chemicals, paper, construction equipment, automobiles, semiconductors, shipping, and energy contain familiar examples, but cyclicality is a property of cash-flow drivers rather than an industry label. A company can be partly cyclical if one segment or customer group responds strongly to the cycle while another is supported by recurring service revenue or a structural trend [1][2]. The valuation problem is therefore not solved by placing a company in a sector bucket. The analyst must identify which operating drivers fluctuate, how those drivers interact, and whether the historical pattern still represents the business.

The problem begins with operating leverage. Many cyclical businesses carry fixed plants, fleets, labor, distribution systems, or development programs through both strong and weak demand. When volume rises, fixed costs are spread across more units and incremental revenue can produce a disproportionate increase in operating profit. When volume falls, the same mechanism works in reverse. Commodity producers add price leverage because the selling price may move substantially while a large part of the cost base changes more slowly. Damodaran emphasizes that the resulting volatility reaches not only revenue and earnings but also margins, returns on capital, reinvestment, working capital, and the cost of financing [2]. Normalizing only the income statement while retaining trough capital expenditure, peak working capital, or crisis financing costs therefore combines assumptions that did not coexist in one sustainable state.

Relative valuation can invert intuition at cycle extremes. At a peak, current earnings may be unusually high, so a price-to-earnings ratio can look low immediately before earnings fall. At a trough, the same company may show a high or undefined P/E because earnings are depressed or negative even though its long-run earning capacity remains intact. CFA Institute addresses this problem by defining normalized EPS as the level a business could achieve under current mid-cyclical conditions. It identifies two basic methods: average EPS over a recent full cycle, or average return on equity over the cycle multiplied by current book value per share [3]. These methods are useful starting points, but neither substitutes for examining changes in scale, business mix, capital intensity, and leverage.

The modern valuation literature developed several ways to avoid current-year anchoring. Damodaran describes three broad normalization methods: average absolute earnings over a full cycle; average scaled variables, such as margins or returns on capital, applied to the current revenue or capital base; and sector averages when the company's own history is short or structurally discontinuous [2]. The first is simple but fails when the firm has changed size. The second adjusts for scale but still assumes that the historical margin or return remains relevant. The third provides a broader reference class but can erase persistent company advantages or disadvantages. The methods are alternatives and cross-checks, not mechanical recipes.

DCF provides a different route but does not remove the normalization problem. Free cash flow to the firm equals after-tax operating earnings plus noncash charges minus fixed-capital investment and working-capital investment; free cash flow to equity also reflects after-tax interest and net borrowing [4]. A current earnings number cannot be inserted into a DCF without reconciling the associated investment, tax, and financing assumptions. CFA Institute explicitly warns that net income, EBIT, EBITDA, and cash flow from operations are not complete cash-flow measures for valuation because each can omit or double-count parts of the cash-flow stream [4]. For a cyclical company, the omission is especially dangerous because capital spending and inventories may be deferred at the trough or expanded at the peak.

The distinction between maintenance and growth investment is central. In Berkshire Hathaway's 1986 shareholder letter, Warren Buffett defined owner earnings as reported earnings plus depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other noncash charges, less the average annual capitalized expenditures and additional working capital required to maintain competitive position and unit volume [9]. He also stated that the required maintenance amount is an estimate rather than a deceptively precise accounting figure [9]. For an asset-heavy cyclical business, current depreciation can differ from future replacement cost, and current capital expenditure can be depressed by temporary deferral or elevated by capacity expansion. Sustainable cash flow therefore requires an economic estimate of through-cycle maintenance, not a reflexive use of either depreciation or one year's spending.

Empirical work supports the broader expectation that profitability is not a permanent extension of the latest observation. Fama and French estimated approximately 40 percent annual mean reversion in profitability in a partial-adjustment model, with faster reversion when profitability was below its mean or farther from its mean in either direction [5]. Nissim and Penman documented long-run and steady-state behavior for operating and financing ratios using firm data from 1963 through 1999 and showed why forecasting profitability and investment in net operating assets must precede forecasting free cash flow [6]. Mean reversion does not prove that every peak will fall to a historical average or every trough will recover. It provides a disciplined prior that current profitability requires explanation before it is capitalized.

A further complication is structural change. A historical average is useful only if the business, industry capacity, cost curve, technology, regulation, and customer demand remain comparable. A company may emerge from a downturn with a permanently lower cost base, lose a formerly protected market, change its product mix, or face a new source of demand that alters the old cycle. McKinsey therefore recommends valuing both a normal-cycle scenario based on prior cycles and a new-trend scenario based on recent evidence, then assigning probabilities rather than pretending that one forecast is certain [1]. The author's synthesis is that normalization is not an instruction to force every company back to its old mean. It is a method for separating temporary cycle position from evidence that the sustainable economics have changed.

## Core Concepts

### Define the cycle through operating drivers

Normalization should begin outside the valuation formula. The analyst maps revenue into economically observable drivers: units, selling prices, product mix, utilization, end-market demand, customer inventories, dealer inventories, backlog conversion, and market share. Costs are then divided into variable, semi-variable, and fixed components. This creates a causal model of why margins changed rather than an average that merely records that they changed [1][2]. For a commodity producer, the key state variables may be benchmark prices, grade, volume, freight, energy, currency, and position on the cost curve. For an industrial manufacturer, orders, dealer inventories, end-user sales, utilization, labor absorption, and input costs may dominate.

A cycle should not be defined solely by a fixed number of calendar years. The relevant window must include enough variation to observe both strong and weak conditions and should correspond to the drivers of the business. CFA Institute's full-cycle language and Damodaran's guidance both make the economic cycle, not a five-year spreadsheet convention, the unit of analysis [2][3]. If the available history contains only an expansion, an average of that history is still a peak-biased estimate. If it contains a crisis unlike ordinary recessions, the analyst should explain whether that observation belongs in the normal state, a downside scenario, or both.

The cycle map should also distinguish company execution from external conditions. Market volume and commodity prices are largely external; share gains, yield improvement, procurement, fixed-cost control, and mix can reflect company-specific choices. A normalized estimate that averages everything together can punish an improving company or reward a deteriorating one. The author's synthesis is to construct a bridge from an external mid-cycle environment to company-specific normalized performance: normalized industry demand and price, multiplied by a defensible market position and cost structure. This preserves evidence of durable operating differences without assuming that today's external tailwind or headwind lasts forever.

### Normalize scale, margins, and returns rather than one dollar figure

The simplest method is absolute averaging:

`Normalized earnings = average reported earnings over a complete cycle.`

This can be informative for a mature company whose capacity, share count, business mix, and accounting have remained stable. Its weakness is scale. Damodaran notes that averaging the earnings of a company that doubled its revenue during the period understates the earnings power of the current enterprise [2]. Acquisitions, divestitures, restructuring, inflation, share issuance, and repurchases create the same problem. Absolute averages should therefore be accompanied by a bridge showing why the historical dollars describe today's asset base.

Scaled normalization addresses this defect:

`Normalized operating income = current sustainable revenue x through-cycle operating margin.`

`Normalized net income = current book equity x through-cycle return on equity.`

`Normalized NOPAT = current net operating assets x through-cycle return on net operating assets.`

Damodaran recommends averaging margins or returns and applying them to a current scale; CFA Institute presents the analogous average-ROE method for normalized EPS [2][3]. Nissim and Penman's decomposition adds discipline by separating operating profitability, asset turnover, operating liability leverage, financial leverage, and borrowing cost [6]. A margin estimate alone can conceal the capital required to produce the sales. A return estimate alone can conceal whether the result came from operating performance or leverage. The normalized model should preserve both income-statement and balance-sheet economics.

Revenue itself may require normalization. Current sales at a commodity-price peak or at unsustainably high utilization are not a neutral scale base. For a commodity company, normalized revenue can be built from sustainable volume multiplied by a normalized commodity price, with costs rebuilt consistently [2]. For an industrial company, sustainable units may reflect replacement demand, installed-base activity, or a probability-weighted set of end-market scenarios. The analyst should avoid applying an average margin to peak revenue unless the historical evidence supports that combination.

### Normalize the complete cash-flow system

The central consistency rule is that every normalized component should describe the same state. CFA Institute expresses FCFF as:

`FCFF = EBIT x (1 - tax rate) + depreciation - fixed-capital investment - working-capital investment.`

It expresses FCFE as FCFF less after-tax interest plus net borrowing [4]. These identities show why normalized earnings are not normalized cash flow. A mid-cycle EBIT estimate must be paired with mid-cycle taxes, maintenance and replacement investment, working-capital needs, and financing assumptions. Damodaran explicitly warns against replacing depressed earnings with normalized earnings while leaving recession-year capital spending, working capital, and financing costs untouched [2].

Maintenance capital expenditure is the spending required to preserve unit volume and competitive position; growth capital expenditure expands capacity or capability [9]. The distinction is economic, not an accounting label. Management classifications can be informative, but the analyst should test them against asset age, depreciation policy, capacity, inflation in replacement costs, maintenance outages, safety requirements, and historical spending over the cycle. A trough-year reduction in capital expenditure may represent deferred maintenance rather than sustainable efficiency. Conversely, peak-year spending may include expansion that should not be deducted from no-growth earnings power. The normalized figure is usually a range because the maintenance boundary is uncertain [9].

Working capital also moves with the cycle. Inventories and receivables can build during expansions, while payables may provide temporary financing; a downturn can release working capital or produce involuntary inventory accumulation and collection risk. A single year's working-capital cash flow can therefore exaggerate sustainable free cash flow in either direction. Damodaran recommends scaling working capital to revenue or capital across the cycle rather than relying on an absolute current-year figure [2]. The analyst should distinguish recurring operating working capital from excess, obsolete, or distressed balances that require separate adjustment.

Taxes should follow normalized taxable income, not the current effective rate without review. Loss carryforwards can shelter a recovery for a limited period, while peak profits may generate tax rates that differ from a normal state. DCF can model the transition explicitly. A capitalized-earnings method should either use a sustainable tax rate or separately value temporary tax assets. The rule is to avoid treating a temporary tax shield or peak tax burden as perpetual.

### Treat leverage and claims as part of the cycle

Cyclicality affects common equity through both operating earnings and fixed financial claims. Debt service does not decline automatically when volume falls. A company that appears inexpensive on normalized enterprise earnings may still have fragile equity if a trough creates refinancing, covenant, dilution, or distress risk before earnings recover. CFA Institute notes that FCFE changes with net borrowing and that EV/EBITDA is generally more comparable than P/EBITDA across different leverage structures because EBITDA is a pre-interest flow to all capital providers [3][4]. This supports starting with operating enterprise value, then reconciling all senior claims to common equity.

The enterprise-to-equity bridge should include cash, debt, leases, pensions, preferred stock, noncontrolling interests, options, and other claims consistently. Current net debt may itself be cycle-sensitive. A peak can permit rapid deleveraging and make leverage look safer than it is; a trough can temporarily inflate debt-to-EBITDA because EBITDA collapses. The analyst should examine debt maturity, liquidity, covenants, and cash needs under the downside path rather than divide debt by a single normalized EBITDA and declare the capital structure safe.

Cost of capital also requires consistency. Cyclical operating risk should be reflected in the cash-flow distribution and in a discount rate estimated from relevant business risk, not by mechanically adding an arbitrary premium to a normalized forecast. Damodaran notes that financing costs can change through the cycle and warns against carrying a current extreme into a normalized valuation [2]. The author's synthesis is to model distress and refinancing in scenarios when they are discrete risks, while using a through-cycle required return for a going concern. Double-counting the same cycle risk in severely depressed cash flows, an extreme discount rate, and an additional valuation haircut produces an opaque result rather than conservatism.

### Apply DCF, multiples, and earnings power to compatible bases

A cycle-consistent DCF can be built in two ways. One approach forecasts the expected path from the current cycle position through recovery, peak, slowdown, and a normalized terminal state. The other values coherent scenarios, such as normal-cycle, prolonged-trough, and structurally improved or impaired cases, then probability-weights them [1][2]. The path method is useful when the near-term sequence matters for liquidity or claims. The scenario method is preferable when timing is too uncertain for one path. In both cases, continuing value must be based on normalized profit and reinvestment, not on the last explicit year's accidental cycle position [1].

A normalized multiple method first estimates a sustainable denominator and then selects a compatible multiple. Applying a mid-cycle multiple to peak earnings capitalizes optimism twice; applying a trough multiple to normalized earnings can embed pessimism twice. CFA Institute recommends normalized EPS for P/E analysis and notes that EV/EBITDA is useful across differing leverage, but it also warns that EBITDA is not cash flow because it omits working capital and fixed-capital investment [3]. Capital-intensive cyclicals therefore require an EV/EBIT, EV/(EBITDA less maintenance capex), or DCF cross-check where depreciation and sustaining investment are economically important.

Peer selection must also be cycle-aligned. A peer trading multiple is a ratio of price to an earnings measure that may sit at a different point in its own cycle. The analyst should compare normalized-to-normalized or current-to-current figures and explain differences in capacity, cost position, leverage, product mix, geography, and accounting. Liu, Nissim, and Thomas found that forward-earnings multiples generally explained prices better than historical earnings, cash flow, book value, or sales in their broad sample [7]. That evidence supports using informed forward measures, but it does not validate consensus forecasts that extrapolate a cyclical peak. The denominator must still be economically normalized.

Earnings power value offers a conservative cross-check. It capitalizes normalized after-tax operating earnings without credit for growth and then adjusts for nonoperating assets and claims. For a mature cyclical company, this can separate value supported by existing capacity from value requiring expansion or favorable prices. The method remains sensitive to the normalized margin, maintenance investment, and cost of capital; calling the result "no growth" does not excuse inconsistent cash-flow assumptions.

### Use ranges and falsification tests

A single mid-cycle number can become another form of false precision. The analyst should report at least a normalized base range and coherent downside and upside states. Scenario variables should move together: lower volume can reduce margin, release or impair working capital, alter maintenance timing, pressure covenants, and change financing access. A strong cycle can raise volume and price while also increasing working capital and encouraging uneconomic expansion. McKinsey's two-scenario method formalizes this logic by valuing a normal-cycle trend and an alternative new trend, then assigning explicit probabilities [1].

The most useful sensitivities test the assumptions that create normalized value: sustainable units, price, margin, maintenance capital expenditure, working capital intensity, normalized ROIC, discount rate, and compatible multiple. Reverse valuation adds a market check by solving for the mid-cycle margin or cash flow implied by the current enterprise value. The result is not proof that the market is right. It identifies the operating state the investor must accept or reject.

The author's synthesis is to record invalidation conditions before relying on the estimate. Examples include capacity additions that prevent price recovery, a cost curve that moves against the company, a structural loss of market share, maintenance spending above the modeled range, a debt maturity before plausible recovery, or evidence that an apparent cycle is a secular decline. This turns normalization from an assertion that "earnings will come back" into a testable valuation thesis.

## Evidence

### McKinsey's cyclical-company analysis shows current-earnings anchoring

McKinsey examined indexed EPS and share prices for 15 companies with a four-year cycle and compared DCF behavior under different levels of foresight [1]. Because DCF aggregates cash flows over time, high-cycle cash flows offset low-cycle cash flows, so a correctly specified value series should be materially less volatile than current earnings. The observed share prices were more volatile, and the authors found that consensus forecasts for cyclical companies tended to slope upward whether the company was near a peak or a trough [1]. Their interpretation was that the DCF framework was not the source of the inconsistency; forecasts that ignored cyclicality were.

The method matters because it separates a valuation identity from a forecasting failure. If one year's earnings contribute only a small part of a going concern's present value, a large valuation change requires information about the long-run trend, not merely the current point. McKinsey's proposed remedy is to construct a normal-cycle scenario from prior cycles, ensure that continuing value uses normalized profits, construct an alternative new-trend scenario, and assign probabilities [1]. This evidence supports scenario-based normalization and cautions against a consensus forecast that simply extends the latest direction.

### Damodaran's Toyota and Exxon examples require full-system normalization

Damodaran's study treats cyclical and commodity companies as businesses whose reported results depend heavily on an external macro variable [2]. In the Toyota case, the valuation models an explicit transition from depressed operating performance toward a normalized margin and return on capital rather than replacing one base-year number and assuming immediate recovery. The displayed forecast moves operating margin from a negative first-year level toward a 7.33 percent terminal margin and links stable growth to reinvestment through the return on capital [2]. The method demonstrates that timing matters: a company that takes several years to normalize is worth less than the same normalized earnings assumed to arrive immediately.

For Exxon Mobil, Damodaran estimated a historical relationship between operating income and average oil price with an R-squared of 90.2 percent, then used a normalized oil price to estimate operating income and a consistent return on capital and reinvestment rate [2]. The exact normalized price remains an assumption, so the case does not eliminate commodity-price judgment. It makes the judgment visible and carries it through revenue, earnings, reinvestment, and value. Damodaran also shows that oil-company P/E ratios differ substantially depending on whether current, trailing, forward, or five-year average earnings are used [2]. The case illustrates why a low P/E at a commodity peak can be a denominator illusion.

### Profitability and operating ratios exhibit mean reversion

Fama and French studied profitability and earnings in a partial-adjustment framework and estimated mean reversion of approximately 40 percent per year [5]. Reversion was not uniform: it was faster when profitability was below its mean and when profitability was farther from the mean in either direction. They also found that profitability mean reversion produced predictable variation in earnings [5]. The study supports a strong prior against capitalizing extreme profitability indefinitely, while its nonlinear result warns against assuming one constant reversion speed for all firms and states.

Nissim and Penman developed a hierarchical financial-statement framework using operating profitability, asset turnover, operating liabilities, financial leverage, borrowing costs, growth, and unusual items [6]. Their empirical analysis documented ratio distributions and time-series behavior over several decades and traced portfolios for five years after formation to examine convergence toward common levels. They reported mean-reverting behavior in operating drivers, including changes in asset turnover, and emphasized the accounting identity that free cash flow cannot be forecast without forecasting operating profitability and investment in net operating assets [6]. The implication for cyclicals is that normalized margin and normalized investment should be modeled together rather than selected from unrelated periods.

Neither study authorizes blind use of an industry mean. Competitive advantage, regulation, structural change, and accounting measurement can alter the relevant steady state. The author's synthesis is narrower: extreme current returns require an explicit persistence case, and a normalized forecast should decompose the mechanism of reversion instead of assuming it.

### Evidence on DCF and multiples supports triangulation, not one universal method

Kaplan and Ruback compared DCF estimates with transaction values for 51 highly leveraged transactions completed from 1983 through 1989 [8]. Using management cash-flow forecasts, their DCF estimates were within 10 percent of completed transaction values on average and performed at least as well as comparable-company and comparable-transaction methods [8]. The sample is specialized and transaction prices are not known intrinsic values, so the result does not prove universal DCF accuracy. It shows that structured cash-flow forecasts can carry meaningful valuation information when definitions and discounting are controlled.

Liu, Nissim, and Thomas examined a broad list of multiples and found that forward-earnings multiples produced pricing errors within 15 percent of observed stock prices for about half of their sample [7]. Their ranking generally placed forward earnings first, historical earnings second, cash flow and book value next, and sales last; they also cautioned that requiring positive measures and analyst forecasts excluded many firm-years [7]. For cyclical companies, the evidence has two implications. Forward-looking denominators can improve comparability, but a forecast is only useful if it incorporates the cycle. A precise consensus forecast that extrapolates the present state is not normalization.

The two studies together support triangulation. A cycle-consistent DCF tests cash-flow mechanics and the timing of recovery. A normalized multiple tests the result against market pricing for comparable economics. A reverse analysis tests what the quoted price implies. Agreement does not prove correctness because all methods can share the same peak margin or optimistic recovery assumption. Disagreement is diagnostic because it reveals where the assumptions differ.

### Primary company evidence shows why revenue, margin, cash flow, and backlog must be separated

Caterpillar's 2025 annual report provides a contemporary industrial example. The company reported record sales and revenues of $67.6 billion, while operating profit margin declined to 16.5 percent from 20.2 percent in 2024; profit of consolidated and affiliated companies declined to $8.882 billion from $10.788 billion [10]. At the same time, enterprise operating cash flow was $11.7 billion, year-end enterprise cash was $10.0 billion, and order backlog rose to approximately $51.2 billion from $30.0 billion [10]. These facts describe different dimensions of the business rather than one simple cycle signal.

The report does not establish Caterpillar's intrinsic value or prove where its cycle stood. It demonstrates why a valuation cannot infer sustainable economics from one headline. Revenue reached a record while margin and profit declined, cash generation remained substantial, and backlog increased sharply [10]. A normalized model would need to separate end-user demand, dealer inventory, product mix, price realization, costs, backlog conversion, capital expenditure, and financing rather than average EPS alone. The author's assessment is that this is the practical purpose of the driver map: reconcile apparently conflicting indicators before selecting a mid-cycle state.

### Owner earnings clarifies the maintenance-capital problem

Buffett's 1986 appendix compared two businesses with identical economics but different purchase-accounting charges and argued that reported GAAP earnings did not identify the relevant owner cash flow [9]. He defined owner earnings by adding noncash charges to reported earnings and subtracting the average capital expenditure and working capital required to maintain competitive position and unit volume [9]. The method explicitly accepts estimation uncertainty because maintenance requirements cannot be read mechanically from one accounting line.

For cyclical valuation, the importance is procedural. A trough can suppress capital spending below maintenance, making current cash flow look stronger than sustainable cash flow even while reported profit is weak. A peak can combine high earnings with expansion spending, making current free cash flow look weaker than steady-state owner earnings. The maintenance estimate should therefore use asset condition and through-cycle evidence, while growth spending should be valued through the cash flows it is expected to create. This is a synthesis of Buffett's owner-earnings definition with the complete normalization requirement in Damodaran and CFA Institute [2][4][9].

## Implications

### For investors: reverse the usual P/E intuition

The first implication is that low current P/E can be a warning rather than a bargain. If earnings are near a peak, the market may already expect reversion, and the low multiple may disappear when the denominator falls. Conversely, a high or undefined P/E at a trough can coexist with substantial value if the company has durable assets, liquidity, and a credible path to normalized cash flow. CFA Institute's normalized-EPS methods and Damodaran's full-cycle framework provide the appropriate correction [2][3]. The investor should compare price with a range of normalized operating values, not declare cheapness from a single ratio.

The second implication is that the investment thesis should state the cycle assumption in operating language. "Earnings normalize" is incomplete. A decision-useful thesis identifies sustainable units, price, margin, maintenance capital expenditure, working-capital intensity, tax, leverage, and timing. It also identifies what would prevent normalization. This makes the thesis falsifiable and prevents each new decline from being reinterpreted automatically as a deeper bargain.

The third implication is to separate enterprise resilience from equity resilience. A sound operating business can still be a poor common-equity investment if debt matures before recovery, covenants force asset sales, or dilution transfers recovery value to new capital. Starting with normalized enterprise cash flow and then stress-testing all claims avoids treating leverage as a cosmetic adjustment. The downside scenario should ask whether the company survives to reach the mid-cycle state, not merely what it would be worth after it gets there.

A margin of safety should be measured against the lower end of a defensible normalized range and a survivable capital structure. It should not be a fixed percentage subtracted from a peak-based target. If value disappears under a modest change in sustainable margin, maintenance spending, or recovery timing, the apparent discount is model sensitivity rather than protection. Reverse DCF can clarify how much recovery the current price already requires.

### For analysts: build an auditable normalization schedule

A reproducible analysis should contain at least five linked schedules. The first maps operating drivers and identifies the historical cycle. The second bridges reported earnings to underlying operating earnings by removing only documented nonrecurring or nonoperating items. The third normalizes revenue, margin, taxes, maintenance capital expenditure, and working capital on a consistent scale. The fourth values normal, downside, and changed-trend scenarios using DCF and at least one compatible multiple. The fifth reconciles enterprise value to diluted common equity and tests liquidity, covenants, and maturities.

Every historical series should state whether it is nominal or inflation-adjusted, continuing or restated, and affected by acquisitions, divestitures, accounting changes, or share-count changes. Absolute averages should be avoided when the company's scale changed materially unless the figures are restated to the current perimeter. Scaled measures should be tested for denominator changes. Sector averages should disclose why the peers share the same cycle and cost structure. These controls follow from the limitations of the three normalization methods [2].

The analyst should keep separate columns for measurement uncertainty, cycle uncertainty, and structural uncertainty. Measurement uncertainty concerns current earnings, maintenance investment, or claims. Cycle uncertainty concerns the depth and timing of recovery. Structural uncertainty concerns whether the old average remains relevant at all. A sensitivity table can address measured inputs; scenarios are needed for cycle paths; an alternative new-trend case is needed for structural change [1]. Combining all three into one higher discount rate hides the source of risk.

Citations and source dates matter because normalized value can be distorted by stale data. Commodity prices, peer multiples, debt, share counts, backlog, and inventory should share a defined valuation date. Company guidance should be distinguished from audited results and analyst assumptions. Market evidence should be labeled as observed price information, not proof of intrinsic value. The topic's cited academic and practitioner evidence supports methods and priors; it does not replace company-specific diligence.

### For corporate managers and boards: manage through the cycle without manufacturing smoothness

Managers should not treat normalized earnings as permission to ignore current weakness or to smooth reported results. The purpose is economic planning. A through-cycle view can help distinguish maintenance capital from expansion, protect liquidity before a trough, and prevent peak cash flow from being committed to fixed costs or acquisitions that require peak conditions to succeed. Buffett's owner-earnings definition and Damodaran's reinvestment framework both direct attention to the cash required to preserve the business, not to cosmetic stability in accounting profit [2][9].

Boards can use the same model for capital allocation. Expansion should be evaluated against normalized prices, utilization, and returns rather than against peak spot economics. Repurchases at a trough can create value only if liquidity and debt capacity remain adequate; repurchases at a peak can destroy value if current earnings make the shares appear cheaper than normalized economics justify. Debt issuance should be tested against trough coverage and refinancing access, not peak EBITDA. These implications follow from aligning operating and financing assumptions across the cycle [2][4].

Management communication should explain volume, price, mix, cost, inventory, backlog, capital expenditure, and working-capital movements separately. Caterpillar's 2025 figures illustrate why record revenue, lower margin, strong operating cash flow, and higher backlog can coexist [10]. Investors can then distinguish temporary absorption or mix effects from durable changes in competitiveness. The objective is not to provide a single management-defined "normalized" profit but to supply enough evidence for outsiders to reconstruct one.

### For screeners and model builders: treat normalized earnings as a range

A quantitative screen can reduce peak-earnings traps by replacing current P/E with normalized measures. Possible inputs include full-cycle median margin, average ROE applied to current book equity, average ROIC applied to current invested capital, EV divided by normalized EBIT, and price divided by normalized owner earnings [2][3][9]. The screen should also flag leverage, near-term maturities, capital-intensity differences, large acquisitions, and structural changes that make historical averages unreliable.

A screen cannot decide which average is economically valid. Its role is to surface cases for analysis. Companies with apparently low EV/normalized EBIT but deteriorating volume, rising maintenance needs, or a debt wall may be false positives. Companies with high current P/E but low price relative to conservative mid-cycle owner earnings may be false negatives. The model should retain the historical distribution and the assumptions used to transform it, not only the final normalized number.

Scenario outputs are more honest than one target. At minimum, a model should show a prolonged trough, a normal cycle, and a changed trend. The probabilities should not be engineered to reproduce the market price. A reverse calculation can instead state what probability or normalized margin the market price implies. The decision then rests on evidence about the business, not on the number of decimal places in the spreadsheet.

### Boundary of the method

Normalizing cyclical earnings is a security-valuation method, not a claim to forecast recessions, commodity prices, or sector returns. The analyst may use macroeconomic or commodity variables to define scenarios, but the output is the value of a specific set of operating assets and claims under stated conditions. Detailed macro forecasting belongs outside this domain; the valuation task is to prevent an extreme current state from silently becoming a perpetual one.

The method also should not be used when the evidence points to secular decline, technological obsolescence, permanent overcapacity, or a broken capital structure. In those cases, a historical mid-cycle average can overstate value because the old mean no longer exists. Asset value, runoff value, restructuring scenarios, or liquidation analysis may be more appropriate. The related cyclical-versus-secular framework is therefore a prerequisite to normalization, not an optional qualitative overlay.

The durable rule is consistency. Use a normalized operating state, normalized reinvestment, normalized working capital, sustainable taxes, a compatible cost of capital or multiple, and a complete bridge to equity. Then expose uncertainty through ranges and scenarios. A valuation that follows those rules can be wrong about the future, but it will not be wrong merely because it capitalized one point in the cycle as if it were the business.
