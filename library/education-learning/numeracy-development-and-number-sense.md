# Numeracy Develops When Quantities, Symbols, and Procedures Stay Connected

Numeracy develops when learners connect quantities, number words, written symbols, representations, operations, and estimates instead of treating calculation as the execution of detached rules. Effective instruction builds conceptual understanding and procedural fluency together, because each can create opportunities for the other to grow, while assessment and intervention identify which connection has broken down for a particular learner [1][2][10].

## Background

Number sense is an organized understanding of numerical magnitude, relations, operations, and reasonableness, not a single inborn faculty or a synonym for fast arithmetic. A learner shows number sense by recognizing that seven denotes the same cardinal quantity across different arrangements, locating seven relative to other numbers, decomposing it in useful ways, anticipating how an operation will change it, and judging whether an answer is plausible. Numeracy is broader: it includes using those numerical relations to interpret, communicate, and solve quantitative problems. Both constructs concern usable knowledge rather than isolated performance on a particular test [1][2].

Young children enter formal education with unequal but meaningful quantitative experience. They can often discriminate approximate quantities, recognize very small collections without counting, recite some number words, or enact informal sharing before they understand the exact meanings of written numerals and operations. These abilities do not fuse automatically. The development of exact number knowledge requires learners to coordinate one-to-one correspondence, the stable order of count words, cardinality, ordinal position, spoken labels, written symbols, and the structure of the quantities being counted [2][4][7].

The gap between recitation and understanding is visible in research on early counting. Le Corre and Carey found that children could recite number words beyond the quantities for which they knew exact meanings; some knew the meanings of only a few early words even while producing a longer count list. Their findings support the distinction between repeating a symbolic sequence and understanding that the last count word represents the number of items in the set. The authors' proposed account of how small-set representations produce the cardinal principle remains debated, so the evidence should not be read as settling a single cognitive mechanism [7].

As exact whole-number knowledge expands, learners must coordinate several representations at once. The written number 42 must be understood as a position in an ordered system, a quantity composed of four tens and two ones, a distance from zero, a member of multiple additive and multiplicative relations, and a value that can be renamed as 3 tens and 12 ones without changing. Research across four classroom projects identified increasingly integrated ways children conceptualized two-digit numbers and showed how those structures supported multiple valid addition and subtraction methods [9]. Place value is therefore not merely a rule about digit positions; it is a system for composing and recomposing units [1][9].

Fractions expose the limits of whole-number habits. A larger denominator does not by itself make a fraction larger, multiplication can produce a smaller result, and numbers between consecutive whole numbers are not exhausted by familiar counting sequences. The IES fractions guide accordingly recommends treating fractions as numbers with magnitudes, using number lines as a central representation, connecting common fractions with decimals and percentages, explaining why computational procedures work, and developing ratio and proportion strategies before presenting cross-multiplication as a detached shortcut [6].

This developmental account does not imply one rigid age schedule. The National Academies describes learning paths as ordered supports while explicitly noting that children vary, can exceed age-linked expectations, and may learn later content without complete mastery of every earlier step when instruction and practice are sufficient. Grade-level benchmarks are curricular guides, not biological stages or ceilings [2][3]. A useful progression therefore specifies dependencies while leaving room for diagnostic evidence, alternative representations, and accelerated or intensified instruction.

The modern instructional problem is often framed as a choice between understanding and skill. That choice is false. The National Research Council defined mathematical proficiency through interwoven strands: conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, strategic competence, adaptive reasoning, and productive disposition. Procedural fluency means flexible, accurate, efficient, and appropriate use of procedures, including mental, written, technological, and representational methods; it is not mere speed. Research on decimal-fraction learning further indicates that conceptual and procedural knowledge can develop iteratively rather than in one invariant order [1][10].

The phrase "meaning before procedures" is therefore best treated as a warning against meaningless procedure, not as a rule that all procedures must be delayed until conceptual knowledge is complete. A learner may discover a relation by comparing procedures, gain conceptual insight through successful practice, or use a representation to make a procedure intelligible. The author's synthesis is that instruction should establish quantitative meaning before and alongside formal procedures, then use explanation, comparison, estimation, and varied practice to keep the two forms of knowledge connected [1][10].

Within the education-learning domain, the central question is how numerical knowledge becomes usable through teaching, practice, assessment, and intervention. Formal mathematical proof belongs to mathematics as a discipline, while generalized test-preparation tactics concern the format of an assessment rather than the development of number knowledge. Numeracy instruction can use tests as evidence, but its target is a network of concepts, procedures, representations, and dispositions that transfers beyond the tested item [1][5].

## Core Concepts

### Quantity, magnitude, and symbols

Numerical development begins with distinctions that later instruction must connect rather than conflate. Nonsymbolic comparison asks which collection appears more numerous without requiring number words or numerals. Symbolic magnitude knowledge asks what spoken or written numbers mean and how they relate. A meta-analysis of 45 articles, 284 effect sizes, and 17,201 participants found reliable associations between magnitude-comparison performance and mathematical competence, with a stronger association for symbolic comparison than for nonsymbolic comparison. Because these are correlations, they do not establish that training approximate quantity discrimination will cause broad mathematical gains [8].

Exact number depends on symbolic and relational knowledge. A numeral does not carry its meaning in its shape: learners must map it to a number word, a cardinal quantity, an order relation, and eventually a position on a number line. Instruction can make these mappings explicit by asking learners to build a set for a numeral, select the numeral for a set, compare two quantities, place a number between benchmarks, and explain how two representations show the same amount. The IES early-mathematics guide places subitizing, counting, magnitude comparison, numeral use, and simple operations within a developmental progression rather than teaching them as unrelated objectives [4].

A useful distinction is between approximate magnitude and exact cardinality. Approximation supports quick comparison and reasonableness, but exact arithmetic depends on learned symbols, counting principles, unit structures, and relations among numbers. The stronger meta-analytic association for symbolic magnitude processing and the observed lag between count-list recitation and exact word meanings argue against reducing school numeracy to an allegedly pure, nonsymbolic "number sense" [7][8]. The author's synthesis is that approximate quantity is one input to numerical development, while culturally taught symbols and operations reorganize what learners can represent exactly.

### Cardinality, order, and the unit

Counting coordinates a sequence of labels with a collection so that each item receives one label and the last label represents the collection's cardinality. Order adds the understanding that successive whole numbers differ by one and that later numbers in the count sequence represent larger quantities. Learners also need conservation: rearranging objects does not change their number. These ideas can be probed through tasks that vary spacing, object size, arrangement, and starting point rather than rewarding a memorized count alone [2][4].

The unit is the hidden variable in much numerical reasoning. Ten ones can be treated as one ten; a length can be measured in centimeters or meters; a fraction names equal parts relative to a specified whole; and a ratio compares two quantities through a multiplicative relation. Many apparent errors are coherent responses to an unnoticed unit. A learner who adds numerators and denominators separately may be treating each visible whole number as an independent count rather than preserving a common fractional unit. Instruction should therefore ask "one what?" and "equal parts of which whole?" whenever the unit could shift [1][6].

### Place value and operation meanings

Base-ten notation compresses quantity through recursive units: ten ones form a ten, ten tens form a hundred, and the value of a digit depends on both its face and its position. Learners must link physical or drawn groups, spoken number names, expanded notation, and standard notation. Fuson and colleagues described several conceptions of two-digit number, from treating all items as undifferentiated ones to coordinating tens as countable composite units with a remaining sequence of ones. These conceptions supported different invented methods for multidigit addition and subtraction [9].

Operations should be learned as families of relations, not as key words. Addition can combine or increase; subtraction can remove, compare, or find an unknown addend; multiplication can represent equal groups, arrays, scaling, rates, or area; division can determine group size or number of groups. Different situations can share an operation, and similar language can conceal different structures. Diagrams, equations with the unknown in varied positions, and explanations of what each quantity means help learners map a situation to a mathematical model instead of grabbing visible numbers and applying a familiar operation [1][5].

Properties of operations create flexible computation. Commutativity permits 8 + 27 to be viewed as 27 + 8; associativity permits 25 + 38 + 75 to become 100 + 38; distributivity permits 7 x 18 to become 7 x 20 - 7 x 2. These are not decorative vocabulary terms. They explain why mental strategies and standard algorithms work, support estimation, and give learners ways to check or repair a procedure [1]. Fluency improves when learners can select among such relations rather than reproduce one path regardless of the numbers.

### Conceptual understanding and procedural fluency

Conceptual knowledge concerns principles and relations; procedural knowledge concerns sequences of action that solve classes of problems. They differ analytically but interact during learning. In decimal-fraction studies, Rittle-Johnson, Siegler, and Alibali found that initial conceptual knowledge predicted procedural gains, while procedural gains predicted later conceptual improvement. Experimentally supporting correct problem representations with prompts and partitioned number lines improved representation and procedural learning [10].

This iterative relation changes how practice should be designed. Early examples can make a relation visible, guided problems can stabilize a method, comparison can reveal why two methods agree, and varied problems can require the learner to choose rather than imitate. Retrieval of basic facts can reduce working-memory demands during complex work, but fluent recall is educationally valuable only when facts remain connected to properties, decompositions, and reasonableness. The National Research Council's framework therefore joins efficiency with flexibility and appropriateness [1][10].

Worked examples are useful when they expose decisions rather than invite copying. A teacher or text can model how to identify the unit, choose a representation, estimate a range, execute a procedure, and check the answer. Guidance should then fade as the learner completes missing steps, explains choices, compares examples, and solves new forms. This sequence connects to cognitive-load principles: novices benefit from explicit support, while learners with stronger schemas need opportunities to select and adapt strategies rather than receive every step [5].

### Representations as bridges, not decorations

Concrete objects, drawings, ten frames, base-ten blocks, arrays, bar models, number lines, tables, and equations highlight different relations. No representation is self-explanatory. A base-ten block can become a toy, a number line can be read as a row of labels rather than equal distances, and an attractive context can draw attention away from the relevant structure. Teachers must name what each feature represents and explicitly connect corresponding features across forms [5][13][14].

A meta-analysis of 55 studies involving 7,237 learners from kindergarten through college found small-to-moderate advantages for instruction with concrete manipulatives over instruction using only abstract symbols, but effects varied with instructional and methodological features. Effects were stronger for retention than for problem solving, transfer, or justification. This means manipulatives are not a universal cure; their value depends on whether learners map actions on objects to the intended mathematical relations [13].

Concreteness fading is one way to support that mapping. The sequence begins with an interpretable concrete form, moves through simplified or pictorial forms, and arrives at abstract symbols while preserving explicit correspondences. A systematic review argued that this can combine grounding with generalization, but also identified unanswered questions and variation across tasks and learners. The defensible principle is not that every lesson must follow one concrete-pictorial-abstract script, but that transitions among representations must be taught and checked [14].

The number line is especially powerful because it represents numbers as positions and distances within one ordered system. It can connect counting with magnitude, operations with movement, fractions with measurement, and ratios with scale. Its conventions must still be taught: intervals are equal, zero is an origin rather than the first object in a count, and a point denotes a number while the segment from zero denotes its magnitude. IES gives strong-evidence recommendations for number-line use in intervention and moderate-evidence support for number lines as a central fraction representation [5][6].

### Fractions, ratios, and proportional reasoning

Fractions require learners to broaden the number system while revising whole-number expectations. The same rational number can be represented as a common fraction, decimal, percentage, point on a line, part-whole relation, quotient, operator, or ratio. Equivalent forms such as 1/2, 0.5, and 50 percent preserve magnitude while changing notation. Instruction that emphasizes only shaded shapes or procedures can leave learners unable to compare, estimate, or apply fractions in new settings [6][11].

Magnitude is a unifying idea across whole numbers and fractions. Siegler, Thompson, and Schneider found that fraction-magnitude knowledge among sixth- and eighth-grade students was closely related to fraction arithmetic and overall mathematics achievement, and accounted for achievement variance beyond fraction-arithmetic proficiency. Their integrated theory treats numerical development as progressively extending the set of numbers understood to have magnitudes, while also recognizing important differences between whole and rational numbers [11]. These findings are correlational within the reported study and do not by themselves prove a single instructional sequence.

Whole-number bias appears when learners assume that rules for whole numbers transfer unchanged to fractions. They may judge 1/8 larger than 1/6 because 8 is larger than 6, believe multiplication must increase a value, or apply addition rules to products. Counterexamples, benchmark fractions, number-line placement, estimation before computation, and explanation of units can expose these conflicts. The IES fractions guide recommends connecting procedures to fraction meanings rather than presenting algorithms as arbitrary changes to numerator and denominator [6].

Proportional reasoning concerns multiplicative comparison, not merely executing cross-multiplication. Boyer, Levine, and Huttenlocher found that children's success on proportional-equivalence tasks depended strongly on representation. Continuous displays often supported better performance, while discrete displays could invite misleading absolute-number or part-whole matches; age improved performance but did not eliminate representation effects [12]. Instruction should therefore build ratio, rate, and scale through interpretable quantities and diagrams before compressing the relation into a formal algorithm [6][12].

### Estimation, language, assessment, and affect

Estimation is a way to represent uncertainty and reasonableness, not careless calculation. Learners estimate set size, locate numbers on bounded lines, round quantities, approximate operations, and choose useful benchmarks. An estimate should be judged against its purpose: selecting an order of magnitude may require little precision, while checking a decimal computation may require tight bounds. Estimation before computation can reveal an implausible algorithmic answer that exact symbol manipulation alone fails to detect [1][11].

Mathematical language supports distinctions that ordinary language may blur. Terms such as factor, multiple, numerator, denominator, difference, equivalent, per, and unit rate identify relations, but vocabulary instruction must connect words to examples, nonexamples, diagrams, gestures, and equations. The 2021 IES intervention guide assigns strong evidence to teaching clear mathematical language and supporting learners' use of it to communicate conceptual understanding [5]. Language should clarify thought rather than become a second list of definitions to memorize.

Formative assessment is a decision process: elicit evidence of current thinking, interpret the strategy or misconception, and choose the next instructional move. A correct answer can conceal counting, guessing, or an inflexible procedure; an incorrect answer can reveal a coherent but overgeneralized rule. Useful probes therefore ask learners to represent a quantity, compare methods, estimate first, explain why an answer is reasonable, or solve a structurally similar problem in a changed form [4][5]. Screening identifies risk, but diagnosis requires examining the numerical ideas and representations behind performance.

Mathematics anxiety is reliably associated with achievement, but the direction is not settled by correlation. Barroso and colleagues synthesized 747 effect sizes and found an average correlation of r = -0.28 between anxiety and achievement, with substantial variation across measures and groups; the approximate-number-system subgroup was the exception to the otherwise negative pattern. Weak performance may contribute to anxiety, anxiety may consume attention during demanding work, and both may reflect prior experiences or other factors [15]. Instruction should therefore combine substantive mathematical support with psychologically safe participation rather than treating reassurance as a substitute for teaching.

## Evidence

The strongest conclusion comes from convergence rather than one decisive experiment. National Academies consensus reports integrate research across early quantity, whole numbers, rational numbers, operations, instruction, and proficiency. They independently support developmental progressions and the interdependence of conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, strategy, reasoning, and disposition. Their authority lies in expert synthesis, not in the causal certainty of a single randomized trial, and their recommendations should be updated when stronger evidence changes a component claim [1][2].

The IES practice guides translate reviewed research into bounded recommendations and state the evidence level for each. The early-childhood guide gives moderate evidence to teaching number and operations through a developmental progression, while its recommendations on progress monitoring, mathematical description, and daily integration have lower evidence ratings. The 2021 elementary-intervention guide rates systematic instruction, mathematical language, representations, number lines, word problems, and timed fluency activities as strong-evidence recommendations. The fractions guide gives moderate evidence to treating fractions as numbers on a number line and to explaining computation procedures, while some sharing, proportionality, and professional-development recommendations rest on more limited evidence [4][5][6].

Rittle-Johnson, Siegler, and Alibali provide a direct test of the relation between concepts and procedures. In two studies with fifth- and sixth-grade learners working on decimal-fraction number-line tasks, initial conceptual knowledge predicted procedural improvement, and procedural improvement predicted later conceptual growth. In the experimental study, prompts to attend to the tenths digit and number lines partitioned into tenths improved correct representation and procedural learning. The evidence supports an iterative relation and a role for representation, but the outcomes were proximal to the taught domain and do not prove that every concept and procedure develop in the same order [10].

Schneider and colleagues tested how magnitude processing relates to broader mathematics. Their meta-analysis pooled 284 effects from 45 articles and 17,201 participants. The average relation was positive, and symbolic magnitude comparison correlated more strongly with mathematical competence than nonsymbolic comparison. Associations were stronger for outcomes that depended heavily on magnitude, such as early mathematics and mental arithmetic. The studies were primarily correlational, task reliability was not always reported, and the result does not show that isolated magnitude-comparison training causes transfer to school mathematics [8].

Carbonneau, Marley, and Selig evaluated the widespread recommendation to use concrete manipulatives. Across 55 comparative studies with 7,237 learners, manipulative-based instruction showed small-to-moderate average advantages over abstract-symbol-only instruction. The benefit differed by instructional and methodological conditions; retention effects were larger than effects on problem solving, transfer, and justification. This pattern supports purposeful use of manipulatives while rejecting the inference that physical materials automatically produce abstract understanding [13].

Research on place value shows why representation and unit structure matter. Fuson and colleagues synthesized four problem-solving-oriented classroom projects and identified a sequence of conceptions for multidigit numbers, including undifferentiated ones, tens as a counted sequence, and more integrated tens-and-ones structures. Students used those structures to construct and interpret several addition and subtraction methods. Because the paper compares research projects rather than randomly assigning one universal sequence, it establishes a useful descriptive framework more directly than a single best pedagogy [9].

Fractions and proportional reasoning provide tests of transfer beyond whole-number routines. Siegler, Thompson, and Schneider reported strong relations among fraction-magnitude knowledge, fraction arithmetic, and general achievement in sixth and eighth grades, with magnitude contributing information beyond arithmetic performance. Boyer, Levine, and Huttenlocher found in two studies that proportional judgments varied with whether quantities were represented continuously or discretely and with whether misleading whole-number matches were available. Together, the studies show that learners' errors are sensitive to representation and prior number knowledge, not merely to effort [11][12].

Anxiety evidence requires similar restraint. Barroso and colleagues included 747 correlations from research conducted between 1992 and 2018 and found a robust negative average association between mathematics anxiety and achievement. Heterogeneity was high, samples were disproportionately Western and included many college students, and most effects were observational. The study therefore establishes a recurrent association across much of the literature, while leaving reciprocal causation, confounding, and the effects of specific interventions unresolved [15].

The intervention literature is most useful when its package nature is kept visible. The IES elementary guide summarizes small-group interventions that combine sequenced content, explicit modeling, representations, guided practice, feedback, language, review, and progress monitoring. Its evidence tables caution that multi-component studies generally cannot identify which single component caused an effect. Targeted instruction is therefore evidence-based as a coordinated design, but claims that one worksheet, manipulative, timing rule, or representation is the active ingredient often exceed the design [5].

The author's synthesis is that five propositions have broad support. First, symbolic magnitude and exact number relations must be taught, not assumed to emerge from approximate quantity alone. Second, conceptual and procedural knowledge are distinguishable but mutually productive. Third, representations help when their correspondences are made explicit. Fourth, whole-number knowledge must be reorganized, not merely extended, for fractions and proportion. Fifth, assessment and intervention should locate the broken relation rather than label a learner by a total score [1][5][8][10][13].

Important uncertainties remain. Developmental sequences describe common pathways without imposing fixed stages; correlations between numerical processes and achievement do not establish direction; representation effects depend on task and prior knowledge; and intervention packages limit component attribution. Evidence would materially change the framework if replicated randomized studies showed durable far transfer from isolated nonsymbolic training, a universal advantage for one concept-procedure order, or stable benefits from concrete materials without explicit mapping. Current evidence does not justify those stronger claims [2][5][8][10][13][14].

## Implications

For curriculum designers, coherence matters more than the number of topics touched. A progression should revisit cardinality, magnitude, units, place value, operation meanings, fraction magnitude, and proportional comparison at increasing ranges and complexity. Each new notation should connect to known quantities and relations, while later content should deliberately expose where earlier rules no longer apply. Such sequencing treats prerequisites as instructional dependencies to diagnose and support, not as reasons to deny learners access to meaningful grade-level ideas [2][3][6].

For teachers, a useful lesson architecture begins with an elicitation task, establishes a quantitative meaning, links two or more representations, models a strategy with its rationale, provides guided variation, and then asks learners to choose, explain, estimate, and apply. Practice should include enough repetition for accuracy and efficiency, but vary surface features and problem structures so that learners must recognize relations rather than copy steps. Retrieval and timed activity can contribute to fluency, yet neither should become the definition of proficiency or the only evidence used for instructional decisions [1][5][10].

Examples should be chosen for contrast. Comparing 38 + 27 with 38 + 20 + 7 highlights decomposition; comparing 3/5 with 3/8 exposes the effect of equal numerators; comparing 2/3 of 12 with 12 divided by 3 and multiplied by 2 connects operator and quotient meanings. Nonexamples should be selected to reveal predictable overgeneralizations, such as transferring whole-number ordering to denominators or assuming multiplication always increases a quantity. The author's synthesis is that carefully contrasted examples turn an error into evidence about the learner's rule [6][9][11].

Representations should be treated as a coordinated system. Physical objects can establish actions and units, drawings can preserve relevant structure while removing irrelevant detail, number lines can express order and distance, and symbols can compress the relation for efficient reasoning. Teachers should ask learners to translate in both directions and identify what stays invariant. If a learner can execute with blocks but cannot interpret an equation, the instructional task is not more object handling alone; it is explicit mapping between the forms [5][13][14].

Formative assessment should separate at least five possibilities: the learner does not know the quantity-symbol mapping; misunderstands the unit or operation; knows a concept but lacks a reliable procedure; executes a procedure but cannot judge reasonableness; or knows both but is disrupted by language, attention, or anxiety in the task. Short interviews, number-line placements, estimation prompts, method comparisons, and requests for representation can distinguish these cases more effectively than a total accuracy score. The next lesson should respond to the diagnosed relation, then reassess it with a changed example [4][5].

For learners with persistent difficulty, intervention should be more explicit and systematic, not merely slower or easier. The IES guide supports sequenced modeling, clear mathematical language, carefully chosen concrete and semi-concrete representations, number lines, deliberate word-problem instruction, cumulative review, feedback, and regular fluency activity. Progress monitoring should determine whether the learner is acquiring the intended relation and whether intensity or representation must change. A support that increases answer production without improving meaning, retention, or transfer has not solved the full learning problem [5].

Fluency should be measured through accuracy, efficiency, flexibility, and appropriate selection. Fast recall of basic facts can free resources for multidigit work and problem solving, but speed under pressure can also hide strategy, discourage checking, and interact with anxiety. Timed activity is one possible practice format within the IES guide, not a universal assessment of number sense. Educators should pair it with untimed explanation, estimation, strategy choice, and application so that automaticity remains connected to reasoning [1][5][15].

Mathematics anxiety should be addressed without lowering intellectual demand. Predictable routines, adequate think time, opportunities to revise, private formative checks, and feedback focused on strategies can reduce avoidable threat while preserving the mathematical goal. Because achievement and anxiety are associated in both directions in plausible accounts, emotional support should be paired with instruction that repairs missing number relations and builds successful, interpretable practice. The evidence does not support replacing mathematics teaching with confidence messages alone [5][15].

Families and caregivers can support numeracy by making quantities and relations visible in ordinary activity: compare prices and package sizes, estimate travel time, partition food fairly, measure ingredients, interpret percentages, or discuss whether an answer is reasonable. The instructional value comes from conversation about units, representations, and strategies rather than from turning every activity into a quiz. Early number talk can connect informal knowledge to formal symbols, while adults should avoid presenting speed or effortless performance as proof of mathematical ability [2][4][15].

For educational technology, adaptation should target a model of knowledge rather than a streak of correct answers. A useful system distinguishes counting from cardinality, fact retrieval from operation meaning, whole-number comparison from fraction magnitude, and procedural accuracy from transfer. It should select tasks that test a hypothesized misconception, expose representations and units, and provide explanations or prompts matched to the learner's response. Automating item difficulty without diagnosing the underlying relation risks making practice more efficient while preserving the misconception [4][5].

For researchers and system leaders, outcome choice determines what an intervention appears to accomplish. A program can raise performance on practiced items without improving delayed retention, transfer, estimation, explanation, or confidence. Evaluations should therefore include proximal and distal measures, describe implementation, report who benefits, and avoid attributing a multi-component effect to one favored feature. Screening and accountability scores can identify patterns at scale, but they are insufficient descriptions of how numerical knowledge develops [5][13].

The practical standard is not whether a learner can reproduce yesterday's procedure. It is whether the learner can identify the quantity and unit, choose a fitting representation or operation, execute accurately, explain the relation, estimate a reasonable range, detect an implausible answer, and adapt the knowledge to a changed problem. The author's synthesis is that this standard unifies conceptual meaning and fluency without turning either into an ideological substitute for the other [1][6][10].

Numeracy instruction succeeds when procedures become compressed expressions of understood quantitative relations and when those relations remain available for checking, adaptation, and transfer. That outcome requires developmental sequencing, explicit mapping among representations, purposeful practice, diagnostic assessment, and targeted support. It is educational development of usable number knowledge, not formal mathematical theory and not coaching learners to imitate the surface form of a test [1][2][5].
