# Durable Engagement Grows When Learners Expect Progress, Value the Work, Belong, and Retain Agency

Motivation initiates and directs learning, while engagement is the behavioral, emotional, cognitive, and agentic activity through which motivation becomes observable. Durable engagement is therefore not produced by rewards, entertainment, pressure, or learner choice alone; it is more likely when learners can see a credible path to competence, find value in the work, experience belonging, pursue meaningful goals, and act with support rather than coercion [1][2][3][4].

## Background

Motivation and engagement are related but not interchangeable. Motivation concerns why a learner begins, continues, redirects, or stops an activity. Engagement concerns what the learner does, feels, and thinks while participating. Fredricks, Blumenfeld, and Paris organized school engagement into behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions: participation and persistence; interest, belonging, or affect; and psychological investment or strategic thought [1]. Later work within self-determination theory added agentic engagement, in which students contribute questions, preferences, and suggestions that shape instruction rather than only responding to it [6]. A learner may therefore comply behaviorally while remaining emotionally detached and cognitively superficial, or may think deeply without speaking frequently. Attendance, visible activity, enjoyment, and learning cannot be treated as equivalent measures [1].

Early educational practice often treated motivation as a quantity to be raised through reward and punishment. The later research traditions used in this topic distinguish the quality, object, and context of motivation. Self-determination theory separates activity pursued for inherent interest or personally endorsed value from activity controlled by reward, punishment, guilt, or social pressure [3][4]. Expectancy-value theory asks whether learners expect success and whether the task has intrinsic, attainment, or utility value after its perceived costs are considered [2]. Social-cognitive theory emphasizes self-efficacy, a task- and domain-specific belief about capability that is shaped especially by mastery experiences [15]. Achievement-goal theory distinguishes goals organized around developing competence from goals organized around demonstrating or avoiding the appearance of incompetence [16]. These theories overlap, but they do not name the same mechanism.

This plurality corrects the idea that a person simply is or is not motivated. Motivation can differ by subject, task, time, social setting, and intended outcome. A student may value biology for a future career but dislike one laboratory task, believe improvement is possible in writing but not mathematics, participate in a discussion to avoid embarrassment, or study an uninteresting prerequisite because it has been integrated into a personally meaningful goal [2][4]. Howard and colleagues' meta-analysis found different associations for intrinsic motivation, personally valued regulation, ego-involved regulation, externally controlled regulation, and amotivation; compressing these forms into one score would conceal consequential differences [4].

The same distinction applies to engagement. Behavioral engagement includes attendance, attention, submission, effort, and participation, but these indicators do not establish cognitive investment. Emotional engagement includes interest, enjoyment, anxiety, boredom, and identification with a class or institution. Cognitive engagement includes strategic learning, self-regulation, elaboration, and willingness to expend mental effort. Agentic engagement includes constructive contributions to the learning environment [1][6]. These dimensions can reinforce one another, but they can also diverge. Quiet concentration can represent deep cognitive engagement, while high activity can represent procedural compliance. The authors of the foundational engagement review warned that overlapping definitions and self-report measures make source and meaning difficult to identify [1].

The historical movement from reward control toward contextual and cognitive explanations did not prove that external incentives are always harmful. Cerasoli, Nicklin, and Ford's 40-year meta-analysis found that intrinsic motivation and incentives can jointly predict performance, with intrinsic motivation contributing more to performance quality and incentives contributing more to performance quantity [14]. The relationship depended on how directly the incentive was tied to performance. This evidence replaces the simple claim that rewards either always work or always undermine learning with a conditional question: what behavior is rewarded, what quality is needed, and what happens when the reward disappears?

Educational motivation research also moved from describing individual differences toward changing learning environments. Lazowski and Hulleman identified 74 published and unpublished papers that experimentally manipulated theory-based motivational conditions in authentic educational settings. Across 92 independent effects and 38,377 participants, the average effect was positive, although randomized designs produced smaller effects than quasi-experimental designs [5]. This literature establishes that motivation is malleable, but not that one intervention works for every learner, subject, age, or institution.

Belonging became central because learners interpret difficulty socially as well as cognitively. A setback can be read as normal evidence that learning is hard, or as evidence that people like the learner do not belong. Fredricks and colleagues reviewed associations between belonging, teacher relationships, and engagement, while later randomized studies tested brief interventions designed to normalize belonging concerns and make improvement seem possible [1][10][11]. Those trials show both promise and boundary conditions: an adaptive interpretation can help where an institution affords genuine belonging, but a message cannot compensate for a context that persistently excludes or mistreats learners [11].

The domain boundary is applied education. General mechanisms of reward, identity, emotion, and social cognition also belong to psychology-behavior; personal habit systems belong to self-improvement; and education platforms belong to technology. Here the organizing question is how teachers, curricula, assessments, peers, and learning systems influence attention, persistence, strategy, and knowledge acquisition. The author's synthesis is that motivation should be treated neither as a fixed learner trait nor as a technique for extracting effort. It is a changing relation among learner, task, goal, social context, evidence of progress, and available agency [1][2][3][4].

## Core Concepts

### Motivation Has Direction and Quality, Not Only Intensity

A learner can work hard for incompatible reasons. Intrinsic motivation concerns interest or enjoyment in the activity itself. Identified regulation concerns an activity accepted as useful or personally important even when it is not enjoyable. Introjected regulation concerns internal pressure such as guilt, pride, or fear of disappointing others. External regulation concerns reward or punishment. Amotivation concerns the absence of a credible reason or perceived link between action and outcome [4]. These forms can coexist; a learner may enjoy a topic, value a credential, and fear a grade at the same time.

The distinction matters because immediate effort does not identify what will sustain future activity. Howard and colleagues synthesized 344 samples with 223,209 participants. Intrinsic motivation was associated with success and well-being, identified regulation was especially associated with persistence, introjected regulation was associated with some persistence and performance goals but also ill-being, external regulation was not associated with performance or persistence and was associated with lower well-being, and amotivation was associated with poor outcomes [4]. These are associations rather than guarantees for an individual, but they show why a single total-motivation score is inadequate.

### Engagement Is a Profile, Not a Participation Count

Behavioral, emotional, cognitive, and agentic engagement answer different questions. Did the learner attend and act? Did the learner feel interest, safety, or connection? Did the learner invest thought and use learning strategies? Did the learner help shape the conditions of learning? [1][6] The dimensions should be measured separately before they are combined. A discussion count, for example, can measure speaking frequency but not whether a contribution contained reasoning, whether a silent learner was mentally engaged, or whether participation was safe and accessible.

Procedural engagement is especially easy to mistake for learning. A learner can complete visible requirements with the least cognitive investment needed for compliance. Substantive engagement requires attention to meaning, explanation, strategy, and transfer [1]. The author's assessment is that systems should treat observable behavior as a signal that prompts further inquiry, not as a transparent reading of motivation. Work products, explanations, delayed performance, learner reports, and contextual observations answer different questions.

### Expectancy, Value, and Cost Govern Commitment

Expectancy-value theory predicts that learners are more likely to choose and persist in work when they believe they can succeed and value the task [2]. Value is not one thing. Intrinsic value is enjoyment or interest. Attainment value concerns the importance of doing well for identity. Utility value concerns usefulness for present or future goals. Cost includes effort, time, lost alternatives, anxiety, and possible identity threat. A high-value task can still be rejected if expected success is near zero or cost is intolerable.

This model explains why relevance messages alone can fail. Telling a struggling learner that mathematics is important may increase pressure without increasing expectancy. Conversely, making a task easy enough to guarantee success can remove challenge and value. Hulleman and colleagues used randomized writing activities that asked learners to generate connections between course content and their own lives. The intervention increased perceived utility and interest, especially among learners with lower expected or prior performance [8]. The active ingredient was not a teacher announcing relevance but a learner constructing a credible connection.

### Self-Efficacy Must Be Built From Interpretable Success

Self-efficacy is confidence about performing a defined action, not a global statement of worth. Byars-Winston and colleagues synthesized 61 academic studies with 8,965 participants using four proposed sources: mastery performance, vicarious learning, social persuasion, and affective arousal. Prior performance accomplishments had the strongest unique association with self-efficacy, and relationships varied by subject and learner characteristics [15]. This finding supports designing attainable but meaningful progress rather than relying on generic encouragement.

Evidence of progress has to be interpretable. Success on a task that was completed by a peer, heavily prompted, or unrelated to the target may not strengthen confidence for independent performance. Failure on a task with missing prerequisites may be misread as lack of ability. The author's synthesis is that educators should make the learning target explicit, adjust challenge so effort can produce information, and show which strategy or knowledge change produced improvement. This joins self-efficacy to assessment and feedback rather than treating confidence as a speech delivered before work begins [12][15].

### Autonomy and Structure Are Complements

Autonomy is volitional participation, not the absence of instruction. Autonomy-supportive teaching takes the learner's perspective, offers meaningful rationales and bounded choices, acknowledges difficulty, invites contribution, and minimizes controlling language [6]. Structure clarifies goals, expectations, strategies, boundaries, and feedback. In observations and surveys across 133 high-school classrooms and 1,584 students, Jang, Reeve, and Deci found that autonomy support and structure were positively related and both predicted observed behavioral engagement; autonomy support uniquely predicted self-reported engagement [7]. Because the design was correlational, it does not by itself establish causation.

Reeve and Cheon's review of 51 autonomy-supportive teaching interventions, including 38 randomized trials, concluded that teachers can learn this style and that it produces student, teacher, and classroom benefits [6]. Autonomy support therefore does not mean offering unlimited menus or asking novices to discover everything alone. Choice without knowledge can overwhelm; structure without agency can produce dependence or resistance. The author's assessment is that a sound design gives learners influence over routes, examples, questions, pacing within limits, or forms of contribution while preserving a clear and demanding learning goal.

### Belonging Changes the Meaning of Difficulty

Belonging is the experience of being accepted, valued, included, and able to participate in a learning community [1]. It affects engagement partly by changing how ambiguous events are interpreted. Critical feedback, a low grade, an unanswered question, or social awkwardness may be treated as temporary and actionable when belonging feels secure. The same event may be interpreted as proof of exclusion when belonging is uncertain. This process is especially consequential during transitions into a new school, course, discipline, or institution [10][11].

Belonging is not entertainment, friendliness, or the demand that learners suppress disagreement. It requires credible access to participation, respect, relationships, and institutional support. Walton and colleagues' multisite randomized trial found that a brief intervention improved first-year full-time completion where contexts afforded belonging, but not where those affordances were inadequate [11]. The implication is structural as well as psychological: educators should not use a belonging message to excuse exclusionary practice.

### Goals Organize What Counts as Success

Achievement goals define the standard against which competence is judged. Mastery-approach goals emphasize learning, improvement, or task mastery. Performance-approach goals emphasize demonstrating competence, often relative to others. Avoidance forms emphasize preventing failure, decline, or the appearance of incompetence [16]. Goal labels are not sufficient because framing and standards change their effects.

Noordzij, Giel, and van Mierlo synthesized 90 experiments with 11,247 participants. Induced mastery-approach goals improved performance relative to performance goals and no-goal conditions, but did not improve motivation on average. Effects depended on whether standards were task-referenced or socially comparative and on how the goal was framed [16]. The finding discourages a slogan such as "always use mastery goals." A useful learning goal identifies a meaningful capability, supplies criteria, supports revision, and does not convert every error into a public judgment of ability.

### Feedback Is Information for a Next Action

Feedback can influence competence, expectancy, strategy, and emotion, but its effect depends on information content. Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie synthesized 435 studies, 994 effects, and more than 61,000 learners. The average effect on learning was d = 0.48, with substantial heterogeneity; effects were larger for cognitive and motor outcomes than for motivational and behavioral outcomes [12]. High-information feedback that identifies a discrepancy and supports correction differs from reward, punishment, or praise directed at the person.

Feedback should answer what the target is, where current work differs, and what action is likely to reduce the gap. It should arrive while action remains possible and preserve learner responsibility for using it. Koenka and colleagues found that grades improved achievement relative to no performance feedback but reduced motivation, while comments outperformed grades on both achievement and motivation in the included comparisons [13]. The result does not mean grades can be abolished in every institution. It means a grade alone is a weak instructional message and can redirect attention from learning to evaluation.

### Rewards Can Buy Output Without Building Commitment

External incentives can increase behavior, particularly measured quantity, but they can also redirect attention toward the reward. Cerasoli and colleagues found that intrinsic motivation remained a predictor of performance whether incentives were present and contributed more to quality, while incentives contributed more to quantity [14]. Wisniewski and colleagues found that motivational feedback effects were smaller than learning effects and that most negative motivational effects in their data involved uninformative rewards or punishments [12].

The practical distinction is between short-term compliance and durable engagement. A token may increase completed items during a session without increasing value, confidence, strategy, or future voluntary participation. A reward can still be useful when the immediate goal is bounded output and the incentive does not displace the reason for learning. The author's synthesis is to specify the time horizon and target before using an incentive, monitor behavior after its removal, and avoid rewarding proxies that learners can maximize without learning.

### Challenge Must Be Coupled With Support

Engagement is unlikely when work is trivial, impossibly difficult, or disconnected from usable knowledge. Expectancy, efficacy, and competence depend on a task being hard enough to reveal progress but structured enough for effort and strategy to matter [2][3][15]. Support can include prerequisites, examples, worked guidance, peer explanation, feedback, accessible materials, or smaller intermediate goals. Lowering every challenge may protect immediate success while preventing mastery; increasing difficulty without support may convert struggle into evidence of inability.

The author's assessment is that task design should manage a three-part relation: a valued purpose, a reachable next step, and evidence that distinguishes productive struggle from a missing prerequisite or ineffective strategy. This relation can be adapted across age groups and subjects without assuming that the same topic, reward, or degree of choice motivates everyone.

### Motivation and Engagement Form a Reciprocal System

Motivation influences engagement, but engagement also changes motivation. Acting on a task can create mastery evidence, interest, social connection, and value. Avoidance can preserve uncertainty and prevent corrective experiences. Teachers also respond to learner behavior; agentic questions can elicit more responsive instruction, while disengagement can be misread as defiance and elicit tighter control [6]. This reciprocity makes one-time labels dangerous.

The author's synthesis is a diagnostic loop: define the desired learning, observe engagement by dimension, ask which motivational mechanism is constrained, change one relevant condition, and inspect both immediate participation and later independent learning. A learner who does not begin may face low expectancy, low value, excessive cost, unclear instructions, or threat. A learner who begins but does not persist may lack feedback, belonging, strategy, or a credible path through difficulty. A learner who completes work superficially may be optimizing a grade, attendance rule, or entertainment feature rather than the knowledge goal.

## Evidence

### Theory-Based Interventions Produce Moderate Average Effects With Variation

Lazowski and Hulleman's meta-analysis included 74 papers, 92 independent effects, and 38,377 participants in ecologically valid educational settings. The average effect of motivation interventions on authentic educational outcomes was d = 0.49, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.43 to 0.56. Randomized studies had smaller effects than quasi-experimental studies, and other moderator differences were largely descriptive rather than statistically established [5]. The study supports malleability but warns against treating the average as the expected effect of any particular program.

The evidence base spans different mechanisms. Some interventions change value, some support autonomy, some alter goals or interpretations of difficulty, and some improve competence information. Pooling them answers whether theory-based motivational changes can matter on average; it does not identify one universal technique. The author's assessment is that implementation should name the mechanism and measure it rather than applying the general label "motivation program."

### Motivation Quality Predicts Different Outcomes

Howard and colleagues' self-determination meta-analysis covered 344 samples and 223,209 participants across 26 outcomes. Intrinsic motivation had strong links with success and well-being, identified regulation was particularly linked with persistence, and external regulation was not linked with performance or persistence in the aggregate [4]. Bureau and colleagues separately synthesized pathways to autonomous and controlled motivation across 144 studies and more than 79,000 students. Competence was the strongest positive predictor of self-determined motivation, followed by autonomy and relatedness, and teacher autonomy support was more strongly related to student need satisfaction and motivation than parental autonomy support [3].

Bureau and colleagues explicitly note that their synthesis is correlational, so it cannot establish that increasing one measured need will cause a specific outcome [3]. The results can also reflect reciprocal effects: engaged students may elicit support, and successful students may feel more competent. Intervention evidence is therefore needed alongside correlations.

### Autonomy-Supportive Teaching Is Teachable, but Choice Alone Is Not the Treatment

Reeve and Cheon reviewed 51 interventions, 38 of them randomized, designed to help teachers become more autonomy supportive. The review reports that teaching style changed and that greater autonomy support produced a range of student and teacher benefits [6]. The practices form a coherent interpersonal style rather than a single choice prompt: taking the learner's perspective, acknowledging negative affect, providing rationales, inviting initiative, using informational language, and allowing time for self-directed action.

Jang and colleagues' 133-classroom study complements the intervention evidence by showing that autonomy support and structure were positively associated rather than opposing poles [7]. The combination matters because learner agency cannot operate on an invisible goal, while clear instruction need not be controlling. The evidence does not support either total teacher withdrawal or rigid direction as a general rule.

### Value Can Be Changed Through Learner-Generated Relevance

Hulleman and colleagues conducted two randomized experiments, one in a laboratory and one in a college class, in which learners wrote about how mathematics or psychology material related to their lives. The activities increased utility-value perceptions and interest, especially among learners with lower expected or prior performance, and perceived utility mediated intervention effects on interest [8]. This design tests a narrow causal claim about self-generated relevance rather than the broader claim that any real-world example motivates every learner.

Asher and colleagues later tested three utility-value writing assignments in a randomized gateway-chemistry trial with 2,505 students. At 2.5 years, 74 percent of intervention students and 70 percent of control students remained STEM majors. For students from marginalized and underrepresented racial or ethnic groups, the corresponding rates were 69 percent and 55 percent. Engagement with course assignments and plans to earn a STEM degree partially mediated persistence effects [9]. The subgroup sizes were smaller than the overall sample, so precision and replication remain important, but the longitudinal administrative outcome strengthens the evidence beyond self-reported interest.

### Belonging Interventions Work Through Context, Not Words Alone

Murphy and colleagues randomized 1,063 first-year students at a broad-access university to a customized belonging exercise or control. Among students defined in advance as socially disadvantaged, continuous enrollment through the second year was 86 percent in the intervention condition and 76 percent in control; through the third year it was 73 percent and 64 percent. Increased social and academic fit mediated the longer-term effect [10]. The study provides causal evidence within one institution and specified populations.

Walton and colleagues extended the question to 26,911 students across 22 institutions. The brief precollege intervention increased first-year full-time completion especially for groups with historically lower completion, but effects varied across institutions. It did not improve persistence where institutional conditions provided inadequate affordances for belonging [11]. This heterogeneity is not a nuisance to average away. It shows that psychological interpretation and material context interact.

### Feedback Effects Depend on Content and Outcome

Wisniewski and colleagues integrated 435 feedback studies with 994 effects and more than 61,000 learners. The mean effect was d = 0.48 after removing extreme values, but effects varied substantially. Information content moderated results, and feedback effects were larger for cognitive and motor outcomes than for motivation and behavior [12]. Twenty-one percent of motivational effects were negative; most of those negative cases involved uninformative reward or punishment [12].

Koenka and colleagues directly compared grades, comments, and no performance feedback in four meta-analyses of elementary and secondary studies. Grades improved achievement but reduced motivation relative to no feedback. Compared with comments, grades produced poorer achievement and less adaptive motivation, with variation by construct, subject, comment type, learner characteristics, and method [13]. These results support actionable comments while ruling out the assumption that any response from a teacher is motivational.

### Goals, Incentives, and Efficacy Have Boundary Conditions

Noordzij and colleagues synthesized 90 studies, 235 effects, and 11,247 participants on experimentally induced achievement goals. Mastery-approach goals improved performance relative to performance and no-goal conditions but did not raise motivation on average. Standards and framing moderated effects [16]. A mastery label is therefore not enough; learners need a task-referenced standard and goal content that directs attention toward learning.

Cerasoli and colleagues synthesized 183 studies and 212,468 participants across school, work, and physical domains. Intrinsic motivation was a medium-to-strong predictor of performance, but its contribution depended on incentive salience and on whether performance quality or quantity was measured [14]. Because the dataset crosses domains, direct translation to one classroom should preserve these moderators rather than cite only the overall relationship.

Byars-Winston and colleagues' model-based meta-analysis of 61 academic studies found that mastery performance had the strongest unique association with self-efficacy, with subject area, grade, sex, and ethnicity moderating parts of the model [15]. The evidence is primarily about associations among proposed efficacy sources, not a guarantee that arranging one success will cause durable confidence. It nevertheless supports using real progress and culturally attentive learning opportunities rather than empty praise.

### Measurement Determines the Claim

Most motivation and engagement constructs are partly internal, so measurement commonly relies on self-report. Self-reports can identify value, affect, efficacy, and perceived support, but they are vulnerable to wording, reference group, memory, and demand effects [1][3]. Observations capture visible behavior but can mistake quiet thought for disengagement or compliance for learning. Administrative measures capture attendance, submission, course taking, or persistence but not why the behavior occurred. Performance assessments show what a learner can do under specified conditions but may not reveal interest or belonging.

The author's synthesis is to use a measurement portfolio. A causal intervention study should include a proposed mechanism, a behavioral indicator, a learning or persistence outcome, and implementation evidence when feasible. Correlations should be labeled as correlations, subgroup effects should preserve denominators, and engagement should not be declared from clicks, attendance, or enjoyment alone. This standard separates evidence that a learner was present, active, invested, improving, and willing to continue.

## Implications

### Diagnose Disengagement Before Prescribing Motivation

The first practical question is not "How do we motivate this learner?" but "What evidence describes the breakdown?" A learner may fail to start, stop after difficulty, attend without thinking, avoid public participation, submit work without revising, or disengage only in one subject. These patterns point toward different hypotheses: low expectancy, weak value, excessive cost, low efficacy, unclear goals, controlling conditions, belonging threat, inaccessible task design, missing prerequisites, or feedback that does not support a next action [1][2][3].

A diagnosis should preserve alternatives. If a student does not submit online work, low motivation is only one explanation; access, caregiving, interface difficulty, language, time, health, or unclear instructions may dominate. If a class is quiet, fear, cultural norms, processing time, or thoughtful note-taking may explain the behavior. The author's assessment is that motivation should be inferred only after the task and context have been inspected. Otherwise a system converts its own design failure into a learner trait.

A reversible diagnostic cycle can use four observations. First, define the exact learning behavior and outcome. Second, ask the learner about expectancy, value, cost, belonging, and available choice. Third, inspect the task, prerequisite, feedback, and social conditions. Fourth, change the condition most directly connected to the hypothesis and look for improvement in both participation and independent learning. The cycle treats disengagement as information rather than misconduct.

### Build Value Without Turning Every Lesson Into Entertainment

Value can come from curiosity, identity, usefulness, contribution to others, or connection to a larger goal [2][8][9]. Teachers can explain a rationale, show authentic uses, invite learners to generate applications, connect examples to diverse goals, and make disciplinary questions visible. Utility-value trials show that brief, learner-generated relevance activities can change interest and longer-term persistence, particularly for learners who begin with lower confidence or belong to marginalized groups [8][9].

Relevance should remain intellectually honest. A contrived promise that every topic has immediate personal use can reduce trust. Some foundational knowledge becomes useful only after a sequence of study. In those cases, the rationale can explain the dependency: what future performance this concept enables and why the current exercise is necessary. The author's synthesis is that entertainment can attract attention, but durable value requires a credible relation between the work and something the learner endorses.

### Pair Agency With Clear Structure

Autonomy support should give learners meaningful influence without abandoning them to unstructured choice. Teachers can offer bounded options in examples, topics, partners, sequence, or response format when those options preserve the learning goal. They can invite questions and objections, acknowledge that a task is difficult, provide a rationale for nonoptional work, and use language that informs rather than threatens [6]. At the same time, they should state success criteria, model unfamiliar processes, supply prerequisites, set limits, and provide timely feedback [7].

For younger learners or novices, agency may begin with small choices and opportunities to explain preferences. For advanced or self-directed learners, it may extend to question selection, project design, source choice, pacing, and evaluation criteria. The amount of autonomy should depend on knowledge and consequences, not on a universal teaching ideology. The worst inversion is to call absence of support freedom; autonomy requires enough competence and information for choice to be meaningful.

### Create Mastery Evidence and Protect Challenge

Confidence grows most credibly when learners can attribute improvement to knowledge, strategy, and effort under interpretable conditions [15]. Instruction should therefore create short cycles in which learners attempt a meaningful task, receive useful information, revise, and later perform with less support. Examples and scaffolds can make a first success possible, but they should fade so the learner can distinguish assisted completion from mastery.

Goals should describe capability rather than activity alone. "Complete ten problems" is a workload target; "choose and justify the correct model for unfamiliar problems" describes learning. Task-referenced mastery goals generally support performance better than goals centered on avoiding failure or appearing competent, but the goal still needs useful framing and standards [16]. The author's synthesis is to preserve challenge while reducing irrelevant obstacles: remove ambiguity, inaccessible presentation, and missing prerequisites before lowering the intellectual goal.

### Make Belonging a Property of the Environment

A belonging-supportive environment communicates that participation is legitimate, difficulty is normal, help is available, and identity does not determine expected capability. Concrete practices include learning names and pronunciations, using examples that do not cast one group as peripheral, structuring peer work so status does not monopolize contribution, responding to error without humiliation, making help-seeking routine, and acting on exclusion. These practices are consistent with the belonging and autonomy evidence, but each local implementation should be evaluated rather than assumed effective [1][6][10][11].

Brief belonging exercises can be useful during transitions, but the multisite evidence shows that context constrains their effects [11]. Institutions should therefore measure whether students actually encounter respectful relationships, accessible support, fair procedures, and routes into academic participation. A message that doubts are temporary becomes deceptive if barriers remain unchanged. The author's assessment is that psychological interventions should amplify real affordances, not substitute for them.

### Use Feedback to Restore Control

Feedback should leave the learner with a feasible decision. A score reports location but often not direction. Actionable feedback identifies what is correct, locates the most consequential discrepancy, connects it to criteria, and proposes or elicits a next step [12][13]. It can ask the learner to compare an answer with a model, classify an error, select a strategy, or revise and retest. The learner should use the information rather than merely receive it.

Praise should be specific to process or evidence when it is used, not a judgment of fixed character. Negative feedback should protect competence by separating the current product from the learner's worth and by identifying an attainable correction. Comments should be prioritized while revision remains possible, because feedback delivered after the learning opportunity has closed becomes justification rather than instruction. The evidence also supports reducing the motivational dominance of grades by pairing them with information and opportunities for use [12][13].

### Treat Rewards as Bounded Tools

Rewards can be appropriate for short, mechanical, or transitional behaviors, especially when the target is quantity and no durable intrinsic interest is expected [14]. They are less suited as the primary engine for inquiry, creativity, long-term persistence, or quality. Before introducing a reward, educators should state the target behavior, time horizon, possible proxy, and withdrawal plan. They should then check whether participation, quality, and willingness persist after the reward ends.

Surveillance and public ranking are especially risky because they can turn a learning environment into a performance display. Attendance points, streaks, badges, and leaderboards may increase visible activity while leaving cognitive engagement unchanged. The author's synthesis is that these tools should never be accepted as proof of learning. If they are used, they require independent evidence of understanding, transfer, and voluntary continuation.

### Measure Participation Without Converting It Into Surveillance

A defensible engagement system measures only what is needed for a stated educational decision. Behavioral data might include attendance, attempt, revision, or help seeking. Cognitive evidence might include explanation quality, strategy choice, error correction, or transfer. Emotional and motivational evidence usually requires voluntary learner report. Belonging evidence should include experience of the environment, not only institutional participation. Learning outcomes require valid assessment [1].

No single indicator should trigger a high-stakes label. A dashboard can flag a missing assignment, but it cannot know whether the cause is confusion, low value, access, illness, caregiving, or deliberate reprioritization. Learners should know what is collected, how it is interpreted, who can see it, and how to correct an error. The author's assessment is that measurement becomes educational when it opens a supportive inquiry and becomes surveillance when it converts ambiguous traces into judgments without context or recourse.

Evaluation should distinguish leading and lagging indicators. Participation and mechanism measures can show whether a change is operating now; delayed learning, course completion, or continued study shows whether it lasted. A program that raises enjoyment but not learning may still have value, but it has not established instructional effectiveness. A program that raises immediate submissions but produces no independent retention has improved compliance, not durable engagement.

### Adapt the Mechanism Across Settings

In early schooling, motivation is strongly mediated by teacher relationships, task accessibility, family context, and repeated experiences of success. In secondary education, identity, peer norms, subject choice, evaluation, and future pathways become increasingly visible. In higher education, belonging, utility, self-regulation, workload, financial conditions, and institutional navigation interact. In self-directed learning, the learner must supply goals, structure, feedback, and social support that formal education might otherwise provide [1][2][10][11]. These are broad patterns rather than deterministic age rules.

Subject differences also matter. Efficacy sources varied between STEM and non-STEM studies in the meta-analysis, and motivation constructs can have different meanings when tasks, standards, and opportunities differ [15]. Utility-value and belonging interventions tested in one course or institution should not be copied as scripts without local evidence. The mechanism can transfer while the examples, barriers, and measures change.

For a self-directed learner, a practical design is to select a valued outcome, define an attainable next capability, arrange a real performance check, choose a supportive community or accountability channel, and preserve the option to revise the plan. If persistence falls, the learner can diagnose expectancy, value, cost, belonging, structure, and feedback rather than attributing the result to deficient willpower. This paragraph is the author's application of the combined frameworks [2][3][4][15].

### Keep Causal Claims Narrow and Ethical

Motivation research contains large correlational literatures, experiments with bounded tasks, field trials in selected institutions, and meta-analyses that aggregate heterogeneous constructs. Each supports a different claim. Associations between autonomy support and engagement do not prove that one causes the other. A successful brief intervention does not prove that all disengagement is psychological. An average effect does not guarantee benefit for each subgroup or setting [3][5][11].

The ethical constraint is to support learners' agency rather than use psychological knowledge to extract compliance. A school may need attendance, deadlines, and assessment, but it should state those constraints honestly, provide rationales and appeal routes, and avoid disguising control as choice. The worst outcome is a system that increases visible participation while weakening knowledge, well-being, trust, or the ability to learn independently.

The author's final assessment is a six-part test for durable engagement. Can the learner identify the goal? Does success appear possible with an intelligible strategy? Is the work valuable enough relative to its costs? Does the environment afford belonging and help? Can the learner exercise meaningful agency within clear structure? Does feedback create a next action and eventually independent performance? Evidence across motivation theories and interventions supports these questions as a diagnostic framework, while the heterogeneity of findings requires local verification rather than a universal formula [3][5][6][9][11][12].
