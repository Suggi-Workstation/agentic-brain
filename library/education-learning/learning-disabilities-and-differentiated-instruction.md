# Differentiated Instruction Works When It Removes Learning Barriers Without Lowering the Goal

Learning disabilities can make particular academic processes persistently difficult without defining a learner's overall capacity or justifying a less ambitious curriculum. Effective differentiation therefore preserves the intended learning goal, identifies the barrier between the learner and that goal, applies an evidence-based support, and uses progress data to decide whether the support should continue, change, or intensify ([1] [2] [3] [12]).

## Background

Learning disability is an educational category concerned with unexpectedly persistent difficulty in acquiring or using particular academic skills. In United States federal special-education regulation, specific learning disability includes disorders affecting processes involved in understanding or using spoken or written language and may appear as difficulty listening, thinking, speaking, reading, writing, spelling, or calculating; the definition excludes problems primarily attributable to sensory or motor disability, intellectual disability, emotional disturbance, or environmental, cultural, or economic disadvantage ([1]). The legal definition is jurisdiction-specific, but its educational logic is broadly useful: a low score is not itself an explanation, and instruction must distinguish a skill-specific barrier from inadequate opportunity, language difference, sensory access, and other possible causes.

Historically, schools often identified learning disability through a discrepancy between an intelligence score and academic achievement. That practice created a wait-for-failure problem because a large measurable gap could take years to appear, and it could deny support to poor readers whose achievement and intelligence scores were both low. Research summarized by the U.S. National Institute of Child Health and Human Development found no basis for separating poor readers by IQ-discrepancy status in the studied brain-activation patterns and argued that reading difficulty, not a high IQ discrepancy, should govern access to reading support ([16]). Current U.S. regulation does not require a severe discrepancy and permits evidence based on a learner's response to scientific, research-based intervention, while also requiring teams to consider whether underachievement reflects inadequate instruction or limited English proficiency ([2]).

This shift moved educational attention from labeling alone toward observable performance, instructional opportunity, intervention response, and growth. Response-to-intervention models screen learners, monitor those at risk, and provide increasingly intensive support when progress is insufficient; their primary purpose is prevention and remediation, while their data may also inform evaluation decisions ([17]). The distinction matters because an intervention is not a clinical diagnosis and a weak response to one program is not proof of an intrinsic disorder. Instructional response is interpretable only when the target skill, measure, dosage, implementation fidelity, and comparison with an expected trajectory are known ([12] [13] [17]).

Differentiated instruction developed as a broader attempt to respond to learner variation inside ordinary teaching. It can involve changes in grouping, time, examples, scaffolding, representation, practice, feedback, or task complexity. However, the term has often been used so broadly that unlike practices receive the same label. Reviews of primary and secondary education therefore warn that differentiation is not one treatment with one stable effect: the operational practice, instructional program, subject, age group, implementation quality, and outcome measure all matter ([5] [6]). Grouping learners is merely an arrangement; it becomes instructionally meaningful only when the group receives content and teaching matched to a diagnosed need.

The most important boundary is between an accommodation and a modification. An accommodation changes how a learner accesses instruction or demonstrates learning without reducing the intended expectation. A modification changes what the learner is expected to learn or lowers the performance expectation. The National Center on Educational Outcomes gives this distinction explicit importance because an assessment adaptation can either remove an irrelevant access barrier or alter the construct the assessment was meant to measure ([4]). A text-to-speech tool, for example, may be an appropriate accommodation when a history assessment is intended to measure historical reasoning, but it would alter the target if independent decoding is what the assessment is designed to measure. The same device can therefore be valid in one task and invalid in another.

U.S. individualized education program requirements illustrate how goals and supports are supposed to connect. An IEP records present academic and functional performance, explains how disability affects progress in the general curriculum, states measurable annual goals, identifies services and supports, and specifies how progress will be measured ([3]). This is not a complete instructional theory, and legal requirements differ across jurisdictions. It nevertheless establishes a disciplined sequence: describe current performance, set an intended outcome, provide support related to the barrier, and evaluate progress. That sequence is more defensible than choosing an adaptation from a disability label or from a generic list of preferred teaching styles.

Universal Design for Learning adds a complementary design perspective. CAST organizes UDL around multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression, with the aim of enabling access to meaningful and challenging learning opportunities ([15]). A systematic review of thirteen pre-K through grade 12 studies found promising applications but also a limited and heterogeneous evidence base, which means UDL should be treated as a framework for anticipating barriers rather than as proof that every option or multimedia format improves learning ([14]). Universal design can reduce avoidable barriers for a whole class; it does not eliminate the need for explicit, domain-specific intervention when a learner has a persistent reading, writing, or mathematics difficulty.

The resulting field combines two obligations that can pull in opposite directions. Education must be accessible enough for a learner to engage with the content, yet demanding enough for the learner to acquire knowledge and skill rather than merely complete an activity. The author's synthesis is that differentiated instruction is sound when it changes the route in response to evidence while keeping the destination explicit. When the destination itself must change, that decision should be acknowledged as a modification, connected to an individualized plan, and not disguised as ordinary differentiation ([3] [4] [18]).

## Core Concepts

### Start With the Learning Goal and the Construct

Every adaptation should begin with a precise statement of what the learner is expected to know or do. A broad instruction such as "make the worksheet easier" provides no protection against removing the very thinking the lesson is meant to develop. A construct-first question is more useful: is the target decoding, reading comprehension, historical explanation, multiplication fluency, algebraic reasoning, written composition, spelling, or the physical act of handwriting? Assessment guidance treats validity as a property of the interpretation and use of performance evidence, so an access support must be judged against the target rather than accepted or rejected in the abstract ([4]).

This distinction separates target skills from access skills. If the target is scientific explanation, decoding a dense prompt may be an access demand rather than the intended science outcome; audio presentation could allow the learner to reason about the same science. If the target is decoding, audio presentation supplies the target performance and cannot show whether decoding improved. Extra time similarly helps only when speed is not central to the construct or when the interpretation explicitly allows a timed-access adjustment. The support is not inherently fair or unfair: its validity depends on what performance is supposed to mean ([4]).

A useful planning rule is to write the invariant before selecting the adaptation. The invariant may be the concept, standard, reasoning process, accuracy criterion, or transfer demand that must remain common. Teachers can then vary the route: amount of guided practice, size of the step, mode of presentation, response format, pacing, feedback frequency, or temporary scaffold. This sequence protects ambitious goals because the invariant cannot silently disappear when materials are adapted.

### Describe the Barrier, Not Just the Label

A disability label does not specify an instructional move. Two learners described as having dyslexia may differ in phonological awareness, decoding accuracy, spelling, fluency, vocabulary, oral-language knowledge, attention, prior instruction, and accumulated content knowledge. Mathematics difficulty can involve number magnitude, fact retrieval, calculation procedure, mathematical language, representation, or word-problem structure. Evidence-based practice guides therefore organize recommendations around component skills and observable instructional needs rather than around one undifferentiated learner type ([7] [8] [9]).

Barrier analysis should combine several forms of evidence. Screening indicates who may need closer attention; diagnostic assessment identifies a plausible component weakness; work samples reveal error patterns; observation shows strategy use and task conditions; progress monitoring estimates whether the learner is improving under instruction. U.S. eligibility rules also require attention to appropriate instruction and relevant exclusionary factors, underscoring that low attainment has multiple possible causes ([2]). None of these data sources is sufficient alone, but together they support a testable instructional hypothesis.

The hypothesis should be specific enough to disconfirm. "The learner is visual" is too vague because it neither identifies a deficient academic process nor predicts a measurable response. "The learner reads multisyllabic words inaccurately because vowel patterns and morpheme boundaries are not applied consistently" identifies a teachable pattern and suggests explicit word analysis, guided practice, and decoding measures. "The learner cannot do math" is similarly unhelpful; an analysis of number-line placement, fraction magnitude, operation selection, mathematical vocabulary, and worked errors can separate concept, representation, language, and procedure ([8] [9]).

### Preserve Goals Through Accommodations and Explicit Instruction

Accommodations remove barriers that are not essential to the target. Common categories include presentation, response, setting, scheduling, and assistive technology. Their use should be individualized and routinely available during instruction if they are expected during assessment, but an instructional accommodation is not automatically valid for every assessment. The decisive question remains whether the adaptation changes the knowledge or skill being inferred from the result ([4]).

An accommodation provides access; it does not teach the weak skill. A learner who uses text-to-speech to study grade-level science may still need direct decoding intervention. These two actions solve different problems. Access prevents a reading difficulty from unnecessarily blocking science learning, while intervention develops reading. Treating access technology as remediation can leave the underlying academic difficulty unchanged; withholding access until remediation succeeds can cause the learner to lose years of content knowledge. The author's synthesis is a dual-track model: maintain participation in ambitious content and teach the impaired component directly ([7] [15] [18]).

Explicit instruction makes hidden processes visible. It states the goal, models the reasoning or procedure, sequences examples, prompts active responses, checks understanding, provides corrective feedback, and gradually transfers responsibility. This is not equivalent to uninterrupted teacher talk. Its defining feature is managed support around the exact skill until the learner can perform more independently. Reading and mathematics practice guides recommend explicit, systematic attention to component skills, purposeful practice, representations, language, and application because persistent difficulty is rarely corrected by exposure alone ([7] [8] [9]).

### Match Instruction to the Academic Domain

For reading difficulty, intervention should follow the learner's profile and the demands of the grade. A 2023 meta-analysis of elementary interventions for students with or at risk for dyslexia found a positive mean effect on norm-referenced reading outcomes and found larger effects with higher dosage; outcome domain also mattered, with comprehension effects smaller than word-reading and spelling effects ([7]). For grades 4 through 9, the What Works Clearinghouse recommends building multisyllabic-word decoding, purposeful fluency, and a coordinated set of comprehension practices including knowledge, vocabulary, questioning, gist, and monitoring ([8]). These recommendations imply that "more reading" is not a sufficient prescription: instruction must address the bottleneck while preserving meaningful text and knowledge-building.

For mathematics difficulty, the instructional target may be conceptual, representational, linguistic, procedural, or strategic. The What Works Clearinghouse recommends systematic instruction, clear mathematical language, carefully selected concrete and semi-concrete representations, number lines, deliberate word-problem instruction, and fluency work in elementary intervention ([9]). A synthesis of mathematics intervention research also identifies explicit instruction, multiple examples, learner verbalization, visual representation, strategy instruction, formative assessment, and peer-assisted learning as recurring components ([10]). Representations should expose mathematical structure and then connect to symbols; they should not become decorative objects or permanent substitutes for the intended abstraction.

Writing difficulty likewise requires decomposition even though the evidence set gathered for this topic concentrates on reading and mathematics. Handwriting or keyboarding fluency, spelling, sentence construction, planning, revision, and genre knowledge impose different demands. The transferable principle is not to infer a complete writing profile from a slow written product. A response accommodation may permit content learning while explicit instruction targets the component that limits independent composition. This statement is a cross-domain synthesis of the construct-first and dual-track principles rather than a separate claim about a particular writing program ([3] [4] [12]).

### Use a Data-Based Individualization Cycle

Data-based individualization is a structured process for learners whose needs remain severe or persistent. The National Center on Intensive Intervention describes it as the systematic use of assessment data, a validated intervention platform, diagnostic information, and research-based adaptations; it is a process rather than a branded program ([12]). A team begins with an intervention supported for the target need, defines a measurable goal, implements with adequate fidelity, gathers frequent progress data, and decides whether performance is approaching the goal. Insufficient progress triggers diagnosis and adaptation rather than indefinite repetition of an unchanged lesson.

Progress monitoring differs from repeatedly administering broad achievement tests. The measure must be sensitive to growth in the target skill, feasible to administer regularly, and interpretable against a goal or expected trajectory. NCII recommends specifying the tool, goal, collection frequency, and review process before intervention; educators then graph results, compare them with the goal, and mark changes when intervention is adapted ([13]). A short sequence of variable scores should not invite a new intervention every week. Teams need enough observations to distinguish a weak trend from ordinary measurement noise.

Adaptation can change quantitative intensity or qualitative design. Quantitative changes include more instructional time, smaller groups, more response opportunities, or more frequent feedback. Qualitative changes include a different explanation, tighter example sequence, explicit vocabulary instruction, a representation that exposes structure, or a strategy matched to the error pattern. The author's synthesis is that increasing minutes without changing the mechanism may deliver more of an ineffective experience, while changing methods without adequate dosage may under-test a promising approach. Intensity and instructional fit must therefore be considered together ([7] [9] [12]).

Fidelity is necessary for interpretation but not a demand for rigidity. If the planned intervention was not delivered, poor growth does not establish that the intervention failed. If it was delivered and the learner did not improve, repeating the program unchanged is not fidelity; it is failure to use the data. The useful record includes what was taught, how often, for how long, in what group size, with what adaptations, and how the learner responded. That record supports team reasoning and prevents hindsight from turning an unsystematic sequence into a false account of evidence-based intervention ([12] [13] [17]).

### Combine Universal Design With Targeted Support

Universal design anticipates predictable variability before individual failure occurs. Clear layouts, explicit vocabulary, captions, accessible digital text, multiple examples, structured choices, and several valid response modes can reduce barriers across a class. CAST's framework emphasizes engagement, representation, and action and expression, while its stated goal is learner agency within meaningful and challenging opportunities ([15]). These options can also reduce the administrative burden of arranging a separate exception for every learner.

Yet choice is not automatically beneficial. A novice who lacks background knowledge may need a carefully sequenced explanation rather than unrestricted exploration, and a learner practicing a weak target skill must actually perform that skill. The systematic review by Ok and colleagues found only thirteen qualifying pre-K through grade 12 studies and variation in how UDL principles were implemented, so the framework's plausibility and accessibility value should not be mistaken for a uniform treatment effect ([14]). Universal design is the accessible base; targeted intervention is the additional precision required by persistent, skill-specific difficulty.

Differentiation likewise should not become permanent low-track instruction. In a primary-education review of twenty-one studies and seventy-eight extracted effect sizes, computerized differentiation tools and differentiation embedded in broader programs showed small-to-moderate positive effects, while homogeneous ability grouping had a small negative effect for lower-achieving learners and no effect for others ([5]). The result supports flexible, instructional use of grouping rather than stable identity groups. Groups should change when the diagnosed need changes, and all groups should retain access to the curriculum's central ideas.

### Distinguish Equality, Equity, and Expectation

Equality provides the same resources or conditions. Appropriate support provides what is necessary to access the same meaningful goal. Modification changes the expected goal. These are different decisions, and collapsing them creates predictable errors: identical conditions can preserve an irrelevant barrier, while unexamined support can accidentally replace the target. The accommodation-modification distinction provides an operational test: did the adaptation change access, or did it lower what the learner is expected to know and demonstrate? ([4] [18]).

Fairness therefore does not require that every learner receive the same tool, time, or pathway. It requires that evidence about learning be interpretable and that opportunities remain ambitious. A support should be neither a reward nor a stigma; it is a design response to a documented barrier. At the same time, high expectations are not established by assigning identical work and ignoring predictable inaccessibility. The author's synthesis is that rigor resides in the goal and quality of thinking, not in avoidable friction surrounding the task ([3] [4] [18]).

## Evidence

### Differentiation Produces Conditional, Not Automatic, Benefits

Deunk and colleagues conducted a systematic review of differentiation practices in primary language and mathematics education. They included twenty-one studies published since 1995 and extracted seventy-eight effect sizes. Their synthesis found small-to-moderate positive effects when computerized systems were used as differentiation tools or when differentiation was embedded in a broader program or reform; homogeneous grouping across or within classes had a small negative effect for lower-achieving students and no effect for other students ([5]). This pattern matters because it separates an adaptive instructional system from mere sorting. Group membership did not itself produce an effective lesson.

Smale-Jacobse and colleagues reviewed empirical research from 2006 through 2016 on within-class differentiated instruction in secondary education. The review found promising positive results in parts of the literature but concluded that evidence was scarce, practices were heterogeneous, and stronger research was needed before firm conclusions could be drawn about specific approaches ([6]). The methodological lesson is restraint: "differentiated" may refer to mastery learning, grouping, tiering, individualized technology, teacher adaptation, or multi-component reform. A single headline effect cannot identify which mechanism will help a particular learner.

The UDL evidence has a similar structure. Ok and colleagues systematically reviewed pre-K through grade 12 UDL interventions and found thirteen qualifying studies, then analyzed how UDL principles were applied and what academic and social outcomes were reported ([14]). The small and heterogeneous set supported UDL as a promising access framework but did not justify treating every multiple-format lesson as an evidence-based intervention. CAST's guidelines provide a coherent design vocabulary, while independent evaluation remains necessary for the learning effect of a specific implementation ([14] [15]).

### Reading Intervention Helps, but Outcomes and Dosage Differ

Hall and colleagues synthesized forty years of experimental and quasi-experimental reading intervention research for elementary students with or at risk for dyslexia. Fifty-three studies reported in fifty-two publications contributed data from 6,053 students, and the authors used robust variance estimation to address dependent effects. The mean effect on norm-referenced reading outcomes was statistically significant at Hedge's g = 0.33, with substantial heterogeneity; dosage and reading outcome domain significantly moderated effects, and comprehension effects were smaller than word-reading and spelling effects ([7]). The result supports intervention while rejecting a promise of rapid or uniform remediation.

The What Works Clearinghouse grades 4-9 practice guide reviewed studies under defined evidence standards and rated its recommendations. It reports strong evidence for multisyllabic-word decoding, purposeful fluency-building, and coordinated comprehension-building practices; it also addresses opportunities to practice with challenging text ([8]). These components show why differentiation should preserve age-appropriate meaning while treating foundational gaps. An older student may require explicit decoding instruction and still need access to knowledge-rich content, vocabulary, discussion, and comprehension work.

Fletcher and Vaughn's review of response-to-intervention models explains the broader delivery system. RTI combines universal screening, progress monitoring for learners at risk, and increasingly intensive evidence-based intervention. The review identifies improved prevention and remediation as the primary goal and special-education decision data as a secondary use, while warning that implementation quality and evidence-based teaching are essential ([17]). This supports an intervention-first posture without reducing eligibility evaluation to a single response score.

NICHD's report on dyslexia and IQ provides evidence against a common classification shortcut. In the summarized study, researchers compared brain activity among typical readers, IQ-discrepant poor readers, and poor readers whose IQ was also low. They found no basis in those activation patterns for distinguishing the poor-reader groups by IQ discrepancy and concluded that children with severe reading difficulty should receive reading support regardless of IQ ([16]). The finding does not make cognitive assessment irrelevant to every educational question; it rejects the use of discrepancy as a gate that withholds instruction from a struggling reader.

### Mathematics Benefits From Explicit Structure and Representation

The 2021 What Works Clearinghouse elementary mathematics guide drew on systematic evidence reviews to issue six recommendations, all rated as supported by strong evidence on the guide page: systematic instruction, mathematical language, concrete and semi-concrete representations, number lines, deliberate word-problem instruction, and timed activities as one route to fluency ([9]). The recommendations span concepts, language, models, application, and fluency rather than reducing mathematics intervention to repeated calculation. They also make representations purposeful: a model is selected to reveal a mathematical relationship and connected to notation.

Gersten and colleagues synthesized experimental and quasi-experimental intervention research concerning students with learning disabilities or mathematics difficulty. Their work identified explicit instruction and heuristic or strategy instruction as particularly consequential components, alongside visual representations, learner verbalization, multiple examples, formative assessment, and structured peer support ([10]). Because the synthesis draws components from multi-feature interventions, it does not prove that every component has an independent effect in every context. It does support the design of instruction that makes problem structure and decision steps observable.

Lein, Jitendra, and Harwell meta-analyzed thirty-three studies of mathematical word-problem interventions for K-12 students with learning disabilities or mathematics difficulties. After two influential data points were removed, thirty-one experimental or quasi-experimental studies contributed thirty-four independent effect sizes; the random-effects mean was g = 0.56 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.40 to 0.72. Schema-based transfer instruction showed larger effects than several comparison categories in moderator analysis ([11]). This finding supports teaching learners to recognize and apply problem structures across varied examples rather than memorizing surface cues from one worksheet.

### Data Systems Make Adaptation Testable

NCII's data-based individualization framework integrates validated intervention, progress monitoring, diagnostic evidence, adaptation, and repeated monitoring ([12]). Its progress-monitoring guidance requires teams to define the measure, goal, collection frequency, and review plan, then graph results and compare observed progress with the target ([13]). These are procedural sources rather than randomized trials, but their value is epistemic: they make an adaptation visible enough to evaluate. Without a baseline, implementation record, and outcome series, a teacher cannot reliably distinguish improvement, nonresponse, inconsistent delivery, or task-specific fluctuation.

The legal and assessment sources create additional constraints on interpretation. U.S. regulation requires evaluation teams to consider adequate achievement, patterns or response to intervention, appropriate instruction, and relevant exclusionary conditions when determining specific learning disability ([2]). NCEO distinguishes accommodations that preserve expectations from modifications that lower them and cautions that an adaptation valid for instruction may alter the construct of an assessment ([4]). RAND survey data further show that teachers commonly alter, add, or create materials for students with disabilities, while warning that the educational meaning of substantial modification depends on curriculum quality and learner need ([18]). Together, these sources indicate that adaptation is common but not self-validating.

Across these evidence streams, no single branded method solves learner variability. The author's synthesis is that the strongest convergence lies in a mechanism: define the target, identify the component barrier, teach it explicitly with suitable examples and representations, preserve access to meaningful content, monitor growth, and adapt according to data. Differentiation has value when it operationalizes that mechanism; it becomes weak when it means only more choices, easier work, fixed grouping, or unmeasured customization ([5] [6] [7] [9] [12]).

## Implications

### For Classroom Teachers

Planning should begin with a common learning intention and success criteria, followed by a barrier map. Teachers can ask four questions: What knowledge or performance must remain invariant? Which task features are necessary to elicit it? Which features are incidental barriers? What evidence will show whether the adaptation helps? This construct-first sequence aligns accommodations with assessment meaning and reduces the risk that a simplified worksheet quietly becomes a different curriculum ([4]).

A practical lesson design can operate on two tracks. The access track enables participation in grade-level ideas through supports such as accessible text, read-aloud where decoding is not the target, structured notes, reduced copying, explicit vocabulary, additional processing time, or an alternative response mode. The intervention track provides direct practice in the weak academic component, such as decoding, spelling, number magnitude, mathematical language, or word-problem schema. The tracks should meet over time as the learner gains independence; access is not postponed until remediation is complete, and remediation is not replaced by access technology ([7] [8] [9] [15]).

Flexible grouping should be short-lived, skill-specific, and connected to different instruction. A teacher might group several learners for work on multisyllabic decoding, regroup them after progress data show mastery, and maintain mixed groups for knowledge-building discussion. Permanent "low" groups can narrow content and expectations, and the primary-education meta-analysis found a small negative effect of homogeneous grouping for lower-achieving students ([5]). Grouping is thus a scheduling tool, not a diagnosis or an intervention.

Examples and scaffolds should be selected to expose the target structure. In mathematics, concrete or visual representations should connect explicitly to mathematical language and symbols; in reading, controlled attention to a decoding pattern should connect to words and meaningful text; in content subjects, a graphic organizer should cue the reasoning needed rather than merely reduce writing. Guided support should fade when performance becomes accurate and stable. The instructional aim is independent transfer, not permanent success under maximum prompting ([8] [9] [11]).

### For Special Educators and Intervention Teams

Teams should translate broad goals into measurable subskills without losing sight of the larger curriculum. A goal such as "improve reading" is difficult to monitor, while a narrow fluency count can become educationally empty if disconnected from accuracy, vocabulary, knowledge, and comprehension. A coherent plan records present performance, an annual or term outcome, component measures, intervention dosage, review intervals, and rules for intensification or fading ([3] [12] [13]).

Progress meetings should examine trend, error pattern, and fidelity together. If performance is below the goal but improving steadily, the appropriate decision may be to continue. If the trend is flat and delivery was consistent, diagnostic analysis should guide a substantive adaptation. If delivery was inconsistent, the first correction is implementation rather than a new explanation of the learner. Phase changes should be recorded so later reviewers know which instruction produced which observations ([12] [13]).

Evaluation should remain multidisciplinary and should not confuse instructional response with diagnosis. A learner's nonresponse may reflect a mismatched method, insufficient dosage, unreliable measurement, attendance, language, sensory access, or a more persistent academic disability. U.S. regulation explicitly requires multiple considerations and prohibits relying on a single criterion, while RTI scholarship treats progress data as one contributor to decision making ([2] [17]). The ethical implication is to provide useful support during evaluation rather than making failure the price of eligibility.

Accommodations should be specified precisely enough to implement and review. "Extra time as needed" may produce inconsistent access and no evaluable evidence; a better plan states the setting, amount or decision rule, task types, target barrier, and whether the support applies during assessment. Teams should also identify circumstances where the support would invalidate the target, such as text-to-speech on a decoding measure. This precision turns an accommodation from a benevolent intention into a testable educational design ([4]).

### For School Leaders and Systems

Differentiation requires infrastructure, not only teacher goodwill. Schools need valid screening and progress measures, protected intervention time, access to evidence-based materials, professional learning in explicit instruction, collaboration between general and special educators, and schedules that permit groups to change. RAND's survey findings show that teachers frequently alter or create materials for students with disabilities and point to curriculum supports and collaboration as system-level needs ([18]). If each teacher must invent every adaptation independently, quality and continuity will vary.

Leaders should audit opportunity to learn as well as student performance. A learner can appear busy, compliant, and successful while receiving less grade-level content, fewer cognitively demanding tasks, or extensive adult prompting. Conversely, exposing every learner to an identical inaccessible task does not establish rigor. A useful audit compares the intended curriculum, task demand, actual content taught, support supplied, learner response, and independence over time. U.S. IEP requirements make progress in the general curriculum an explicit concern, and federal guidance distinguishes access from lowered expectations ([3] [4]).

Procurement decisions should demand evidence at the level of mechanism and implementation. An adaptive platform may sort tasks efficiently but still provide weak explanations, narrow practice, or opaque placement rules. A UDL-labeled curriculum may offer many formats without requiring the intended thinking. Reviews of differentiated instruction and UDL both show heterogeneity, while positive differentiation findings often involve broader, coherent programs rather than isolated features ([5] [6] [14]). Systems should therefore ask what diagnostic information drives adaptation, what instructional change follows, which outcomes were tested, and whether teachers can inspect and override the logic.

### For Families and Learners

Families can ask whether a proposed support changes access or changes the learning expectation. They can request a concrete description of the target skill, present performance, intervention, frequency, progress measure, and next decision point. These questions do not require technical expertise; they make the educational logic visible. Where an individualized plan applies, measurable goals and methods for reporting progress should connect services to outcomes rather than merely list accommodations ([3]).

Learner participation is also important. A student can often report which task component is effortful, which support improves access, and when a tool is stigmatizing or cumbersome. That testimony should inform but not replace performance evidence. The long-term aim is strategic agency: the learner understands the barrier, can select a valid support, knows when a target skill must be practiced directly, and can explain what still requires assistance. CAST places learner agency at the center of UDL, while data-based individualization adds the discipline of testing whether a choice improves progress ([12] [15]).

Expectations should be communicated without implying that effort alone erases disability. Evidence from reading intervention shows meaningful average benefits but also heterogeneity and modest effects, especially for broader comprehension outcomes ([7]). A credible message is therefore neither fatalistic nor promotional: skills can improve with explicit, sufficiently intensive instruction, access supports can protect broader learning, and progress may require sustained adjustment. This stance respects the learner because it combines ambition with accurate uncertainty.

### For Assessment and Accountability

Scores should be interpreted with a record of the conditions under which performance was obtained. If an accommodation preserves the intended construct, the adapted score may offer a fairer estimate; if it supplies the target skill or changes expectations, the result answers a different question. Instructional measures, diagnostic measures, progress measures, and accountability assessments serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable ([4] [13]).

Accountability systems should also avoid rewarding reduced opportunity. If short-term proficiency rates improve because struggling learners receive easier content, the apparent success may conceal weaker preparation for later study. The author's synthesis is that systems need both attainment and access-to-curriculum indicators, plus direct review of intervention quality. Progress matters, but growth toward a narrowed destination is not equivalent to progress toward the intended curriculum ([3] [18]).

### Decision Rule

The practical decision rule is simple enough to remember but demanding to execute. Keep the learning goal explicit. Remove barriers that are irrelevant to that goal. Teach the impaired component directly with evidence-based methods. Measure progress using a tool sensitive to the target. Change intensity or method when a stable pattern shows that the learner is not on course. Call a lowered goal a modification and govern it through an explicit individualized decision rather than hiding it inside differentiation ([3] [4] [12] [13]).

The author's final assessment is that the worst outcome is not unequal materials; it is invisible goal erosion. A learner can receive extensive help, complete every assignment, and still be denied both the curriculum and effective intervention. Differentiated instruction prevents that failure only when support is tied to a defined barrier, evidence, and a preserved destination.
