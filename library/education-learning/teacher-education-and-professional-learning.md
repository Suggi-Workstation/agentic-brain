# Teacher Education and Professional Learning -- Instructional Expertise Is Built by Practicing and Getting Feedback on Specific Teaching Work, Not by Attending Isolated Workshops

Teachers improve most reliably when professional learning links specific subject and student-thinking knowledge to rehearsal, observation, and feedback on their own classroom work. Knowledge-only or one-off training often fails to change classrooms: a randomized mathematics program designed to deliver 114 contact hours changed neither teacher knowledge nor student scores, while coaching programs raised measured instruction by 0.49 standard deviations (SD) and achievement by 0.18 SD on average, with effects that shrink as programs grow [2][3]. This topic explains how instructional expertise is built across preparation, induction, coaching, lesson study, collaborative inquiry, and evaluation, and what the evidence does and does not establish.

## Background

The question of what a teacher must know has changed several times, and the changes explain why professional learning is designed the way it is today. Lee Shulman, in his 1985 presidential address to the American Educational Research Association (published in 1986), documented the starting point. In the nineteenth century, teacher examinations were almost entirely tests of subject matter. Shulman reported that on the 1875 California Teachers Examination only 50 of 1,000 possible points went to a ten-item subtest on the theory and practice of teaching, and that in the examinations he reviewed from that period ninety to ninety-five percent of the content was subject matter. The assumption was that knowing the subject was the prerequisite and that knowledge of teaching methods played a decidedly secondary role [1].

By the 1980s the emphasis had reversed. Shulman examined a list of evaluation categories prepared by a state he had advised, which he described as similar to those emerging in other states: organization of instructional plans, evaluation, recognition of individual differences, cultural awareness, understanding youth, management, and educational policies and procedures. The subject matter had disappeared from the list. He traced this to the research program on teaching effectiveness that policymakers cited as their basis. That research was valid on its own terms, but in simplifying classroom life it ignored the subject matter itself. In his words, no one asked how subject matter was transformed from the knowledge of the teacher into the content of instruction, or how particular formulations of content related to what students came to know or misconstrue [1]. From this gap he proposed a framework of teacher knowledge (described under Core Concepts) that became a standard reference point in teacher education research.

A second line of work made the framework measurable. Hill, Rowan, and Ball built a survey measure of the mathematical knowledge that teaching actually uses, such as giving explanations for common rules and procedures, working with representations, and analyzing unusual student solution methods, and tested whether it predicted student gains in a sample of thousands of first and third graders [13]. Earlier "production function" studies had often relied on proxies such as course counts or certification status; Hill and colleagues argued that inadequate measurement of teacher knowledge could have limited the conclusions of that literature [13].

A third line of work concerned in-service professional development (PD). Before the 2000s, few rigorous studies existed. A 2007 review by Yoon and colleagues identified nine studies with rigorous designs, involving only 5 to 44 teachers each, with programs delivered by the people who developed them; the review could not identify which PD features mattered, because every program was a workshop or summer institute with some follow-up support [3]. The U.S. Institute of Education Sciences (IES) then funded larger randomized trials in several areas, including early reading, middle school mathematics, and teacher induction [3][4].

The most recent phase is synthesis. The Learning Policy Institute reviewed 35 studies with comparison-group or statistically controlled designs and identified seven features common to PD that was associated with improved teaching and student outcomes [8]. Kraft, Blazar, and Hogan pooled 60 causal studies of coaching [2]. Sims and colleagues, in a 2021 Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) report, searched ten scholarly databases, screened 3,140 abstracts, reviewed 347 papers, and analyzed 104 papers reporting 205 effects from experimental evaluations of PD [9]. The field's question shifted from which program works to which active ingredients, and under what conditions, make a program work.

The scale of the enterprise explains the stakes. In the OECD's 2018 Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), 94 percent of teachers across the OECD countries reported taking part in at least one type of professional development in the 12 months before the survey, with teachers attending about four types of activity. Participation was highest for in-person courses or seminars (76 percent) and reading professional literature (72 percent), and lower for collaborative forms: only 44 percent took part in training based on peer or self-observation and coaching [10]. Interpretation: teachers spend substantial time in professional learning, but the collaborative, school-embedded forms that have causal evidence of effect reach fewer teachers than courses and seminars do.

## Core Concepts

### Knowledge for teaching: three categories and three forms

Shulman distinguished three categories of content knowledge in a teacher's mind [1]. Subject matter content knowledge is the amount and organization of knowledge in the teacher's mind, including what Joseph Schwab called the substantive structures (how a discipline's concepts and principles are organized) and syntactic structures (how truth or validity is established in the discipline). A teacher must understand not only that a proposition is true but why it is warranted, why it is worth knowing, and how it relates to other propositions. Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) is the form of content knowledge "most germane to its teachability": for the most regularly taught topics, the most useful representations, analogies, illustrations, examples, explanations, and demonstrations, together with an understanding of what makes a topic easy or hard to learn and what preconceptions students of different ages and backgrounds bring. Because no single representation is most powerful, Shulman argued that the teacher needs a "veritable armamentarium" of alternatives. Curricular knowledge is familiarity with the range of programs and materials available for a subject and the indications and contraindications for using them, including what students study in other subjects at the same time (lateral knowledge) and what came before and comes after in the same subject (vertical knowledge) [1].

He also separated three forms in which knowledge can be held [1]. Propositional knowledge consists of principles (from empirical research), maxims (the accumulated wisdom of practice), and norms (moral and ethical commitments). Propositions are economical, but they are hard to remember in long lists and are stripped of context, which makes a principle such as "check for understanding" difficult to apply. Case knowledge is knowledge of specific, richly documented events; Shulman proposed prototypes (cases that exemplify theoretical principles), precedents (cases that carry principles of practice), and parables (cases that convey values). A case is not merely an incident: calling something a case makes a theoretical claim that it is "a case of" something, and case knowledge without theory is "mere anecdote." Strategic knowledge is invoked when principles collide. His example is that research on wait time says longer pauses raise the level of student thinking, while research on classroom management warns that slowing the pace too much increases discipline problems; neither principle settles what to do in a given lesson. Professional judgment is the capacity to reason when rules conflict [1].

The measurable counterpart appears in Hill, Rowan, and Ball's work. Their instrument sampled the specialized mathematical knowledge used in teaching, not general mathematics proficiency, and it was the strongest teacher-level predictor of student gains in their models, ahead of credential and coursework variables (details under Evidence) [13]. The practical point is that "knowing the subject" and "knowing how to teach the subject" are separable, and professional learning that targets only one leaves the other unchanged.

### The forms of teacher learning

The literature uses several terms for different structures, and the evidence differs by structure.

Pre-service preparation combines coursework with clinical practice. In the New York City study by Boyd and colleagues, programs were characterized by five features: required mathematics content courses, required English language arts content courses, the share of methods and learning-and-development instructors who were tenure-line faculty, the program's oversight of student teaching, and whether a capstone project (such as a portfolio, research paper, or action research project) was required. The authors described the last two as capturing a link between preparation and practice [11].

Induction and mentoring support beginning teachers. The IES evaluation distinguished "informal" induction, which may pair a new teacher with another full-time teacher without training, supplemental materials, or release time, from "comprehensive" induction, which used trained full-time mentors, a structured curriculum of support, monthly PD sessions, opportunities to observe veteran teachers, and an end-of-year colloquium [4].

Coaching places an instructional expert alongside teachers to model practice and help them incorporate it. Kraft and colleagues found competing definitions in the literature, ranging from implementation support that ensures a practice taught in a group session is executed with fidelity, to a development tool that shows teachers how and why strategies matter, to "responsive" versus "directive" styles [2].

Lesson study is a collaborative cycle in which teachers study curriculum and long-term goals, plan a research lesson, teach it while colleagues collect data on student learning, and reflect on that data [12]. Professional learning communities (PLCs) are ongoing, job-embedded groups; the Learning Policy Institute noted that many PLC efforts have been poorly implemented and superficial, while well-implemented ones can improve practice and learning through collaborative analysis of student work [8].

Evaluation with feedback treats classroom observation as a source of information. In Cincinnati's Teacher Evaluation System, teachers were observed four times in an evaluation year (three times by a trained peer evaluator, once by an administrator) against a rubric with four performance levels, and received written feedback within ten days of each observation [5]. Peer pairing asks a high-performing and a lower-performing colleague in the same school to work on specific skills identified in prior evaluations [7].

### The chain from learning to student outcomes

Most PD research assumes a chain: PD changes teacher knowledge or skill, which changes classroom practice, which changes student learning. Kraft and colleagues state this as the theory of action behind coaching and note an important limit: they can interpret the effect of coaching on instruction and on achievement causally, but not the relationship between instruction and achievement, because coaching could affect achievement through other routes [2]. Sims and colleagues use a similar logic model and add that the school environment interacts with each step [9]. The consequence is that a program can succeed or fail at different links, and a good evaluation measures more than one.

### Active ingredients: from feature lists to mechanisms

The Learning Policy Institute's synthesis of 35 studies listed seven features: content focus; active learning; collaboration; models of effective practice; coaching and expert support; feedback and reflection; and sustained duration [8]. The authors cautioned that studies typically examine bundled models, so the list does not establish the efficacy of any individual component [8].

Sims and colleagues proposed a finer model. They coded 14 "mechanisms," each of which has evidence from settings outside teacher PD, grouped by four purposes: gaining new insights (managing cognitive load, revisiting prior learning), pursuing new goal-directed behaviors (goal setting, credible source, praise or reinforcement), acquiring techniques (instruction, practical social support, modelling, feedback, rehearsal), and embedding changes in practice (prompts and cues, action planning, self-monitoring, context-specific repetition) [9]. The authors allocated each mechanism to the purpose it primarily serves in theory and acknowledged that some of these allocations are arguable. Their framework predicts specific failure modes. If PD addresses insight, goals, and technique but not embedding, teachers revert to established routines. If it addresses insight and goals but not technique or practice, a "knowing-doing gap" remains. If it addresses goals, technique, and practice but not insight, teachers may apply a technique without understanding when it works, which can render it ineffective, a pattern the education literature calls a "lethal mutation" [9]. This is a theoretical model that the authors tested only partly; the test is reported under Evidence.

### Rehearsal on the actual work

Several strands converge on one idea: learning to teach requires practicing the work of teaching, not only studying about it. Boyd and colleagues found that preparation focused on the work of the classroom, such as oversight of student teaching and a capstone project, and opportunities to practice tasks like listening to a child read aloud for assessment or planning a guided reading lesson, were associated with greater first-year effectiveness [11]. Sims and colleagues coded rehearsal as a mechanism [9]. In lesson study, observing students during a research lesson and discussing the data were among the activities that teachers' own reflections linked to knowledge growth [12].

### Judgment for diverse learners and assessment practice

Responding to learner variation is a case of Shulman's strategic knowledge: no single rule dictates what to do. TALIS found that across the OECD, 62 percent of teachers had been trained to teach in mixed-ability settings during formal education, but only 44 percent felt prepared to do so when they finished; training in teaching students with special needs was the PD topic with the highest share of teachers reporting a high need (22 percent) [10]. Assessment-related practice is also a target of PD: Sims and colleagues found that programs with more mechanisms showed larger effects within the subset focused on formative assessment and inquiry-based teaching, though not within the subset focused on data-driven instruction [9]. For the content of such practices, see `library/education-learning/assessment-and-testing.md` and `library/education-learning/learning-disabilities-and-differentiated-instruction.md`.

## Evidence

### Teacher knowledge and preparation

Hill, Rowan, and Ball (2005) used a linear mixed-model design in which student gains over one year were nested within teachers and schools: 1,190 first graders and 1,773 third graders, with 334 and 365 teachers respectively, in 115 schools across 42 districts in 15 states. Teachers' score on the mathematical-knowledge-for-teaching measure was significantly related to student gains in both grades after controlling for student and teacher covariates. In the strongest model, students gained about 2.25 additional points on the Terra Nova scale per SD of teacher knowledge, which the authors expressed as roughly one-half to two-thirds of a month of additional growth; it was the strongest teacher-level predictor and, in third grade, comparable in size to the effect of socioeconomic status. Teacher certification was not a significant predictor, and mathematics coursework in preparation was just outside conventional significance in third grade (p = .06). The authors listed limitations: the Terra Nova covered the focal topics of the knowledge measure only partly (43 percent of items at the kindergarten level and 54 percent at the third-grade level), and they argued that such misalignment would bias estimates downward [13]. The design is observational, so the association is not proof that raising teacher knowledge raises achievement.

Boyd and colleagues (2008 working paper) linked New York City teachers' value-added scores to the features of their preparation programs, using an analysis of over 30 programs and a survey of first-year teachers. Average effectiveness varied across institutions; the gap between the average institution and the highest was about 0.05 SD in math and 0.04 SD in English language arts, similar in size to the difference between first-year and second-year teachers. Preparation more closely tied to the work of the classroom was associated with greater first-year effectiveness, and the authors reported that programs with more oversight of student teaching or a required capstone project supplied significantly more effective first-year teachers, with coefficients of at least 0.04 SD for the capstone in both subjects. These program-level advantages did not hold for second-year teachers, which the authors read as first-year differences converging as teachers learn practice on the job; math content coursework, by contrast, showed small positive associations in the second year but not consistently in the first. They cautioned that program features were estimated singly and may reflect omitted correlated variables, that the capstone requirement could be a proxy for program rigor, that their instruments had not been validated, and that New York State has demanding certification requirements, so low-preparation teachers were absent from the sample [11].

### Induction and workshop-intensive programs

Glazerman and colleagues (2010) conducted an IES-funded randomized trial of comprehensive induction: 418 elementary schools in 17 urban districts were assigned by lottery, covering 1,009 beginning teachers. Mentors worked full time at a ratio of 12 teachers to 1. The program increased support received (for example, mentor time of 85 versus 68 minutes per week in one-year districts), yet it produced no impact on classroom practice in the first year, no impact on teacher retention in the school, district, or profession over four years, and no impact on satisfaction or feelings of preparedness. For teachers offered one year of induction, student achievement did not differ in any of the first three years. For teachers offered two years, achievement did not differ in the first two years; in the third year, students of treatment teachers outscored comparison students by 0.11 SD in reading and 0.20 SD in math, but only in the subset of teachers whose students had both current and prior-year scores. Without the prior-year requirement the researchers found no impact [4].

Garet and colleagues (2011) tested intensive mathematics PD in a randomized trial of 7th-grade teachers. Schools within 12 districts were randomly assigned; the treatment included a summer institute, school-year seminars, and in-school coaching, designed to deliver 114 contact hours over two years (68 in year one, 46 in year two), focused entirely on rational numbers and on explaining concepts, identifying student misconceptions, and using representations. The report noted that the annual amount of PD offered exceeded what most mathematics teachers typically receive in a year, that the PD did not generally require teachers to spend time outside the institutes and coaching sessions studying content or practicing techniques, and that teachers were not shown their results on the study's knowledge test. Implementation was close to plan, but turnover limited dosage: second-year teachers received 68 percent of the full intended dosage on average, and 22 of the 45 treatment teachers present at the end of the two-year program had not been present at the beginning. At the end of the second year there was no statistically significant impact on teacher knowledge (effect size 0.05) or on student achievement (effect size -0.01) [3]. The report also summarized an earlier IES study of early reading PD: one year of PD had significant impacts on some dimensions of teacher knowledge and practice at the end of the PD year, which did not persist, and no impact on student achievement [3].

### Coaching, feedback, and practice-based learning

Kraft, Blazar, and Hogan (2018) pooled 60 studies using causal designs. Across 43 studies with instructional outcomes the pooled effect was 0.49 SD, and across 31 studies with achievement outcomes it was 0.18 SD; 51 of the 60 studies involved prekindergarten or elementary teachers and 40 evaluated content-specific coaching. The effects varied substantially across programs. Smaller programs (fewer than 100 teachers) produced pooled effects of 0.63 SD on instruction and 0.28 SD on achievement, while larger programs (100 or more teachers) produced 0.34 SD and 0.10 SD. The authors found no relationship between the number of coaching hours and effect size, and an association between larger effects and pairing coaching with group training (0.31 SD larger on instruction). Only five of the 60 studies reported outcomes in a follow-up year after coaching ended [2].

Allen and colleagues (2011) randomly assigned 78 secondary school teachers in 12 schools to My Teaching Partner-Secondary or ordinary in-service training. Teachers submitted video of their lessons and, about twice a month over a school year, held 20- to 30-minute phone conferences with a consultant about teacher-student interactions, after an initial workshop and with a closing booster workshop. In the intervention year the effect on student test scores was not significant. In the following year, with a new class and no coaching, students of intervention teachers scored 0.22 SD higher, equivalent to moving the average student from the 50th to the 59th percentile; analysis was consistent with change in observed teacher-student interactions as the mediating mechanism. The program required about 20 hours of teacher time over 13 months and cost $3,700 per teacher in consultant and equipment costs [6].

Taylor and Tyler (2011 working paper, published 2012) studied mid-career teachers in Cincinnati who were phased into the evaluation system at different times, allowing comparison of each teacher's own results before, during, and after evaluation. With controls, students scored 0.062 SD higher in math in the evaluation year and 0.113 SD higher in later years; the post-evaluation gain was largest for teachers with bottom-quartile evaluation scores (0.279 SD), and a conservative attrition adjustment reduced the later-year estimate to 0.066 SD. The program cost $7,000 to $7,500 per evaluated teacher, compared with about $695 per teacher in the district's formal PD budget, plus the lost achievement from moving effective teachers into full-time evaluator roles [5].

Papay, Taylor, Tyler, and Laski (2016 working paper, published 2020) randomly assigned 14 schools in one Tennessee district (seven treatment, seven control). In treatment schools, a lower-performing teacher and a higher-performing colleague were matched on specific skills from prior evaluations and asked to work together. Students of the lower-performing "target" teachers scored 0.12 SD higher than students in control classrooms, and the improvement persisted, and perhaps grew, in the following year [7].

Lewis and Perry randomly assigned 39 volunteer teams of educators, mostly elementary teachers, to lesson study supported by a mailed fractions resource kit or to control conditions in which teams studied self-chosen topics other than fractions. Resource-kit teams worked without guidance from the researchers over an average of 91 days. Teachers in the 13 resource-kit lesson study groups significantly improved three of four measured facets of fractions knowledge (understanding the whole, unit fractions, and fractions as numbers), while control teachers did not; the paper reports that the larger trial found a significant impact on students' fractions knowledge as well. The design could not separate the resource kit from the lesson study process [12].

### Syntheses

Sims and colleagues found an average effect of PD on pupil test scores of 0.05 SD (95 percent confidence interval 0.03 to 0.07), about one month of additional progress. Programs with zero of the 14 coded mechanisms had effects around zero; programs with nearly all 14 had effects around 0.17 SD. Balanced designs, with at least one mechanism for each of the four purposes, averaged 0.15 SD versus 0.05 SD for unbalanced designs, but the difference was statistically uncertain (p = .22). Effect sizes were much lower in pre-registered experiments and in experiments with high attrition. A review of implementation studies found that PD is often implemented with low fidelity and adapted locally, and is more likely to be delivered as intended when it aligns with school needs and existing practice and is planned around teachers' limited time [9].

## Implications

### For teacher educators and program designers

Interpretation: the evidence supports building preparation and induction around the work teachers will do rather than around topics alone. Boyd's finding that practice-linked preparation was associated with first-year effectiveness, together with Shulman's argument that propositions without cases are hard to apply, implies that programs should pair each principle with documented cases and with supervised practice of the specific task, such as planning a guided reading lesson or analyzing student work [1][11]. The comprehensive induction trial adds a caution: adding a mentor and monthly sessions did not by itself change first-year practice or retention, so mentoring should be evaluated by what mentor and novice actually do together, not by the presence of a program [4]. Preparation should also address both kinds of knowledge the mathematics evidence separates. Knowledge of the specialized content of teaching predicted student gains, but the 114-hour trial shows that a program aiming to deepen that knowledge may not raise it, so design needs to be tested rather than assumed [3][13].

A related implication concerns judgment. Because strategic knowledge is exercised when principles conflict, case discussion and deliberate comparison of competing courses of action are more apt than lists of competencies [1]. TALIS suggests the need is unmet: fewer than half of teachers in the OECD felt prepared for mixed-ability classrooms when they finished their training [10]. Comparative systems differ in how they select and prepare teachers; see `library/education-learning/comparative-education-systems.md`.

### For school and district leaders who commission or run professional learning

Interpretation: a leader choosing between a workshop and an alternative should ask which of the mechanisms the program actually includes. A one-day session may supply insight, but if it lacks rehearsal, feedback, and prompts that embed change, the Sims model predicts reversion to prior routines [9]. Coaching has a sizable body of causal evidence for changing instruction (60 studies), but that evidence also warns that effects at scale were about half those of small programs on instruction and about one-third on achievement, which Kraft and colleagues attribute in part to the difficulty of building a corps of capable coaches matched to teachers' needs [2]. Leaders should therefore invest in coach selection, coach development, and monitoring, and should not assume that a program proven in a small trial will transfer intact.

Several low-cost structures have experimental support. Peer pairing on specific skills produced gains of 0.12 SD in a small trial, and lesson study with research-based resources improved teacher knowledge in a randomized comparison; both draw on colleagues rather than external experts [7][12]. Observation-based evaluation, when conducted by trained evaluators who give timely written feedback against a clear rubric, was associated with later gains, especially for lower-scoring teachers, but it was expensive and the study design relied on one district [5]. Content focus is common among effective programs: the Learning Policy Institute's seven features begin with it, and the Lewis and Perry teams studied fractions content. The IES mathematics program also centered on rational-number content yet showed no effect on its own outcomes, so content focus appears insufficient by itself [3][8][12].

The most important implementation lesson concerns fidelity and time. Sims and colleagues found that PD is frequently adapted and implemented with low fidelity, and that planning around teachers' limited time improves fidelity [9]. In TALIS, 54 percent of teachers agreed that PD conflicts with their work schedule and 48 percent that there are no incentives to participate; the report recommends allocating hours within the regular work schedule for formal and informal professional learning [10]. The Cincinnati example adds a cost lens: its evaluation program cost roughly ten times the district's per-teacher PD budget, yet the authors estimated returns that exceeded costs under their assumptions, so the relevant comparison is cost per unit of change in instruction, not the headline price [5].

### For policymakers and funders

Interpretation: policy should treat professional learning as a structure that needs time, trained facilitators, and measurement, not as a count of hours. Hours alone did not predict outcomes in the coaching meta-analysis, which found no dosage relationship, and the 114-hour trial produced null results [2][3]. A policy that mandates a number of PD hours without specifying the mechanisms risks resembling the null-result programs. The Allen trial reached its effect with roughly 20 hours of teacher time spread over 13 months of video-based coaching; read together with the null dosage finding, this suggests (interpretation) that how hours are structured matters more than how many there are [2][6]. Funders should also expect effect sizes at scale to be smaller than demonstration results and should require evaluation designs with follow-up beyond the program year, since only five of 60 coaching studies reported such follow-up [2].

TALIS data point to a system-level gap between what teachers value and what is offered: teachers identified collaborative learning as one of the most impactful aspects of training, yet only 44 percent took part in peer-learning and networking training [10]. Separately, teacher turnover diluted dosage in the IES mathematics trial [3]. Interpretation: retention and stable teaching teams are preconditions for the cumulative learning that most models assume, and systems that want more collaborative learning must create time and incentives for it, since schedule conflicts and lack of incentives were the two most commonly reported barriers [10].

### For evaluators and researchers

Interpretation: evaluation of professional learning should measure more than one link in the chain and should look beyond the program year. The results above show that programs can move teacher practice without moving student scores (first-year reading PD), show student-score gains in a subsample without a first-year change in practice (induction in its third year), or show student effects only after the coaching ends (My Teaching Partner) [3][4][6]. A study that measures only one outcome at one time point can therefore misclassify a program. Pre-registration and attrition reporting matter because Sims and colleagues found markedly smaller effects in pre-registered experiments and in experiments with high attrition [9]. Implementation data, including dosage actually received, turnover, and fidelity, belong in every report, because the IES mathematics trial's null result cannot be interpreted without knowing that only 68 percent of the intended dosage reached the average teacher [3].

### For teachers and mentors

For an individual teacher, the evidence suggests selecting learning opportunities that include observation of practice with specific feedback, a chance to rehearse a technique before using it, and a plan for embedding it, rather than relying on exposure to ideas [9]. Mentors and coaches can use Shulman's distinction: help novices build a repertoire of representations for the topics they teach and of student misconceptions to expect, and discuss cases where principles conflict instead of prescribing a single rule [1].

## Criticisms

The evidence base has recognized limits. Kraft and colleagues note that much of the coaching evidence comes from literacy programs for prekindergarten and elementary teachers in the United States, that the link between better instruction and higher achievement is not causally identified in the pooled data, and that publication bias may explain part of the difference between small and large programs, although many large trials were reported regardless of significance [2]. The Learning Policy Institute review selected studies that found a positive link between PD, practice, and outcomes; its authors located six strong studies that found no impact on student learning but state that they cannot comment on studies of PD without positive results, and that the review cannot isolate individual components [8]. Sims and colleagues report that associations between mechanisms and impact are between-study associations, that evidence for balanced designs is statistically uncertain, and that effect sizes in pre-registered and low-attrition experiments are much lower, which suggests inflation in some published estimates [9]. The positive third-year induction result depended on a subsample of teachers with prior-year scores [4]. Several of the positive trials were small or used volunteers: 14 schools in one district for peer pairing, volunteer teams for lesson study with control teams studying other topics, and one district for evaluation [5][7][12]. In the My Teaching Partner trial the achievement effect appeared only in the year after the program, and the authors noted that teachers selected their focal class in that year [6]. The teacher knowledge and preparation findings are observational and rest on measures that the authors themselves flagged as imperfectly aligned or unvalidated [11][13].
