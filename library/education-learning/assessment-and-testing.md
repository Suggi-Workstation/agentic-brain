# Assessment and Testing -- What We Measure Shapes What We Learn, and Most Tests Measure the Wrong Things

Assessment is not a neutral measurement tool. It is the single most
powerful lever for shaping student behavior, curriculum design, and
institutional priorities in education. The backwash effect -- the
principle that assessment drives learning more than any curriculum
document -- means that what we choose to test determines what students
choose to learn. Yet the majority of assessments, from classroom
quizzes to high-stakes standardized exams, test recall of facts rather
than understanding, application, or synthesis. This topic examines the
science of educational measurement: how validity, reliability, and
assessment design interact, why different formats measure different
things, and what emerging technologies and credentialing models mean
for the future of testing.

## Background

Formal educational assessment has roots in ancient China, where the
imperial examination system (established during the Sui dynasty, c.
605 CE) used standardized written tests to select civil servants based
on merit rather than birth. This system, which lasted over 1,300 years,
was the world's first large-scale standardized testing program and
established the core idea that a written examination could measure
competence independently of social status. It directly influenced the
development of civil service examinations in 19th-century Britain and,
through them, modern educational testing.

The scientific study of assessment began in the late 19th and early
20th centuries. Francis Galton's work on individual differences (1880s)
and James McKeen Cattell's "mental tests" (1890) laid groundwork for
psychometrics. Alfred Binet's 1905 intelligence scale, developed to
identify French schoolchildren needing remedial education, introduced
the concept of age-normed scoring. This evolved into the Stanford-Binet
Intelligence Scales (1916) under Lewis Terman at Stanford, which
produced the intelligence quotient (IQ) metric.

The First World War accelerated testing's reach: the U.S. Army Alpha
and Beta tests (1917-1918), developed by a committee led by Robert
Yerkes, administered intelligence tests to nearly two million recruits
to classify them by mental ability. After the war, group-administered
standardized testing entered American education at scale. The College
Board's Scholastic Aptitude Test (SAT), first administered in 1926,
adapted Army testing techniques for college admissions, and by the
postwar era standardized testing had become embedded in educational
systems worldwide.

The modern period produced two major developments. First, Benjamin
Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (1956) provided a
hierarchical framework organizing cognitive skills from simple recall
(Knowledge) through comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis,
and evaluation. The 2001 revision by Anderson and Krathwohl recast
these as Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create --
with the crucial insight that most assessments test only the bottom two
levels. Second, the accountability movement of the 1980s-2000s,
culminating in the U.S. No Child Left Behind Act (2001), tied
standardized test results to school funding, teacher evaluation, and
institutional survival -- dramatically increasing the stakes of
assessment and, critics argue, narrowing the curriculum to what could
be tested.

## Core Concepts

### Validity: Does the Test Measure What It Claims to Measure?

Validity is the central concept in educational measurement. It is not a
property of a test but of the inferences drawn from test scores. A test
may be valid for one purpose and invalid for another. The modern
unitary concept of validity, articulated by Samuel Messick (1989),
integrates multiple forms of evidence into a single argument that test
score interpretations are justified.

Content validity addresses whether test items adequately sample the
domain being measured. A driving test that only assessed knowledge of
traffic signs but never required actual driving would lack content
validity. Construct validity -- the most fundamental form -- concerns
whether the test actually measures the theoretical construct it claims
to measure. Does an IQ test measure general intelligence, or does it
measure a narrower combination of pattern recognition, vocabulary, and
test-taking skill? Criterion-related validity examines how well test
scores predict real-world outcomes: the predictive validity of the SAT
for first-year college GPA, for instance. Face validity, the weakest
form, simply asks whether a test appears to measure what it claims -- a
consideration that matters for test-taker motivation and public trust
even though it provides no substantive evidence about actual validity.

A critical insight from the measurement literature is that validity is
always context-dependent. Loken and Gelman (2017) demonstrated that
measurement error does not simply attenuate effect sizes -- a common
assumption in educational research. In noisy, small-sample studies,
measurement error can actually inflate observed effects through
selection bias, contributing directly to the replication crisis. The
implication is that validity evidence gathered from one population
or setting may fail to generalize, and researchers who assume that
"true" effects are always larger than observed ones are frequently
wrong.

### Reliability: Does the Test Produce Consistent Results?

Reliability concerns the consistency of measurement. A reliable test
produces similar results under similar conditions. The classical
framework identifies several forms: test-retest reliability (the same
test administered twice yields similar scores), internal consistency
(individual items within a test correlate with each other), inter-rater
reliability (different scorers assign similar marks to the same
response), and parallel-forms reliability (different versions of the
same test produce equivalent scores).

Reliability sets an upper bound on validity: an unreliable test cannot
be valid, because scores that fluctuate randomly cannot carry meaningful
information about the construct being measured. However, a perfectly
reliable test can still be perfectly invalid -- a test that consistently
measures the wrong thing is reliably wrong.

Multiple-choice tests achieve high reliability because scoring is
objective and items can be extensively piloted and calibrated. This is
their great strength and the reason standardized testing programs rely
on them so heavily. Essay and performance assessments trade reliability
for depth: they can assess higher-order thinking that multiple-choice
items miss, but inter-rater reliability is harder to achieve.
Well-designed scoring rubrics and rater training can bring inter-rater
reliability above 0.80, but this requires substantially more resources
than machine-scored formats.

### The Backwash Effect: Assessment Drives Learning

The backwash effect (also called washback) is the phenomenon whereby
assessment shapes what and how students learn, often more powerfully
than the stated curriculum. The concept was articulated by Lewis Elton
(1987) and developed by John Biggs and Catherine Tang (2011), who
argued that assessment, not the syllabus, is the de facto curriculum
from the student's perspective.

Negative backwash occurs when assessment design encourages surface
learning. In an exam-dominated system where multiple-choice tests of
factual recall determine grades, students rationally allocate their
effort to memorization rather than understanding. The strategy of
learning becomes more important than the substance. Griffin (2024)
documented precisely this pattern in higher education: when assessment
consisted of multiple-choice exams, student effort clustered around
exam dates and produced surface learning, with students unable to apply
concepts in other courses. When the same module was redesigned to
include portfolios and performance tasks alongside traditional exams,
students demonstrated deep learning -- they critiqued theories, made
connections across courses, and applied knowledge to practice.

Positive backwash harnesses assessment deliberately. When assessments
require analysis, synthesis, and application, students adapt their
learning strategies accordingly. The key design principle is
constructive alignment (Biggs & Tang, 2011): learning objectives,
teaching activities, and assessment tasks must all target the same
cognitive levels. If the objective is "students will critically
evaluate competing theories," but the assessment is a multiple-choice
test, the system is misaligned and students will not develop critical
evaluation skills regardless of what the syllabus says.

### Formative and Summative Assessment

Summative assessment evaluates learning at the end of an instructional
unit -- the final exam, the term paper, the standardized test used for
college admission. Its purpose is judgment: what did the student learn,
and to what standard? Formative assessment occurs during instruction
and its purpose is improvement: what does the student currently
understand, what gaps exist, and what should the teacher and student do
next?

The research on formative assessment is among the most robust in all of
education. Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam's (1998) landmark review
demonstrated that formative assessment practices -- such as diagnostic
questioning, feedback without grades, peer assessment, and student
self-assessment -- produced some of the largest effect sizes ever
measured in educational research, particularly for lower-achieving
students. The mechanism is straightforward: formative assessment
provides the learner and teacher with actionable information at a point
when it can still change the outcome, whereas summative assessment
provides information when it is too late to act.

Despite the evidence, formative assessment remains underused relative
to its demonstrated effectiveness. Several structural factors work
against it: class sizes that make individual diagnostic interaction
difficult, accountability systems that privilege summative scores, and
the fact that formative assessment is harder to standardize and scale
than summative testing.

### Major Assessment Formats and What They Actually Measure

Multiple-choice tests are the dominant format in large-scale assessment
because they are efficient, objective, and statistically well-understood.
They excel at testing factual recall and basic comprehension. They are
poor at measuring the ability to construct arguments, synthesize
multiple sources, or produce original explanations. A well-designed
multiple-choice item can target higher cognitive levels -- asking
students to analyze a scenario and select the best interpretation --
but designing such items requires expertise that most classroom
teachers do not have.

Essay assessments can measure analysis, argumentation, synthesis, and
written expression. Their weakness is reliability: different raters
assign different scores to the same essay. Rubrics improve consistency
but do not eliminate rater effects. The tension between depth and
reliability is inherent: the richer the response, the harder it is to
score objectively.

Performance assessments ask students to perform a task that mirrors
real-world application: conducting a science experiment, delivering a
presentation, writing a research paper, diagnosing a patient. They
offer the highest face validity and, when well-designed, strong
construct validity for complex skills. They also require the most time
to administer and score, and reliability can be difficult to achieve.

Portfolios collect evidence of learning over time, providing a richer
picture than any single test. They are well-suited to assessing growth
and process. Their challenge is standardization: when every portfolio
is different, comparisons across students or programs become
problematic. Portfolios also impose heavy scoring burdens and require
clear rubrics to achieve acceptable reliability.

Standardized tests (SAT, ACT, state assessments) provide a common
yardstick for comparing students from different schools and
backgrounds. This is their core argument: a 1300 on the SAT represents
the same measured performance regardless of where the student went to
high school. Grade inflation makes this argument stronger -- a 2024
ACT study found that average high school GPA has risen to 3.36 while
test scores have remained flat or declined, suggesting that grades are
becoming less comparable across schools. The counterargument is that
standardized tests measure socioeconomic advantage as much as academic
ability: access to test preparation, familiarity with the format, and
reduced test anxiety all correlate with family resources. Research on
colleges that eliminated test requirements found only a 0.05 GPA
difference in college performance between test-optional and
test-requiring institutions, raising questions about the incremental
predictive value of standardized scores beyond high school grades.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

Black and Wiliam's (1998) synthesis of over 250 studies established the
empirical case for formative assessment. Effect sizes ranged from 0.4
to 0.7 standard deviations, with the largest effects for low-achieving
students. Subsequent meta-analyses have confirmed these findings,
though some researchers note that the original estimates may be
inflated by the difficulty of implementing formative assessment at
scale under rigorous experimental conditions.

The evidence on standardized testing's predictive validity is more
mixed. Research consistently finds that high school GPA is a stronger
predictor of college success than standardized test scores. A study of
850 U.S. colleges that adopted test-optional policies found minimal
differences in graduation rates and college GPA between submitters and
non-submitters of test scores, with a graduation rate difference of
only 0.6%. However, recent research has complicated the test-optional
narrative: some studies find that standardized scores add incremental
predictive power beyond grades, particularly for identifying
high-potential students from disadvantaged backgrounds who might
otherwise be overlooked due to grade inflation at less rigorous
schools.

The empirical literature on assessment format effectiveness reveals a
consistent pattern. Traditional assessments (multiple-choice,
short-answer) reliably measure lower-order cognitive skills but fail to
capture higher-order thinking. A 2022 study comparing authentic and
traditional assessments in higher education found that educators
acknowledged the limitations of traditional formats while continuing to
use them due to practical constraints: ease of construction, speed of
scoring, and the objectivity that protects against grade challenges.
Students in the same study showed no clear preference between formats
but appreciated the transparency of clear rubrics regardless of the
assessment type.

Loken and Gelman's (2017) work on measurement error and the replication
crisis has particular relevance for educational assessment research.
They demonstrated that in small studies -- which characterize much
educational research -- measurement error combined with selection for
statistical significance produces systematically inflated effect sizes.
This means that published findings about assessment interventions may
overstate their true effectiveness, and practitioners should interpret
single-study results cautiously, preferring meta-analytic estimates
where available.

The backwash effect has been empirically documented across educational
contexts. Griffin's (2024) study of an Irish undergraduate psychology
module provided direct evidence: when assessment shifted from
exam-based (remember and understand) to portfolio-based (apply,
analyze, evaluate), student work demonstrated deeper engagement with
the material, and students independently transferred concepts to other
courses -- an outcome the exam-based version never produced. Gibbs and
Simpson (2004) showed that student effort distribution tracks assessment
deadlines precisely, with engagement dropping sharply outside
assessment periods, confirming that assessment timing and design
directly control student behavior more than teaching quality.

## Implications

For educators: the single highest-leverage action is to align assessment
with learning objectives. If the goal is critical thinking, the
assessment must require critical thinking. If it is memorization of
facts, a multiple-choice exam will suffice -- but educators should be
honest about what they are actually measuring. The backwash effect
means that assessment reform is curriculum reform: change what you
test, and you change what students learn.

For educational institutions: accountability systems that rely
exclusively on standardized test scores produce predictable distortions.
Teaching to the test, curriculum narrowing, and the neglect of
untested subjects (arts, physical education, civic education) are not
bugs -- they are rational responses to the incentives the system
creates. Multiple measures -- combining standardized assessments,
performance tasks, and student portfolios -- reduce the risk of
gaming any single metric.

For test designers: the trade-off between reliability and depth is
fundamental and cannot be eliminated by better methodology. Every
assessment format has strengths and weaknesses. The goal is not to find
the perfect format but to use multiple formats that compensate for each
other's blind spots. A well-designed assessment system triangulates
evidence from different measurement approaches.

For students: understanding the backwash effect is a metacognitive
advantage. Students who recognize that they will study to the test can
choose to deliberately study beyond the test -- seeking deep
understanding even when the assessment only rewards surface recall.
This is the difference between earning a grade and acquiring durable
knowledge.

For the library system: assessment design principles apply directly to
how this knowledge base is structured. The quality gates (G1-G12) in
the library topic template are a form of assessment rubric. The
audit cycle -- write, audit, approve, reject -- is a formative
assessment process applied to knowledge artifacts. Understanding
validity and reliability in educational measurement provides a
framework for evaluating whether the library's own quality assurance
mechanisms measure what they claim to measure.

For self-directed learners: assessment is not just a tool that
institutions use on learners. Self-assessment -- the ability to
accurately judge one's own knowledge and identify gaps -- is one of
the highest-leverage metacognitive skills. Research shows that most
people are poor self-assessors: the Dunning-Kruger effect describes
the systematic tendency of low-performers to overestimate their
ability and high-performers to underestimate theirs. Calibration
exercises, where learners predict their performance and then compare
predictions to actual results, improve self-assessment accuracy over
time. In an era where formal credentials are being supplemented by
demonstrated competence, the ability to honestly evaluate what you
know -- and what you do not -- becomes a competitive advantage.

For assessment reform advocates: the history of assessment reform is
littered with innovations that showed promise in pilot studies but
failed at scale. The lesson is not that reform is futile but that
assessment systems are complex adaptive systems: changing one
component (the test format, for example) without changing the
incentives (accountability requirements, teacher training, resource
allocation) produces displacement rather than transformation. Lasting
assessment reform requires simultaneous changes to policy, practice,
and professional development -- an insight that applies as much to
institutional testing programs as to the design of the library system
that contains this topic.

## Emerging Trends and Controversies

### Skills-Based Credentialing and the Unbundling of Degrees

The rise of skills-based credentialing -- microcredentials, digital
badges, competency-based assessments -- challenges the monopoly of
the traditional degree as the primary signal of educational
achievement. Employers increasingly value demonstrated competencies
over institutional pedigree, and technology platforms enable continuous
skill verification rather than one-time certification at graduation.
The assessment challenge is enormous: designing valid, secure,
scalable assessments for thousands of discrete skills is a measurement
problem that no existing testing infrastructure is designed to solve.

### Automated Essay Scoring and AI in Assessment

Automated essay scoring (AES) uses natural language processing to
evaluate written responses. The field originated with Ellis Page's
Project Essay Grade (1964) and has advanced dramatically with deep
learning approaches. Modern AES systems achieve correlations with
human raters above 0.80 on structured writing tasks, and organizations
like ETS now use AI to assist with scoring high-stakes exams including
the GRE and TOEFL.

However, AES systems have known vulnerabilities. They can be gamed
by writing strategies that exploit the scoring algorithm (using
sophisticated vocabulary without coherent argument, for example). They
struggle with original writing that departs from trained patterns, and
they cannot reliably evaluate the truth of factual claims in source-based
writing. The fundamental question is not whether machines can score
essays -- they can -- but whether score prediction is the same as
evaluation. A system that predicts human scores with high accuracy
may still miss important dimensions of writing quality that matter
for learning.

### AI and the Changing Meaning of Assessment

Large language models capable of producing competent essays,
solving math problems, and generating computer code challenge the
premises of existing assessment. When an AI can produce a B-grade
essay in seconds, the essay as an assessment format loses validity --
the score reflects the student's ability to use AI tools at least as
much as their own understanding. This forces a fundamental
reconsideration: if a task can be automated, is it still worth
assessing?

The likely direction is a shift toward assessing processes rather than
products: evaluating how students arrive at answers rather than the
answers themselves, using oral examinations, collaborative
problem-solving observations, and iterative project work that reveals
thinking over time. But these formats are expensive, hard to scale,
and difficult to standardize -- the same trade-offs that have defined
the assessment field for a century.
