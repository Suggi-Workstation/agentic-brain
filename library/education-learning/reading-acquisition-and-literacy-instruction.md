# Reading Acquisition Requires Explicit Code Instruction and Deliberate Growth of Language, Knowledge, and Meaning

Learning to read requires learners to connect a writing system to spoken language, make word recognition increasingly accurate and automatic, and construct meaning from vocabulary, syntax, discourse, and relevant knowledge. Effective literacy instruction therefore teaches the alphabetic code explicitly while also building oral language, fluent text reading, comprehension processes, and knowledge; phonics is necessary for beginning reading in English, but it is not a complete literacy curriculum [1][2][3].

## Background

Reading acquisition has repeatedly been framed as a choice between teaching the written code and surrounding children with meaningful language and books. The long-running "reading wars" opposed approaches centered on explicit relations between letters and sounds to approaches that emphasized meaning, context, authentic text, and the learner's construction of language. A major tutorial review by Castles, Rastle, and Nation concluded that this opposition misstates the evidence: systematic phonics has a central role in initial alphabetic learning, while skilled reading also requires fluent word recognition and text comprehension grounded in language and knowledge [1]. The National Reading Panel similarly treated phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension as connected areas of instruction rather than as substitutes for one another [2].

The underlying problem is that print must become language before it can reliably carry meaning. In an alphabetic writing system, letters and letter combinations represent units of speech, although English mappings are more complex than one-letter-to-one-sound rules. Beginning learners must notice the sound structure of spoken words, connect phonemes with graphemes, blend printed sequences into pronunciations, and analyze spellings when they write. The Institute of Education Sciences practice guide rates evidence as strong for linking segments of speech to letters and for teaching learners to decode, analyze word parts, write, and recognize words [3]. These foundations are means to independent reading: they reduce dependence on pictures, sentence context, or another person supplying the word.

Word recognition then changes through experience. Ehri's account of orthographic mapping explains how connections among a word's spelling, pronunciation, and meaning are retained in memory. Phonemic awareness and grapheme-phoneme knowledge enable these mappings; with repeated successful encounters, familiar printed words can activate pronunciation and meaning rapidly rather than requiring laborious letter-by-letter analysis each time [6]. "Sight word" in this account does not mean a word memorized as an unanalyzed visual shape. It means a word whose written form has become securely bonded to language through knowledge of the writing system [6].

Reading comprehension introduces a second necessary constraint. Hoover and Gough's Simple View characterized reading comprehension through decoding and linguistic comprehension and tested the model longitudinally with English-Spanish bilingual children in grades 1 through 4 [4]. The model's practical force is clear: weak word recognition can block access to language, while accurate word reading cannot produce understanding when the reader does not understand the language or ideas expressed. Later research has shown that the two broad components overlap and are joined by processes including vocabulary, fluency, and morphological awareness. Duke and Cartwright's Active View also makes self-regulation, motivation, executive processes, text, and context visible rather than treating comprehension as the automatic result of two isolated capacities [5].

Development therefore changes the instructional bottleneck. Early in acquisition, phonological awareness, alphabetic knowledge, decoding, and spelling require concentrated teaching and practice. As words become recognizable with greater accuracy and automaticity, limitations in oral language, vocabulary, syntax, inference, discourse knowledge, and background knowledge increasingly constrain comprehension [1][5]. This is not a clean switch from "learning to read" to "reading to learn." Oral language and knowledge should grow before and during code instruction, and older learners may still need explicit work on multisyllabic decoding or fluency [1][13].

The development of literacy also depends on the language and writing system a learner already knows. The National Academies concluded that English learners need explicit literacy instruction while also developing oral English and gaining access to academic content; established knowledge and literacy in a home language can be resources rather than deficits [12]. A learner may decode English words accurately while still lacking enough English vocabulary, grammar, or cultural and topical knowledge to comprehend the text. Conversely, difficulty with an English phoneme, spelling pattern, or word may reflect the interaction between languages rather than a general inability to learn. Instruction and assessment must therefore distinguish code knowledge, oral-language proficiency, reading comprehension, prior literacy, and opportunity to learn [4][12].

This topic addresses literacy instruction, developmental progression, assessment, intervention, multilingual learning, and the selection of practice texts. It does not diagnose dyslexia or another disability, and it does not treat a low score as a diagnosis. Those questions require broader evaluation and are developed in the related topic on learning disabilities and differentiated instruction. The instructional objective here is narrower and more operational: identify which part of the path from print to language to meaning is limiting independent reading, teach that component explicitly, preserve access to meaningful language and knowledge, and verify transfer in unfamiliar text.

## Core Concepts

### Reading coordinates word recognition and meaning construction

Reading is not visual guessing, oral performance, or comprehension strategy use in isolation. A reader must identify written words accurately enough to activate their pronunciations and meanings, parse relationships within and across sentences, connect ideas with relevant knowledge, monitor coherence, and revise an interpretation when the text contradicts it. The Simple View usefully establishes that decoding and linguistic comprehension are both necessary, while the Active View adds bridging processes and active regulation that help explain why learners with apparently adequate scores in the two broad components can still struggle [4][5].

This coordination has an instructional consequence: performance should be decomposed without fragmenting the final goal. Isolated phoneme work, word lists, vocabulary lessons, and strategy demonstrations can each target a component, but competent reading requires their reintegration in connected text. The author's synthesis is that component instruction should answer two questions: which process is currently weak, and how will practice reconnect that process to independent meaning-making? A lesson that improves an isolated score but never changes text reading has not yet demonstrated transfer [3][5][13].

### Phonological awareness makes spoken structure available for print

Phonological awareness is the ability to attend to and manipulate the sound structure of spoken language, including larger units such as syllables and onsets and smaller units such as phonemes. Phonemic awareness concerns individual phonemes and can be practiced without print, but its instructional value for reading is realized when sounds are linked to letters. The National Reading Panel analyzed 52 phonemic-awareness studies yielding 96 treatment-control comparisons and reported effects that lasted beyond training and were limited to the targeted domain, supporting a causal instructional contribution rather than a general novelty effect [2].

Useful activities include blending a sequence of spoken phonemes into a word, segmenting a spoken word into phonemes, and changing one sound to produce another word. Instruction should not remain an extended oral routine after learners can perform the relevant operations. The IES guide specifically joins awareness of sound segments to letter knowledge, because alphabetic reading requires coordination of phonemes and graphemes [3]. The author's synthesis is that assessment should determine whether a learner cannot perceive or manipulate the sound sequence, does not know its spelling correspondences, or knows both separately but cannot coordinate them during word reading.

### Systematic phonics teaches a generative code

Phonics instruction teaches relations between graphemes and phonemes and how to use those relations in reading and spelling. "Systematic" means that correspondences and word structures follow a planned, cumulative sequence rather than appearing only when a story, error, or incidental word makes them convenient. "Explicit" means that the relation and the operation are made visible, modeled, practiced, checked, and reviewed. The aim is generative: learners should use a finite body of code knowledge to read unfamiliar words, not memorize every printed word as a separate visual object [1][2][3].

Decoding begins with applying known correspondences and blending sounds, then expands to common spelling patterns, syllable types, affixes, and analysis of multisyllabic words. Spelling or encoding is complementary because it requires learners to segment pronunciations and select graphemes rather than merely recognize an answer. The IES foundational guide combines decoding, word-part analysis, writing, and word recognition in one strong-evidence recommendation [3]. For students in grades 4 through 9 who still struggle, the later IES guide gives strong-evidence recommendations for teaching complex multisyllabic word reading rather than assuming that older age has removed the foundational need [13].

### Orthographic mapping turns decoded words into readily recognized words

Orthographic mapping is a memory process, not a separate classroom program. When a learner encounters a printed word, identifies its phonemes and graphemes, connects its spelling to a pronunciation, and links that form to meaning, the word can become more securely represented in memory. Ehri describes development from incomplete visual or partial alphabetic connections toward full grapheme-phoneme and later consolidated syllabic and morphemic connections [6]. This account explains why accurate decoding and spelling practice can build rapid word recognition rather than compete with it.

Repeated exposure matters, but repetition should reactivate the mapping. Asking a learner to look at and memorize a whole word can preserve an arbitrary visual cue without establishing why the letters represent that word. Reading the word, pronouncing it, spelling it, analyzing a difficult part, and using it in meaningful language provide connected routes among form, sound, and meaning [6]. The author's synthesis is that irregular high-frequency words should not be treated as unanalyzable pictures: regular correspondences can be identified, and the unexpected part can receive explicit attention.

### Fluency is accurate, increasingly effortless, and appropriately phrased reading

Fluency connects word recognition with comprehension by reducing the attention required to identify words and by expressing meaningful phrasing across connected text. Rate alone is incomplete. Accuracy prevents a fast sequence of substitutions, and prosody reflects phrasing, stress, and punctuation that can both reveal and support syntactic interpretation. Duke and Cartwright classify fluency as a bridge between word recognition and language comprehension rather than as a purely mechanical outcome [5].

Fluency-building should be purposeful. The IES grades 4-9 guide recommends activities that help students read effortlessly, while the National Reading Panel found that guided repeated oral reading with feedback had positive effects on word recognition, fluency, and comprehension [2][13]. Appropriate routines include a fluent model, supported reading, repeated reading of a manageable passage, error correction, attention to phrasing, and a new passage that tests transfer. A words-per-minute gain on the rehearsed passage is useful process evidence, but independent reading of an unfamiliar passage is stronger evidence that fluency has generalized.

### Vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and discourse make decoded language understandable

A pronunciation is not comprehension. Readers need breadth and depth of vocabulary, including multiple meanings and relations among words; they need morphology to interpret roots and affixes; syntax to follow how clauses and phrases express relations; and discourse knowledge to track reference, sequence, causality, contrast, and genre. These resources are developed through oral interaction, reading, explicit teaching, writing, and cumulative encounters in varied contexts [1][5][12].

Vocabulary instruction is strongest when learners actively use and relate words rather than copy definitions. Wright and Cervetti's review of 36 studies found that teaching word meanings usually improved comprehension of passages containing the taught words, especially when instruction included active processing, but evidence for generalized comprehension gains was limited [8]. That boundary prevents a common overclaim: teaching a list of words can make a related text more accessible without creating a general comprehension capacity. Wide reading, discussion, writing, and coherent knowledge-building are needed because no feasible list can preteach every future word.

Morphological instruction helps learners analyze meaningful word parts and can support both code and language. Goodwin and Ahn synthesized 92 standardized mean differences from 30 studies and found a moderate overall effect, with significant effects on morphological knowledge, phonological awareness, vocabulary, decoding, and spelling but not on reading comprehension or fluency [10]. Morphology should therefore be taught as one component of word analysis and vocabulary development, not advertised as a complete comprehension intervention.

### Comprehension depends on knowledge, inference, text structure, and monitoring

Comprehension is the construction of a coherent representation of what a text says, implies, and assumes. Readers connect propositions across sentences, resolve references, infer unstated relations, distinguish central from supporting ideas, identify text structure, and integrate the text with relevant knowledge. When word recognition becomes adequate, language and knowledge increasingly constrain what can be understood [1][5]. This is why comprehension instruction cannot be reduced to repeated practice of generic question types.

Strategies can make invisible processes available for guided practice. Learners can ask and answer questions, identify the gist, summarize, map causal or comparative structures, clarify an inconsistency, and monitor whether a passage remains coherent. The National Reading Panel concluded that combinations of comprehension techniques can support recall, question answering, question generation, and summarization when students use them appropriately [2]. Text-structure instruction also has positive effects, but the effect depends on the outcome and the opportunity for independent practice [9]. Strategies are scaffolds for constructing meaning; they should not consume so much instructional time that learners repeatedly practice a procedure on disconnected, knowledge-poor passages.

Background and content knowledge change comprehension because texts omit information that authors expect readers to supply and because knowledge supports vocabulary, inference, and organization. Hwang, Cabell, and Joyner's meta-analysis of 35 quasi-experimental or experimental studies found positive effects of integrated literacy and science or social-studies instruction on vocabulary, comprehension, and content knowledge, including a positive effect on standardized comprehension measures [7]. The instructional implication is cumulative: sequences of related texts, discussion, writing, and explicit vocabulary can build connected knowledge that later texts can recruit.

### Developmental progression is overlapping, not a rigid stage sequence

Learners do not complete phonics and then begin meaning. Young children can develop vocabulary, narrative language, syntax, concepts about print, and topical knowledge through conversation and read-alouds before they can decode independently. At the same time, they need direct opportunities to read words and connected text using the code they have been taught [3][14]. As accuracy grows, practice should broaden across spelling patterns, word parts, sentence structures, genres, and knowledge domains. Guidance should fade according to demonstrated performance, not a calendar.

Text selection should serve the immediate learning purpose. Controlled or decodable text can give beginners concentrated practice applying taught correspondences in connected language. Rich read-alouds and shared texts can expose learners to vocabulary, syntax, ideas, and genres beyond what they can yet decode. Increasingly varied independent texts test whether word recognition and comprehension transfer beyond a practiced pattern. The author's synthesis is that no single text type should perform every function: code-aligned text supports initial independent decoding, while teacher-supported and later independent complex text develops language and knowledge [1][3][14].

Practice should also preserve meaning. Learners can reread to improve accuracy and phrasing, but they should still discuss what the passage means. They can analyze a spelling pattern, but the word should reconnect to pronunciation and meaning. They can answer comprehension questions, but should also explain the evidence and the relation among ideas. The path from print to language to meaning becomes durable when component practice repeatedly returns to purposeful reading.

### Assessment identifies the bottleneck; it does not replace diagnosis

A useful literacy assessment system samples component skills and connected performance. Screening estimates risk efficiently. Diagnostic follow-up examines plausible sources of difficulty, such as phonemic awareness, grapheme-phoneme knowledge, decoding accuracy, multisyllabic word analysis, word-reading fluency, oral language, vocabulary, syntax, or comprehension. Progress monitoring asks whether the selected instruction changes the targeted performance. Outcome and transfer measures ask whether the learner reads unfamiliar text more independently [3][13].

Measures must be interpreted according to their purpose. Oral reading rate can flag a fluency problem but cannot by itself distinguish weak decoding, limited language, unfamiliar content, anxiety, or inattention. A comprehension score depends on the text, background knowledge, question format, decoding demand, and language of assessment. The related assessment topic develops these validity requirements in full. The author's synthesis is that an instructional hypothesis should be specific and revisable: teach the hypothesized bottleneck with sufficient intensity, monitor a sensitive measure, and change the method or target when a stable pattern contradicts the hypothesis.

### Multilingual reading joins literacy development with language development

Multilingual learners use the same broad architecture of code, language, and meaning, but the language attached to print may still be developing. Hoover and Gough tested the Simple View with English-Spanish bilingual children, and the National Academies review concluded that explicit literacy instruction remains relevant while oral English, academic language, and content access require dedicated support [4][12]. Home-language phonological, orthographic, morphological, vocabulary, and comprehension knowledge can transfer when the learner recognizes the relation, but transfer depends on the languages, writing systems, proficiency, and instructional opportunity.

Instruction should not interpret an English vocabulary or syntax gap as a decoding disorder, nor assume that accurate English word reading establishes English comprehension. Teachers can compare what a learner understands orally and in print, in English and where feasible in a stronger language; explicitly teach unfamiliar English sounds, spellings, word meanings, sentence forms, and discourse patterns; connect cognates and morphemes when valid; and maintain access to grade-appropriate ideas through explanation, visuals, discussion, and read-alouds [12]. The author's synthesis is that equity requires both access and instruction: language support without code instruction can preserve dependence on others, while code instruction without language and knowledge can produce accurate oral reading with weak understanding.

## Evidence

The evidence base supports a coordinated rather than one-method account of literacy. The National Reading Panel applied methodological criteria to research on component instruction. For phonemic awareness, 52 studies yielded 96 treatment-control comparisons, and the panel reported that the effects of instruction extended beyond the end of training [2]. Its phonics synthesis drew on 38 experiments and 66 treatment-control comparisons. Castles and colleagues report an overall standardized effect of 0.41, with a larger effect when instruction began early (0.55) than after first grade (0.27), and effects on decoding, spelling, and text comprehension [1]. These averages support early systematic instruction but do not specify one commercial sequence, an exact daily duration, or the optimal number of correspondences to teach at once; the National Reading Panel explicitly left several implementation questions open [2].

The IES foundational-skills guide translates a later review into graded recommendations. It rates evidence as strong for teaching awareness of sound segments linked to letters and for teaching decoding, word-part analysis, writing, and word recognition. It rates daily connected-text reading for accuracy, fluency, and comprehension as supported by moderate evidence, while its academic-language recommendation carries a lower evidence rating within that guide [3]. These grades concern the guide's defined evidence base and recommendation, not the intrinsic importance of language. Independent converging evidence in the broader literature supports oral language as a predictor and intervention target for later comprehension [1][5][12].

Evidence for fluent text reading is positive but bounded. The National Reading Panel included 16 guided-oral-reading studies in its meta-analysis and used another 21 qualifying studies in qualitative interpretation; it concluded that guided repeated oral reading with feedback had positive effects on word recognition, fluency, and comprehension [2]. Hudson and colleagues reviewed 16 studies published from 2000 through 2019 involving elementary students with reading difficulties. Most interventions used repeated reading, effects on rate and accuracy ranged from negligible to large, comprehension effects ranged from nonsignificant to large, and many studies emphasized speed more than prosody [11]. The review supports modeled, feedback-rich repeated reading while identifying uncertainty about alternative interventions, prosody, and sustainability [11].

Evidence on vocabulary illustrates the difference between proximal success and broad transfer. Wright and Cervetti reviewed 36 studies and found that teaching target-word meanings usually improved comprehension of passages containing those words; active processing was generally more useful than definitions or dictionary work alone. They found limited evidence that even extensive direct word instruction improves generalized comprehension [8]. Castles and colleagues report a compatible meta-analytic pattern: vocabulary instruction produced an effect of 0.50 on custom passages containing taught words but only about 0.10 on standardized comprehension measures [1]. The evidence supports explicit vocabulary teaching while rejecting the inference that isolated word lists alone build general reading comprehension.

Knowledge-building provides a complementary route. Hwang, Cabell, and Joyner synthesized 35 experimental or quasi-experimental studies of integrated elementary literacy and science or social-studies instruction. Random-effects estimates were positive for vocabulary (0.91), comprehension (0.40), and content knowledge (0.89); the standardized comprehension estimate was smaller but positive (0.25), while the standardized vocabulary estimate was not significant [7]. These differences across measures matter. Instruction predictably has its largest effects on the material and outcomes it directly teaches, but the standardized comprehension result indicates some transfer beyond researcher-made passages [7]. The authors also found no significant moderators, possibly because the number of studies limited statistical power [7].

Strategy and structure instruction can improve specific comprehension performances. The National Reading Panel reviewed text-comprehension research and favored combinations of techniques that support recall, questioning, and summarization [2]. Bogaerds-Hazenberg and colleagues synthesized 44 quasi-experimental or experimental studies of text-structure instruction in grades 4 through 6. Immediate effects varied by outcome: 0.25 for comprehension questions, 0.57 for summarization, 0.37 for recall, and 0.38 for text-structure knowledge [9]. The review excluded second-language learners and students with severe reading difficulties, and weak reporting of instructional features limited moderator interpretation [9]. Its positive results therefore support explicit structure instruction for the studied population without establishing a universal program for every learner.

Morphology supplies evidence for a bridge process. Goodwin and Ahn synthesized 92 standardized mean differences from 30 independent studies of English morphological instruction. The overall effect was 0.32, with significant effects on morphological knowledge, phonological awareness, vocabulary, decoding, and spelling. Effects on reading comprehension and fluency were not significant, and effects were larger in younger learners and in quasi-experimental or researcher-designed measures than in more rigorous or standardized comparisons [10]. The pattern supports teaching roots and affixes while warning that a component gain should not be promoted as automatic comprehension transfer.

The evidence also supports intervention beyond the primary grades. The 2022 IES grades 4-9 guide gives strong-evidence recommendations for complex multisyllabic decoding, purposeful fluency practice, and a routine set of comprehension-building practices: world and word knowledge, questioning, gist, and comprehension monitoring. It gives a moderate-evidence recommendation for supported practice with challenging text [13]. This directly contradicts the assumption that an older struggling reader should receive comprehension questions without assessment of word reading or, at the other extreme, phonics drills without meaningful text and knowledge.

Multilingual evidence requires separate attention because foundational reviews do not always represent multilingual learners adequately. The National Academies synthesis reports that instructional approaches used with English-proficient students can benefit English learners but are insufficient without attention to oral language development, academic language, and content access [12]. It also describes evidence and theory supporting use of established home-language knowledge and literacy, while noting that program effects depend on implementation and learner characteristics [12]. The instructional conclusion is not a separate science of reading for each language. It is that code instruction must be specific to the writing system and that assessment must separate English-language development from word-reading and comprehension difficulty.

Across the sources, the strongest convergence is architectural. Learners need explicit access to the code, repeated successful reading that builds automatic word recognition, language and knowledge sufficient to interpret what words and sentences mean, and instruction that helps them construct and monitor a coherent representation of text [1][2][3][5][13]. The evidence is weaker for exact recipes: one universal scope and sequence, a fixed decodability percentage, an ideal balance of time across components, or a single assessment that identifies every instructional need. The author's assessment is that this asymmetry should govern implementation: make the well-supported components non-negotiable, but make dosage, grouping, text mix, and intervention responsive to verified learner performance.

## Implications

### For classroom instruction

Plan literacy as a coordinated block rather than a contest among methods. Beginning readers need explicit work on phonological awareness linked to letters, a cumulative phonics and spelling sequence, and daily opportunities to apply taught patterns in connected text [3]. They also need oral language, read-alouds, discussion, writing, vocabulary, and content-rich experiences that exceed what they can yet read independently [1][7][14]. The two strands should interact: a word decoded during reading should connect to meaning, while a word learned orally should later become recognizable in print.

Use a gradual release tied to evidence. Model how to blend, analyze, spell, phrase, infer, summarize, or monitor; guide the learner through a similar task; then remove support and use a new item or passage. If performance collapses only when the prompt disappears, the learner has demonstrated assisted performance rather than independent reading. This distinction should determine whether to reteach, provide more cumulative practice, change the representation, or move to a harder text [3][13].

Select text by purpose. During code practice, choose connected text with enough taught patterns that the learner can apply the intended decoding process rather than guess. During language and knowledge building, use teacher read-alouds, shared reading, discussion, and supported complex text so limited independent decoding does not restrict access to ideas. As skill grows, widen independent reading across authors, genres, sentence structures, and topics. The author's synthesis is a three-part text diet: code-aligned practice, language- and knowledge-rich supported text, and increasingly varied independent text. No one category should crowd out the others [1][3][13][14].

Protect meaning during foundational work. After rereading a passage for accuracy and phrasing, ask what changed, why an event occurred, or which sentence supports an inference. After analyzing an affix, compare how it changes meaning across several words. After spelling a word, use it in a spoken and written sentence. These steps are not decorative comprehension activities; they prevent the instructional component from becoming detached from the reading system it is meant to support [5][6][10].

### For assessment and targeted intervention

Use a sequence of screening, diagnostic follow-up, targeted teaching, progress monitoring, and transfer checks. A broad screener identifies who may need attention but should not dictate a program by itself. Follow-up should locate the plausible constraint: sound awareness, letter-sound knowledge, blending, spelling, multisyllabic analysis, automatic word recognition, oral reading fluency, vocabulary, syntax, oral comprehension, knowledge, or comprehension monitoring. The selected intervention should match that profile and be monitored with a measure sensitive to the target [3][13].

Maintain a dual criterion for success. First, did the targeted component improve? Second, did independent reading of unfamiliar connected text become more accurate, fluent, or comprehensible? A learner can improve a rehearsed rate without transferring fluency, memorize taught vocabulary without improving general comprehension, or complete a phonics worksheet without decoding new words. The evidence on fluency, vocabulary, morphology, and text structure repeatedly shows why proximal and distal outcomes must be kept separate [8][9][10][11].

Increase intensity without narrowing the destination. Additional time, smaller groups, more explicit modeling, cumulative review, and more immediate feedback can help a learner with persistent difficulty. Yet intervention should preserve access to grade-appropriate ideas, oral language, and meaningful text. Older learners who need decoding instruction also need vocabulary, knowledge, comprehension, and dignified age-appropriate materials [13]. This is an instructional principle, not a disability diagnosis; a learner whose progress remains weak under well-implemented instruction requires broader multidisciplinary evaluation, as described in the related learning-disabilities topic.

### For multilingual learners

Assess language and literacy in relation to one another. Ask what the learner can understand and express orally in English, what literacy and knowledge exist in another language, how the writing systems correspond, and whether the learner has had adequate instruction in the tested pattern. An English comprehension error may arise from vocabulary, syntax, cultural or topical knowledge, decoding, or several constraints together. A nonword or word-reading task can isolate some code knowledge, but it cannot represent the learner's full literacy profile [4][12].

Teach English code and English language concurrently. Explicitly teach relevant English phonemes, graphemes, spelling patterns, vocabulary, morphology, syntax, and discourse; provide repeated opportunities to listen, speak, read, and write; and connect home-language knowledge when the connection is valid. Do not delay meaningful content until English decoding is perfect, and do not assume that exposure to meaningful English will independently produce knowledge of its writing system. The National Academies evidence supports dedicated oral-language development and access to academic content alongside literacy instruction [12].

Use multilingualism as information, not as a confound to ignore. Cognates, shared morphological roots, narrative knowledge, comprehension strategies, and literacy routines may transfer, while unfamiliar phonemes, scripts, word orders, or spelling conventions may require direct contrast. Instruction should make such relations explicit rather than expect spontaneous transfer. Progress should be interpreted against both instructional opportunity and English-language development so that difference is not mislabeled as disorder and genuine reading difficulty is not dismissed as language learning [12].

### For curriculum and school leadership

Build a cumulative literacy curriculum. Early sequences should coordinate phonological awareness, phonics, spelling, connected reading, oral language, and writing. Later sequences should retain attention to complex word reading and fluency while systematically expanding morphology, academic vocabulary, sentence comprehension, text structure, content knowledge, and independent reading across disciplines [3][7][10][13]. Curriculum maps should show when knowledge and patterns recur, how text demands increase, and where teachers check prerequisites rather than merely list weekly topics.

Invest in implementation, not labels. A program called "structured," "balanced," "knowledge-rich," or "science-based" can still fail if its sequence is incoherent, teachers cannot diagnose errors, practice does not match instruction, language development is neglected, or assessment rewards only immediate performance. Castles and colleagues identify a gap between reading research and classroom knowledge, while the IES guides present recommendations as decision tools rather than as cookbooks [1][3][13]. Professional learning should therefore include the structure of the writing system, oral language and comprehension, analysis of learner errors, modeled lessons, observation, feedback, and review of transfer data.

Audit time and opportunity. Count not only minutes assigned to literacy but what learners do during them: listen to and discuss rich language, analyze and spell words, read connected text, receive corrective feedback, build knowledge, write about ideas, and read independently. A nominal reading block can be consumed by worksheets, transitions, guessing from pictures, or teacher explanation that leaves learners with little successful reading. The author's synthesis is that instructional quality is visible in the distribution of cognitive work and in later unaided performance, not in the adopted program's label.

### For families and learners

Reading together should combine access and independence. Adults can read texts beyond a child's decoding level, discuss vocabulary and ideas, and invite predictions or explanations. Children should also read text they can substantially decode, receive concise help on difficult words, and reread selected passages for accuracy and expression. Correcting every error instantly can remove productive effort, while allowing repeated guessing can strengthen an unhelpful strategy. The smallest useful prompt is one that directs the learner back to the word's letters, sounds, parts, sentence, and meaning without doing all of the work [3][6].

Practice should produce evidence. Instead of asking whether a text felt easy, ask the learner to read a new passage, spell a related pattern, explain a sentence, retell the causal sequence, or define a word in a new context. Celebrate growth in accuracy, independence, vocabulary, and understanding rather than speed alone. The governing standard is transfer: the learner increasingly turns unfamiliar print into language and meaning without requiring the teacher, parent, picture, or memorized passage to supply the answer.

The worst instructional outcome is a false choice that denies one side of reading. Code instruction without language and knowledge can produce narrow accuracy with weak comprehension. Meaning-focused activity without explicit access to the code can leave learners dependent on context and adult support. A defensible literacy system prevents both failures by teaching each component according to developmental and assessed need, reconnecting the components in purposeful text, and checking whether the learner can read and understand something genuinely new [1][3][5][13].
