# Berkshire and American Express -- A Franchise Can Outlive a Balance-Sheet Crisis

Warren Buffett's investments in American Express illustrate a repeatable distinction: a company can suffer a large, measurable financial loss while its customer franchise remains intact. Buffett first applied that distinction after the 1963 salad-oil fraud and Berkshire later used convertible preferred stock, common-stock purchases, and long holding periods to build an American Express position that cost about $1.3 billion by 1995 and remained a major holding decades later [2][5][8].

## Background

American Express began in 1850 as an express-delivery company. It introduced the Travelers Cheque in 1891, entered travel services in 1915, and launched its first payment card in 1958 [1][13]. The card business extended capabilities the company already possessed: a trusted brand, relationships with travelers and merchants, and systems for authorizing and settling payment claims. American Express later added revolving credit through the Optima Card in 1987, but charge cards, merchant acceptance, service, and travel remained important parts of its identity [2].

The event that drew Buffett's attention occurred in late 1963. American Express Warehousing, a subsidiary, had certified tanks of purported salad oil that were used as collateral for loans. The inventory did not exist in the represented quantity. American Express's 2024 annual report states that the subsidiary faced $144 million of loan liabilities, more than three times the parent company's capital, and that speculation surrounding the scandal caused the share price to lose more than half its value [2]. American Express chose to settle legal claims rather than attempt to isolate the losses at the subsidiary. That decision imposed a large immediate cost but defended the company's reputation for honoring obligations [2].

American Express's retrospective account says that a young Warren Buffett recognized that the core business remained profitable and sound and initiated a stake after the scandal [2]. Buffett later described the operating investigation behind that judgment. In Berkshire's 1997 shareholder letter, he recalled sending his friend Frank Olson to a Hertz location at Kennedy Airport in 1964. Olson found that customers continued to use American Express Travelers Cheques and the American Express Card. Buffett wrote that the company had been financially damaged but that its products were not affected [6]. The important evidence was not simply that the stock had fallen. It was that customer behavior had not fallen with it.

The Berkshire-era investment began under different circumstances. In 1991, Berkshire purchased $300 million of American Express convertible preferred stock carrying an 8.85% dividend. Berkshire's letter called the instrument "Percs" and explained that it would automatically convert after three years into common shares under a formula with a maximum of 12,244,898 shares [3]. The formula provided income while Berkshire waited and common-stock participation if the business improved. The preferred dividend equaled $26.55 million annually before conversion, a direct calculation from the stated principal and coupon.

The conversion did not produce an immediate windfall. American Express's share price was below $24.50 when the Percs converted in 1994, so Berkshire received the maximum 12,244,898 shares. At year-end 1994, Berkshire reported 27,759,400 American Express common shares, a 5.5% interest, at a total cost of $723.9 million and a market value of $818.9 million [4]. During 1995, Berkshire purchased another 21,697,500 shares, increasing the holding to 49,456,900 shares, or 10.0% of the company, at a total cost of $1.393 billion [5]. The incremental 1995 cost was $668.7 million, calculated from Berkshire's year-end cost tables.

By year-end 1998, Berkshire held 50,536,900 shares at a tax-basis cost of $1.470 billion and a market value of $5.180 billion [7]. Buffett reported that the holding had been increased only slightly during 1998. In the 2022 shareholder letter, he summarized the longer arc: Berkshire's American Express purchases were essentially complete in 1995 at a cost of about $1.3 billion; annual dividends had risen from $41 million to $302 million; and the holding was then recorded at $22 billion [8]. This history separates the case into two episodes with one analytical method: Buffett Partnership bought after a reputational crisis in the 1960s, while Berkshire accumulated a much larger position through preferred and common stock in the 1990s.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Separate a finite liability from an impaired franchise

The first question in a crisis is not whether the loss is frightening. It is whether the loss can be bounded and whether the event changed the behavior that generates future cash flows. American Express's $144 million warehousing exposure was enormous relative to its capital, but the core test was whether customers and merchants had stopped trusting the Travelers Cheque and card products [2][6]. Frank Olson's observation suggested that they had not. Buffett therefore distinguished damage to the balance sheet from damage to the product franchise.

This distinction is not permission to dismiss liabilities. A finite loss can still exhaust capital, trigger regulation, or force dilutive financing. The analyst must estimate the maximum credible liability, the timing of payments, available liquidity, and second-order effects. Only after that work can the franchise be valued separately. In this case, American Express chose to settle claims, preserving the institution's promise to customers and counterparties at significant financial cost [2]. The author's synthesis is that the payment itself was both a liability resolution and an investment in brand credibility.

### 2. Treat brand as observed behavior, not an adjective

Calling a brand "strong" is not evidence. The relevant evidence is what customers, merchants, and partners do when confidence is tested. Buffett's account of the 1964 investigation focused on continuing product use, not surveys or slogans [6]. A payment franchise has value when customers keep presenting its instruments, merchants keep accepting them, and counterparties believe settlement will occur.

American Express's history also shows why reputation had economic content. The company moved from express delivery to travelers cheques, travel, and payment cards over more than a century [1][2][13]. Each transition depended on customers accepting an American Express promise in place of immediate cash or physical delivery. The author's assessment is that the scandal threatened precisely this accumulated trust. Management's decision to meet claims mattered because refusing them could have protected short-term capital while weakening the asset that enabled the company to issue payment promises.

### 3. Use scuttlebutt to test the causal mechanism

Scuttlebutt is useful when it tests a specific hypothesis. The hypothesis here was: "The scandal is financially severe, but ordinary customers still trust the products." The observation at Hertz was relevant because a travel counter was a natural place to see Travelers Cheques and payment cards in use [6]. If customers had abandoned the instruments or clerks had refused them, the evidence would have contradicted the thesis.

The method should not be confused with collecting favorable anecdotes. A good field check is selected before the result is known, samples the behavior that drives economics, and includes a disconfirming outcome. For a modern payments company, analogous checks might examine acceptance, card usage, account retention, service failures, or partner economics. The author's synthesis is that scuttlebutt has the highest value when accounting statements cannot yet reveal whether an intangible asset has survived.

### 4. Match the security to the uncertainty

Berkshire's 1991 Percs investment combined a contractual return with conversion exposure. The $300 million principal earned 8.85%, or $26.55 million per year, while the conversion formula capped the common shares at 12,244,898 [3][4]. This structure did not eliminate business risk. It altered the path by which Berkshire bore it: the preferred dividend paid for waiting, while conversion preserved participation if American Express recovered.

The 1994 outcome is instructive because the security did not create an instant bargain. The stock price was below the conversion floor, so Berkshire received the maximum share count, and Buffett stated that the common shares received were worth only $246 million at conversion [4]. Berkshire nevertheless bought much more common stock in 1995 [5]. The sequence shows that downside protection and conviction are different things. A security can improve terms, but the investor still has to decide whether the underlying business deserves more capital after new evidence arrives.

### 5. Understand the integrated payments model without oversimplifying it

American Express operates card issuing, merchant acquiring, and a card network. Its 2025 annual report says these businesses connect participants across the commerce path and provide payment, financing, fraud-prevention, data, travel, and service capabilities [10]. This integration can create richer information and coordinated economics across card members and merchants. It also allows American Express to design rewards, service, underwriting, and merchant programs as parts of one system.

The common shorthand "closed loop" is directionally useful but incomplete. American Express also licenses third parties to issue cards and acquire merchants, and programs such as OptBlue let outside acquirers set pricing for smaller merchants [10]. At year-end 2025, American Express reported 86.6 million proprietary cards and 66.2 million cards issued by third parties [10]. The moat therefore does not arise from doing every activity internally. It arises from the combined network, brand, customer relationships, data, products, and partner economics. The author's assessment is that an analyst should map which relationships are direct and which are delegated before assigning value to integration.

### 6. Let a shrinking denominator compound ownership

A long-term owner benefits when an investee repurchases shares below intrinsic value, because the owner's percentage interest rises without another purchase. Buffett made this mechanism explicit in the 2022 letter: repurchases by American Express increased Berkshire's ownership "without any cost to us," while warning that overpaying for repurchases harms continuing shareholders [8].

Berkshire reported 50,536,900 American Express shares at year-end 1998 [7]. American Express subsequently completed a three-for-one stock split, making that count equivalent to 151,610,700 current shares [14]. Berkshire's 2025 annual report reported 151.6 million shares, representing 22.1% of American Express [9]. The near identity of those split-adjusted counts supports the conclusion that the increase in percentage ownership came principally from American Express reducing its outstanding shares, not from Berkshire continually adding stock. The result is economically powerful only when repurchases are made at sensible prices and the underlying franchise continues to grow.

### 7. Distinguish concentration supported by knowledge from concentration supported by confidence

Both American Express episodes involved meaningful concentration, but the relevant defense is not Buffett's reputation. The defense is the quality of the evidence: the liability was investigated, customer behavior was checked, security terms were understood, and later purchases were supported by operating and capital-allocation evidence [3][4][5][6]. Concentration magnifies errors as well as correct judgments.

A disciplined concentration decision therefore requires independent ways to be wrong. The warehousing liabilities could have exceeded estimates; brand usage could have deteriorated; the card model could have lost merchant acceptance; credit losses could have impaired capital; or management could have destroyed value through repurchases or partner terms. The author's synthesis is that position size should follow the narrowest margin of safety among these factors, not the most attractive part of the thesis.

### 8. Monitor the franchise with operating evidence

A permanent holding still needs a falsifiable monitoring system. For American Express, relevant measures include billed business, card growth, fee revenue, credit performance, merchant coverage, partner concentration, and regulatory capital. In 2025, the company reported $1.670 trillion of proprietary billed business, 152.8 million total cards in force, $9.993 billion of net card fees, and a 2.3% net write-off rate on Card Member loans and receivables [10]. These figures describe scale and current economics; none alone proves durability.

Concentrations require separate attention. The Delta cobrand portfolio represented about 13% of worldwide billed business and 21% of worldwide Card Member loans at year-end 2025 [10]. The Costco experience shows why partner economics matter: American Express said it could not renew the U.S. arrangement on terms that made economic sense [11], and a 2015 investor presentation identified Costco-related activity as about 9% of worldwide billings and 19% of worldwide loans [15]. The analytical lesson is not "never accept concentration." It is to measure the exposure, understand contract economics, and test whether the platform can replace lost volume.

## Evidence

### Case 1: The 1963 crisis did not stop product use

The evidence combines a primary corporate record with Buffett's contemporaneous operating logic. American Express's 2024 annual report quantifies the crisis: $144 million of warehousing loan liabilities, more than three times parent capital, and a stock-price decline of more than half [2]. It also records management's decision to settle claims. Berkshire's 1997 letter supplies the behavioral test: Frank Olson observed customers using American Express Travelers Cheques and cards at a Hertz counter in 1964, leading Buffett to conclude that the company was financially damaged but its products were intact [6].

The method is a before-and-after causal test. The fraud created a known financial shock. The observed outcome was continued customer use of the core products. That does not prove that every customer or merchant was unaffected, and it does not by itself value the liabilities. It does support the narrower claim that the operating franchise survived the initial reputational shock. The original investment case becomes intelligible only when both records are used together: the liability was real, and so was the continuing demand.

### Case 2: The 1991-1995 accumulation was staged, not a single conversion windfall

Berkshire's annual letters provide a dated audit trail. The 1991 letter records a $300 million preferred purchase at an 8.85% coupon and a conversion formula capped at 12,244,898 shares [3]. The 1994 letter records conversion at the maximum share count because the market price was below $24.50; it also reports 27,759,400 total shares, 5.5% ownership, $723.9 million of cost, and $818.9 million of market value at year-end [4]. The 1995 letter reports a further purchase of 21,697,500 shares, bringing the total to 49,456,900 shares, 10.0% ownership, and $1.393 billion of cost [5].

This sequence corrects two common simplifications. First, the preferred did not convert into 14 million shares; the contractual maximum was 12,244,898. Second, Berkshire's roughly $1.3 billion commitment was not created by the preferred alone. It combined earlier common stock, the converted preferred, and substantial 1995 purchases. The evidence shows iterative capital allocation: obtain favorable terms, observe the business, accept an initially modest conversion outcome, and add common stock when the long-term economics remain attractive.

### Case 3: Market value and dividends compounded after purchases slowed

At year-end 1998, Berkshire's 50,536,900 shares had a $1.470 billion tax-basis cost and a $5.180 billion market value, about 3.52 times cost [7]. Berkshire's 2022 letter then provides a longer holding-period comparison: purchases were essentially complete in 1995 at about $1.3 billion; annual dividends grew from $41 million to $302 million; and the holding was recorded at $22 billion at year-end 2022 [8].

This is not proof that any purchase price is justified for a high-quality company. It is evidence for a specific compounding mechanism. Operating progress increased dividends and market value, while investee repurchases raised Berkshire's ownership percentage. Berkshire's 2025 annual report reported 151.6 million shares and 22.1% ownership [9]. On a split-adjusted basis, the share count is effectively the same as the 1998 count, which isolates denominator shrinkage as the main reason ownership rose.

### Case 4: Current operating scale coexists with credit and partner risk

American Express's 2025 annual report gives the current operating test. Proprietary billed business reached $1.670 trillion, total cards in force reached 152.8 million, net card fees reached $9.993 billion, and the Card Member loan and receivables net write-off rate was 2.3% [10]. The same filing states that the Delta cobrand portfolio represented about 13% of worldwide billed business and 21% of worldwide Card Member loans [10]. The method is to read scale and concentration together rather than treating growth as a complete risk assessment.

The Costco exit is a historical stress test. American Express reported that it declined to renew the U.S. agreement on terms it considered uneconomic [11]; its 2015 investor presentation showed Costco-related exposure equal to about 9% of worldwide billings and 19% of worldwide loans [15]. The business lost a large partner but continued to operate and later reached new highs in billed business and fee revenue [10][11]. This supports resilience, but it does not eliminate the recurring risk that a large cobrand partner can renegotiate, leave, or worsen the economics.

### Case 5: Competition is evolving toward another integrated issuer-network model

Capital One completed its acquisition of Discover on May 18, 2025. Its announcement states that Discover-branded cards would continue and that the Discover, PULSE, and Diners Club International networks would join Capital One's offerings [12]. American Express's 2025 filing identifies Visa, Mastercard, China UnionPay, JCB, Discover, and Diners Club among network competitors and notes competition from issuers, wallets, payment services, and lenders [10].

The author's assessment is that the transaction matters because it joins a large issuer with network assets, making the competitive comparison with American Express more direct. It does not prove that Capital One can reproduce American Express's brand, customer mix, merchant relationships, or economics. It does create a concrete test for the thesis: monitor whether Discover network volume, merchant acceptance, rewards economics, and Capital One card migration weaken American Express's growth or pricing power.

The comparison also demonstrates why evidence must be dated. Capital One's closing announcement establishes ownership of the Discover, PULSE, and Diners Club International networks, while American Express's filing establishes its own current scale and competitive set [10][12]. Neither source yet establishes the long-run operating result of the combination. The appropriate method is prospective: record a baseline for network volume, acceptance, card migration, customer acquisition, credit performance, and merchant economics, then compare later filings with that baseline. The finding as of the review date is a changed competitive structure, not demonstrated erosion of the American Express franchise. That distinction prevents a current event from being converted into an unsupported forecast.

## Implications

### For investors: diagnose the object that is damaged

A falling share price says that expectations changed; it does not identify what changed. The American Express case suggests a four-part diagnosis. First, quantify the direct liability and financing need. Second, test whether customers and counterparties changed behavior. Third, identify whether the event weakens a durable asset such as trust, distribution, cost advantage, or network participation. Fourth, compare the surviving earning power with the total enterprise price. The first two steps are evidenced by the 1963 liability record and the 1964 field check [2][6]; the latter two are analytical applications.

This framework guards against opposite errors. One error is treating every scandal as temporary because a famous investor once profited from one. The other is assuming that a severe accounting or legal event necessarily destroys a franchise. The correct classification depends on the transmission mechanism. If customers leave, merchants refuse the product, regulators constrain the model, or funding disappears, the franchise may be impaired. If the loss is bounded and core behavior persists, price and value can diverge.

### For security selection: structure can create time, not truth

Berkshire's Percs reduced some timing risk through an 8.85% dividend and preserved common-stock participation through conversion [3]. The structure paid Berkshire while uncertainty resolved, but it did not make American Express successful. At conversion, the common shares received were worth less than the $300 million principal [4]. The investment worked because Berkshire continued to judge the business favorably and committed more capital at later prices [5].

The practical implication is to value the operating business and the security separately. For a convertible, calculate cash coupons, conversion ratios, floors, caps, call rights, maturity, and dilution. Then ask whether the common equity is worth owning if the protective features disappear. Attractive terms can improve expected value, but they cannot rescue a fundamentally wrong business thesis.

### For long-term owners: repurchases are a capital-allocation decision

The American Express holding demonstrates how a fixed share count can become a larger ownership percentage. Berkshire's 2022 letter attributes part of that increase to American Express repurchases and explicitly conditions their benefit on value-accretive prices [8]. Berkshire's 22.1% interest at year-end 2025, compared with 5.5% reported at year-end 1994, reflects both Berkshire's 1995 purchases and the subsequent reduction in American Express shares outstanding [4][5][9].

The investor should therefore monitor repurchases with the same discipline applied to acquisitions. Relevant questions include: Was the stock bought below a conservative estimate of intrinsic value? Did repurchases compete with required investment or regulatory capital? Were they funded with resilient cash flows rather than fragile leverage? Did equity compensation offset the reduction? The author's assessment is that repurchases deserve credit only for per-share value created, not dollars spent.

### For franchise analysis: integration is a system, not a label

American Express's issuer, acquirer, and network roles provide coordinated data and multiple revenue streams, but partner-issued cards and third-party acquiring are material parts of the system [10]. A useful moat analysis must therefore map the participants: card members, merchants, issuing partners, acquiring partners, cobrand partners, regulators, and funding providers. For each, identify why it joins, what it pays, what it can switch to, and who owns the data and service relationship.

This map prevents two mistakes. The first is assuming that integration automatically produces superior economics. Integration can also concentrate operational, regulatory, credit, and technology risk. The second is assuming that an open network lacks customer information or pricing power. Competitors can combine issuer data, merchant data, and network assets through contracts or acquisitions. Capital One's ownership of Discover's networks makes that possibility more concrete [12].

### For risk managers and boards: protecting trust can be economically rational

American Express's decision to settle warehousing claims despite the possibility of isolating them at a subsidiary illustrates a governance trade-off [2]. A board may preserve legal capital in the short run yet damage the institution's ability to make credible promises. In businesses built on payment, insurance, custody, deposits, or guarantees, counterparties price both legal enforceability and expected conduct.

The implication is not that every disputed claim should be paid. The decision should compare legal exposure, precedent, moral hazard, liquidity, and franchise effects. The author's assessment is that risk reports should include a "trust balance sheet": which promises customers believe extend beyond strict contract terms, what a refusal would signal, and how quickly behavior could change. This makes intangible franchise risk explicit rather than rhetorical.

### For monitoring: combine growth, credit, and concentration

American Express's 2025 figures show why a single metric is insufficient. High billed business and fee revenue indicate customer activity; the write-off rate describes realized credit pressure; the Delta exposure identifies partner concentration; and total versus proprietary cards distinguishes direct economics from network-partner scale [10]. These measures should be followed together because one can deteriorate while the others appear healthy.

A practical monitoring dashboard would include proprietary billed business, cards and accounts, net card fees, discount revenue, loan growth, delinquency and write-off rates, reserve coverage, merchant acceptance, partner concentration, funding mix, and capital ratios. Historical stress tests should include both the salad-oil liability and the Costco exit. The author's assessment is that a thesis should be revised when customer behavior or unit economics changes, not merely when the market quotation changes.

### For case-study readers: preserve chronology and denominator discipline

This case is often compressed into "Buffett bought American Express after the salad-oil scandal and held forever." The source record is more precise. Buffett Partnership initiated the first investment after the 1963 crisis; Berkshire's separate accumulation began with preferred stock in 1991; conversion occurred in 1994; major common purchases followed in 1995; and the share count was only slightly increased in 1998 [2][3][4][5][7]. Mixing those episodes obscures what information and terms were available at each decision date.

Denominators matter equally. A 22.1% ownership interest does not mean Berkshire bought 22.1% of today's company. Berkshire's current share count is approximately the split-adjusted equivalent of its 1998 holding, while American Express reduced the denominator [7][9]. The author's synthesis is that accurate case analysis requires a dated ledger of principal, security type, shares, cost, ownership percentage, dividends, and company shares outstanding. Without that ledger, a persuasive story can be numerically wrong.

## Common Pitfalls

1. **Equating price decline with franchise impairment.** Price is an output. Test liabilities, customer behavior, merchant acceptance, and funding separately [2][6].
2. **Treating one favorable observation as proof.** Scuttlebutt should test a pre-specified causal hypothesis and seek disconfirming evidence.
3. **Misstating the Percs.** Berkshire invested $300 million at 8.85%; the maximum conversion was 12,244,898 shares, not 14 million [3][4].
4. **Combining the 1960s and 1990s purchases.** They were different decisions by different investment entities under different conditions [2][3].
5. **Crediting all ownership growth to purchases.** Repurchases can increase a continuing owner's percentage without another share purchase [8][9].
6. **Calling the network completely closed.** American Express uses partner issuers and third-party acquirers in addition to proprietary relationships [10].
7. **Ignoring concentration because the franchise is strong.** Delta and the former Costco relationship show that partner exposure remains measurable and economically important [10][11][15].
8. **Using hindsight as the thesis.** Later returns validate neither an unsupported historical claim nor an undisciplined purchase price.
