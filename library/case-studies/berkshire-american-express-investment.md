# Berkshire Hathaway's American Express Investment -- Trust, Network Effects, and a Two-Stage Circle of Competence

Berkshire Hathaway's investment in American Express unfolded in two
distinct stages separated by three decades: a 1964 purchase made
during the salad oil scandal, and a much larger 1991-1998 building of
a position that has since grown to roughly 20 percent of the company
through share repurchases alone. The case demonstrates how prior
knowledge of a business can deepen a circle of competence without
turning familiarity into complacency, and how a closed-loop payment
network with entrenched brand trust can compound shareholder value
over generations. It is one of the longest-held equity positions in
Berkshire's history and a textbook study in distinguishing a
transient crisis from structural damage to a franchise.

## Background

American Express was founded in 1850 as an express mail and freight
forwarding company in Buffalo, New York. Over the following century
it built a reputation as one of the most trusted names in global
finance, pioneering travelers cheques in 1891 and launching its
first charge card in 1958. The card was deliberately priced one dollar
above Diners Club's five-dollar fee -- a signal of exclusivity rather
than a bid for market share through low prices. This decision set
the strategic DNA of the company: premium over ubiquity, membership
over transaction volume. By the early 1960s American Express was
synonymous with financial integrity worldwide. When President
Roosevelt closed the banks during the Great Depression, he exempted
American Express travelers cheques, effectively allowing them to
substitute as US currency. That reputation was the company's most
valuable asset -- and the reason a young Warren Buffett paid
attention when a scandal threatened to destroy it.

The first crisis arrived in November 1963. Anthony "Tino" De
Angelis, a commodities trader and owner of Allied Crude Vegetable
Oil, had been issuing fraudulent warehouse receipts for nonexistent
vegetable oil inventory. American Express had acquired a field
warehousing subsidiary (formerly Lawrence Warehouse, renamed
Lawrence American Field Warehousing Corp.) earlier that year, and
that subsidiary had guaranteed warehouse receipts for De Angelis.
The fraud was elaborate: tanks were filled with water with a layer
of oil floating on top to fool inspectors, hidden compartments
reduced the oil needed to fill tanks, and the same oil was shuttled
from tank to tank to be counted multiple times. When the scheme
collapsed, Allied Crude owed over 50 creditors more than 180 million
dollars in 1963 currency (roughly 1.89 billion in 2025 dollars).
American Express faced claims of approximately 150 million dollars
against its warehousing subsidiary -- a sum that dwarfed the
company's annual earnings of roughly 40 million dollars at the time.
The stock price fell by more than 50 percent.

Warren Buffett, then managing the Buffett Partnership, recognized
that the scandal affected a small subsidiary while the core charge
card and travelers cheque businesses remained intact. He
investigated by spending an evening at Ross's Steak House in Omaha,
observing whether customers continued using their American Express
cards. They did. Buffett concluded that the market was pricing
American Express as if its franchise were permanently impaired, when
the damage was confined to a subsidiary whose losses, while large,
were survivable. He invested approximately 13 to 20 million dollars
for roughly 5 percent of the company. The position grew to as much as
40 percent of the partnership's portfolio and was later sold for a
profit of roughly 20 million dollars. This was Buffett's first major
lesson in separating a temporary reputational crisis from structural
damage to a business franchise.

The second stage began nearly three decades later. By the late
1980s, American Express had been led astray by its chairman James D.
Robinson III, who attempted to build a full-service financial
conglomerate through the acquisition of Shearson Loeb Rhoades (a
brokerage), Lehman Brothers (investment banking), and other
businesses. This "diworsification" left the company with too much
debt, no strategic focus, and a credit card franchise losing market
share to Visa and other issuers. The stock price had been cut in
half. In 1991, American Express needed capital. Buffett provided 300
million dollars through a private placement of convertible preferred
stock with an 8.5 percent dividend and conversion rights at 33 to 37
dollars per share. The preferred was structured as PERCs (Premium
Equity Redemption Cumulative Stock) -- a hybrid instrument that
would automatically convert to common shares in August 1994 if the
stock did not reach the specified price range. This deal became the
template for Buffett's later capital infusions into companies like
Bank of America and Goldman Sachs during the 2008 financial crisis.

## Core Concepts

### Stage One: The Salad Oil Crisis as a Mispricing Opportunity

The 1963 salad oil scandal is a canonical example of market
overreaction to a contained problem. The fraud at Allied Crude
affected a warehouse subsidiary, not the charge card or travelers
cheque businesses that constituted American Express's true franchise.
Buffett's analytical contribution was not recognizing that a fraud
had occurred -- the entire market knew that -- but distinguishing
between the subsidiary's liability and the parent company's economic
moat. The warehouse receipts were guaranteed by a subsidiary, and
while the parent would ultimately bear the loss, the loss was finite.
The charge card business, by contrast, had no exposure to the
vegetable oil fraud. Buffett's steak house visit was not a random
anecdote; it was an empirical test of whether consumer behavior --
the ultimate driver of the franchise's value -- had changed. It had
not. The market had conflated a subsidiary's liability with a
franchise's impairment, creating a gap between price and value that
Buffett exploited. He bought 5 percent of the company for 13 to 20
million dollars, implying a total enterprise value of roughly 150
million dollars for a company whose brand was, as Buffett later
noted, "synonymous with financial integrity and money substitutes
around the world."

### Stage Two: The 1991 Preferred and the Conversion Decision

Buffett's 1991 investment was structurally different from the 1964
purchase. In 1964, he bought common stock at a distressed price. In
1991, he provided capital to a company that needed it, in exchange
for a preferred instrument with a fixed dividend and conversion
optionality. The 8.5 percent dividend yielded 25.5 million dollars
annually, providing a return even if the common stock did not
appreciate. The conversion range of 33 to 37 dollars gave Buffett
upside if American Express recovered. The PERCs structure included
an automatic conversion mechanism: if the stock did not reach the
conversion range by August 1994, the preferred would convert to 14
million common shares at a preset ratio. This meant Buffett would
receive common stock regardless of the share price at conversion
time. By August 1994, the stock had not reached the conversion range,
and the preferred converted to 14 million shares. The initial 300
million dollar investment had grown to 385 million dollars in value,
plus three annual dividend payments of 25.5 million dollars each --
a total return of roughly 64 percent over three years, even before
the common stock position was established.

### The Frank Olson Golf Game and the Power of Industry Expertise

The conversion in August 1994 presented Buffett with a decision: hold
or sell the 14 million common shares. He was initially inclined to
sell. American Express faced "relentless competition from a multitude
of card issuers, led by Visa," and Buffett was uncertain whether the
company's franchise could withstand that pressure. The pivotal
moment came during a golf game at Prouts Neck, Maine, with Frank
Olson, the CEO of Hertz. Olson was, in Buffett's words, "a brilliant
manager with intimate knowledge of the card business." Buffett spent
the round quizzing Olson about the industry. By the second green,
Olson had convinced Buffett that American Express's corporate card
business was a "terrific franchise." On the back nine, Buffett turned
from seller to buyer. Within months, Berkshire owned 10 percent of
the company. Buffett later described this as an example of learning
from other people to complement what he learned from reading: "I
couldn't have talked to a better guy than Frank Olson. He could give
me an answer in five minutes that would be better than I could
accomplish in hours and hours of hours or weeks of roaming around and
doing other things." This episode illustrates a critical nuance of
the circle of competence concept: competence is not built solely by
reading and independent analysis. It is also built by identifying the
right expert and asking the right questions. Buffett's prior
knowledge of American Express from the 1960s gave him the context to
evaluate Olson's insights; Olson's industry expertise filled the gap
that Buffett's own analysis could not close.

### The Closed-Loop Network as a Structural Moat

American Express's competitive advantage rests on a closed-loop
payment network. Unlike Visa and Mastercard, which operate open
four-party models (cardholder, issuing bank, merchant, acquiring
bank, with the network as a thin rail in between), American Express
in its proprietary US business acts as its own issuer, network, and
merchant acquirer simultaneously. This three-party closed-loop
structure gives American Express three structural advantages that
Visa and Mastercard cannot replicate without rebuilding their
architecture. First, American Express sees both sides of every
transaction -- it knows what its cardholders buy, where, and how
much -- producing a proprietary spending dataset that no open
network can match. Second, because it owns the cardholder
relationship, it can charge merchants a premium to reach its
affluent customer base; that premium funds richer rewards, which
attract more affluent cardholders, creating a self-reinforcing
flywheel. Third, because it controls both ends of the loop, it can
build proprietary risk models on combined cardholder and merchant
data, resulting in fraud loss rates significantly below industry
averages. The closed loop is not merely a cost structure; it is an
information advantage that compounds with every transaction. This is
the structural feature Buffett recognized -- first as a diner
counting green cards in 1964, then as an investor listening to Frank
Olson explain the corporate card franchise in 1994.

### Brand Trust as an Irreplaceable Asset

Buffett has said of American Express: "You can't create another
American Express. I could create another shoe store. I could create
another business publication. I could do all kinds of things with
hundreds of billions of dollars. But I can't put in the minds of
people what is in their minds about American Express." This
statement captures a core principle of value investing: some assets
are not reproducible regardless of capital available. American
Express's brand trust was built over more than a century of
guaranteeing travelers cheques and payment integrity. A competitor
can replicate the closed-loop architecture -- as Capital One
demonstrated by acquiring Discover in 2025 for 35.3 billion dollars
-- but it cannot replicate the century of conditioned consumer and
merchant behavior that makes the loop valuable. The brand is the
entry barrier; the closed loop is the mechanism that monetizes it.
Buffett's 1964 steak house visit tested whether this brand trust had
survived the salad oil scandal. It had. His 1994 decision to hold
and buy more tested whether the brand could survive the
conglomerate era's mismanagement and Visa's competitive pressure.
Frank Olson's analysis suggested it could. The subsequent three
decades of results confirmed it.

### Share Repurchases and the Compounding of an Idle Stake

Berkshire Hathaway has not purchased American Express stock since the
late 1990s. Yet its ownership stake has grown from approximately 10
percent in 1995 to roughly 20 percent by 2022, and continued upward
thereafter. This occurred entirely through American Express share
repurchases. When a company buys back its own stock, each remaining
shareholder's percentage ownership increases without the
shareholder spending a dollar. American Express's consistent
repurchase program -- including a 4 billion dollar authorization in
2012 and subsequent multi-billion dollar programs -- steadily
reduced the share count, mechanically increasing Berkshire's stake.
Buffett noted in his annual letter that his ownership interest in
American Express was "likely to increase in the future," a direct
reference to this dynamic. This is the same mechanism that drove
Berkshire's Apple position upward, and it illustrates a broader
principle: when a value investor identifies a business with durable
competitive advantages and shareholder-oriented management, the
investor's primary task after the initial purchase may be to do
nothing. The business compounds value on its own, and repurchases
concentrate the investor's stake for free. By 2025, Berkshire's
American Express position, originally costing approximately 1.3
billion dollars across the 1991-1998 period, was worth approximately
56 billion dollars, with American Express paying Berkshire hundreds
of millions in dividends annually.

### The Two-Stage Circle of Competence Pattern

The American Express case demonstrates a pattern that recurs across
Buffett's career: a business first enters the circle of competence
through a small, early investment; years later, that prior knowledge
enables a much larger and better-informed commitment. In 1964,
Buffett learned American Express's franchise dynamics by observing
consumer behavior during a crisis. That knowledge did not expire.
When the 1991 opportunity arose, Buffett already understood the
brand's durability, the closed-loop economics, and the company's
institutional resilience. The 1964 investment was tuition; the 1991
investment was the return on that tuition. This pattern also
appeared with GEICO (a 1951 train ride visit led to a 1976
investment and eventually full ownership) and Coca-Cola (observed for
decades before the 1988 purchase). The lesson is that the circle of
competence is not static; it expands through experience, and
experience compounds when a business is observed across multiple
cycles and crises. The risk, however, is that familiarity breeds
complacency. Buffett mitigated this by seeking external expertise
(Frank Olson) at the critical decision point, rather than relying
solely on his own prior knowledge.

## Evidence

### The Salad Oil Scandal and the 1964 Purchase

The salad oil scandal of November 1963 is documented in detail by
Wikipedia's entry on the event, which records that De Angelis's
fraud cost creditors over 180 million dollars and that American
Express's warehouse subsidiary guaranteed receipts that proved
worthless. American Express's annual earnings at the time were
approximately 40 million dollars, meaning the subsidiary's potential
liability was nearly four times annual earnings -- a serious but
survivable shock for a company with a strong core franchise. The
stock fell by more than 50 percent. Buffett's response is documented
in multiple sources: he spent time at Ross's Steak House observing
whether cardholders continued to use their American Express cards
(they did), concluded the core business was unaffected, and invested
approximately 13 to 20 million dollars for roughly 5 percent of the
company. He later sold for a profit of approximately 20 million
dollars. The Fortune article "Warren Buffett changed his investing
strategy starting with American Express" (2024) identifies this
investment as a turning point in Buffett's evolution from
cigar-butt investing toward paying for quality franchises. The
investment also demonstrated Buffett's willingness to concentrate
heavily: the position reached as much as 40 percent of the Buffett
Partnership portfolio, hitting the partnership's self-imposed
concentration limit. The fundamental analytical insight -- separating
subsidiary liability from franchise value -- became a recurring
pattern in Buffett's approach to crisis-driven mispricing.

### The 1991 Preferred Investment and the 1994 Conversion

The 1991 preferred stock investment is documented by the New York
Times (February 15, 1995), which reported that Berkshire's initial
300 million dollar investment was made in August 1991 "when American
Express was floundering under its empire-building chairman, James D.
Robinson III, and badly needed capital." The instrument was
exchangeable preferred stock yielding 8.5 percent with conversion
rights at 33 to 37 dollars per share. When the stock did not reach
the conversion range by August 1994, the preferred automatically
converted to 14 million common shares. By that point, the
investment had grown to 385 million dollars in market value, plus
three annual dividend payments of 25.5 million dollars each -- a
total return of approximately 64 percent over three years. The
Forbes/GuruFocus analysis (2013) describes this investment as "a
prototype of more recent Bank of America and Goldman Sachs capital
infusions to ease distress." The structure -- fixed income with
conversion optionality, issued to a company in distress by an
investor who understands the franchise -- became a signature
Buffett transaction type. The New York Times also reported that by
December 31, 1994, Berkshire held 27.76 million shares, and by
February 1995, Buffett had purchased an additional 20.74 million
shares, bringing the total to 48.5 million shares or 9.8 percent of
the company. The SEC filing that disclosed this stake caused
American Express shares to surge, illustrating the signaling effect
of Buffett's endorsement.

### The Frank Olson Golf Game and the 1995-1998 Position Build

The Frank Olson golf game is recounted by Buffett himself in
Berkshire Hathaway's 1997 annual letter, as documented by the
Investment Masters Class Buffett Series and Marc Rubinstein's Net
Interest newsletter. Buffett wrote: "During that month of decision, I
played golf at Prouts Neck, Maine with Frank Olson, CEO of Hertz.
Frank is a brilliant manager, with intimate knowledge of the card
business. So from the first tee on I was quizzing him about the
industry. By the time we reached the second green, Frank had
convinced me that Amex's corporate card was a terrific franchise,
and I had decided not to sell. On the back nine I turned buyer, and
in a few months Berkshire owned 10 percent of the company." The Net
Interest analysis notes that Buffett credited Charlie Munger's
influence for the decision to re-examine American Express: "Charlie's
focus on quality over price persuaded him to look again." This marks
a philosophical evolution from the 1964 purchase, which was primarily
a price-driven decision during a crisis, to the 1991-1998
investment, which was driven by an assessment of franchise quality
and network economics. The Motley Fool analysis (2007) documents
that Buffett began buying common stock in 1993, owned 2.4 percent
of the company at year-end 1993, and 5.5 percent by year-end 1994.
By 1995, Berkshire controlled 9.8 percent. By 1998, the position was
fully built at a total cost of approximately 1.3 billion dollars.

### The Closed-Loop Network and the Flywheel

The structural analysis of American Express's closed-loop network
is supported by the company's own SEC filings and multiple
independent analyses. American Express describes itself in its 10-K
as "the issuer, network, and a merchant acquirer" operating "under
one roof" with "direct relationships with both Card Members and
merchants." The Stratrix moat-anatomy series (2025) provides the
most detailed structural analysis, arguing that the closed loop is
the "plumbing" while the true moat is the "flywheel" flowing through
it: affluent cardholders attract merchants, merchants pay a premium
to reach those cardholders, the premium funds rewards, rewards
attract more affluent cardholders. The data supports this: in 2024,
American Express processed 1.551 trillion dollars in billed
business across 83.6 million proprietary cards, earned 8.4 billion
dollars in net card fees (up 16 percent year over year), and
reported a net write-off rate of approximately 1.9 percent against
the commercial-bank credit card average of approximately 4.17
percent. The AINVEST analysis (2025) characterizes the moat as
combining "high switching costs, a formidable brand, and robust
network effects." The Stratrix analysis of the Capital One-Discover
merger (February 2025, 35.3 billion dollars) notes that a competitor
can now buy a closed-loop network, but "cannot buy the spending
habit that makes the network valuable" -- supporting Buffett's
assertion that American Express cannot be replicated regardless of
capital available.

### Share Repurchases and Stake Growth Without New Purchases

The mechanism by which Berkshire's stake grew without new purchases
is documented by The Wealth Advisor (2022): "Berkshire Hathaway
hasn't purchased any American Express stock since the late 1990s, but
its stake in AmEx has continued to increase as a result of stock
buybacks. Between 1998 and 2005, Berkshire's stake climbed from 11.2
percent to 12 percent." By September 2022, Berkshire held 151,610,700
shares, or 20.29 percent of the total. The Forbes/GuruFocus analysis
(2013) documents American Express's shareholder-friendly capital
allocation, including an 11 percent dividend increase in 2012 and a
4 billion dollar share repurchase authorization. StockCircle data
indicates Berkshire's cost basis of approximately 8.42 billion
dollars against a market value of approximately 50.5 billion dollars
(as of the tracked period), representing a gain of approximately 500
percent, before accounting for the dividends received. This
evidence confirms that the compounding of the American Express
position after the late 1990s was driven not by Berkshire's capital
deployment but by American Express's own capital allocation --
repurchases that concentrated Berkshire's stake at no additional
cost.

### The Costco Departure as a Stress Test

The end of the Costco co-brand partnership in March 2016 served as a
real-time stress test of American Express's franchise resilience.
According to the Wall Street Journal (2015) and multiple subsequent
analyses, Costco had been approximately 8 percent of American
Express's global billed business, 20 percent of worldwide loans, and
10 percent of cards-in-force. The loss was treated by the market as a
near-death scare; the stock plunged and the company lost over 40
billion dollars in shareholder value. Yet American Express recovered
within two years, replacing the lost volume with organic growth and
new partnerships. The Stratrix analysis (2025) uses this episode as a
precedent for assessing the current Delta co-brand concentration
(approximately 12 percent of worldwide billed business and 21
percent of card member loans as of the 2024 10-K), noting that
single-partner concentration is a recurring structural risk for the
flywheel model. Buffett retained the position through the Costco
departure, consistent with his thesis that the franchise's core
dynamics were intact even when individual partnerships ended.

## Implications

### For Value Investors: The Two-Stage Circle of Competence Model

The American Express case provides a reusable framework for value
investors evaluating whether prior knowledge of a business justifies
a larger commitment. The pattern has three components: (1) an early,
smaller investment that establishes foundational knowledge of the
business's economics; (2) a passage of time during which the investor
observes the business across cycles, crises, and management changes;
(3) a later, larger investment made with the accumulated knowledge.
The critical discipline is distinguishing between familiarity that
compounds and familiarity that breeds complacency. Buffett
mitigated complacency risk by seeking external expertise (Frank
Olson) at the decision point rather than relying solely on his own
analysis. The implication for investors is that the circle of
competence should be tested against external expertise and fresh
evidence at each major commitment, not treated as a static asset.
The American Express case also illustrates that the best
investments may require the investor to do nothing after the initial
purchase -- the business compounds value, and repurchases compound
the stake, without further capital deployment. This is the
opposite of the trading mindset and requires the temperament to
hold through multiple business cycles, management transitions, and
competitive threats.

### For Business Analysts: Network Effects and the Irreproducible Franchise

The American Express closed-loop network demonstrates a principle
that extends beyond payments: the most durable competitive advantages
combine structural architecture with accumulated behavioral data
that cannot be reproduced by capital alone. The Capital One-Discover
merger proved that the closed-loop structure is replicable -- a
competitor can buy the architecture. But the century of conditioned
spending behavior, the merchant consensus that American Express
cardholders are worth a premium to reach, and the proprietary
spending dataset are not for sale. For business analysts, the
implication is that when evaluating a company's moat, the question
is not "can a competitor copy the structure?" but "can a competitor
reproduce what flows through the structure?" American Express's
flywheel -- premium spenders pulling in merchants, merchants funding
rewards, rewards pulling in the next premium spenders -- is the
asset; the closed loop is merely the pipe. Analysts who value
American Express as a pure closed-loop network are, in the Stratrix
formulation, "valuing the plumbing and missing the data." The
broader lesson for competitive analysis across industries is that
structural moats are increasingly reproducible through acquisition
or technology, but behavioral moats built over decades of consumer
conditioning are far harder to replicate.

### For Capital Allocators: Share Repurchases as Stealth Compounding

The American Express case provides the clearest available example of
share repurchases compounding an investor's stake without any
action by the investor. Berkshire's ownership grew from 10 percent
to 20-plus percent purely through American Express's repurchase
programs. For capital allocators, this demonstrates that when
selecting a business for long-term compounding, the quality of the
company's own capital allocation is as important as the entry price.
A company that repurchases shares at reasonable valuations
concentrates the ownership of long-term holders for free. Buffett's
statement that his ownership interest was "likely to increase in the
future" was not a prediction about Berkshire's actions but about
American Express's. The implication is that value investors should
evaluate not only the business's competitive moat but the
management's capital allocation discipline -- specifically, whether
management repurchases shares at prices below intrinsic value rather
than at prices inflated by market enthusiasm. American Express's
repurchase discipline, combined with its franchise economics, made
Berkshire's inaction the highest-return strategy available.

### For Risk Assessment: Concentration and the Complacency Trap

The American Express case also carries a cautionary dimension. The
very factors that made the investment successful -- prior knowledge,
brand trust, network effects -- are the same factors that can
produce complacency in less rigorous hands. Buffett's decision to
seek Frank Olson's expertise at the conversion decision point
reflects an awareness that his own prior knowledge, while valuable,
was insufficient to assess the competitive threat from Visa without
domain-specific insight. The Costco departure in 2016 and the
current Delta co-brand concentration (12 percent of billed
business, 21 percent of card loans as of 2024) illustrate that even
durable franchises carry concentration risks that compound over
time. For risk assessment, the implication is that the circle of
competence must be continuously tested against new competitive
threats, regulatory changes, and partner concentration dynamics --
not treated as permanently established by a prior successful
investment. The two-stage pattern works only when the second stage
incorporates fresh evidence, not merely the conviction of
familiarity.

### For Portfolio Construction: When Inaction Is the Optimal Strategy

The American Express case challenges the active-management premise
that value creation requires continuous decision-making. After the
late 1990s, Berkshire made no new purchases of American Express
stock. The position grew from approximately 10 percent to over 20
percent of the company through the mechanical operation of share
repurchases, and the market value grew from approximately 1.3
billion dollars to over 50 billion dollars through the compounding
of the underlying business. The annual dividend income alone grew
to hundreds of millions of dollars. For portfolio construction, the
implication is that the identification of a business with durable
competitive advantages, shareholder-oriented management, and
disciplined capital allocation may be the last active decision the
investor needs to make. The subsequent work is the harder and less
glamorous discipline of holding through competitive threats,
earnings volatility, partner departures, and market crises -- all of
which American Express experienced between 1998 and 2025. The
investor who sold during the Costco scare in 2016 would have
missed the subsequent recovery and the continued compounding. The
investor who held did nothing, and that nothing was the
highest-return action available.

### For Crisis Investing: Distinguishing Contained Damage From Structural Decay

The salad oil stage of the American Express investment offers a
durable template for crisis-driven investing. The analytical task in
1963 was not to assess the magnitude of the fraud -- that was
public information -- but to determine whether the damage was
contained to a subsidiary or structural to the franchise. Buffett's
steak house visit was a direct empirical test of the franchise's
intactness. The same analytical framework applies to any
crisis-driven mispricing opportunity: identify the specific asset or
business line that is impaired, determine whether the impairment is
finite and survivable, and test whether the core franchise's
customer behavior has changed. If the answer is that the franchise is
intact and the loss is finite, the market's panic creates a gap
between price and value. The risk is that the investor mistakes a
structural decline for a contained crisis -- the opposite of
Buffett's 1963 insight. The discipline is to verify franchise
intactness through direct observation of customer behavior rather
than through market sentiment or analyst commentary.

## Common Pitfalls

- Conflating subsidiary liability with franchise impairment. The
  1963 salad oil crisis damaged a warehouse subsidiary, not the
  charge card or travelers cheque businesses. Investors who
  conflated the two sold at the bottom.
- Treating prior knowledge as sufficient for a larger commitment.
  Buffett's 1991-1994 position was informed by his 1964 experience,
  but he sought Frank Olson's expertise before deciding to hold and
  buy more. Familiarity without external validation is complacency,
  not competence.
- Assuming the closed loop is the moat rather than the pipe. The
  Capital One-Discover merger (2025) proved the architecture is
  replicable. The moat is the flywheel flowing through it -- the
  spending behavior and the data -- not the structure itself.
- Underestimating single-partner concentration risk. The Costco
  departure (2016) cost approximately 8 percent of billed business.
  The Delta co-brand represents approximately 12 percent of billed
  business and 21 percent of card loans as of 2024. A durable
  franchise is not the same as an unconcentrated one.
