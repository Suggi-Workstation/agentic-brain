# Berkshire Hathaway's Washington Post Investment -- Market Mispricing, Governance, and a Shrinking Media Moat

Berkshire Hathaway's 1973 purchase of Washington Post Company stock at roughly one-quarter of estimated intrinsic business value is one of the purest documented examples of Graham-and-Dodd value investing ever executed at scale. Warren Buffett paid about $10.6 million for a position the market had priced near $80 million while informed buyers would have valued the underlying assets at $400 to $500 million. The case is instructive not only for the initial mispricing but for the four-decade arc that followed: a bear-market drawdown that tested conviction, a governance relationship with Katharine Graham that compounded business value far beyond what passive ownership would have produced, the gradual erosion of the newspaper moat that made the original thesis obsolete, and a 2014 tax-aware share exchange that exited the position without triggering a capital gains tax bill estimated near $400 million.

## Background

The Washington Post Company in 1973 was not the struggling newspaper
it would become. It was a diversified media conglomerate with three
main segments: the flagship Washington Post newspaper, Newsweek
magazine, and a portfolio of television and radio stations. The
newspaper held a dominant position in the Washington D.C.
metropolitan market, a local monopoly that was the structural
foundation of its profitability. Local newspapers of that era
operated as near-monopolies in their geographic markets: they were
the primary vehicle for classified advertising, retail advertising,
and local news, and the economics of distribution made a second
competitor uneconomic in most cities (Source 3, Source 4). The
television stations added a second layer of regulated, scarce
spectrum assets that generated cash with high margins.

Katharine Graham had taken control of the company after her husband
Philip Graham's death in 1963. She was named CEO in 1972, becoming
the first female CEO of a Fortune 500 company, and chairwoman in
1973 (Source 5). The company had gone public in 1971, partly to
provide liquidity and partly to resolve ownership complexities left
by Philip Graham's estate. Graham inherited a company with
significant assets but also a deep personal uncertainty about her
own competence in business. As Buffett would later describe it, she
had a "priesthood approach" to business and feared that without the
right background she could not succeed (Source 5). Her board was
entirely male and initially expected her to merely collect dividends
(Source 2).

The market environment in 1973 was hostile to equities. The 1973-1974
stock market crash, triggered by the collapse of the Bretton Woods
system, the oil crisis, and rising inflation, drove the Dow Jones
Industrial Average down over 45 percent from its January 1973 peak
to its December 1974 trough of 577.60 (Source 6). Real GDP growth
slowed from 7.2 percent in 1972 to negative 2.1 percent in 1974,
while CPI inflation jumped from 3.4 percent to 12.3 percent (Source
6). The Nifty Fifty -- the large-capitalization growth stocks that
had dominated the previous bull market -- collapsed, and the general
devaluation of equities created the conditions in which a company
trading at one-quarter of private-market value could be found.

Buffett had known the Washington Post since childhood. His father,
Howard Buffett, was elected to Congress in 1942, and the family
moved to Washington D.C. Buffett delivered the Washington Post as a
paperboy at age 13, waking at 4:30 a.m. (Source 3). This personal
familiarity gave him a long-running observation of the business, but
the investment decision was driven by arithmetic, not nostalgia. He
calculated that the sum of the company's parts -- the newspaper, the
magazine, the broadcast stations -- could be sold to any of ten
buyers for not less than $400 million, and probably appreciably more
(Source 8). The stock market was offering the entire company for
roughly $80 million. The gap between price and value was not subtle;
it was a factor of five.

The intellectual lineage matters here. Buffett's framework was
Benjamin Graham's: buy shares in good businesses when market prices
stand at a large discount to underlying business value. The
Washington Post purchase required, in Buffett's own words, "no
unusual insights" to calculate the price-to-value ratio (Source 1,
Source 7). What it required was the attitude to act when the
calculation was clear, at a time when institutional investors were
under the spell of the Efficient Market Theory -- the academic claim
that calculations of business value were irrelevant because market
prices already incorporated all available information (Source 1,
Source 7). Buffett's edge was not superior information; it was a
different theory of what drove prices and a willingness to act on
it.

The Graham family's ownership structure added a governance
dimension from the start. The company had a dual-class share
structure: Class A shares held by the Graham family carried
superior voting rights, while Class B shares held by the public had
limited voting power. Buffett bought Class B shares. This meant he
acquired an economic interest without control -- a position that
required trust between investor and management, and that placed a
premium on the quality and orientation of the people running the
business. The case would turn on whether that trust was justified.

## Core Concepts

### Intrinsic Value vs. Market Price: The Five-to-One Gap

The central analytic insight of the Washington Post investment is
the magnitude of the divergence between market price and intrinsic
business value that prevailed in 1973. Buffett estimated the
company's intrinsic value at $400 to $500 million. The stock market
capitalized the company at roughly $80 to $100 million. This is a
gap of four to five times -- the market was pricing the entire
enterprise at 20 to 25 cents on the dollar of what informed buyers
would pay for its assets in a private transaction (Source 1, Source
7, Source 8).

The calculation was not exotic. As Buffett emphasized, most security
analysts, media brokers, and media executives would have arrived at
the same $400 to $500 million estimate (Source 1, Source 7). The
television stations alone were valuable regulated assets. The
newspaper was a local monopoly with durable advertising revenue.
Newsweek was a national magazine with established circulation. The
sum of these parts, valued at replacement cost or private-market
transaction multiples, produced the $400 million floor. The book
value of the company was approximately $80 million, and the
approximate replacement cost was $100 million (Source 3), meaning
Buffett was paying no more than 100 to 125 percent of book value and
less than replacement cost for a growing company with a large moat
(Source 3). The margin of safety was not a thin discount; it was the
difference between buying a business for 20 cents on the dollar and
buying it for a dollar.

This concept -- that the gap between price and value can be large
enough that the investment is not speculative -- is the foundation
of the Graham-and-Dodd framework. When the gap is a factor of five,
the investor does not need to be right about the precise intrinsic
value. A wide range of plausible values all produce a purchase price
far below any reasonable estimate. The investor needs only the
patience to wait for the gap to close and the conviction to hold
through the period when the gap widens before it narrows.

### The Mr. Market Framework in Practice

The 1973-1974 period provided a textbook illustration of Graham's
Mr. Market allegory. Buffett bought at one-quarter of intrinsic
value. By year-end 1974, the position showed a loss of about 25
percent, with market value at $8 million against a cost of $10.6
million (Source 1, Source 7). At one point during 1974, the loss
reached nearly 40 percent (Source 9). The market, in Buffett's
phrase, marked the stock down to "well below 20 cents on the
dollar of intrinsic value" (Source 1, Source 7).

The Mr. Market framework treats the stock market as a moody
business partner who offers to buy or sell shares at prices that
fluctuate for reasons unrelated to the underlying business. The
investor's job is not to predict Mr. Market's moods but to exploit
them: buy when the price is far below value, sell or hold when the
price approaches or exceeds value. The Washington Post case
demonstrates the discipline this requires. The business was doing
fine -- intrinsic value was growing -- while the stock price was
falling. The rational response was to hold or buy more, not to
sell. Buffett held, and the gap eventually closed from both
directions: intrinsic value rose, and the market price rose faster
as the discount narrowed.

Buffett's capacity to suffer a 25 to 40 percent paper loss without
abandoning the thesis is itself a core concept. It is the
behavioral component of value investing, and it is the part most
investors fail at. The arithmetic of the purchase was sound; the
test was whether the investor could maintain conviction when the
market declared him wrong.

### Governance Without Control: The Dual-Class Structure

The Washington Post Company's dual-class share structure created a
governance arrangement that shaped the entire investment. The
Graham family held Class A shares with superior voting rights,
giving them control of the company regardless of how many Class B
shares Buffett accumulated. Buffett bought Class B shares, which
carried limited voting power. He could not vote to change
management, sell the company, or direct capital allocation. His
influence depended entirely on the personal relationship he built
with Katharine Graham and, later, her son Donald Graham.

This structure is common in family-controlled media companies and
was central to the case. It meant that Buffett's investment thesis
depended on the quality and orientation of the people who held
control. If the Grahams were incompetent or self-dealing, Buffett
had no mechanism to protect his investment through governance
action. The thesis required, and the outcome confirmed, that the
controlling family was both able and shareholder-oriented.

Buffett made this explicit in his 1985 letter: the extra $160
million Berkshire earned beyond what a passive media investment
would have produced "came, in very large part, from the superior
nature of the managerial decisions made by Kay as compared to those
made by managers of most media companies" (Source 1, Source 7). The
governance structure did not prevent value creation -- it channeled
it through the controlling family's decisions, and the controlling
family made decisions that compounded value.

### Board-Level Influence: Mentor, Not Manager

Buffett joined the Washington Post board in the fall of 1974 and
served two stints: from 1974 to 1986 (departing to make room for
the Capital Cities acquisition, which created a conflict of
interest) and from May 1996 to 2011 (Source 2, Source 5). His role
was not operational. He did not run the newspaper, choose editors,
or set advertising rates. His influence was advisory and
educational, directed at Katharine Graham's development as a CEO and
at capital allocation decisions.

Graham described the relationship as "like going to business
school with Warren Buffett" (Source 2, Source 3). Buffett would
arrive at board meetings with about 20 annual reports and walk
Graham through them, teaching her to read financial statements and
think like an owner (Source 2, Source 3). His guidance was
specific: he recommended that the company's defined benefit pension
plan be invested with one or two outstanding investment managers
rather than the typical corporate fund approach. The Grahams
accepted the advice; Don Graham later estimated the company saved
over $1 billion in pension contributions as a result (Source 5). He
taught Graham the mathematics of stock repurchases -- that buying
back undervalued stock increases every remaining shareholder's
stake at a bargain price -- and the company undertook aggressive
repurchases at the depressed prices of 1974 (Source 5, Source 10).

The governance lesson is that board-level influence can compound
value without control. Buffett never sought the CEO seat, never
agitated for operational changes, and never publicly criticized
management. His influence was exercised through trust, education,
and the quiet weight of his reputation. A longtime board member,
George Gillespie, described Buffett's style: "He would never say,
'Don't do that,' but something more subtle, along the lines of, 'I
probably wouldn't do that for these reasons, but I'll support
whatever you decide'" (Source 10).

### The Newspaper Moat: Monopoly Economics and Their Erosion

The durability of the Washington Post investment depended on the
economics of local newspaper monopolies, and the case's later
chapters are about the erosion of those economics. In the 1970s, a
dominant local newspaper had a powerful moat. It was the primary
channel for classified advertising (jobs, real estate, cars),
retail advertising (department stores, local businesses), and local
news. The economics of printing and distribution created high fixed
costs that made a second competitor uneconomic in most markets. A
newspaper that achieved local dominance could raise advertising
rates with near-pricing power, because advertisers had no
alternative way to reach the local audience at comparable scale
(Source 3, Source 4).

The television stations added a second regulated moat. Broadcast
spectrum was scarce and licensed by the FCC, limiting competition.
The stations generated cash flow with high margins and required
relatively modest capital investment compared to the printing
presses and delivery infrastructure of the newspaper.

The erosion began in the 1990s and accelerated in the 2000s.
Craigslist and other online classified platforms dismantled the
classified advertising revenue stream that had been the backbone of
newspaper profitability (Source 11, Source 12). Digital advertising
did not recoup the decline in print advertising, because online
advertisers could measure efficacy through clicks and drove down the
price per impression (Source 12). Circulation fell: the Washington
Post's daily circulation dropped from a peak of 832,332 to 474,767
over the decade before the 2013 sale (Source 12). The local monopoly
that had underpinned the moat was eroded by the internet, which made
geographic distribution irrelevant and eliminated the fixed-cost
barrier that had prevented competition.

The Graham family recognized the structural decline. In 2013, Don
Graham sold the newspaper to Jeff Bezos for $250 million (Source 11,
Source 12, Source 13). The company renamed itself Graham Holdings
and retained the television stations, education, and healthcare
assets. The newspaper moat that had justified the 1973 investment
was, by 2013, a liability. The holding company was worth more
without the newspaper than with it.

### The Triple Dip: How Value Compounded

Buffett's 1985 letter described the mechanism by which the
Washington Post investment compounded as a "triple dip" (Source 1,
Source 7). Three forces worked simultaneously:

1. **Business value rose.** The company's underlying operations --
   the newspaper, the magazine, the broadcast stations -- grew
   their earnings and cash flow under Graham's management. The
   intrinsic value of the enterprise increased.

2. **Per-share value rose faster.** Graham repurchased large
   quantities of stock at the depressed prices of 1974 and
   afterward. Each share repurchased below intrinsic value increased
   the per-share intrinsic value of every remaining share. Buffett
   had taught her the mathematics; she applied it aggressively at
   exactly the right time.

3. **The discount narrowed.** The stock price, which had traded at
   20 cents on the dollar of intrinsic value in 1974, moved toward
   intrinsic value as investors recognized the economics of the
   business. The narrowing of the discount meant the stock price
   outpaced even the per-share business value gain.

The compounding was not the product of a single factor. A passive
investment in a media company at fair value would have produced the
first effect (business value growth). The second effect (per-share
acceleration through repurchases) required a CEO who understood and
acted on the mathematics. The third effect (discount narrowing)
required the market to eventually recognize what Buffett had seen
in 1973. The interaction of all three is what produced the 21-times
return over 12.5 years documented in the 1985 letter (Source 1,
Source 7).

### The 2014 Tax-Aware Exit: The Cash-Rich Split-Off

The final chapter of the Washington Post investment is the 2014
exit, and it introduces a concept absent from the original
purchase: the tax efficiency of the disposition. By 2014,
Berkshire held 1,727,765 shares of Graham Holdings (the renamed
company) with a cost basis of approximately $11 million and a
market value exceeding $1.1 billion (Source 14, Source 15). A
direct sale would have triggered a capital gains tax liability
estimated at roughly $400 million, applying a 38 percent combined
federal, state, and local rate (Source 14).

Berkshire and Graham Holdings structured a "cash-rich split-off"
under IRC Sections 355 and 361, which permit tax-free treatment if
the transaction meets specific requirements (Source 15). The
mechanism: a new subsidiary ("NewSub") was created. Graham Holdings
contributed to NewSub a Miami television station (WPLG), valued at
$364 million; $327.7 million in cash; and approximately $400
million in Berkshire Hathaway stock (2,107 Class A and 1,278 Class
B shares). Berkshire contributed its 1,620,190 Graham Holdings
Class B shares to NewSub. NewSub was then distributed: Berkshire
received the TV station, the cash, and its own stock back; Graham
Holdings received its shares back (Source 15, Source 16).

The key to qualifying for Section 355 tax-free treatment was that
NewSub could not be a "disqualified investment corporation" -- its
assets could not be two-thirds or more investment assets (cash and
stock). The $364 million TV station value kept the investment
assets below the threshold (Source 14, Source 15). Tax expert
Robert Willens noted that the deal "breaks new ground" because the
investment assets of the distributed subsidiary included stock of
the very shareholder to whom the subsidiary's stock was being
distributed -- a structure not previously attempted (Source 14).

The lesson is that the exit decision is as much a part of the
investment as the entry. A $400 million tax saving on a $1.1
billion position is not a rounding error. The structure preserved
capital that would otherwise have been transferred to the
government, and it did so through legal engineering that required
both counterparties to cooperate. The Graham family's willingness to
unwind the cross-holding in a tax-efficient manner was itself a
product of the four-decade relationship.

## Evidence

### The 1985 Berkshire Chairman's Letter: The Authoritative Account

The most authoritative primary source for the Washington Post
investment is Buffett's own 1985 letter to Berkshire Hathaway
shareholders (Source 1, Source 7). The letter provides the key
numbers: purchase price of $10.6 million in mid-1973; intrinsic
value estimate of $400 to $500 million; market capitalization of
roughly $100 million at purchase and $80 million at the 1974 low;
year-end 1974 market value of the position at $8 million (a 25
percent loss); and a year-end 1985 value (including redemption
proceeds) of $221 million, roughly 21 times cost.

The letter's methodological value is its transparency about what
the calculation required and what it did not. Buffett states
explicitly that "calculating the price/value ratio required no
unusual insights" and that "most security analysts, media brokers,
and media executives would have estimated WPC's intrinsic business
value at $400 to $500 million just as we did" (Source 1, Source 7).
The advantage was "attitude" -- the willingness to act on a
calculation that the Efficient Market Theory said was pointless. The
letter is a direct rebuttal of the academic theory that market
prices are always efficient, presented not as an argument but as a
case study with numbers.

The letter also attributes a specific portion of the return to
management quality. Buffett calculates that a passive investment in
a half-dozen favored media companies would have grown the $10.6
million to $40 to $60 million by year-end 1985. The actual result
was $221 million. The difference -- roughly $160 million -- is
attributed to Graham's managerial decisions, particularly stock
repurchases at depressed prices (Source 1, Source 7). This
decomposition separates the return attributable to industry
economics from the return attributable to management quality, a
distinction that matters for any investor evaluating whether to pay
for good management.

### The 1973-1974 Drawdown: Evidence of Conviction Under Loss

The 1974 annual report of Berkshire Hathaway provides contemporaneous
evidence of the drawdown. The report disclosed that preferred and
common stocks of unaffiliated companies were carried at $2.86 million
and $50.7 million at cost, but only $1.84 million and $34.8 million
at market value (Source 9). Berkshire's entire equity portfolio was
in a net loss position of almost $16 million at year-end 1974, of
which the Washington Post position accounted for $2.6 million
(Source 9). At one point during 1974, the Washington Post
investment showed an almost 40 percent loss (Source 9).

The evidence matters because it tests the central behavioral claim
of value investing: that the investor should hold or add when the
business is sound and the price falls. The business was sound --
Buffett states that "through 1973 and 1974, WPC continued to do fine
as a business, and intrinsic value grew" (Source 1, Source 7). The
price fell. Buffett held. The eventual outcome validated the
discipline, but at the time the decision required acting against the
consensus that the market was correctly pricing a deteriorating
outlook.

Portfolio allocation evidence adds a dimension Buffett did not
emphasize. At year-end 1974, Berkshire had $181 million in total
investable assets, of which only 28 percent was in equities at
market value. The Washington Post position was about 20 percent of
the equity portfolio at cost but only 6 percent of the total
portfolio (Source 9). The concentration was real within the equity
sleeve, but the overall portfolio was positioned so that a 25
percent loss on the position translated to only a 1.5 percent loss
for Berkshire as a whole (Source 9). The conviction to concentrate
was supported by a portfolio structure that limited the downside if
the thesis was wrong.

### The Graham Relationship: Evidence of Governance Value

The relationship between Buffett and Katharine Graham is documented
in multiple sources and provides evidence for the governance
dimension of the return. Graham's autobiography, "Personal History"
(1997), records that Buffett sent her a letter after his purchase,
stating he would "never buy another share unless you personally okay
it" and signing an agreement to that effect (Source 2, Source 4).
This voluntary restraint addressed Graham's fear of a hostile
takeover and established the trust that defined the relationship.

Graham flew to Omaha to meet Buffett and Charlie Munger. She
returned saying they were "the two smartest people she had ever met"
and wanted Buffett on the board (Source 5). Buffett joined the board
in 1974. His specific contributions are documented: pension plan
reform (saving an estimated $1 billion over time, per Don Graham),
stock repurchase guidance, and general capital allocation advice
(Source 5, Source 10). Graham's stock price rose more than 3,000
percent during her tenure as publisher (Source 2), and Buffett
himself stated the stock "went up 40-for-1 when she was CEO" (Source
3).

The evidence supports a causal claim: that Buffett's governance
influence materially increased the per-share value of the company
beyond what passive ownership would have produced. The mechanism
was not operational control but capital allocation guidance --
specifically, repurchases at depressed prices and pension
investment reform -- combined with Graham's willingness to act on
that guidance. This is the $160 million of incremental return
Buffett identified in the 1985 letter.

### The 2014 Exchange: Evidence of Tax Engineering

The SEC 8-K filing dated July 7, 2014, documents the exchange
transaction in formal detail (Source 15). The filing confirms:
Graham Holdings acquired 1,620,190 Class B shares from Berkshire;
Berkshire received all shares of a wholly owned Graham Holdings
subsidiary owning WPLG (the Miami TV station), 2,107 Class A and
1,278 Class B Berkshire shares, and $327.7 million in cash. The
filing states the transaction "qualifies as a tax-free distribution
under IRC Section 355 and 361" (Source 15).

The Wall Street Journal and TheStreet reporting on the transaction
provide the tax analysis (Source 14). Robert Willens, an
independent tax expert, estimated that a direct sale would have
produced approximately $400 million in tax liability at a 38 percent
combined rate. The cash-rich split-off structure reduced that
liability to zero, contingent on the IRS accepting that NewSub was
not a disqualified investment corporation (Source 14). Willens
noted the structure was unprecedented because the investment assets
of the distributed subsidiary included stock of the distributing
shareholder itself (Source 14).

The Washington Post's own reporting confirmed the transaction
value: Berkshire's stake was valued at more than $1.1 billion, and
the exchange ended a "four-decade-old marriage" between the company
and Berkshire (Source 16). The evidence demonstrates that the exit
was engineered with the same attention to value preservation that
characterized the entry, and that the structure required
counterparty cooperation that the long relationship had made
possible.

## Implications

### For Investors: The Template for Concentrated Value Investing

The Washington Post case is the most complete template in the
Berkshire case-studies cluster for concentrated value investing.
It demonstrates every stage of the process: identifying a gap
between price and value large enough that the investment is not
speculative; buying during a market dislocation that has depressed
prices for reasons unrelated to the underlying business; holding
through a drawdown that tests conviction; compounding value through
governance influence rather than operational control; and exiting
through a tax-efficient structure that preserves the gain.

For investors screening for similar opportunities, the case
identifies the conditions that produce a five-to-one gap between
price and value. The 1973-1974 crash was a systemic dislocation that
depressed prices across the market. The Washington Post was a
good business caught in a bad market, not a bad business priced
correctly. The gap existed because institutional investors were
following a theory (Efficient Market Theory) that told them the gap
was an illusion. The investor who exploits such a gap needs a
different theory of price formation and the conviction to act when
the consensus says the calculation is pointless.

The case also carries a warning. The conditions that produced the
gap in 1973 -- a systemic bear market combined with an academic
consensus that dismissed intrinsic value -- are rare. An investor
who waits for a five-to-one gap will make few investments. The
lesson is not that every investment should have this margin of
safety, but that when the gap is this large, the position should be
sized accordingly, and the investor should be prepared to hold for
years.

### For Governance: Influence Without Control

The governance implications extend beyond the specific case. The
Washington Post demonstrates that a minority investor can compound
value through a controlling family without acquiring control,
provided three conditions are met: the investor has a reputation
that earns trust; the controlling family is able and
shareholder-oriented; and the investor exercises influence through
education and advice rather than confrontation.

Buffett's approach was to build trust, teach the mathematics of
value creation, and let the CEO make the decisions. He signed an
agreement not to buy more shares without Graham's permission,
voluntarily restricting his own flexibility to address her fear. He
joined the board and used the position to educate, not to dictate.
The result was that Graham repurchased stock at depressed prices,
reformed the pension plan, and ran the business in a way that
compounded per-share value. The investor who seeks to replicate
this model must first determine whether the controlling family
meets the second condition -- ability and shareholder orientation
-- because without it, influence without control produces nothing.

This has implications for family-controlled companies generally.
Dual-class share structures are common in media, technology, and
family businesses. The Washington Post case suggests that such
structures are not inherently value-destroying; they can be
value-creating if the controlling family is competent and aligned
with minority shareholders. The investor's analysis must extend
beyond the financial statements to an assessment of the people who
hold control.

### For Media Industry Analysis: The Lifecycle of a Moat

The Washington Post case is a longitudinal study of a competitive
moat from formation to erosion. The local newspaper monopoly of the
1970s was a genuine moat: geographic distribution created a natural
monopoly, classified and retail advertising had no alternative
channel, and the fixed costs of printing and distribution prevented
a second competitor. The moat was durable for decades.

The erosion was driven by a technology shift -- the internet -- that
made the geographic distribution advantage irrelevant. Craigslist
dismantled classified advertising. Online advertising dismantled
retail advertising. Digital distribution eliminated the fixed-cost
barrier. The moat did not erode because management failed; it eroded
because the structural conditions that created it disappeared.
This is the distinction between a moat that is eroded by competition
and a moat that is eroded by technological change. The former can be
defended; the latter often cannot.

For investors analyzing moats, the case carries two lessons. First,
a moat that depends on a specific distribution technology is
vulnerable to a technology shift that makes that distribution
irrelevant. Second, the time to recognize erosion is before the
moat-dependent business becomes a liability -- the Graham family's
2013 sale of the newspaper to Bezos was a recognition that the
holding company was worth more without the newspaper than with it.
The investor who confuses a durable moat with a permanent moat will
hold the asset through the erosion and capture the decline.

### For Tax Planning: The Exit as Part of the Investment

The 2014 exchange introduces a concept that the 1973 purchase did
not require: the tax efficiency of the disposition. On a $1.1
billion position with an $11 million cost basis, the capital gains
tax on a direct sale would have consumed roughly $400 million. The
cash-rich split-off structure reduced that to zero. The lesson is
that the exit decision is not separate from the investment
decision; it is a continuation of it.

For investors holding large concentrated positions with low cost
bases, the case demonstrates that the disposition structure can
preserve a substantial fraction of the gain. The cash-rich
split-off is a specific tool that requires a cooperative
counterparty and a non-investment asset of sufficient value to meet
the Section 355 requirements. Not every position can be exited this
way. But the principle -- that the after-tax return is what matters,
and that the exit structure should be planned before the exit is
executed -- is general.

The case also illustrates the value of a long relationship in
enabling complex transactions. The 2014 exchange required Graham
Holdings to cooperate in unwinding the cross-holding in a way that
benefited both parties. The four-decade relationship between
Buffett and the Graham family made this cooperation possible. An
investor who has built trust with a controlling family over decades
has an option that an arms-length investor does not: the ability to
structure an exit that requires counterparty cooperation.
