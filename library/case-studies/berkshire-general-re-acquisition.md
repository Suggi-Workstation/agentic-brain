# Berkshire Hathaway's General Re Acquisition -- Why a Sound Strategic Category Produced a Costly Mistake and What It Reveals About Diligence, Incentives, and Tail Risk

In June 1998, Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway agreed to acquire
General Re Corporation, one of the world's largest reinsurance
companies, in a stock-for-stock transaction valued at approximately $22
billion. The deal, which closed on December 21, 1998, was the largest
in Berkshire's history at the time and was intended to dramatically
expand Berkshire's insurance float and global reinsurance footprint.
Instead, it became one of Buffett's most candidly acknowledged
mistakes -- not because reinsurance was a bad business, but because
General Re arrived with underpriced risk, inadequate reserves, a
derivatives book Buffett considered dangerous, and a cultural mismatch
that took years to repair. The case separates the soundness of a
strategic category from the costliness of a specific purchase, and it
extracts durable lessons about acquisition currency, underwriting
discipline, tail risk, and the incentive structures that govern
reinsurance accounting.

## Background

The intellectual lineage of Berkshire's General Re acquisition runs
through Buffett's decades-long conviction that insurance float --
premiums collected and held before claims are paid -- is one of the
most powerful engines of compounding available to an investor. Buffett
had built Berkshire's insurance platform piece by piece: National
Indemnity in 1967, the founding of the reinsurance operation at
Berkshire's own reinsurer, and the progressive acquisition of GEICO,
which became wholly owned in 1996. By the late 1990s, Berkshire's
insurance float stood at roughly $7.1 billion, and Buffett was
actively seeking ways to expand it. Reinsurance -- insurance for
insurers -- was especially attractive because its long-duration
policies generated float that could be invested for years before
claims materialized. The longer the tail, the more valuable the
float, provided the underwriting was disciplined.

General Reinsurance Corporation, known as Gen Re, was a natural
target. Founded in 1921 as General Casualty and Surety Reinsurance
Corporation through the merger of two Norwegian-controlled insurers
with $800,000 in initial capital, the company had grown over seven
decades into a global reinsurance powerhouse. By 1997, the year before
the acquisition, General Re reported revenues of $8.3 billion and net
income of $968 million, with shareholders' equity of $8.16 billion.
Its business spanned four segments: North American property and
casualty reinsurance (roughly 48 percent of revenue), international
property and casualty reinsurance (anchored by a 1994 controlling
stake in Cologne Re of Germany), life and health reinsurance, and a
small financial services segment. The combined ratio for its North
American business averaged 100.3 percent over the 1987-1997 period --
better than the industry norm of 105 to 110 percent, but still an
underwriting loss that was covered by investment income on the float.

The strategic logic Buffett articulated at the announcement was
compounding. Berkshire's diversified balance sheet and permanent
capital would let General Re write larger and more volatile policies
than it could as a standalone public company, free from quarterly
earnings pressure. Berkshire would gain access to General Re's $24.6
billion investment portfolio and its established float, nearly
doubling Berkshire's investable assets. The combination would
strengthen global distribution through Cologne Re's European network.
And Buffett cited General Re's management team, led by CEO Ronald
Ferguson and CFO Joseph Brandon, as a key asset being acquired. The
merger proxy described the transaction as one in which General Re's
"outstanding management can now do what it does best, unfettered by
the constraints that have limited its growth."

The deal was structured as a stock-for-stock merger. Berkshire offered
General Re shareholders approximately 0.697 shares of Berkshire Class
A stock (or 21.7 shares of Class B) per General Re share, implying a
value of roughly $276.50 per share -- a premium of about 26 percent
over General Re's closing price of $220. The transaction closed on
December 21, 1998, at a reported value of approximately $22 billion.
Crucially, the consideration was paid in Berkshire stock, not cash.
Berkshire issued 272,200 new shares, increasing its outstanding share
count by 21.8 percent. This single design choice -- using stock as
acquisition currency at a moment when Berkshire's shares traded at
roughly twice book value -- would become the central financial regret
of the deal.

The timing is essential context. The late 1990s stock market was in
the final stages of a historic bull run; the S&P 500 had risen
approximately 20 percent per year for three consecutive years.
Berkshire's own equity portfolio had swollen to 115 percent of
shareholders' equity by the end of 1997, and its shares traded at a
steep premium to book. Buffett himself had cautioned shareholders in
his 1997 letter that "prices are high for both businesses and stocks"
and that Berkshire was "not pleased with our prospects for committing
incoming funds." The decision to pay for General Re with stock that
Buffett implicitly believed was expensive -- rather than cash, which
would have required selling portions of the equity portfolio -- set
up a tension between strategic desire and valuation discipline that
the subsequent years would expose.

## Core Concepts

### Acquisition Currency and the Cost of Stock Payment

The most consequential concept the General Re case illustrates is the
asymmetry between paying for an acquisition in cash and paying in
stock. When a company uses cash, the cost is fixed at the moment of
the transaction: the buyer gives up a known quantity of purchasing
power and receives the target. When a company uses its own stock, the
true cost is not known until the market eventually prices the
exchanged shares. If the acquirer's stock is undervalued at the time
of issuance, the seller receives a bargain; if the acquirer's stock
compounds at a high rate over subsequent decades, the effective price
paid by the acquirer's existing shareholders can be a multiple of the
headline figure.

Buffett articulated the principle directly in his 1999 shareholder
letter, written in the aftermath of the General Re deal: "From the
economic standpoint of the acquiring company, the worst deal of all
is a stock-for-stock acquisition. Here, a huge price is often paid
without the acquirer's shareholders knowing it." The General Re
transaction issued 272,200 Berkshire shares worth approximately $15.9
billion in 1998. By 2023, the same 272,200 shares were worth
approximately $148 billion -- nearly ten times the original value, a
magnitude of opportunity cost that Buffett called a "terrible
mistake." The shares given to General Re's former shareholders
compounded at Berkshire's exceptional rate of return, meaning
Berkshire's existing shareholders gave up a claim on future
compounding whose value dwarfed the insurance business they received
in exchange. Buffett later modified his discipline to an absolute
rule, stating he would "rather prep for a colonoscopy than issue
Berkshire shares."

The lesson is not that stock deals are always wrong, but that the
cost of stock as currency must be measured against the acquirer's
expected rate of compounding, not against the headline transaction
value. A company whose equity is expected to compound at 15 percent
annually is effectively paying 15 percent more each year it holds the
alternative of not issuing shares. This is invisible in conventional
deal accounting, where stock issuance is treated as a non-cash
transaction with no immediate income-statement cost, but it is
profoundly real in its effect on long-run per-share value.

### Reinsurance Underwriting and the Asymmetry of Reserving

Reinsurance is structurally different from primary insurance because
the policies are larger, the claims are less frequent, and the lag
between writing a policy and discovering whether it was priced
correctly can stretch over decades. This long tail creates a
fundamental information problem: the underwriting result reported in
any given year is substantially an estimate, because the ultimate
cost of claims on policies written years earlier may not be known for
many years to come. Management has, in Buffett's words, "enormous
latitude in figuring their underwriting results," and the combined
ratio -- the sum of the loss ratio and the expense ratio -- is only
as reliable as the reserve assumptions embedded in it.

General Re's reported performance before the acquisition was
respectable by industry standards, but the numbers carried hidden
risk. The North American combined ratio averaged 100.3 percent from
1987 to 1997, and the international segment was worse at 103 percent.
More tellingly, General Re's loss development history showed that
reserves established in the late 1980s had been deficient, requiring
upward revisions in subsequent years. The apparent improvement in
underwriting from 1993 onward -- the loss ratio declining from 75
percent to 68 percent -- may have reflected genuine discipline, but
it also depended on reserve estimates that could prove optimistic.
This is the core challenge of reinsurance analysis: a reinsurer can
report strong results for years by under-reserving, and the
deficiency is only exposed when claims finally arrive.

The problem compounds because reinsurance is a business where the
most dangerous policies are often the most profitable-looking at
inception. Long-tailed catastrophe coverage, asbestos and
environmental liability, and terrorism risk all generate large
premiums and no immediate claims. The underwriting profit appears on
the books immediately; the true cost is deferred, sometimes for a
generation. A reinsurer incentivized on premium volume or short-term
reported earnings will systematically underprice this tail risk,
because the individuals making the pricing decisions will have
collected their bonuses and departed long before the claims arrive.
This incentive structure is the structural reason reinsurance cycles
exist: pricing discipline erodes when capital is abundant and
competition is fierce, the industry underprices, and the losses
arrive years later.

### Derivatives Exposure and the Difficulty of Exit

General Re arrived at Berkshire with a derivatives subsidiary,
General Re Securities, that Buffett and Charlie Munger did not want
and immediately moved to close. The operation had been established in
1990 to serve the hedging needs of General Re's insurance clients, but
it had grown into a portfolio of 23,218 derivative contracts with
approximately 900 counterparties around the world, some with terms
extending as long as 100 years. Buffett told a government panel that
even if he had hired "15 of the smartest people, math majors, Ph.D.'s"
with "carte blanche to devise any reporting system," he could not have
gotten his mind around the exposure. The counterparty list included
"probably 200 names I can't pronounce."

The derivative unwind consumed years and produced cumulative pre-tax
losses of $409 million. By the start of 2002, the portfolio still
stood at 23,218 contracts; by 2005 it was down to 2,890; by 2006,
197. The difficulty of exit was structural. As Buffett wrote in his
2002 letter, "In either industry [reinsurance or derivatives], once
you write a contract -- which may require a large payment decades
later -- you are usually stuck with it. True, there are methods by
which the risk can be laid off with others. But most strategies of
that kind leave you with residual liability." A derivatives book is
not a portfolio that can be sold; it is a web of bilateral obligations
that must be individually negotiated, closed, or allowed to mature,
often over decades.

The deeper lesson Buffett drew was about valuation and incentives.
Derivatives are marked to market, but when no liquid market exists,
they are marked to model -- what Buffett called "mark-to-myth." The
parties to a contract can use differing models and both show profits
for years. The marking errors, Buffett observed, are not
symmetrical: "Almost invariably, they have favored either the trader
who was eyeing a multi-million dollar bonus or the CEO who wanted to
report impressive earnings." The bonus is paid, the CEO exercises
options, and only years later do shareholders learn the reported
earnings were a sham. General Re Securities was a contained example
of a systemic problem: derivatives create accounting flexibility that
incentive structures exploit, and the full cost is discovered only
when the positions are unwound under pressure.

### Finite Reinsurance and the AIG Scandal

General Re's reputation suffered a further blow beginning in 2004,
when investigations revealed that the company had entered into
"finite reinsurance" transactions with American International Group
(AIG) that regulators alleged were designed to inflate AIG's reported
loss reserves. The central transaction occurred in late 2000, when
AIG CEO Maurice "Hank" Greenberg contacted General Re CEO Ronald
Ferguson to discuss a loss portfolio transfer. The deal involved $500
million in premium from AIG to General Re, structured so that General
Re bore minimal risk -- approximately $100 million for six to nine
months, with 98 percent of the funds withheld by AIG. Regulators
alleged the transaction transferred no meaningful risk and was purely
a vehicle for AIG to add $500 million in phony reserves to its
balance sheet.

The fallout was severe. AIG restated its earnings downward by $3.4
billion and paid $1.6 billion to settle federal and state charges in
2006. Four General Re executives -- Ferguson, CFO Elizabeth Monrad,
assistant general counsel Robert Graham, and senior vice president
Christopher Garand -- along with AIG's head of reinsurance Christian
Milton, were convicted of fraud in February 2008. The convictions
were overturned on appeal in 2011, and prosecutors agreed to drop
charges under deferred prosecution agreements in which the executives
admitted "aspects" of the deal were fraudulent. Two General Re
executives had earlier pleaded guilty. General Re itself entered a
deferred prosecution agreement and paid $72 million to settle investor
claims related to the decline in AIG's stock price, plus $92.2 million
to end investigations into its role. Joseph Brandon resigned as CEO
of General Re in April 2008.

Buffett cooperated with the investigations and was not implicated, but
the episode exposed a cultural problem: General Re's leadership had
been willing to structure transactions whose primary purpose was to
help a counterparty misrepresent its financial condition. This was
precisely the kind of incentive-driven behavior Buffett had spent
decades warning about, and it reflected a gap between Berkshire's
reputation-centric culture and General Re's transaction-oriented one.

### Cultural Mismatch and the Long Repair

The General Re acquisition exposed a cultural gap between Berkshire's
underwriting ethos and General Re's operational style. Berkshire's
insurance subsidiaries, particularly National Indemnity under Ajit
Jain, operated on the principle that underwriting discipline matters
more than premium volume: when pricing is inadequate, the right
response is to write less business, not more. General Re, by
contrast, had been managing to reported results and market share,
incentivizing its underwriters on premium growth. The result was that
General Re continued writing business at prices that did not
adequately compensate for the risk, even as the market hardened after
September 11, 2001.

The repair was slow and deliberate. Berkshire changed General Re's
incentive compensation structure to reward conservative underwriting
in the style of National Indemnity. General Re was instructed to hold
its premium volume flat rather than grow in an environment of
inadequate pricing. The derivatives operation was unwound over five
years. The combined ratio was brought under control, and General Re
finally returned to an underwriting profit in 2003, posting $145
million on $8.2 billion of premium earned. But the recovery came at
the cost of scale: premiums written shrank to $2.56 billion by 2016.
General Re became consistently profitable but smaller -- a testament
to the reality that fixing a reinsurer requires sacrificing volume for
quality, a process that can take a decade.

## Evidence

### The 1999-2001 Underwriting Losses

The most immediate evidence that General Re was not the disciplined
underwriter Berkshire believed it was purchasing came in the years
immediately following the acquisition. In 1999, General Re's
reinsurance operations produced large net underwriting losses -- $1.2
billion on $6.9 billion of premium income, a combined ratio far
above the 100.3 percent average it had reported as a standalone
company. The losses continued in 2000. In 2001, Berkshire's
reinsurance operations recorded an underwriting loss of $4.3 billion,
roughly three times the prior year, driven by $1.9 billion in losses
related to the September 11 terrorist attacks. Buffett acknowledged
that 2001 would have been a bad year even without September 11,
admitting that he was "coming to think the General Re acquisition was
a mistake."

The cumulative underwriting losses from 1999 through 2001 reached
approximately $6.5 billion. This figure is the empirical heart of the
case: a business Berkshire bought for $22 billion, largely in stock
worth multiples of that figure in hindsight, generated losses in its
first three years that exceeded 30 percent of the acquisition price.
The losses were not the result of bad luck alone. They reflected
underpricing that predated the acquisition: General Re had been
writing reinsurance at prices that did not adequately compensate for
tail risk, particularly terrorism exposure, and the reserve estimates
supporting its pre-acquisition profitability proved optimistic. The
September 11 losses were catastrophic, but they exposed risks that
were already present and inadequately priced in the book of business.

### The Derivatives Unwind: $409 Million Over Five Years

The General Re Securities derivatives operation provided a
controlled experiment in the difficulty and cost of exiting complex
financial positions. When Berkshire began the unwind in early 2002,
the portfolio stood at 23,218 contracts with 900 counterparties. By the
start of 2005, the count was down to 2,890. The reduction to 741
contracts in 2005 alone cost $104 million in pre-tax losses. By the
end of 2006, the portfolio was down to 197 contracts, and Buffett
announced it would be his "last discussion of the losses at General
Re's derivative operation." Cumulative pre-tax losses totaled $409
million, with only $5 million occurring in 2006. Charlie Munger
quipped that if the $409 million had been properly classified on the
2001 balance sheet, it would have been labeled "Good Until Reached
For."

The derivatives episode is instructive because Berkshire was, in
Buffett's own assessment, "a better-than-average candidate to exit
gracefully." General Re was a relatively minor derivatives operator.
The unwind occurred in a benign market, free of financial pressure
that might have forced a less-efficient liquidation. The accounting
was conventional and considered conservative. There was no evidence
of bad behavior by any individual. And yet the process took five
years and cost over $400 million. Buffett drew the explicit
implication: if Berkshire, with its capital strength and patience,
found exiting a modest derivatives book this costly and slow, the
systemic risk posed by larger derivatives dealers under stress would
be commensurately greater. The episode directly informed Buffett's
subsequent warnings about derivatives as "financial weapons of mass
destruction."

### The AIG Finite Reinsurance Investigation

The AIG-General Re finite reinsurance transactions provide the most
concrete evidence of the cultural and incentive problems within
General Re. The transactions occurred in late 2000 and early 2001,
after Berkshire's acquisition but under General Re's existing
management. The structure -- $500 million in premium, minimal risk
transfer, a secret $5 million fee, and a side deal to return the $10
million premium payment -- was documented in telephone recordings
introduced at trial. Four General Re executives and one AIG executive
were convicted in February 2008, though the convictions were
overturned on appeal in 2011 and charges were dropped under deferred
prosecution agreements.

The financial consequences were substantial. AIG restated earnings
downward by $3.4 billion, paid $1.6 billion to settle regulatory
charges, and Greenberg was forced to step down as CEO in March 2005
after leading the company since 1967. General Re paid $72 million to
settle investor claims and $92.2 million to end investigations.
Brandon resigned as CEO in April 2008. The episode demonstrated that
General Re's management had been willing to accommodate a
counterparty's desire to manipulate its financial statements, a
behavior fundamentally at odds with Berkshire's culture of
reputational primacy.

### Buffett's Own Admission

The most authoritative evidence is Buffett's own retrospective
assessment, expressed across multiple shareholder letters. In his
2016 letter, Buffett wrote: "Unfortunately, I followed the GEICO
purchase by foolishly using Berkshire stock -- a boatload of stock --
to buy General Reinsurance in late 1998. After some early problems,
General Re has become a fine insurance operation that we prize. It
was, nevertheless, a terrible mistake on my part to issue 272,200
shares of Berkshire in buying General Re, an act that increased our
outstanding shares by a whopping 21.8%. My error caused Berkshire
shareholders to give far more than they received."

This admission is notable for its precision: Buffett does not regret
owning General Re, which he describes as "a fine insurance operation
that we prize." He regrets the form of payment. The strategic
category -- acquiring a major reinsurer to expand float and global
distribution -- was sound. The execution -- paying with stock whose
compounding power made the effective price a multiple of the headline
figure -- was the error. This distinction between a sound strategic
category and a poor purchase is the analytical core of the case and
the source of its most transferable lessons.

## Implications

### For Investors: Acquisition Currency as a Diligence Failure

The primary lesson for investors is that the form of acquisition
consideration is a first-order variable, not an accounting detail. A
stock-funded acquisition transfers a share of the acquirer's future
compounding to the seller, and the cost of that transfer is measured
not at the transaction date but at every future date until the
acquirer's growth rate changes. For a company like Berkshire, whose
per-share value has compounded at roughly 20 percent annually over
long periods, issuing shares is extraordinarily expensive: a share
given away in 1998 is worth ten times as much by 2023. Investors
evaluating any acquisition should ask not just whether the price is
fair, but whether the currency used to pay it is undervalued or
overvalued, and what the acquirer's expected compounding rate implies
about the true long-run cost.

This lesson has a concrete behavioral corollary. Buffett's response
to the General Re mistake was to make cash the default and stock
issuance the rare exception. He later stated he would "rather prep for
a colonoscopy than issue Berkshire shares," and Berkshire's
subsequent large acquisitions -- BNSF in 2009, Lubrizol in 2011, the
 Precision Castparts deal in 2016 -- were cash transactions. The
discipline that emerged from the General Regret is a generalizable
principle: when the cost of a payment method is uncertain and
potentially very large, default to the method whose cost is known and
bounded. Cash has a known cost; stock has an open-ended cost that
depends on the acquirer's entire future performance.

### For Insurance Analysts: Underwriting Discipline and Reserve Quality

For analysts evaluating insurance and reinsurance companies, the
General Re case underscores that reported underwriting results are
estimates whose reliability depends entirely on the conservatism of
reserve assumptions. A combined ratio of 100 percent is meaningless
without knowing whether the loss reserves supporting it are adequate.
The analyst's tools are limited: loss development triangles show
whether reserves have been revised upward or downward over time, but
they cannot reveal whether current reserves are adequate until the
future arrives. The practical implication is that reinsurance
analysis requires evaluating management's incentive structure and
track record on reserving, not just the reported ratios.

The case also illustrates the structural incentive problem in
reinsurance: underwriters rewarded on premium volume will
systematically underprice tail risk because the consequences arrive
after the bonus is paid. Berkshire's response -- changing General Re's
incentive structure to reward conservative underwriting, accepting
years of shrinking premium volume -- is a template for how to fix the
problem, but it is slow and costly. An investor acquiring a reinsurer
should expect that fixing incentive misalignment will require years
of lower revenue and should price that cost into the acquisition.

### For Acquirers: Due Diligence on Culture and Hidden Liabilities

The General Re acquisition is a case study in the limits of
diligence when buying a complex financial institution. Berkshire did
not discover the derivatives book, the reserve deficiencies, or the
cultural incentive problems until after closing. The lesson is that
financial-institution acquisitions carry hidden liability risk that
standard diligence may not surface: derivatives positions whose
mark-to-model valuations may be optimistic, reserve estimates that
depend on management judgment, incentive structures that reward
short-term reported results over long-term adequacy, and cultural
norms that may tolerate transactions whose primary purpose is
accounting manipulation.

The practical implication is that acquirers of financial institutions
should build in a margin for hidden liabilities and a plan for
cultural integration. Berkshire's experience suggests that even a
sophisticated acquirer with the capital and patience to absorb losses
will find the repair process takes years and costs more than
anticipated. The alternative -- avoiding financial-institution
acquisitions entirely -- is too restrictive, since insurance float is
a genuine compounding engine. The balanced approach is to discount
the target's reported value for the probability of hidden liabilities
and to have a clear plan for aligning incentives before closing.

### For Risk Managers: Tail Risk and the Limits of Models

The General Re derivatives episode and the September 11 losses both
illustrate that tail risk -- low-probability, high-severity events --
is systematically underpriced in competitive insurance and derivatives
markets. General Re had underpriced terrorism risk before September
11, and its derivatives book contained contracts whose potential
losses "could not be measured," in Buffett's words. The lesson for
risk managers is that models based on historical data will
underestimate the probability and severity of events that have not
occurred in the sample period. Terrorism risk, in particular, had
limited actuarial history before 2001, and the derivatives book
contained instruments with 100-year maturities whose reference
variables spanned decades of unknown future states.

The implication is that risk management for long-tailed exposures
requires stress testing beyond historical distributions and explicit
attention to correlation under stress. Buffett's observation that
"if severe trouble occurred, we knew it was likely to correlate with
problems elsewhere in financial markets" is the core insight: tail
events are not independent. A terrorist attack, a financial crisis, or
a sovereign default will simultaneously stress multiple positions in
ways that diversification assumptions fail to capture. The General Re
experience informed Berkshire's subsequent approach to reinsurance,
which emphasized pricing for tail correlation and maintaining the
capital to absorb correlated losses.

### For Capital Allocators: Sound Category Versus Poor Purchase

The most broadly transferable lesson of the General Re case is the
analytical separation of a strategic category from a specific
purchase within that category. Reinsurance was a sound category for
Berkshire: it expanded float, leveraged Berkshire's permanent-capital
advantage, and fit the insurance platform Buffett had spent thirty
years building. The General Re purchase within that category was
poor: it used expensive stock as currency, acquired a business with
hidden reserve deficiencies and an unwanted derivatives book, and
required a decade of repair. Buffett's own retrospective
distinguishes the two precisely -- he prizes the business he ended up
owning while regretting the currency he used to acquire it.

This distinction matters for every capital allocation decision. A
sound industry or strategy does not guarantee that any particular
transaction within it will create value. The value of an acquisition
depends on the interaction of price, currency, target quality, and
integration execution -- and a failure in any one dimension can
convert a sound category into a value-destroying purchase. The
General Re case demonstrates that even an acquirer of Buffett's
caliber, operating within a category he understood deeply, can
misjudge the target's underwriting discipline, the cost of the
currency, and the time required for cultural integration. The
practical discipline is to evaluate each dimension independently and
to require adequacy on all four before committing capital, rather
than letting the soundness of the category substitute for the rigor
of the specific decision.
