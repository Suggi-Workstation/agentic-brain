# Silicon Valley Bank's 2023 Failure -- Duration Risk Made a Concentrated Uninsured Deposit Base Runnable, and Slow Correction Left No Time

Silicon Valley Bank (SVB), then the 16th largest bank in the United States, was closed on March 10, 2023, after depositors withdrew more than $40 billion in one day and management expected about $100 billion more the next morning [1][3]. The official reviews trace the failure to an interaction rather than a single cause: a bank that roughly tripled in size invested a concentrated, mostly uninsured deposit base in long-dated securities, removed its interest-rate hedges as rates rose, ran weak liquidity and risk governance, and was supervised by authorities who identified problems but did not force their correction before a March 8 announcement set off a run of unprecedented speed [1][2][3][4]. The case matters because a bank whose securities carried little credit risk still failed, and because the response -- a systemic risk exception that protected every depositor -- changed the economics of uninsured deposits for the whole banking system [1][5][7][10].

## Background

SVB was a state-chartered commercial bank and member of the Federal Reserve System, founded in 1983 and headquartered in Santa Clara, California. It was the main subsidiary of SVB Financial Group (SVBFG) and served entrepreneurs and firms in technology, healthcare and private equity; its deposits were mostly linked to businesses financed by venture capital [3]. The California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation was its state regulator and the Federal Reserve its primary federal regulator, with day-to-day supervision run by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (FRBSF) [3]. SVBFG also owned a UK banking subsidiary and other foreign affiliates [3]. At closure SVB was the 16th largest bank in the country, with about $209 billion of assets and $175 billion of deposits at year-end 2022 [3].

The bank's growth followed the venture-capital boom of 2020-2021 and the exceptionally low interest rates that supported it [1][4]. SVBFG grew from $71 billion to over $211 billion in assets between 2019 and 2021 [1]. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) measured SVB's total assets rising from $56 billion in December 2018 to $209 billion in December 2022, and growth of 198 percent over 2019-2021 against 33 percent for the median of 19 peer banks with $100-250 billion in assets [3]. Deposits rose from $103.2 billion in 2020 to $191.4 billion in 2021 [2]. SVBFG placed much of the inflow in securities rather than loans: its securities portfolio grew from $23 billion in 2018 to $125 billion in 2021, and its held-to-maturity (HTM) holdings from $15 billion to $98 billion [2]. At year-end 2022, securities were 55 percent of SVBFG's total assets against 25 percent for large banking organization peers, HTM securities were 78 percent of its securities against 42 percent, and loans were 35 percent of assets against 58 percent [1].

The Federal Reserve then tightened monetary policy. The federal funds rate rose from 0.08 percent in March 2022 to 4.57 percent in March 2023, and 10-20 year and 20-plus year Treasury bonds lost roughly 25 and 30 percent of their market value between the first quarters of 2022 and 2023 [5]. SVBFG's HTM portfolio had a weighted-average duration of 6.2 years at year-end 2022, and most of it consisted of agency mortgage-backed securities with maturities of 10 years or more [1]. Unrealized losses rose from about $1.3 billion on HTM and $313 million on available-for-sale (AFS) securities at year-end 2021 to about $15.2 billion and $2.5 billion at year-end 2022 [2]. GAO put the year-end HTM losses at more than $15 billion, equal to 89 percent of the bank's common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital [3].

The deposit side weakened at the same time. As venture funding slowed, clients drew down cash to fund operations; by early December 2022 SVBFG had recorded $49 billion of net client outflows for the year, 12.5 percent of client balances [1]. Deposits contracted at an 8.5 percent annualized rate during 2022 after an 89.3 percent compound annual growth rate from April 2020 to December 2021 [1]. SVB replaced part of the lost funding with wholesale borrowings, which rose from $121 million in the first quarter of 2022 to $18.4 billion in October 2022 [2].

The regulatory frame had also changed. The Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act of 2018 (EGRRCPA) raised the asset threshold for general application of enhanced prudential standards from $50 billion to $250 billion, while giving the Federal Reserve discretion over firms of $100-250 billion; the Federal Reserve's October 2019 tailoring rule then sorted large banks into four categories [1][3]. SVBFG crossed $100 billion in average assets in June 2021 and became a Category IV firm, the least stringent tier [1][3]. In July 2018 the Federal Reserve had raised the threshold for its Large and Foreign Banking Organization (LFBO) supervisory portfolio from $50 billion to $100 billion, so SVBFG stayed in the Regional Banking Organization (RBO) portfolio through most of its growth [1].

Evidentiary note: the case is reconstructed mainly from four official reviews -- the Federal Reserve Board's review requested by Vice Chair for Supervision Michael S. Barr (April 2023) [1], the material loss review by the Federal Reserve's Office of Inspector General (September 2023) [2], GAO's preliminary review (April 2023) [3], and the Basel Committee's cross-jurisdiction report (October 2023) [4] -- plus two academic studies [5][6], the official records of the resolution [7][8][9][10][11], and a press report of later litigation [12]. The Board review was prepared by staff not involved in supervising SVB, but it remains the supervisor's review of itself; it states that it was written with hindsight in a compressed time frame and that it does not review events after March 8, 2023 [1].

## Core Concepts

### Duration Risk That the Accounting Did Not Show

The first mechanism was interest-rate risk on a large securities book. Securities classified as HTM are carried at amortized historical cost rather than market value, on the condition that the bank intends and is able to hold them to maturity; selling part of the HTM portfolio generally forces the whole portfolio to be reclassified as AFS and marked to market [1]. That rule made SVB's largest asset class both invisible in reported capital and costly to monetize. As a Category IV firm, SVBFG was also allowed to exclude unrealized AFS losses from regulatory capital. The Board review estimates that recognizing those losses, as pre-2019 rules would have required, would have lowered SVBFG's regulatory capital by $1.9 billion and its CET1 ratio from 12.1 percent to 10.4 percent at the end of 2022 [1]. That adjustment covers AFS losses only. The HTM losses, about six times larger, were carried at amortized cost and did not change the assets' balance-sheet value [1][2], which is why the Basel Committee asks whether regulatory capital overstates loss-absorbing capacity when such losses are not reflected [4].

A full mark-to-market view is harsher. Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski and Seru valued bank assets with traded bond and mortgage indexes and estimated that SVB's assets lost 15.7 percent ($34 billion) of value between the first quarters of 2022 and 2023 [5]. Its book equity-to-assets ratio of 6.7 percent became -10.7 percent against marked assets, a measure the authors treat as an extreme benchmark because it ignores the value of a bank's deposit franchise [5]. On losses alone SVB was not an outlier: about 11 percent of US banks, more than 500, had worse marked-to-market losses [5].

Management also reduced protection while the risk grew. SVBFG began removing interest-rate hedges in March 2022, treating rising rates as an opportunity to take profits on hedges that would have constrained net interest income if rates fell, and removed the rest in July 2022; the strategy extended the duration of the securities portfolio [1]. Jiang and coauthors report that SVB hedged about 12 percent of its securities at the end of 2021 and 0.4 percent at the end of 2022 [5]. The Inspector General called the complete removal of hedges "a significant error" by management [2]. The Board review found that the bank managed interest-rate risk for short-run profit and protection against falling rates, and that it changed its own risk-management assumptions, including deposit duration assumptions, so that measured risk fell without the underlying risk being addressed [1].

### Uninsured, Concentrated, Networked Deposits

The second mechanism was the liability side. Each source measures the uninsured share differently, and the measures agree on the conclusion. About 94 percent of SVBFG's total deposits were uninsured at year-end 2022, against a 41 percent average for large banking organizations [1]. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) estimated that 88 percent of deposits were uninsured at the point of failure [10]. GAO found uninsured deposits equal to 70-80 percent of SVB's total assets in every year from 2018 to 2022, roughly double the 31-41 percent peer median [3]. Jiang and coauthors found that uninsured deposits funded 78 percent of SVB's assets in early 2022, placing it in the 99th percentile of US banks [5].

The deposit base was also concentrated in one industry and one social network. SVBFG's clients were heavily concentrated in venture-capital-backed and early-stage firms [1], and some loan agreements required borrowers to keep their primary operating deposit accounts at SVB [1]. A March 2019 examination report already noted that private-equity and venture-capital deposits had irregular, large cash flows and that SVB's funding was concentrated and potentially volatile on short notice [2]. Such clients held large cash balances for payroll and operating expenses [3], and they and their investors communicated intensively on social media [6].

Jiang and coauthors explain why this mattered even though 62 percent of SVB's assets were cash and securities that could be sold [5]. In the classic model of bank runs, runs happen because assets are illiquid. In their model of a "self-fulfilling solvency run", liquid assets do not protect a bank once rising rates have cut their market value: if enough uninsured depositors expect others to withdraw, the marked value of assets left behind may not cover remaining claims, and withdrawing first becomes rational [5]. SVB's marked assets were barely enough to cover its uninsured deposits, and on the authors' capital-ratio measure the bank would fail if half of its uninsured depositors withdrew [5]. Barr summarized the same point: the proximate cause of failure was a liquidity run, but the underlying issue was concern about solvency [1].

### Contingency Liquidity That Existed on Paper

The third mechanism was operational. A November 2021 liquidity review found "foundational shortcomings" in SVB's internal liquidity stress testing, limits framework and contingency funding plan, and FRBSF issued two matters requiring immediate attention and four matters requiring attention [1][3]. After SVBFG became subject to enhanced prudential standards under Regulation YY by crossing $100 billion, it repeatedly failed its own internal liquidity stress tests, and in October 2022 it changed methodologies for unfunded lending commitments and intraday liquidity in ways that reduced the modeled 30-day requirement by about $8 billion and $5 billion respectively [1].

When the run came, the plans could not be executed at speed. SVB had not tested its ability to borrow at the discount window in 2022 and could not quickly move collateral from its custody bank or the Federal Home Loan Bank to the discount window [1]. FRBSF staff told GAO that on the morning of March 10 the bank did not have enough collateral to borrow against its expected obligations [3]. The Board review judged that stronger operational capacity would likely not have prevented the failure but could have facilitated a more orderly resolution [1][4]. The same gap appeared in resolution planning: SVB filed its first insured-depository resolution plan on December 1, 2022, and FDIC staff's preliminary view was that it did not list potential acquirers or detail crisis communication and liquidity needs [3].

### Governance and Incentives

The fourth mechanism was governance. The Board review's first takeaway is that SVB's board and management failed to manage their risks: the full board did not receive adequate risk information and did not hold management accountable [1]. The Basel Committee notes that management's updates to the board did not highlight liquidity problems until November 2022, and that a director told supervisors in 2022 that "controls always lag growth" [4]. The firm said in February 2022 that it would replace its chief risk officer, who left in April 2022; the post stayed vacant until December 2022, and supervisors chose not to cite the vacancy as a violation of the enhanced prudential standards [1].

Incentives pointed the same way. Senior executives' bonuses and equity awards were based on return on equity and total shareholder return, did not include risk metrics, and were left unchanged for 2021 even though executives had not met the objective of building risk management to large-firm standards; the only executive whose pay was cut for that failure was the chief risk officer [1]. The compensation committee declined to reduce pay because it feared attrition [1]. Cash bonuses for 2022 performance were paid to several executives and staff on March 10, 2023, the day the bank failed [1]. When it failed, SVBFG had 31 open supervisory findings, about three times the number at peer firms [1][4].

### Supervision That Identified Problems but Did Not Compel Correction

The fifth mechanism was supervisory. FRBSF rated SVB's liquidity 1, the best grade, from 2018 through 2021, and its overall condition satisfactory until 2022, even though examiners had noted concentrated and potentially volatile funding as early as 2018 [3]. Once SVBFG entered the LFBO portfolio, the new team found deficiencies the earlier program had not identified: in August 2022 it rated governance and controls "Deficient-1" and liquidity "Conditionally Meets Expectations", and in June 2022 it had downgraded the bank's composite and management ratings to 3 [3]. The Inspector General found that SVB was the first bank to move from the RBO to the LFBO portfolio since the Dodd-Frank enhanced standards and that there was no playbook for the transition [2].

Escalation was slow. A memorandum of understanding, an informal enforcement action, took more than seven months to develop and had not been delivered when the bank failed [1][4]. GAO found that FRBSF did not recommend a single enforcement action before the failure and that its actions "lacked urgency" [3]. Interest-rate risk work lagged most: a November 2022 supervisory letter found SVB's interest-rate simulations unreliable [3], and the LFBO team did not conduct an interest-rate risk review before the failure; it had scheduled one for May 2023, which the Inspector General called too late [2]. At failure, SVB's CAMELS composite and management ratings were 3 and its liquidity and market-sensitivity ratings 2, ratings the Inspector General said "did not align with the bank's true condition" [2].

The Board review links these delays to resources and stance. Scheduled supervision hours for SVBFG fell more than 40 percent between 2017 and 2020, and from 2016 to 2022 Federal Reserve supervision headcount fell 3 percent while banking assets grew 37 percent [1]. It also describes an approach that emphasized consensus and the continued accumulation of evidence even as SVBFG deteriorated, and a shift in the stance of supervisory policy that was amplified by the COVID-19 pandemic [1][4]. Surveillance was not blind: a June 2022 special report on unrealized securities losses flagged the issue, and a February 14, 2023 briefing to the Board of Governors named SVBFG as a firm with "significant safety and soundness risks", but the briefing was informational rather than focused on the significant risks it described [1][2].

### Speed: Announcement, Social Media and Digital Withdrawals

The final mechanism was speed. On March 8, 2023, SVBFG announced that it had sold $21 billion of AFS securities at a $1.8 billion after-tax loss, planned to raise $2.25 billion of capital and to increase term borrowings from $15 billion to $30 billion, and that Moody's and S&P were considering negative rating actions; the same day Silvergate Capital announced the wind-down of Silvergate Bank [1]. Uninsured depositors read the announcement as a signal of distress [1]. On March 9 they withdrew about $42 billion, almost 25 percent of SVB's $166 billion of deposits, and requests pending for March 10 totaled about $100 billion [2]. Together these represented roughly 85 percent of the deposit base in two days, against about $10 billion over 8 days at Wachovia and $19 billion over 16 days at Washington Mutual in 2008 [1].

Barr's cover letter argues that social media, a highly networked and concentrated depositor base, and technology that allowed instant withdrawals "may have fundamentally changed the speed of bank runs" [1]. Interviewees told the Inspector General that SVBFG could have managed the messaging better by announcing completed actions rather than intentions, and that management had assumed client loyalty would keep deposits in place [2].

The resolution had three parts. First, the systemic risk exception: under the Federal Deposit Insurance Act the FDIC must normally choose the resolution that costs the Deposit Insurance Fund least, which generally bars protecting uninsured depositors at the fund's expense; the exception requires recommendations from the FDIC and Federal Reserve boards and a determination by the Treasury Secretary in consultation with the President [3][10]. The FDIC and Federal Reserve argued that imposing losses on uninsured depositors would intensify runs at other banks and disrupt payrolls; some SVB depositors had sold their uninsured claims at 90 cents on the dollar on March 10 to meet payroll [3]. The joint statement promised that all depositors would have access to their money on March 13, that shareholders and certain unsecured debtholders would not be protected, that senior management had been removed, and that any loss to the fund would be recovered through a special assessment on banks rather than borne by taxpayers [7].

Second, the Bank Term Funding Program offered loans of up to one year against Treasury, agency and agency mortgage-backed collateral valued at par rather than market value, with up to $25 billion of Treasury Exchange Stabilization Fund money as a backstop, so that banks would not need to sell such securities quickly in times of stress [8]. Third, the bridge bank was sold: First-Citizens bought about $72 billion of assets at a $16.5 billion discount, about $90 billion of securities and other assets stayed in receivership, and the FDIC received equity appreciation rights worth up to $500 million [9].

## Evidence

### Federal Reserve Board Review (April 2023)

Method: staff not involved in SVB's supervision examined the supervisory record and internal communications from 2017 to March 8, 2023, ran independent analysis and interviewed Federal Reserve staff; the Board also released normally confidential supervisory material [1]. Findings: four takeaways -- the board and management failed to manage risk; supervisors did not appreciate the vulnerabilities as the firm grew; when they did identify problems they did not ensure quick correction; and the tailoring approach after EGRRCPA together with a shift in supervisory stance "impeded effective supervision by reducing standards, increasing complexity, and promoting a less assertive supervisory approach" [1]. The review also ran counterfactuals. SVBFG was not subject to the liquidity coverage ratio under the tailored rules; under the full ratio that pre-2019 rules would have applied, its estimated ratio would have fallen from 99.3 percent in March 2022 to 73.2 percent in September 2022 and 82.6 percent in February 2023, a shortfall of about $8 billion of high-quality liquid assets at the end of 2022 and about $14 billion in February 2023, while its net stable funding ratio would have stayed above 100 percent [1]. The review concludes that the stronger requirements would have bolstered resilience and might have led SVBFG to manage liquidity and capital more proactively, while noting that the firm might also have managed its size to avoid crossing the thresholds [1].

### Inspector General Material Loss Review (September 2023)

Method: a statutory review, required when a failure causes a material loss to the Deposit Insurance Fund, of FRBSF and Board supervision from January 2018 through March 2023, based on documents and interviews [2]. Findings: SVB failed because of a concentrated business model, rapid growth, concentrations in uninsured deposits and in long-dated securities, weak governance and risk management, and poorly communicated capital plans [2]. On supervision, it found that FRBSF met examination-frequency requirements but that in both the RBO and LFBO periods the Board and FRBSF "should have acted earlier or taken stronger action to address identified weaknesses", including earlier CAMELS downgrades [2]. Resource figures illustrate the RBO period: FRBSF spent 7,919 and 6,872 hours supervising SVB in 2019 and 2020, while its planning model had recommended 4,870 and 3,920 hours for a bank that grew from almost $70 billion to over $100 billion in assets [2]. The report made seven recommendations on supervisory processes and the RBO-to-LFBO transition, and the Board concurred [2].

### GAO Preliminary Review (April 2023)

Method: GAO compared SVB's and Signature Bank's 2018-2022 financial data with 19 peer banks and reviewed examination records, systemic-risk memoranda and Bank Term Funding Program documents from March 21 to April 28, 2023 [3]. Findings: both banks grew far faster than peers, relied on uninsured deposits that were about twice the peer median relative to assets, and were slow to fix problems regulators had identified, while regulators did not escalate in time [3]. GAO tied the escalation failure to its 2011 recommendation that prompt corrective action include noncapital triggers, because capital is a lagging indicator; regulators had considered but not adopted such triggers [3]. GAO also reported that outstanding Bank Term Funding Program advances were about $74 billion on April 19, 2023, and that discount window borrowing had peaked at about $153 billion on March 15 [3].

### Mark-to-Market Losses and Uninsured Leverage Across US Banks

Method: Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski and Seru marked the loans and securities of all FDIC-insured banks to market using call-report maturity data and traded Treasury and mortgage indexes for Q1 2022 to Q1 2023, then built a model of runs on banks with liquid assets [5]. Findings: US bank assets were worth about $2.2 trillion less than book value, a decline averaging about 10 percent and equal to 96 percent of pre-tightening aggregate bank capital [5]. SVB stood out not on losses but on uninsured leverage. Absent intervention and assuming no fire-sale discounts, if all uninsured depositors withdrew, 1,619 banks with $4.9 trillion of assets would have marked assets too small to repay insured deposits in full; if half withdrew, 186 banks with about $300 billion of assets would [5]. Citing related work by Granja and coauthors, they report that banks with the most fragile funding, like SVB, reduced hedges during the tightening, and they argue that the March 12 interventions may have short-circuited wider runs but did not address solvency-run risk itself [5].

### Social Media and the Run

Method: Cookson, Fox, Gil-Bazo, Imbet and Schiller collected all English original tweets with bank cashtags from January 2020 to March 13, 2023, and related pre-run Twitter exposure and in-run tweet activity to bank stock returns, hourly returns, five-minute returns around tweets, and first-quarter 2023 deposit outflows [6]. Findings in the August 2023 version: SVB had 6,528 "run" tweets from March 8 to March 13, about five times the next bank (First Republic); conversation began with investor tweets using the ticker and then spread to general discussion of the bank, with tweets from startup-community users rising after the first wave; and banks with high pre-run Twitter exposure suffered larger run-period stock losses and larger uninsured deposit outflows, especially when they combined unrealized losses with uninsured funding [6]. A January 18, 2023 Twitter thread on SVB's HTM losses and uninsured deposits had attracted little attention until it went viral during the run [6].

### Basel Committee Cross-Jurisdiction Comparison (October 2023)

Method: the Basel Committee compiled the failures of SVB, Signature Bank and First Republic and the rescue of Credit Suisse from national reports and member input [4]. Findings: four banks with about $900 billion of assets were shut down, put in receivership or rescued in 11 days, the most significant system-wide banking stress since the global financial crisis [4]. Its comparison of deposit outflows puts SVB at 85 percent in two days, against 20 percent in four days for Northern Rock in 2007, 10 percent in 10 days for Washington Mutual and 8 percent in 14 days for IndyMac in 2008, while liquidity coverage ratio outflow assumptions range from 3 to 40 percent over 30 days [4]. It concluded that banks may not be able to rely on a 30-day window to address liquidity problems, and it raised the question whether proportionality frameworks can impede effective supervision by reducing standards and promoting a less assertive approach [4].

### The Cost Record

The FDIC first estimated the cost of SVB's failure to the Deposit Insurance Fund at about $20 billion [9], then about $16.1 billion in May 2023 [2], then $17.8 billion in November 2023 [10]. Applying the 88 percent uninsured share to that total, it attributed $15.7 billion to the protection of uninsured depositors, and $16.3 billion across SVB and Signature Bank [10]. The special assessment charged that amount to banks on their estimated uninsured deposits at December 31, 2022 above $5 billion; the FDIC estimated 114 banking organizations would pay [10], and its April 2026 record shows 110 organizations paid over eight quarters, with the recoverable total then estimated at about $16.7 billion pending litigation with SVB Financial Trust and the end of the receiverships [11]. In August 2026, after a 12-day bench trial, a federal district court in California ruled that the trust, successor to the bankrupt holding company, could not recover about $1.7 billion the parent had held at the bank. The court found that officers had breached fiduciary duties by causing the bank to take excessive interest-rate and liquidity risk, by removing partial hedges, and by approving a $294 million bank-to-parent dividend while the bank was in dire condition, but it did not find bad-faith misconduct; a separate FDIC suit against former officers was pending [12].

## Implications

### For Boards and Executives

The case shows how a board can be informed of individual risks and still miss their combination. SVB's board and its committees received supervisory findings, notice of risk-limit breaches and a chief risk officer vacancy, but the board often treated supervisory issues as a compliance exercise, and its compensation committee kept pay tied to return on equity and shareholder return [1][4]. The Board review found that SVBFG's risk appetite included a net interest income metric but not the economic value of equity; breaches of the longer-run measure went to the board's Risk Committee, and there is no evidence that the full board knew the measure had been breaching limits for years [1]. Interpretation: a board's risk appetite needs at least one measure of long-run value alongside next year's earnings, and compensation needs a link to risk outcomes. In the author's assessment, the clearest warning sign at SVB was not any single ratio but the repeated change of model assumptions when limits were breached, which turned risk limits into reporting conventions [1].

### For Risk Managers and Treasurers

Three operational lessons follow from the sources. Interest-rate exposure should be measured on economic value, including HTM securities, because an accounting classification does not change what the assets would fetch in a forced sale [1][4][5]. Deposit behavior should be modeled by segment, with concentrated, uninsured, networked balances treated as potentially fast money; regulators had asked SVB for exactly this deposit segmentation [3]. Contingency funding must be tested: collateral pre-positioned, discount window borrowing rehearsed, and custody and Home Loan Bank arrangements checked for how fast collateral can move [1][4]. The Basel Committee suggests more focus on the liquidity coverage ratio's role to "buy time" and warns that banks may not always be able to rely on a 30-day window to address liquidity problems [4].

### For Supervisors and Regulators

The supervisory lesson concerns escalation rather than detection. FRBSF and the Board identified liquidity, governance and interest-rate weaknesses, and Board surveillance flagged unrealized losses, yet no enforcement action was issued [1][2][3]. The Board review proposed changing supervisory culture, including tools such as pre-mortems and reverse stress tests, and revisiting the tailoring framework to reduce cliff effects at size thresholds [1]. GAO's noncapital-trigger proposal addresses the same gap from the legal side: a trigger tied to unsafe practices could force action before capital erodes [3]. The Basel Committee frames the issue as the need for supervisory judgement to complement rules, so that supervisors can act on an outlier business model even when no ratio is breached [4]. The later rulemaking status of these proposals is outside the scope of this case.

### For Crisis Communicators

The March 8 announcement combined a realized loss, a planned capital raise and possible rating actions on the same day another crypto-exposed bank announced its liquidation [1][3]. Interviewees told the Inspector General that announcing completed actions instead of intentions would have been safer [2]. The run also showed that depositor communication now happens on public platforms where investors, founders and venture firms read one another in near real time [6]. Interpretation: crisis plans for a bank with concentrated, networked depositors need a sequencing plan in which capital is secured before losses are disclosed, and monitoring of the client community's own channels; the FDIC's preliminary finding that SVB's resolution plan lacked crisis-communication detail points to the same gap [3].

### For Depositors and Corporate Treasurers

For firms that kept operating cash at SVB, the risk was practical: several SVB depositors could not make payroll at the end of the week, payroll processors that banked at SVB delayed paychecks for other companies, and some depositors sold their claims at 90 cents on the dollar [3]. Interpretation: corporate treasurers can reduce this exposure by spreading operating accounts across institutions and by checking whether lending terms require deposits to be kept at the lender, as some SVB loan agreements did [1][3].

### For Deposit-Guarantee Policy

The systemic risk exception protected all depositors, and Federal Reserve staff acknowledged at the time that this could worsen moral hazard and weaken market discipline [3]. The cost was charged to the banks that, in the FDIC's view, benefited most -- large banks with uninsured deposits [10]. Jiang and coauthors argue that liquidity support and deposit guarantees stem runs but do not remove the solvency risk created by unhedged duration and high uninsured leverage, and that recapitalization or higher capital addresses the latter [5]. The case therefore leaves open how far uninsured deposits are de facto insured, and the sources do not resolve that question.

### For Investors and Credit Analysts

Market signals were mixed before the run. As of March 1, 2023, 12 equity analysts covering SVBFG rated it a buy, 11 a hold and one a sell, while short interest had been rising since April 2022, roughly when unrealized losses began to accumulate [1]. The share price had peaked in November 2021 and declined through 2022, but it was relatively stable until the March 8 announcement [1]. The data needed for a warning were public: unrealized HTM losses are disclosed in financial statements [1], estimated uninsured deposits are reported in call reports [5][6], and Jiang and coauthors built their marked-to-market and uninsured-leverage measures entirely from public filings and market indexes [5]. Interpretation: an analyst who netted disclosed unrealized losses against equity and set the result beside the share of runnable funding would have seen SVB's exposure even though no reported regulatory ratio was in breach; Cookson and coauthors add that a bank's exposure to public online conversation is itself a run-risk amplifier [6].

### How the Case Differs From 2008

Interpretation: the library's 2008 case study (see See Also) centers on credit losses in complex securities and runs on wholesale funding, while SVB held mostly government or agency securities with low credit risk and failed through interest-rate losses combined with a run by uninsured depositors [1][5]. Barr's letter adds a further difference: contagion spread from a bank that was not extremely large or highly connected to other financial counterparties [1]. Signature Bank failed two days later and First Republic in May, and both shared high uninsured funding and large declines in asset value [4][5].

## Limits of the Evidence

The official reviews were written within weeks or months of the failure: the Board review describes itself as compressed and written with hindsight [1], and GAO labels its findings preliminary [3]. The Board review covers the Federal Reserve's own conduct and excludes events after March 8 [1], and the Inspector General's review likewise focuses on the causes of failure and on Federal Reserve supervision [2]. The academic studies estimate exposures with public data: Jiang and coauthors mark only part of bank balance sheets and cannot predict which banks will actually run, since their model has multiple equilibria [5]. The social-media study proxies run severity with stock returns and quarterly deposits; it has been revised since the August 2023 version cited here (SSRN record last revised August 2025), so its estimates should be read with the published version in view [6].
