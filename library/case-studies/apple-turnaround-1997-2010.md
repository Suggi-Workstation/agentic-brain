# The Apple Turnaround (1997-2010) -- Focus and an Integrated Product System Rebuilt the Company

Apple's recovery was not the result of one hit product. From 1997 through
2010, the company combined a narrower product portfolio, integrated hardware
and software, direct retail distribution, and new content and application
platforms; the elements reinforced one another while operating discipline
converted product demand into cash and profit. [1][2][5][6][10]

## Background

Apple entered 1997 after two consecutive annual losses and a severe decline in
sales. Its fiscal 1997 Form 10-K reported net sales of $7.081 billion, down 28
percent from 1996, and a net loss of $1.045 billion following an $816 million
loss in 1996. The same filing described lower unit shipments and personal
computer market share, intense price competition, product-line simplification,
headcount reductions, facility closures, more outsourced manufacturing, and an
online store as parts of the restructuring already under way. [1] Contemporary
market data reported by CNET put Apple's worldwide personal-computer share at
3.3 percent in the third quarter of 1997, down from 5.5 percent a year earlier;
that measure was a quarterly shipment estimate, not a universal measure of all
Apple users or revenue. [14]

The crisis was both financial and strategic. Apple's fiscal second-quarter
1997 net loss was $708 million, but the full-year balance sheet still showed
$1.459 billion of cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments. [1][2]
Steve Jobs later said that Apple had been 90 days from bankruptcy when he took
over. That statement is best treated as Jobs's retrospective description of
Apple's forecast and operating trajectory, not as a literal claim that the
reported balance sheet held only 90 days of cash. [4] Distinguishing the two
matters: the company faced a credible survival problem even though an audited
year-end liquidity figure by itself did not show immediate cash exhaustion.

Apple had also failed to deliver a modern successor to the classic Mac OS.
It completed the acquisition of NeXT in February 1997. Apple's filings put the
total purchase price, including assumed net liabilities, at $427 million and
explained that NeXT's technology would contribute to the future Mac OS X.
[1][2] The acquisition brought Jobs back to Apple, and Apple named him interim
chief executive on September 16, 1997, not in July as the previous version of
this topic stated. [1][3] His formal title matters because the turnaround
combined work begun under the board and prior management with decisions made
after Jobs gained operating authority.

The portfolio Jobs inherited was broader than Apple could support. The company
sold multiple Macintosh families as well as printers, displays, MessagePad and
eMate handheld products, and other peripherals. Apple's 1998 Form 10-K records
the measurable simplification more precisely than later retellings: during
1998 Apple moved from approximately 15 separate individual products to three
main product families, significantly reduced imaging and display products,
and discontinued MessagePad and eMate. [2] In a 1997 CNBC interview, Jobs said
Apple was investing equal or greater resources in fewer things so that it could
do a better job on them. [3] The often-repeated four-quadrant whiteboard story
is consistent with that direction, but the audited filing provides the firmer
contemporaneous evidence for the scale of the reduction.

The early recovery was not produced by product focus alone. Apple's 1998 filing
attributes the return to profitability to several concurrent changes: lower
operating expenses from restructuring, a gross-margin recovery from 19 percent
to 25 percent, better inventory management, product-line simplification, and
changes to distribution policies. Operating expenses excluding cost of sales
and special charges fell by $560 million, or 32 percent, from 1997 to 1998. [2]
Those measures included difficult reductions: the combined 1996-1998
restructuring program contemplated terminating about 4,200 full-time employees,
closing or leaving facilities, outsourcing functions, and canceling projects
that management did not consider critical. [2] A case study that attributes the
turnaround only to design or Jobs's personality therefore omits a material part
of the documented mechanism.

The iMac supplied visible product evidence that the narrowed organization could
still create demand. Introduced in August 1998 for consumer and education
markets, it accounted for 278,000 units, or 33 percent of all Macintosh units,
in Apple's fiscal fourth quarter. Apple earned net income in each quarter of
fiscal 1998 and $309 million for the full year, compared with the $1.045 billion
loss in 1997. Yet fiscal 1998 revenue still fell 16 percent, to $5.941 billion.
[2] The first year was thus a stabilization and profitability recovery, not yet
a return to sustained top-line growth. That distinction prevents the iMac's
commercial success from being mistaken for the whole turnaround.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Focus Was an Allocation System, Not a Slogan

Product cancellation changed where engineering time, management attention,
working capital, marketing expenditure, and channel inventory were allocated.
Apple's 1998 filing connects the smaller portfolio to improved demand
forecasting, lower inventory exposure, more use of standard parts, and reduced
component cost. Inventory fell from $437 million at fiscal year-end 1997 to $78
million in 1998, while days of inventory fell from 31 to six. [2] Those figures
do not prove that every dollar of improvement came from simplification, and the
filing also credits restructuring and distribution changes. They do show why
focus can affect both product quality and the balance sheet.

The transferable mechanism is concentration under constraint. When a company
has too many overlapping offers, each one creates forecasting, purchasing,
software-support, sales-training, and service obligations. Removing a product
can release resources across all of those functions rather than only in the
engineering group. The author's assessment is that Apple's focus worked because
management connected portfolio choices to operating changes; merely announcing
fewer strategic priorities without removing budgets, inventory, and support
obligations would not have produced the same result.

Focus also created a clearer decision hierarchy. Apple did not stop research
and development. Its 1998 filing says the company recognized focused investment
in research and development as critical, even after research and development
expense fell during the restructuring. [2] Jobs's 1997 description was likewise
not "spend less on everything" but invest at least as much in fewer things. [3]
This is the difference between indiscriminate austerity and strategic
concentration. The former can shrink a company without improving its products;
the latter attempts to increase the resources and accountability attached to a
small number of chosen outcomes.

### 2. Integration Turned Design Into a System Property

By 2010 Apple described its business strategy as using its ability to design its
own operating systems, hardware, application software, and services to provide
ease of use, seamless integration, and industrial design. [10] That formulation
is more precise than saying Apple won because its products looked better.
Industrial design was one layer in a controlled system that also included the
operating system, applications, device interfaces, content delivery, service,
and distribution.

The iMac was an early demonstration. Apple's 1998 filing identifies its
industrial design, Internet access, and PowerPC G3 processor as a combined
consumer proposition; it also records the decision to discontinue several
other product lines and focus resources on the remaining families. [2] The
product's commercial effect therefore cannot be isolated from the portfolio and
operating context that made it possible. A distinctive enclosure attracted
attention, but a coherent product required component sourcing, software,
manufacturing, marketing, and channel execution.

Integration also let Apple remove features while preserving a usable whole.
That logic is different from feature minimization for its own sake. A removed
component improves a product only if the surrounding system supplies a better
path for the user's task. The author's assessment is that Apple's strongest
simplifications were architectural: the company controlled enough adjacent
layers to remove complexity at one point and absorb it elsewhere. Competitors
could imitate a visible enclosure or interface without necessarily reproducing
the coordination among hardware, software, services, and support.

### 3. The Digital Hub Connected Separate Products Into a Portfolio

In January 2001 Jobs described the Mac as a prospective hub for a growing set of
digital devices, including music players, cameras, camcorders, phones, and
handheld organizers. Macworld's contemporary report quotes him arguing that the
computer could add value through storage, applications, a large display, disc
creation, and Internet connectivity. [5] This strategy changed the unit of
competition. Instead of evaluating each device as an isolated object, Apple
could make the value of a Mac depend partly on how well it managed other digital
activities.

The iPod and iTunes made that idea commercially concrete. Apple reported the
sale of the 100 millionth iPod in April 2007, five and a half years after the
first sale. At that point it also reported more than 2.5 billion songs sold
through iTunes and an ecosystem of more than 4,000 iPod accessories. [7] In
April 2008 Apple said iTunes had passed Walmart to become the largest music
retailer in the United States, based on NPD's MusicWatch unit methodology for
January and February 2008. [8] The denominator and method matter: the claim was
about music units under NPD's conversion of one CD to 12 tracks, not total
retail revenue or every category of entertainment.

This combination produced complementary demand. The device made the software
and store more useful; the store and software reduced the work required to
acquire, organize, and synchronize content; accessory makers expanded the
number of contexts in which the device could be used. [7] The author's
assessment is that this was the economic bridge from a computer turnaround to a
broader consumer-platform strategy. Apple did not merely enter an adjacent
hardware category; it used the Mac, iTunes, content relationships, and the iPod
as a coordinated system.

Claims of permanent lock-in require qualification. Early iTunes purchases could
carry digital-rights restrictions, but Apple launched higher-quality DRM-free
"iTunes Plus" tracks in 2007, initially with EMI's catalog. [13] Switching costs
therefore changed over time and did not consist simply of forcing customers to
repurchase all music. The more defensible claim is that convenience,
synchronization, accessories, accumulated familiarity, and later applications
created several overlapping reasons to remain within the system.

### 4. Owned Retail Joined Distribution, Education, and Feedback

Apple announced its first two stores for May 19, 2001, at Tysons Corner in
Virginia and the Glendale Galleria in California. The launch described stores
organized around hands-on use, demonstrations, software, complementary digital
devices, trained staff, and the Genius Bar rather than only boxes on shelves.
[6] The strategic problem was not simply a shortage of outlets. Apple wanted a
setting in which customers could understand what integrated hardware and
software allowed them to do.

By fiscal year-end 2010 Apple had 317 stores: 233 in the United States and 84
internationally. Its filing states that the stores were placed in high-traffic
locations to control the buying experience, attract new customers, demonstrate
product advantages, and provide service and training. Retail net sales reached
$9.798 billion in fiscal 2010; with an average of 288 stores, average revenue per
store was $34.1 million, up from $26.2 million in 2009. [10] These are segment
figures reported by Apple, not an independent experiment showing what sales
would have been without stores.

Owned retail also carried risk. Apple's filing notes the fixed investment,
lease commitments, personnel costs, and potential closure costs associated with
the segment. [10] The transferable concept is therefore not that every producer
should own stores. It is that direct distribution can be justified when product
value is difficult for intermediaries to explain and when the producer can earn
an adequate return on the fixed cost. Apple's stores linked demonstration,
sales, service, and brand presentation; the economics must be evaluated with all
four functions in view.

### 5. The App Store Changed the iPhone From a Product Into a Governed Platform

By 2010 Apple described iPhone as a mobile communication and media device with
access to iTunes content and applications, while the App Store supplied a
distribution channel for third-party software. [10] By January 2010 Apple
reported more than three billion application downloads in less than 18 months,
available across 20 categories in 77 countries. [9] In fiscal 2010 Apple sold
39.989 million iPhones, compared with 20.731 million in 2009, while the newly
introduced iPad sold 7.458 million units before fiscal year-end. [10]

The platform mechanism differs from ordinary product extension. Developers
invest when they expect access to users and a workable distribution and payment
system; users value a device more when useful applications are available.
Apple's role included setting technical interfaces, reviewing distribution,
operating the storefront, processing transactions, and retaining control over
the device and operating system. The author's assessment is that the same
integration that made the platform attractive also made Apple a gatekeeper.
This created both an advantage and a governance obligation: platform rules can
improve security and consistency, but they also determine who may reach users
and on what terms.

The iPad showed reuse of the platform rather than a wholly separate ecosystem.
Apple's 2010 filing described iPad access to iTunes content and applications,
and its unit table recorded 7.458 million sales in the partial fiscal year after
launch. [10] Existing software, developer relationships, accounts, content, and
retail stores reduced the number of capabilities that had to be created from
zero. This is a system-level form of economies of scope: a new device can draw
on assets built for earlier products.

### 6. The Turnaround Was Sequenced, but the Capabilities Reinforced One Another

Apple first had to stop losses and simplify operations; it then used focused
computer products to restore credibility, expanded into digital music, built
direct retail, and finally scaled a mobile application platform. The sequence
matters because a distressed company cannot assume that later platform effects
will arrive before near-term liquidity and product problems are solved.
Apple's filings show the initial recovery in margins, expenses, inventory, and
profit before the large mobile-device revenue expansion. [1][2][10]

The capabilities were nevertheless cumulative rather than independent. The
Mac and iTunes supplied software and synchronization capabilities for iPod.
The iPod expanded Apple's consumer reach before iPhone. Retail stores supplied
demonstration and service for devices whose value depended on experience.
The iPhone supplied users and distribution for developers; the App Store made
the device more useful. [5][6][7][9][10] The author's assessment is that this
reinforcement, rather than any single launch, explains the durability of the
recovery through 2010.

This interpretation also limits the hero narrative. Jobs made central portfolio,
product, and organizational decisions, but the measured outcomes came from a
company-wide system of designers, engineers, operations staff, content
negotiators, developers, retail employees, suppliers, and customers. The 1998
filing explicitly attributes profitability to several operational causes, and
the 2010 filing describes a strategy spanning multiple technical and commercial
layers. [2][10] Leadership mattered because it aligned and constrained that
system, not because one person independently produced every result.

## Evidence

### Audited Before-and-After Financial Record

The strongest evidence of a completed turnaround is the change in audited
financial outcomes. Apple's fiscal 1997 filing reported $7.081 billion of net
sales, a $1.045 billion net loss, and $1.459 billion of cash, cash equivalents,
and short-term investments. [1] Its fiscal 2010 filing reported $65.225 billion
of net sales, $14.013 billion of net income, and $51.011 billion of cash, cash
equivalents, and marketable securities. [10] Using the unrounded reported sales
figures, revenue increased 9.21 times; over the 13 fiscal-year intervals, that
is an 18.63 percent compound annual growth rate. The calculation describes the
endpoint change and does not identify causality.

The intermediate 1998 result helps separate stabilization from later growth.
Apple returned to profit in all four quarters and earned $309 million for the
year, while revenue declined to $5.941 billion. Gross margin rose from 19 to 25
percent, operating expenses excluding cost of sales and special charges fell 32
percent, and year-end inventory fell from $437 million to $78 million. [2]
Methodologically, these figures are a within-company time comparison drawn from
the audited filing. They support the claim that operating restructuring and
focus restored economic viability before the later expansion; they do not show
what would have happened under a different strategy.

Fiscal 2010 product data show how far the revenue base had shifted. Apple sold
13.662 million Macs, 50.312 million iPods, 39.989 million iPhones, and 7.458
million iPads during that fiscal year. [10] The company was no longer dependent
on one personal-computer line. This product diversification does not contradict
focus: Apple had expanded into a small number of large families connected by
shared software, stores, accounts, and services rather than returning to the
fragmented set of unrelated or overlapping projects documented in the late
1990s.

### Product and Content Adoption

The iMac provides the first product-level test of the focused portfolio. In the
fiscal fourth quarter of 1998, Apple shipped 278,000 iMacs, one-third of all Mac
units in the quarter; total Mac units rose 30 percent from the preceding quarter.
[2] Because the iMac shipped only in August, the observation covers a short
period and coincided with other restructuring changes. It is evidence of rapid
initial demand, not proof that design alone caused the return to profitability.

The iPod/iTunes combination supplies a longer adoption record. Apple reported
100 million cumulative iPod sales by April 2007, more than 2.5 billion iTunes
song sales, and more than 4,000 iPod-specific accessories. [7] A year later it
reported that iTunes had become the largest U.S. music retailer under NPD's
unit-based MusicWatch method, with more than four billion songs sold and more
than 50 million customers. [8] The method combines company-reported cumulative
figures with a named third-party market survey. It supports the claim that the
system achieved material scale across devices, content, and complements.

The App Store supplies evidence for the later platform stage. Apple's January
2010 release reported three billion downloads in less than 18 months. [9] The
company's fiscal filing then records 39.989 million iPhones sold in 2010, up 93
percent from 2009. [10] These observations cannot isolate whether applications
caused device growth or device growth attracted applications; platform effects
are reciprocal by design. They do establish that both the distribution channel
and the hardware base reached large scale within the review period.

### Retail as a Measured Operating Segment

Retail can be evaluated with disclosed operating data rather than anecdotes
about launch-day skepticism. Apple opened the first two stores in May 2001 with
a stated emphasis on customer experience, demonstrations, software, digital
accessories, and support. [6] By 2010 it operated 317 stores and reported $9.798
billion of retail net sales. Average revenue per store was $34.1 million, and
retail operating income was $2.4 billion. [10]

The evidence has limitations. Segment sales include purchases that might have
occurred through another channel, and Apple's filings do not provide a
randomized counterfactual for stores. Retail expansion also required $2.2
billion of cumulative capital-asset purchases through 2010 and substantial
leases. [10] The defensible finding is narrower than "stores caused the
turnaround": owned retail became a large, profitable channel that also performed
demonstration, support, and brand functions consistent with Apple's integrated
strategy.

### Endpoint and External Market Confirmation

The endpoint was visible beyond Apple's own reporting. On August 9, 2011, The
Mercury News reported that Apple briefly passed Exxon Mobil intraday as the
most valuable U.S. public company, although Exxon finished that day ahead. [12]
Apple finished above Exxon at the close on August 10, 2011, with an approximately
$337 billion market capitalization according to Associated Press reporting.
[15] The distinction corrects the previous topic's imprecise statement that
Apple simply became the world's most valuable company "by 2011" without a date
or market-close qualification.

Apple's fiscal 2011 filing, which covers the year in which Jobs resigned as CEO,
reported $108.249 billion of revenue, $25.922 billion of net income, and $81.570
billion of cash, cash equivalents, and marketable securities. It also reported
72.293 million iPhones and 32.394 million iPads sold during the year. [11]
Those figures are outside the title's 1997-2010 analytical window but provide a
bounded epilogue: the capabilities assembled by 2010 continued to scale through
the leadership transition. They do not prove that every later success was
inevitable or solely attributable to the turnaround decisions.

Taken together, the evidence uses four forms of observation: audited financial
statements, contemporaneous product and channel disclosures, third-party market
data, and contemporaneous journalism. The sources converge on a recovery from
losses and contraction to profitability, product adoption, platform scale, and
a leading market valuation. They do not support a single-factor explanation.
The author's assessment is that the multi-cause account fits the evidence better
than narratives centered only on design, marketing, cost cutting, or a founder's
personality.

## Implications

### For Turnaround Leaders: Secure Viability and Define the Product Thesis

Apple shows why a turnaround needs both subtraction and a positive theory of
customer value. Restructuring lowered expenses, inventory, and organizational
complexity, but management also concentrated resources on products intended to
be easier to understand and use. [2][3] A cost program without a product thesis
can preserve a declining organization; a product vision without control of cash,
inventory, and execution can exhaust the company before customers respond.

A practical sequence follows from the case. First, distinguish survival facts
from rhetoric by examining losses, liquidity, obligations, and cash generation.
Second, identify which products and capabilities are critical to a coherent
customer proposition. Third, remove the projects, inventory, facilities, and
channel practices that consume resources without supporting that proposition.
Fourth, measure whether margins, working capital, and customer demand improve.
This sequence is the author's synthesis of Apple's documented actions, not a
claim that every distressed company should copy Apple's particular products.

The 90-days-from-bankruptcy story also teaches evidentiary discipline. Jobs's
recollection communicates urgency, while the filing supplies audited liquidity
and loss data. [1][4] A responsible turnaround diagnosis should preserve both
kinds of evidence and label them correctly. Founder testimony can explain what
management believed; financial statements test what the company reported.

### For Product Organizations: Count the Obligations Behind Every SKU

Apple's reduction from approximately 15 separate products to three main families
in fiscal 1998 was economically important because products create obligations
across the organization. [2] Portfolio reviews should therefore count more than
revenue by SKU. They should include engineering maintenance, software variants,
component commitments, inventory risk, sales training, support complexity,
marketing attention, and management time.

The decision rule is not "always offer four products" or "eliminate 70 percent."
Those popular formulations are historical shorthand, not universal thresholds.
The transferable question is whether each offer strengthens the chosen system
more than it fragments resources. An additional product can be valuable when it
reuses platforms, channels, accounts, and support capabilities, as iPad did in
2010. [10] It is destructive when it duplicates a role without enough demand or
forces the company to maintain a separate stack.

Focus also requires explicit reinvestment. Jobs said Apple was applying equal or
greater resources to fewer things. [3] Leaders should specify where released
resources will go and which customer outcome should improve. Otherwise a
portfolio cut becomes only austerity, and the organization loses capability
without gaining product quality.

### For Strategy Teams: Analyze Systems, Not Isolated Features

Apple's 2010 strategy joined operating systems, hardware, applications,
services, industrial design, and direct distribution. [10] Competitor analysis
that compares only device specifications misses these complementarities. A
better map identifies which assets increase the value of one another, which are
controlled internally, which depend on partners, and where a rival can enter
without reproducing the whole system.

This does not mean vertical integration is always superior. Integration adds
fixed cost, coordination demands, regulatory exposure, and the risk that one
company's weak layer constrains all the others. Apple's retail filing itself
lists substantial capital and lease commitments, while a governed application
store creates continuing rule-setting responsibilities. [10] Integration is
most defensible when coordination materially improves the user outcome and the
company has the competence and volume to carry the cost.

The digital-hub history also shows that a strategy can evolve while preserving
an underlying logic. The Mac first served as the center of digital devices;
later mobile devices and network services reduced the Mac's centrality. [5][10]
The durable element was not one device at the center but Apple's effort to make
its products and services work as a coordinated portfolio. Strategy should be
stated at the level that survives a change in which component is dominant.

### For Channel Designers: Treat Distribution as Part of the Product

Apple opened stores to demonstrate what customers could do, provide advice and
support, and present integrated solutions. [6][10] For products whose value is
experiential or difficult to explain, distribution quality can determine whether
the product's technical capability is visible to customers. Channel design
should therefore be evaluated as part of product design, not only as a logistics
or real-estate decision.

The economics remain decisive. Apple's stores grew into a profitable segment,
but they also required capital, leases, inventory, and personnel. [10] A company
considering direct distribution should compare the incremental gross profit,
service value, customer acquisition, and feedback quality with those fixed
costs and with possible conflict with resellers. The lesson is not to imitate
Apple's architecture; it is to own the customer interface only when control of
that interface creates measurable value that outweighs its cost.

Direct contact can also shorten learning loops. Store staff observe confusion,
usage, support needs, and product comparisons that may be filtered through an
independent reseller. The author's assessment is that this feedback role helped
retail reinforce product development and service, although the public filings
do not quantify that effect separately. The interpretation should therefore not
be presented as an audited causal finding.

### For Platform Builders: Complementors Create Value and Governance Duties

The App Store illustrates reciprocal platform growth: a device base attracts
developers, and useful applications increase the device's value. The three
billion downloads reported by January 2010 and the rapid growth in iPhone units
show that the system reached scale. [9][10] Platform managers should measure
both sides: active users and their engagement, as well as complementor entry,
economics, quality, and retention.

Control is not free. Review rules, technical interfaces, fees, search and
ranking, security decisions, and dispute processes allocate economic opportunity
among complementors. The platform owner gains consistency and can reduce some
user risks, but it also becomes accountable for exclusion and rule changes.
Apple's case therefore supports a broader principle: the more value a company
creates by governing access, the more carefully it must design transparent and
stable governance.

Reuse across devices can lower the cost of entering adjacent categories. The
iPad drew on an existing operating system, application base, content stores,
accounts, developer relationships, and retail channel. [10] Before launching a
new category, a platform builder should identify which assets genuinely transfer
and which require new capability. Adjacency is attractive when reuse is real,
not merely because two products share a brand.

### For Investors and Case-Study Readers: Avoid Single-Cause and Hero Bias

The audited record does not support the statement that Apple began its recovery
without cost cutting. Apple cut headcount, closed facilities, canceled contracts,
reduced products, changed distribution, and outsourced operations; it also
introduced products that restored demand. [1][2] Investors should separate the
sources of improvement: lower cost, lower working capital, margin recovery,
unit growth, category expansion, and platform economics have different limits
and different implications for intrinsic value.

A durable turnaround is stronger when its economics survive the initiating
leader. Apple's fiscal 2011 results after the capabilities were in place provide
some evidence of continuity, but one year cannot prove indefinite durability.
[11] Analysts should ask which decisions became repeatable processes, which
advantages depend on a particular individual, and which depend on external
conditions such as technology cycles, content rights, carriers, or supplier
capacity.

The case also has limits. Apple retained a recognized brand and user base,
controlled a proprietary operating system, acquired NeXT's technology, and
entered expanding digital-music and mobile-computing markets. [1][2][5][7]
Many distressed companies lack those assets or face declining demand that focus
and design cannot reverse. The author's assessment is that Apple is best used as
a mechanism study -- how focus, integration, distribution, and platforms can
reinforce one another -- rather than as a recipe that guarantees a similar
outcome.

Finally, the endpoint should not erase uncertainty along the path. Fiscal 1998
revenue was still declining even as profits returned; retail required large
fixed commitments; mobile categories depended on execution and complementors;
and market capitalization reflected investor expectations as well as completed
results. [2][10][12] Good case analysis preserves those contemporaneous risks.
The Apple turnaround is instructive because several uncertain choices formed a
coherent system, not because success made those choices obvious in advance.
