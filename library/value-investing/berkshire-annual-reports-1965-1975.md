# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 1965-1975 -- How a Spite-Bought Textile Mill Became the Seed of a Compounding Empire

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 1965 through 1975 document
the decade in which Warren Buffett transformed a failing New England
textile manufacturer into the shell of a compounding holding company.
Read in sequence, these eleven reports trace the arc from a Graham-style
cigar-butt asset purchase, through the acquisition of National Indemnity
and the discovery of insurance float, to the early portfolio
investments in Blue Chip Stamps, See's Candies, and the Washington
Post. The transition was neither planned nor painless: Buffett himself
called the original purchase his dumbest investment, and the textile
business consumed capital for two more decades before finally being
shut down in 1985.

## Background

Berkshire Hathaway's corporate lineage stretches back to two New
England textile manufacturers: the Hathaway Manufacturing Company,
founded in 1888 in New Bedford, Massachusetts, and the Berkshire Fine
Spinning Associates, founded in 1889 in Pittsfield, Massachusetts.
The two merged in 1955 to form Berkshire Hathaway, a company with 15
plants employing over 12,000 workers and over $120 million in revenue,
headquartered in New Bedford. Within five years of the merger, seven
plants had been closed and the workforce reduced, as the American
textile industry entered a long structural decline driven by low-cost
Southern and overseas competition. By the early 1960s, Berkshire
Hathaway was a struggling company whose management, led by Seabury
Stanton, was closing unprofitable mills and using the liquidation
proceeds to repurchase shares.

Warren Buffett began buying Berkshire shares in December 1962 through
his investment partnership, Buffett Partnership Ltd. (BPL), at $7.50
per share. The stock traded at a wide discount to its per-share working
capital of $10.25 and book value of $20.20. Buffett's thesis was
straightforward Graham-and-Dodd: every time Berkshire closed a mill,
it bought back shares, and he could sell into the next tender offer
for a quick profit. He described it as picking up a discarded cigar
butt with one free puff remaining. BPL accumulated about 7% of the
1,583,680 shares outstanding by May 1964.

The pivotal moment came in 1964. Stanton verbally agreed to buy back
BPL's shares at $11.50 each. But when the written tender offer
arrived, it read $11.375 per share -- an eighth of a point less than
agreed. Buffett was incensed by the chiseling and refused to tender.
Instead, he aggressively bought more shares at higher prices. By
April 1965, BPL owned 392,633 of Berkshire's 1,017,547 then-outstanding
shares, and at an early-May board meeting Buffett formally took
control and fired Stanton. He installed Kenneth Chace as president
to run the textile operations. Buffett later called the decision
"monumentally stupid" in his 2014 retrospective, estimating that the
emotional reaction cost him roughly $200 billion in foregone
compounding, because the money trapped in the textile business would
have performed far better invested directly through BPL.

At the time of Buffett's takeover, Berkshire's net worth had fallen to
$22 million from $55 million at the time of the 1955 merger. The full
$22 million was required by the textile operation: the company had no
excess cash and owed its bank $2.5 million. Book value per share stood
at $19.46. This was the inheritance Buffett took control of -- not an
investment vehicle, but a dying industrial business with no surplus
capital and a shrinking asset base.

The 1965 fiscal year (ended October 2, 1965) showed a dramatic one-year
improvement under Chace: net earnings of approximately $2.3 million
versus $125,000 the prior year, largely from shutting down the
least-productive plants. But Buffett recognized this as a one-time
restructuring benefit, not evidence that textiles had become a good
business. The structural disadvantage remained intact. The company's
textile operations employed approximately 2,300 workers across seven
mills, producing cotton and synthetic fabrics for apparel and
industrial uses.

The decisive strategic pivot came in early 1967. Buffett redirected
cash generated by the textile business -- and some debt -- toward
acquiring National Indemnity Company, an Omaha-based property and
casualty insurer, for $8.6 million. This was not a detour from the
original strategy; it was the beginning of the insurance float engine
that would power Berkshire's compounding for the next six decades.
Buffett funded the purchase by pulling $4.6 million of working capital
out of the textile business, issuing $2.6 million in 20-year debt at
7.5% interest, and liquidating $1.6 million from the marketable
securities portfolio.

Buffett later confessed that buying National Indemnity through
Berkshire rather than through BPL was his most costly mistake. Had
BPL been the purchaser, his partners would have owned 100% of the
insurer, and growth would not have been impeded by the unproductive
funds trapped in textiles. Instead, he married a 100%-owned excellent
business to a 61%-owned terrible business, a decision he estimated
diverted roughly $100 billion from BPL partners to Berkshire's
legacy shareholders, to whom he had no obligation. The reason he
put National Indemnity into Berkshire rather than BPL remains, in
his own words, a question he has thought about for 48 years without
arriving at a good answer.

The decade that followed saw Buffett build outward from the insurance
base. In 1969, Berkshire acquired 97.7% of the Illinois National Bank
and Trust Company of Rockford, Illinois, a bank built by Eugene Abegg
from $250,000 of net worth and $400,000 of deposits in 1931 to $17
million of net worth and $100 million of deposits by 1969. In 1970,
Buffett and Munger began acquiring shares in Blue Chip Stamps, a
trading-stamp company with substantial float from unredeemed stamps.
In 1972, Blue Chip Stamps acquired See's Candies for $25 million --
the deal that Buffett credited with transforming him from a cigar-butt
investor into a franchise investor. In 1973, Berkshire began buying
Washington Post shares, describing the purchase as buying a growing
company with a large moat at less than replacement value.

By the end of 1975, the tenth anniversary of Buffett's control,
Berkshire's net worth had risen from $22.1 million to $92.9 million,
and book value per share had risen from $19.46 to $94.92, a compounded
annual growth rate of approximately 15%. The transformation from a
textile manufacturer to a diversified holding company was underway,
though far from complete. The textile business would not be shut
down for another decade, and the holding-company structure would
not be fully consolidated until the Blue Chip Stamps merger in 1983.

## Core Concepts

### The Cigar-Butt Investment and Its Limits

Buffett's initial purchase of Berkshire Hathaway was a textbook
Benjamin Graham cigar-butt: a company trading below its net current
asset value, bought for the single free puff remaining in its
liquidating assets. BPL paid $7.50 per share for a company with
$10.25 in per-share working capital and $20.20 in book value. The
thesis was that the textile operation would continue to shrink,
releasing capital through plant closures and share repurchases,
and the investor could exit at a profit on the tender offer.

The cigar-butt approach has a critical limit that the Berkshire
experience exposed: it works only when the investor can exit. When
Buffett refused to tender at $11.375 and instead took control, he
converted a liquidation arbitrage into an operating business
commitment. The asset bargain became a capital trap. The textile
business consumed cash for two more decades. Buffett later said
that "it was a dead horse, and I kept feeding it." The lesson,
articulated repeatedly in later annual reports, is that buying a
bad business at a cheap price is not the same as buying a good
business at a fair price. Charlie Munger's influence pushed Buffett
away from cigar-butt investing toward the franchise model, but
the Berkshire textile mill was the painful teacher of that lesson.

The distinction between asset value and business value is central.
Berkshire's book value of $20.20 per share in 1962 was real -- the
machinery, inventory, and real estate existed. But a textile mill's
assets generate returns only if the business can operate profitably,
and the American textile industry could not, structurally. Book value
that cannot be deployed at adequate returns is not capital -- it is
a liability with a positive number on the balance sheet.

### Insurance Float as Permanent Capital

The single most important concept that emerged from the 1965-1975
period is insurance float as a source of permanent, low-cost
investment capital. When an insurance company collects premiums, it
holds the money for months or years before paying claims. During that
interval, the money -- the float -- can be invested. If underwriting
is profitable, the insurer is paid to hold the float, making its
cost negative. If underwriting breaks even, the float is interest-free.
Buffett recognized this at National Indemnity in 1967, and it became
the engine of Berkshire's compounding.

At the time of the National Indemnity acquisition, the company
carried approximately $19.4 million in float. Berkshire paid $8.6
million for the company, whose tangible net worth was $6.7 million.
In Buffett's framing, he was paying a $1.9 million premium for a
business that typically operated at an underwriting profit and came
with $19.4 million of investable float -- a ratio that made the
purchase price almost irrelevant if the float could be deployed at
adequate returns. By 1970, Berkshire's insurance float had grown to
$39 million. By 2024, it would exceed $170 billion.

The key property of float that distinguishes it from debt is that it
never matures. A conventional loan is repaid and disappears. Float
behaves as a permanent, growing balance: as old claims are paid, new
premiums flow in to replace them, and as the business grows, the pool
grows with it. The principal never comes due all at once. It rolls
forward, decade after decade, available to be invested the entire
time. This is leverage that does not mature, does not charge interest
in a good year, and grows organically as long as underwriting
discipline is maintained.

Buffett's 2004 letter stated that if Berkshire had not acquired
National Indemnity, "Berkshire would be lucky to be worth half of
what it is today." The cost of float ran below zero in most years
since 1967, meaning Berkshire was paid to borrow the money it then
invested. This is the structural advantage that the textile business
lacked: a textile mill requires capital infusions to maintain
operations and generates poor returns; an insurance company receives
capital from its customers and generates returns by investing it.

### The Opportunity Cost of Trapped Capital

The 1965-1975 reports teach a lesson in opportunity cost that Buffett
has emphasized repeatedly. Every dollar trapped in textile working
capital was a dollar not deployed in insurance, banking, or equities.
The textile business required ongoing investment in inventory,
receivables, and machinery just to maintain its position in a
declining industry. When all competitors made identical capital
investments, the resulting cost reductions simply became the new
baseline for lower industry-wide prices. The capital was consumed
without creating any lasting competitive advantage.

The $4.6 million that Buffett extracted from the textile business
to fund the National Indemnity acquisition was the seed capital for
Berkshire's insurance franchise. Every dollar he had kept locked in
textile working capital instead of redeploying into insurance would
have destroyed an extraordinary multiple of its face value. The
opportunity cost of the trapped capital was not merely the foregone
return on the textile investment; it was the compounding return
foregone on all the businesses that the redeployed capital enabled.

Buffett's 2015 comments reinforced this. He said that buying the
Berkshire Hathaway textile mill in 1965 was the worst investment he
ever made, and that the worst part -- costing an estimated $100
billion -- came after he took control, when he used Berkshire to
acquire National Indemnity instead of buying it with BPL. The textile
business did not merely fail to generate returns; it actively
diverted capital from where it could have compounded.

### Management Quality and Founder-Operators

A consistent theme across the 1965-1975 reports is Buffett's
reliance on founder-operators who retained proprietary interest
after selling their companies. Kenneth Chace ran the textile
operations with integrity for nearly two decades. Jack Ringwalt,
founder of National Indemnity, stayed for more than six years after
the 1967 acquisition. Eugene Abegg continued as chairman of the
Illinois National Bank and achieved returns on assets approximately
three times the average of major banks. Victor Raab, who built Home
and Automobile Insurance from a small initial investment, retained
"every bit of the proprietary interest and pride that they have
always had" after selling.

Buffett described these three men -- Ringwalt, Abegg, and Raab -- as
cut from the same cloth: they built their companies from scratch and,
after selling their ownership position for cash, continued to run
the businesses with undiminished energy. This was not a coincidence.
Buffett's acquisition strategy in this period was to find
owner-built businesses where the founder's identity and the
business's identity were inseparable, and to preserve that
relationship by leaving the founder in place. The 1971 annual report
explicitly framed this as a selection criterion, not an accident.

The contrast with the textile business is instructive. The textile
operation had no founder-operator after Stanton's departure. Chace
was a capable professional manager, but he was running a business
in structural decline where no amount of managerial skill could
overcome the industry's economics. Management quality matters, but
it cannot rescue a business with fundamentally poor economics. The
1965-1975 reports show Buffett learning this distinction in real
time -- the difference between a great manager in a great business
(Ringwalt at National Indemnity, Abegg at Illinois National) and a
great manager in a bad business (Chace at textiles).

### The Blue Chip Stamps Float Discovery

Blue Chip Stamps, the trading-stamp company that Buffett and Munger
began acquiring in 1970, provided a second education in float. Blue
Chip sold stamps to retailers, who distributed them to consumers as
a loyalty reward. Consumers pasted stamps into books and redeemed
them for merchandise at Blue Chip's redemption centers. A significant
percentage of stamps were never redeemed -- lost, forgotten, or
accumulated beyond the holder's patience -- creating a pool of
unredeemed-stamp liability that functioned exactly like insurance
float: interest-free money held by the company.

Blue Chip had approximately $90 million in float in 1970 and peak
sales of $126 million. Buffett, Munger, and Rick Guerin invested
$24 million between 1967 and 1970 for what eventually became
controlling ownership. The stamp business was already in decline --
sales would fall by more than 85% over the next decade -- but the
float could be invested while it lasted. Buffett later quipped that
when told even brothels and mortuaries gave stamps to their patrons,
he "felt I had finally found a sure thing."

The Blue Chip Stamps investment introduced a structural complexity:
Buffett held stakes both personally and through Berkshire and
Diversified Retailing, creating overlapping ownership interests that
would attract SEC scrutiny in the mid-1970s. The resolution of that
scrutiny -- merging Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip into Berkshire
in 1983 -- simplified the structure and made Charlie Munger vice
chairman, but it also illustrates that the 1965-1975 period was
not a clean, linear progression. It was a tangle of cross-holdings,
partnership interests, and regulatory entanglements that took a decade
to unwind.

### The Washington Post and the Concept of Permanent Investment

Berkshire's 1973 purchase of Washington Post shares marked a
conceptual turning point that the 1975 report captured explicitly:
for the first time, Buffett described a marketable security as a
"permanent investment." Berkshire purchased over 230,000 Class B
shares for approximately $10.6 million, at a time when the Post's
market capitalization was approximately $80 million. Buffett
estimated the company's intrinsic value at $400-500 million, based
on its earnings power, its television stations, and the moat of its
newspaper franchise. The purchase price represented less than
replacement value for a growing company with a large moat.

The 1975 report's language on this holding articulated what would
become Buffett's signature investment philosophy: "Our equity
investments are heavily concentrated in a few companies which are
selected based on favorable economic characteristics, competent
and honest management, and a purchase price attractive when measured
against the yardstick of value to a private owner." And on market
fluctuations: "With this approach, stock market fluctuations are of
little importance to us -- except as they may provide buying
opportunities." These sentences, written in 1975 about a 1973
purchase, are the explicit formulation of the philosophy that
Buffett had been developing since the National Indemnity acquisition
and that See's Candies would crystallize in 1972.

## Evidence

### The 1965 Annual Report: One-Time Recovery, Structural Decline

The 1965 annual report (fiscal year ended October 2, 1965) provides
the financial baseline for the decade. Net earnings were $2,279,206,
up from $125,086 the prior year. Working capital stood at $17,869,526
($17.56 per share), and stockholders' equity was $24,520,114 ($24.10
per share). The share count had been reduced to 1,017,547 through
retirements. The earnings recovery was attributable to plant
closures and cost reductions, not to revenue growth or margin
expansion in a viable business. Buffett's private assessment, as
reconstructed from later commentary, was that 1965's results
represented a one-time restructuring benefit, not a sustainable
improvement. The textile industry's structural disadvantage -- high
labor costs, aging machinery, capital intensity with no pricing
power, and Southern/overseas competition -- remained intact.

### The National Indemnity Acquisition: $8.6 Million for $19.4 Million of Float

The 1967 acquisition of National Indemnity Company and its affiliate
National Fire and Marine Insurance Company is documented in the
March 1967 purchase agreement and in subsequent annual reports.
Berkshire paid $8.6 million for 99% of the two companies. National
Indemnity was founded by Jack Ringwalt in 1940, specializing in
commercial auto and general liability insurance and writing
"hard-to-place business" that mainstream insurers declined. At the
time of acquisition, the company carried approximately $19.4 million
in float and had a tangible net worth of $6.7 million. The cost of
float had been below zero -- that is, underwriting was profitable --
in 6 of the prior 10 years.

Buffett funded the purchase from three sources: $4.6 million
extracted from textile working capital (optimizing receivables,
payables, inventory, and fixed assets), $2.6 million in 20-year
debt at 7.5% interest, and $1.6 million from liquidating part of the
marketable securities portfolio. The annual reports from 1967
through 1970 document National Indemnity's premium growth and
underwriting results, showing consistent profitability that validated
the float thesis. By 1970, Berkshire's total insurance float had
grown to $39 million. The 2004 annual report confirmed that the $20
million of float from 1967 had grown to $46.1 billion by year-end
2004, and that without National Indemnity, "Berkshire would be lucky
to be worth half of what it is today."

### The Illinois National Bank: 2% Return on Deposits

The 1969 annual report documents the acquisition of 97.7% of the
Illinois National Bank and Trust Company of Rockford, Illinois.
The bank had been built by Eugene Abegg from $250,000 of net worth
and $400,000 of deposits in 1931 to $17 million of net worth and
$100 million of deposits by 1969 -- with no additional outside
capital contributed. Operating earnings (before security losses)
were approximately $2 million in 1969, a return on assets near the
top among larger U.S. commercial banks.

The 1971 report showed after-tax earnings of over 2% on average
deposits, maintained while paying maximum permitted interest rates
to savers, maintaining above-average liquidity, and recording
loan-loss ratios far below average. By 1975, the bank's net loan
losses were just $24,000 on $65 million in loans -- approximately
one of the most profitable banks in the nation by return on assets,
roughly four times the level of the largest banks. The bank was
eventually spun off in 1980 due to the Bank Holding Company Act's
requirement that bank holding companies divest non-banking
subsidiaries, but the decade of ownership demonstrated Buffett's
ability to identify and retain exceptional management in an
acquired business.

### Blue Chip Stamps: $24 Million for a Declining Business with $90 Million of Float

Buffett, Munger, and Rick Guerin began acquiring Blue Chip Stamps
shares between 1967 and 1970, investing $24 million for controlling
ownership. The 1970 annual report context shows Blue Chip with $126
million in sales and approximately $90 million in float from
unredeemed stamps. At year-end 1972, Berkshire owned 19%; at year-end
1973, 22.5%. The investment was made despite the stamp business being
in structural decline -- sales would fall to $19.4 million by 1980
and $1.5 million by 1990.

The evidence that this was a float play, not a business-quality
investment, is in the trajectory: the stamp business collapsed, but
the float and the businesses acquired through Blue Chip -- See's
Candies, the Buffalo News, Wesco Financial -- compounded
spectacularly. By 1983, when Blue Chip was merged into Berkshire,
the initial $24 million investment had become the vehicle for
acquiring businesses worth billions. The 2006 annual report
confirmed that the initial Blue Chip investment was worth
approximately $60 billion in Berkshire shares after 50 years, all
from a business whose core revenue declined by nearly 85% over 10
years.

### See's Candies: $25 Million for a 60% Pre-Tax Return on Tangible Capital

Blue Chip Stamps acquired See's Candies in January 1972 for $25
million. See's had $29 million in sales and $4.2 million in
pre-tax earnings, operating on approximately $8 million in net
tangible assets -- a roughly 60% pre-tax return on invested
capital. The company sold 16 million pounds of candy annually
through roughly 170 stores, almost all in California. Buffett and
Munger initially refused to pay above $25 million when the sellers
asked for $30 million (reflecting $10 million in excess cash, making
the true asking price $30 million); the sellers ultimately accepted
$25 million.

The See's acquisition is the most studied deal in Berkshire's
history because it marks Buffett's transition from cigar-butt
investor to franchise investor. Every retrospective Buffett wrote
about See's -- in 1983, 1991, 1999, 2007, 2011, and 2014 -- adds
another layer to the same argument: the candy company generated
roughly $2.8 billion in cumulative pre-tax earnings on approximately
$65 million of total invested capital, a 43-fold return. The
mechanism was pricing power: revenue grew at 13.1% annually while
volume grew more slowly, meaning See's raised prices every year
without losing customers. The 1965-1975 reports do not yet contain
the full See's story, but the 1972 acquisition is the inflection
point visible within the decade.

### The Washington Post: $10.6 Million for a $400-500 Million Intrinsic Value

Berkshire began buying Washington Post shares in early 1973 and held
over 10% of the non-controlling B shares by year-end. The total cost
was approximately $10.6 million for 467,150 shares. The Post's market
capitalization was approximately $80 million at the time of purchase.
Buffett estimated the company's intrinsic value at $400-500 million,
based on its newspaper franchise, television stations, and growing
earnings power. The purchase price represented roughly 100-125% of
book value and less than replacement value for a growing company
with a large moat.

The 1975 annual report explicitly described this holding as a
"permanent investment" -- the first time Buffett used that label for
a marketable security. The report stated Berkshire's investment
philosophy in terms that would become canonical: concentration in
a few companies with favorable economic characteristics, competent
and honest management, and a purchase price attractive against the
yardstick of value to a private owner. The holding would not be sold
until 2014, when Berkshire exchanged its Post shares for a television
station and cash in a tax-free transaction, at which point the stake
was valued at nearly $1.1 billion.

### The 1975 Retrospective: 15% Annual Compounding Over Ten Years

The 1975 annual report's closing section provided a ten-year
retrospective on Berkshire's progress since Buffett's 1965 takeover.
Net worth had increased from $22.1 million to $92.9 million. Book
value per share had risen from $19.46 to $94.92, a compounded annual
growth rate of approximately 15%. This growth occurred despite 1975
being described as "a major disappointment" on the surface, with net
income falling from $7.0 million in 1974 to $4.7 million in 1975.
The report also noted Berkshire's acquisition of a 31.5% interest
in a large affiliate enterprise (Blue Chip Stamps) and a reduction
in outstanding shares to 979,569, concentrating ownership further.
The 15% compound rate was achieved not by the textile business --
which was stagnant -- but by the redeployment of capital into
insurance, banking, and equity investments.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocation Strategy

The 1965-1975 Berkshire reports are a case study in the most
important decision a CEO makes: where to deploy capital. Buffett's
capital allocation during this decade followed a clear hierarchy.
First, he extracted capital from the declining textile business
rather than reinvesting it. Second, he deployed that capital into
insurance, where it purchased float at a negative cost. Third, he
used the float to acquire operating businesses (Illinois National
Bank, Home and Automobile Insurance) and equity positions
(Washington Post, Blue Chip Stamps) with favorable economics.

This sequence -- extract from bad businesses, deploy into good
businesses, use the good businesses' float to buy more good
businesses -- is the compounding engine that the 1965-1975 reports
document in its earliest, formative stage. The implication for
capital allocators is that the source of capital matters as much as
the use. Capital generated by a declining business that is
systematically extracted and redeployed compounds differently than
capital that is reinvested in the declining business in the hope of
a turnaround that the industry's economics do not permit.

The mistake Buffett identified -- buying National Indemnity through
Berkshire rather than BPL -- carries its own implication. The
choice of vehicle for an acquisition determines who benefits from
the compounding. By placing National Indemnity inside a company
where BPL held only a 61% interest, Buffett diluted the benefit
to his partners and shared it with legacy Berkshire shareholders
to whom he had no obligation. The structural decision about where
an asset sits in the corporate hierarchy is a capital allocation
decision with consequences that compound for decades.

### For Value Investors: The Cigar-Butt to Franchise Transition

The 1965-1975 decade contains Buffett's transition from Graham-style
cigar-butt investing to Munger-influenced franchise investing, and
the reports document the transition in real time. The Berkshire
textile purchase (1962-1965) was a cigar-butt: cheap relative to
assets, bought for a single puff. National Indemnity (1967) was a
business-quality purchase: bought for the float economics, not the
asset discount. Illinois National Bank (1969) was bought for the
management quality and the returns on assets. See's Candies (1972)
was bought for the franchise: the brand, the pricing power, and the
returns on tangible capital. The Washington Post (1973) was bought
for the moat and the margin of safety.

The implication for value investors is that the 1965-1975 reports
show the transition happening, not as a single epiphany but as a
sequence of decisions, each informed by the lessons of the last.
The textile mistake taught the limits of cigar-butt investing.
National Indemnity taught the power of float. Illinois National
Bank taught the value of founder-operators. See's Candies taught
the economics of pricing power and brand moats. The Washington Post
taught the concept of permanent investment in a wonderful company
at a fair price. An investor who reads these reports in sequence
watches the philosophy being built.

### For Business Analysis: Distinguishing Good Businesses from Bad Ones

The 1965-1975 reports provide a comparative framework for
distinguishing good businesses from bad ones, because Buffett was
simultaneously running both. The textile business required capital
infusions, generated poor returns, faced structural decline, and
had no pricing power. National Indemnity generated float at a
negative cost, required no capital infusions, and could grow by
writing more premium. Illinois National Bank generated 2%+ returns
on deposits with minimal loan losses and required no additional
capital. See's Candies generated 60% pre-tax returns on tangible
capital and raised prices annually without losing customers.

The framework that emerges is: a good business generates cash
without requiring capital infusions; a bad business consumes cash
to maintain its position. A good business has pricing power; a bad
business is a price-taker in a commodity market. A good business
has a moat that protects its returns from competition; a bad
business faces structural pressure that erodes returns regardless
of management quality. The textile business had Ken Chace, a
capable and honest manager, and it still failed -- because the
industry's economics overrode management quality. The implication
is that management quality is necessary but not sufficient; the
business must have economics that allow good management to produce
good returns.

### For Investors Evaluating Holding Companies

The 1965-1975 reports are the origin story of the modern Berkshire
Hathaway holding-company structure, and they offer lessons for
investors evaluating any holding company or permanent-capital
vehicle. The key question is whether the structure creates value
beyond the sum of its parts. Berkshire's structure created value
through insurance float (low-cost capital), decentralized
management (founder-operators left in place), and tax-deferred
compounding (no distributions, no realized gains). The textile
business contributed nothing to this structure except the shell;
the value was in the redeployment of its capital and the corporate
form that allowed the redeployment.

The implication for investors evaluating holding companies is to
ask: what is the source of capital, what is its cost, and where is
it being deployed? A holding company funded by insurance float at
a negative cost, deploying capital into businesses with 60%
returns on tangible assets, is compounding on a different curve than
a holding company funded by equity issuance or debt at market rates.
The 1965-1975 reports show the mechanism being assembled, piece by
piece, and the compounding curve beginning to bend upward.

### For Understanding the Cost of Emotional Decisions

The Berkshire story begins with an emotional decision: Buffett's
refusal to accept $11.375 instead of the agreed $11.50, and his
subsequent decision to take control of the company out of spite.
He called it "monumentally stupid" and estimated it cost $200
billion in foregone compounding. The 1975 purchase of Waumbec
Mills -- another textile mill, bought as a "bargain" based on
assets and projected synergies -- was another emotional decision:
a refusal to accept that the textile business was dying. Waumbec
was a disaster, shut down within a few years.

The implication is that emotional decisions in investing have
compounding costs, not merely one-time costs. A bad purchase made
out of spite locks capital into a poor investment for years, and
the opportunity cost compounds over the holding period. The
1965-1975 reports are a cautionary record: the decade's successes
(National Indemnity, Illinois National Bank, See's Candies,
Washington Post) were made with analytical discipline; the decade's
failures (the textile business, Waumbec Mills) were made with
emotional attachment. The lesson Buffett drew and stated repeatedly
in later reports is that temperament, not intellect, is the binding
constraint on investment performance.
