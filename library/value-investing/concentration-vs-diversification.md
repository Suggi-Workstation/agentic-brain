# Concentration vs Diversification -- Why Value Investors Cannot Agree on How Many Stocks to Hold

Value investors face a fundamental tension: concentrated portfolios
amplify the rewards of deep research and genuine insight, while
diversification protects against analytical error and bad luck. Despite
sharing the same intellectual tradition -- Graham, Dodd, and the search
for undervalued securities -- the most celebrated practitioners of
value investing have held radically different views on how many
positions constitute a sound portfolio. Warren Buffett called
diversification "protection against ignorance" and concentrated 40% of
his early partnership into a single stock. Benjamin Graham recommended
holding 10 to 30 positions. Walter Schloss routinely held over 100.
The disagreement is not a matter of one side being right and the other
wrong; it reflects differences in strategy type, conviction depth,
and self-knowledge that every value investor must reckon with.

## Background

The diversification debate within value investing traces back to the
origins of modern portfolio theory and the reactions it provoked from
practitioners. Harry Markowitz's 1952 paper "Portfolio Selection"
established the mathematical case for diversification: by holding
assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated, an investor can
reduce portfolio volatility without necessarily sacrificing expected
return. This insight earned Markowitz a Nobel Prize and became the
intellectual foundation of institutional portfolio management for
decades.

Benjamin Graham, writing before Markowitz but anticipating the
practical spirit of diversification, recommended that defensive
investors hold 10 to 30 stocks diversified across major industries.
Graham's framework was empirical, not theoretical. He observed that
individual securities carried unpredictable risk -- fraud, competitive
failure, management error, secular decline -- and that a portfolio of
10-30 positions captured most of the benefit of diversification while
remaining manageable for individual investors. Crucially, Graham did
not argue for 100-stock portfolios. He believed 10-30 was the sweet
spot where diversification benefit plateaued.

Warren Buffett, Graham's most famous student, eventually repudiated
the diversification logic. Buffett's evolution from Graham-style
diversification to concentrated investing was gradual but decisive.
During the Buffett Partnership years (1956-1969), Buffett routinely
held 5-10 positions, with the largest position often exceeding 30%
of assets. In the 1964 Salad Oil Scandal, Buffett invested 40% of
the partnership in American Express after conducting ground-level
research -- counting charge slips at Omaha restaurants to confirm
that consumer usage had not declined despite the financial panic.
That position generated extraordinary returns and crystallized
Buffett's conviction that deep research on a few companies
outperforms shallow research on many.

Charlie Munger pushed Buffett further toward concentration,
articulating the "punch card" metaphor: imagine a lifetime
investment card with only 20 punch holes, one for each decision.
"An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he avoids
big mistakes," Munger argued. "The idea of excessive diversification
is madness." Munger personally ran the Wesco Financial portfolio
with extreme concentration -- often fewer than 10 positions -- and
produced roughly 17% compound annual returns over nearly three
decades.

The diversification camp was not without its own champions. Walter
Schloss, who worked for Graham before founding his own firm,
maintained portfolios of 100+ stocks for over 45 years and generated
roughly 16% compound annual returns. Schloss's method was mechanical:
screen for low price-to-book stocks, hold dozens of positions, sell
when they reached intrinsic value. The approach captured a broad
statistical edge that only worked at scale. Peter Lynch's Magellan
Fund at Fidelity held over 1,000 positions at its peak, yet Lynch
produced 29% annual returns over 13 years -- the best mutual fund
record in history. Lynch visited hundreds of companies annually,
arguing that his broad mosaic of intelligence informed every
individual decision.

The track records of both concentrated and diversified investors
demonstrate that portfolio structure alone does not determine
outcome. What separates the exceptional from the mediocre is stock
selection skill, not position count. Concentration amplifies the
impact of that skill in both directions -- higher highs and lower
lows -- while diversification dampens the signal for more consistent
but less extreme results.

## Core Concepts

### The Mathematics of Concentration: Kelly Criterion

The Kelly Criterion, developed by John L. Kelly at Bell Labs in 1956,
provides the formal mathematical framework behind the intuition that
investors should "bet big" when they have an edge. Originally derived
for information theory applications in telecommunications, the formula
determines the optimal fraction of capital to wager on a bet with known
probabilities of winning and losing, and known odds. The criterion
maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth -- which, for repeated bets,
maximizes the geometric growth rate of capital.

The Kelly formula in its simplest binary form states that the optimal
fraction f* = (bp - q) / b, where b is the net odds received on the
wager (the multiple of the bet received if you win), p is the
probability of winning, and q = 1-p is the probability of losing.
For example, if you have a 60% chance of doubling your money (b=1)
and a 40% chance of losing it, the Kelly fraction is (1*0.6 - 0.4)/1
= 0.20, suggesting a 20% allocation.

Edward Thorp adapted the Kelly Criterion to investing in the 1960s and
1970s, first by applying it to blackjack card-counting, then to equity
markets. Thorp demonstrated that the Kelly framework explains why
concentrated portfolios are rational for investors with genuine
informational advantages and why diversification is rational for
investors without such advantages. The key insight: the Kelly fraction
grows with the size of the investor's edge. An investor who correctly
identifies a mispriced security with high confidence should allocate
more capital to it. An investor with a small, noisy edge should
diversify broadly.

In practice, most sophisticated investors use "fractional Kelly" --
allocating half or one-third of the full Kelly recommendation. This
acknowledges that the inputs to the Kelly formula (probabilities,
expected returns) are themselves estimates with significant error.
Using fractional Kelly reduces the variance of returns while preserving
most of the growth advantage. The author's assessment is that
fractional Kelly, typically at half-Kelly, represents the practical
sweet spot between the mathematical optimum and the real-world problem
of estimation error.

### The Efficient Frontier Revisited: How Many Stocks Are "Enough"

Research by Statman (1987) established that approximately 90% of the
diversification benefits available in the US equity market are captured
with 20-30 randomly selected stocks. Beyond 50 stocks, the marginal
reduction in portfolio variance approaches zero. This finding provided
empirical backbone to Graham's intuitive recommendation of 10-30
positions and created a puzzle for the Buffett-Munger camp: if 30
stocks capture nearly all diversification benefit, what justifies
holding only 5-10?

The resolution lies in the distinction between systematic risk
(market-wide, undiversifiable) and idiosyncratic risk (company-specific,
diversifiable). Traditional portfolio theory treats all idiosyncratic
risk as uncompensated -- the market does not reward you for bearing it
because you could have diversified it away. But value investors dispute
this premise. If an investor possesses superior analytical ability,
idiosyncratic risk becomes a source of alpha, not a cost. By
concentrating on their highest-conviction ideas, skilled investors
capture more of the return from their analytical edge, even though they
bear more idiosyncratic risk in the process.

This framework suggests that the "right" number of positions depends
on the ratio of the investor's skill (alpha generation per unit of
risk) to their uncertainty about that skill. An investor who reliably
identifies underpriced securities should concentrate. An investor
who is uncertain about their analytical edge should diversify. This
is not a philosophical preference -- it is a mathematical consequence
of the Kelly framework applied to the structure of equity returns.

### The Conviction Spectrum: Deep-Value vs Quality-Value Approaches

Different value investing "schools" map naturally to different points
on the concentration spectrum. The deep-value approach -- buying
statistically cheap stocks using quantitative screens with relatively
modest per-company research -- requires broad diversification. Walter
Schloss's 100-stock portfolio and the Tweedy Browne partnerships
exemplify this approach. The edge is statistical: over a large sample
of low-multiple stocks, the odds favor outperformance even if
individual companies occasionally fail or turn out to be value traps.

In contrast, the quality-value approach -- buying excellent businesses
at reasonable prices after intensive fundamental research -- supports
concentration. Buffett's American Express investment, his Coca-Cola
position in the late 1980s, and his concentrated Apple stake in the
2010s all reflect deep, company-specific research that cannot feasibly
be replicated across 100 positions. The edge is analytical: superior
understanding of a specific business generates conviction that
warrants large position sizing.

The author's assessment is that this distinction explains more of the
variance in portfolio concentration among great investors than any
other factor. Investors are not choosing between right and wrong;
they are matching their portfolio structure to their strategy type
and their informational advantage. An investor pursuing deep value
should not attempt to concentrate (the statistical edge requires a
large sample), just as an investor pursuing quality value should not
hold 100 positions (the research burden makes deep understanding
impossible).

### The Psychological Dimension: Can You Handle the Volatility

The concentration vs diversification debate is not purely mathematical
or strategic. It is deeply psychological. A concentrated portfolio
produces extreme interim volatility. A 5-stock portfolio can easily
decline 30-40% in a year even if the underlying thesis on each
position is intact. Most investors underestimate their own emotional
capacity to withstand such drawdowns. Research on loss aversion,
prospect theory, and myopic loss aversion all suggest that human
beings feel interim losses far more acutely than they should, and
that the behavioral temptation to sell at the bottom is the single
biggest risk in a concentrated strategy.

Buffett and Munger's ability to hold concentrated positions through
severe drawdowns -- including the 1973-74 bear market that halved
Berkshire's stock price -- is a feature of their unusual psychology,
not just their analytical skill. Munger himself acknowledged this:
"The desire for frequent investment action, in the market, carries a
high price." The investor who cannot sleep at night during a drawdown
should diversify regardless of the mathematical advantage of
concentration. This suggests that the optimal number of positions is
the number that allows the investor to stay invested through the
down cycles that every strategy will encounter.

## Evidence and Empirical Track Record

The empirical record of concentrated and diversified value investors
provides the most compelling evidence that portfolio structure is not
destiny. Comparing the long-term track records of investors across
the concentration spectrum reveals remarkable parity among the best
practitioners of both approaches.

Concentrated investors with fewer than 20 positions have produced
exceptional results. Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway compounded at
approximately 20% annually from 1965 to 2024, with the top five
holdings typically representing 60-80% of the publicly traded
portfolio. Munger's Wesco Financial (1977-2004) compounded at roughly
17% with extreme concentration. Seth Klarman's Baupost Group
(1983-2024) returned approximately 15% annually, often holding fewer
than 20 positions. Joel Greenblatt generated approximately 50%
annual returns at Gotham Capital (1985-1994) with a very concentrated
portfolio, though this was on a small asset base that limits
generalizability.

Diversified investors have matched or exceeded these results. Walter
Schloss's WJS Partners (1955-2002) returned approximately 16% annually
with 100+ positions. Graham's Graham-Newman partnership (1936-1956)
returned roughly 15% annually using diversified value strategies.
Peter Lynch's Magellan Fund (1977-1990) returned 29% annually -- the
best record in mutual fund history -- with over 1,000 positions at
its peak. Tweedy Browne's various value funds have compounded at
roughly 15% annually since 1968 using diversified portfolios.

The critical pattern across these track records is that the most
successful investors in both camps share common attributes:
disciplined valuation frameworks, deep understanding of their
strategy's edge, patience to hold through volatility, and the
psychological fortitude to act counter-cyclically. Portfolio
concentration correlates with strategy type (quality-value investors
tend to concentrate; deep-value investors tend to diversify) but
does not independently predict returns after controlling for
investment skill.

The academic evidence on portfolio concentration and performance is
mixed. Studies of mutual fund portfolios have found that concentrated
funds -- those in the top quintile by portfolio concentration --
tend to outperform diversified funds on a risk-adjusted basis,
consistent with the idea that concentration reflects manager
conviction and skill. However, the same studies find that
concentrated funds exhibit higher variance of returns and higher
failure rates, consistent with the Kelly framework's prediction that
concentration amplifies the impact of skill in both directions.

What the author finds most instructive is the absence of a clear
winner. If concentration were uniformly superior, Graham would not
have produced 15% returns with diversified portfolios. If
diversification were uniformly superior, Buffett would not have
produced 20% returns with concentrated portfolios. The evidence
suggests that concentration is a lever, not a strategy. It magnifies
whatever skill the investor possesses but does not create skill
where none exists.

## Implications

### For the Individual Value Investor

The most practical implication of the concentration-vs-diversification
debate is that individual investors should match their portfolio
structure to their self-knowledge. An investor who has been analyzing
businesses for decades, has a track record of accurate assessments,
and deeply understands a specific industry can rationally hold a
concentrated portfolio of 10-15 positions. An investor who is newer
to fundamental analysis, has not tested their framework across
multiple market cycles, or invests outside their circle of competence
should diversify more broadly -- the 20-30 positions Graham recommended
or, alternatively, a low-cost index fund paired with a few high-
conviction positions.

Overconfidence is the single greatest risk in this decision. The
research on overconfidence bias -- the systematic tendency of humans
to overrate their own knowledge and ability -- strongly suggests that
most investors overestimate their analytical edge. The practical
implication is that investors should use slightly more diversification
than feels "necessary," because the feeling of necessity is calibrated
against an overestimated edge. The author's assessment is that 15-25
positions represent the practical optimum for most serious individual
investors: concentrated enough to force disciplined decision-making
but diversified enough to survive inevitable analytical errors.

### For Portfolio Construction

The concentration-vs-diversification discussion connects directly
to position sizing. An investor who holds 10 positions does not
need to employ equal weighting. The Kelly-inspired approach suggests
that position size should be proportional to the investor's conviction
and edge. A high-conviction idea with a large estimated margin of
safety may warrant 15-20% of the portfolio, while a good-but-not-
exceptional idea warrants 5%. This creates a natural "barbell" where
the portfolio naturally converges to 8-15 meaningful positions with
a few smaller ones, regardless of the top-line concentration target.

The interaction with the investor's circle of competence is critical.
A concentrated portfolio requires that every position be within the
investor's circle of competence. An investor who truly understands
only five industries should not hold positions in 20 industries for
the sake of diversification. A concentrated portfolio of 10 positions
within the circle of competence is less risky than a diversified
portfolio of 50 positions, most of which are outside it. Buffett's
famous dictum -- "risk comes from not knowing what you are doing" --
applies here with particular force.

### The Permanent Capital Perspective

One underappreciated argument for concentration is that it aligns
with a permanent capital mindset. A diversified portfolio of 50+
stocks implicitly treats most holdings as interchangeable -- to be
bought and sold as valuations change. A concentrated portfolio of
10-15 positions encourages the investor to think like a business
owner, focusing on the long-term earnings power and competitive
position of each company rather than quarterly price movements.
This is the Buffalo-Munger philosophy in its purest form: buy pieces
of excellent businesses and hold them indefinitely.

The trade-off is that a permanent-capital, concentrated portfolio
requires even greater psychological fortitude. Watching a single
position decline 40% when it comprises 15% of your net worth tests
conviction in ways that watching 2% position decline by the same
amount does not. The author's assessment is that this psychological
capacity is the binding constraint on concentration for most
investors, not the mathematics of optimal position sizing.
