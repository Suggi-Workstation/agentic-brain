# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 2006-2015 -- Crisis Deployment, BNSF, and the Fifty-Year Scorecard

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 2006 through 2015 document a
decade in which the company deployed its cash as a crisis lender of
last resort, completed the largest acquisition in its history, partnered
with 3G Capital to build a packaged-food giant, and produced a
fifty-year retrospective that framed the entire Berkshire enterprise.
Read as a sequence, the ten reports reveal a capital allocation
discipline that swung from opportunistic distress financing to
capital-intensive infrastructure to passive minority stakes, and a
reporting philosophy that used candor about mistakes as a teaching
tool rather than a liability to be managed.

## Background

The decade 2006-2015 is a natural unit of analysis because it brackets
three of the most consequential events in Berkshire's post-1965 history.
First, the 2008-2009 financial crisis tested whether Berkshire's vast
cash reserves and insurance float could function as a source of
countercyclical capital when the global banking system stopped
functioning. Second, the November 2009 announcement and February 2010
closing of the BNSF acquisition -- valued at approximately $44 billion
including assumed debt -- represented Berkshire's largest transaction
ever and a fundamental shift from passive equity holdings toward
capital-intensive, regulated infrastructure. Third, the 2014 annual
report contained a fifty-year retrospective in which both Warren Buffett
and Charlie Munger independently wrote about what had happened at
Berkshire over the prior half-century and what each expected in the
next fifty years, providing the clearest single-document statement of
the Berkshire system and its operating philosophy.

Berkshire's annual shareholder letter is unlike any other document in
corporate America. Buffett used the letter not merely to satisfy SEC
disclosure requirements but to teach capital allocation logic, explain
mistakes openly, and build a relationship of trust with shareholders
based on radical candor. The 2014 retrospective letter stated this
principle explicitly: Berkshire owes shareholders additional commentary
about what they own and how management thinks, beyond the mandated data.
Buffett contrasted this with companies where the word "mistake" was
forbidden at board meetings -- a taboo that, in his view, implied a
false managerial perfection that always made him nervous.

The decade opens with Berkshire already a diversified holding company
with major insurance operations (GEICO, General Re, National
Indemnity), regulated utilities (MidAmerican Energy, later Berkshire
Hathaway Energy), manufacturing (Marmon, Shaw, MiTek, Clayton Homes),
and large equity stakes in American Express, Coca-Cola, Procter and
Gamble, Wells Fargo, and Washington Post. By the end of 2015, the
equity portfolio had shifted (Kraft Heinz, IBM, and Wells Fargo had
become dominant; the Washington Post position had been exchanged for
Graham Holdings stock), and the operating businesses had expanded
dramatically with BNSF, Lubrizol, IMC (International Metalworking
Companies), and a larger Marmon stake.

Insurance float -- money that does not belong to Berkshire but that it
can invest for its own benefit -- grew from approximately $41 billion
at the end of 2005 to approximately $88 billion by the end of 2015,
according to Buffett's 2015 letter. This more-than-doubling of float
during the decade provided the capital base for the crisis-era
preferred-stock investments, the BNSF acquisition, and the bolt-on
manufacturing acquisitions. Buffett described float as a "revolving fund"
rather than a typical liability: as old claims are paid, new premiums
flow in to replace them, and as the insurance business grows, the pool
grows with it. Since 1967, the float compounded at an annual rate of
approximately 21.7 percent through the end of 2015.

The second half of the decade brought a different pattern: partnership
with 3G Capital, first on the 2013 acquisition of H.J. Heinz Company
and then on the 2015 merger of Heinz with Kraft Foods Group. Berkshire
acted as a passive capital partner while 3G managed operations,
applying its cost-cutting playbook to consumer packaged goods. This
represented a structural departure from the BNSF model, where Berkshire
took full ownership and let existing management run the railroad. The
Heinz and Kraft Heinz deals used both preferred stock (for fixed
downside-protected return) and common equity (for upside), deployed
approximately $17.5 billion in aggregate, and reduced Berkshire's
effective ownership through share issuance -- a cost the reports
documented in detail.

The decade also produced two of the clearest teaching documents in the
Berkshire corpus. The 2008 letter, written during the depths of the
financial crisis, explained the preferred-stock investment structure
and the principle of treating capital as a scarce resource deployed
only when terms were favorable. The 2014 retrospective letter explained
why Berkshire's long-term percentage gains could not continue at past
rates, why the company would eventually need to consider dividends or
repurchases, and why the chance of permanent capital loss for patient
shareholders was "as low as can be found among single-company
investments."

## Core Concepts

### The Preferred Stock Crisis Structure

The most distinctive capital allocation pattern of the 2006-2015 decade
was Berkshire's deployment of crisis-era capital through structured
preferred stock investments paired with warrants. When the global
financial system seized up in September 2008, Berkshire did not simply
buy common stock in the open market. Instead, it negotiated newly issued
preferred stock carrying high fixed dividends (typically 10 percent)
and paired with warrants -- options to buy common stock later at a set
price. The preferred paid Berkshire to wait; the warrants captured the
rebound if the company recovered.

The structure was consistent across four major deals. On September 23,
2008, Goldman Sachs announced that Berkshire would purchase $5 billion
of perpetual preferred stock carrying a 10 percent dividend, callable
at a 10 percent premium, alongside warrants to buy $5 billion of common
stock at $115 per share exercisable over five years. Goldman
simultaneously raised at least $2.5 billion of common equity in a
public offering, bringing total capital raised to $10 billion. On
October 1, 2008, General Electric followed with a $3 billion preferred
investment from Berkshire on similar 10 percent terms, with warrants
struck at $22.25 per share. On October 6, 2008, Berkshire funded $6.5
billion toward closing the Mars acquisition of Wrigley, structured as
$4.4 billion of 11.45 percent subordinated notes due 2018 and $2.1
billion of preferred stock paying 5 percent. In August 2011, Bank of
America announced a $5 billion Berkshire investment in 6 percent
preferred stock plus warrants on 700 million shares at $7.142857 each
over ten years.

The crisis structure reveals several capital allocation principles.
First, Berkshire demanded to be paid while waiting: the 10 percent
dividend on the Goldman and GE preferred stocks produced $500 million
and $300 million per year respectively, regardless of the common stock
price. Second, the warrants provided asymmetric upside with no
additional capital at risk: if the company recovered, Berkshire
captured the gain; if it did not, the preferred stock still paid its
dividend. Third, Buffett's name on a deal told the market that a
famously careful investor judged the company a survivor, which mattered
enormously when confidence was the missing ingredient. Buffett
published his reasoning explicitly in a New York Times op-ed on
October 17, 2008, titled "Buy American. I Am."

The outcomes validated the structure. Goldman Sachs redeemed the
preferred stock in April 2011, returning Berkshire's $5 billion plus
the 10 percent premium and accrued dividends. In March 2013, Berkshire
and Goldman renegotiated the warrants into a net-share settlement,
leaving Berkshire holding approximately 2 percent of Goldman without
putting up the original $5 billion of exercise cash. GE redeemed its
preferred in 2011 and the GE warrants were later settled for a smaller
equity stake. The Wrigley subordinated notes were redeemed in October
2013 at 115.45 percent of par, and the Wrigley preferred was
repurchased in September 2016 at 115.45 percent of par. The Bank of
America deal, exercised in August 2017, proved the most profitable of
all, converting the $5 billion investment into a large common-stock
stake with a profit of approximately $13 billion.

### BNSF as a Bet on Regulated, Capital-Intensive Infrastructure

The BNSF acquisition, announced November 3, 2009 and closed February 12,
2010, represented the largest transaction in Berkshire's history and a
structural pivot. Berkshire offered $100 per share in cash or stock for
the remaining 77.4 percent of BNSF (NYSE: BNI) shares it did not
already own. Based on outstanding shares on November 2, 2009, the
transaction was valued at approximately $44 billion, including $10
billion of outstanding BNSF debt assumed by Berkshire. The consideration
was approximately 60 percent cash and 40 percent stock, requiring
Berkshire to issue approximately 95,676 Class A-equivalent shares (or
their Class B equivalent after a 50-to-1 split of the Class B).

Buffett explained the thesis in the 2010 letter: the highlight of the
year was the BNSF acquisition, which was working out better than
expected. He stated that owning the railroad would increase Berkshire's
"normal" earning power by nearly 40 percent pre-tax and by well over
30 percent after-tax. The rationale rested on several principles. First,
railroads move goods efficiently: a train can move a ton of freight
approximately 500 miles on a single gallon of fuel, far more efficient
than trucking, with corresponding cost advantages and environmental
benefits. Second, railroads are the only mode of freight transportation
that can handle the growth in demand that the American economy would
produce. Third, the railroad business had already invested heavily in
infrastructure and technology, and continued investment would compound
the advantage. Buffett told BNSF CEO Matt Rose that he was "looking
forward to our first century together" -- a statement of permanence
unusual even by Berkshire standards.

The BNSF deal tested several value-investing principles. The railroad
was a regulated, capital-intensive business -- the opposite of the
capital-light, franchise-quality businesses that Buffett had spent
decades acquiring (See's Candies, Coca-Cola). The deal required
accepting adequate returns rather than extraordinary returns, because
scale had made capital-light opportunities too small to move
Berkshire's needle. The deal also required issuing Berkshire shares --
a currency Buffett had historically been reluctant to part with -- and
splitting the Class B 50-to-1 to make the stock acceptable to BNSF
shareholders. The 2010 letter noted that the consideration included
$15.9 billion in cash and $10.6 billion in newly issued Berkshire
stock, with an additional $10 billion in BNSF debt assumed.

By March 2011, just 13 months after the acquisition closed, BNSF had
already paid Berkshire $2.25 billion in dividends, nearly triple what
BNSF paid out in a comparable period before the acquisition. The
railroad's top-line growth in the decade following the deal was
relatively modest -- revenue increased by approximately 25 percent --
but the cash generation validated the thesis. BNSF reported roughly
$23.3 billion in railroad transportation revenues for 2025, according
to Berkshire's 2025 annual report.

### The Kraft Heinz Partnership with 3G Capital

Berkshire's partnership with 3G Capital, first on Heinz (2013) and then
on the Kraft Heinz merger (2015), represented a different capital
allocation pattern: passive minority co-ownership of a consumer
packaged-goods company managed by an operating partner. In 2013,
Berkshire and 3G together acquired H.J. Heinz Company. Berkshire's
initial investment consisted of 425 million shares of Heinz Holding
common stock, warrants to acquire approximately 46 million additional
shares at one cent per share (exercised in June 2015), and cumulative
compounding preferred stock with a liquidation preference of $8 billion.
The aggregate cost was $12.25 billion. 3G acquired 425 million shares
for $4.25 billion and took operational control.

In March 2015, Heinz Holding and Kraft Foods Group announced a merger
agreement. Kraft shareholders received one share of newly issued Heinz
Holding common stock per Kraft share, plus a special cash dividend of
$16.50 per share. The aggregate special dividend of approximately $10
billion was funded entirely by an additional equity contribution from
Berkshire and 3G. On July 1, 2015, Berkshire acquired 262.9 million
shares of newly issued Heinz Holding common stock for $5.26 billion;
3G acquired 237.1 million shares for $4.74 billion. After the merger
closed on July 2, 2015, Berkshire owned approximately 26.8 percent and
3G owned 24.2 percent of the combined company (renamed The Kraft Heinz
Company), with the two together controlling approximately 51 percent.

The structure reveals several principles. Berkshire chose to be a
passive capital partner rather than the operating manager -- a
significant departure from the BNSF model, where Berkshire took full
ownership and let existing management run the railroad. The preferred
stock (paying 9 percent per annum, callable after June 7, 2016 at
liquidation value plus premium) provided a fixed return while the
common stock provided equity upside. The deal used equity rather than
debt to fund the special dividend, meaning the merger did not increase
the combined company's debt levels -- a deliberate choice to maintain
investment-grade rating.

The Kraft Heinz investment also illustrates the cost of issuing
Berkshire shares as acquisition currency. The 2015 annual report noted
that the issuance of new common stock by Kraft Heinz for the Kraft
common stock reduced Berkshire's ownership from approximately 52.5
percent to 26.8 percent. Under the equity method, this was accounted
for as if Berkshire had sold a proportionate share of its investment,
producing a non-cash pre-tax holding gain of approximately $6.8
billion in the third quarter of 2015. The gain was real but
instructive: Berkshire's ownership stake shrank because new shares
were issued, and the accounting gain reflected the difference between
carrying value and fair value at the time of dilution.

### The IBM Investment and the Concept of a Value Trap

In March 2011, Buffett disclosed that Berkshire had accumulated a $10.7
billion stake in IBM, purchasing approximately 64 million shares at an
average price of $170 per share. The investment surprised observers
given Buffett's well-known avoidance of technology companies. Buffett
argued that IBM had evolved beyond being a pure technology company and
had become more of a stable, cash-generating business services company.
He emphasized IBM's capital allocation discipline -- the company was
buying back shares aggressively, which mechanically reduced share count
and increased per-share earnings even if total earnings stagnated. At
$170, IBM traded at approximately 13 times 2011 earnings, which
appeared reasonable for a company with sticky enterprise relationships.

The IBM investment became one of the clearest teaching examples of a
value trap in the Berkshire corpus. A value trap is a company trading
at a depressed valuation that appears cheap relative to historical
metrics but is actually expensive relative to its permanent earnings
power because the company faces secular decline, not temporary
problems. IBM's revenue declined for years after the 2011 purchase as
the technology market shifted from on-premise servers and mainframes to
cloud computing (Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud
Platform). IBM's capital-intensive nature -- heavy buybacks and
dividends -- masked deteriorating underlying business economics.

Buffett eventually sold the entire IBM position by mid-2018, having
endured approximately a 20 percent loss on the cost basis. The episode
illustrates that valuation discipline (avoiding overpaying) is
necessary but insufficient -- the quality and durability of the
business matters equally. The IBM mistake is instructive precisely
because Buffett had analyzed the company carefully, identified what he
believed was a moat in enterprise IT stickiness, and was still wrong.
The lesson is not that Buffett was careless but that secular decline can
persist for years, rendering a "cheap" stock increasingly less valuable
as earnings contract.

### The Fifty-Year Retrospective and the Berkshire System

The 2014 annual report contained a fifty-year retrospective in which
Buffett and Munger each independently wrote about what had happened at
Berkshire over the prior half-century and what each expected in the
next fifty years. Neither changed a word of his commentary after
reading what the other wrote. Buffett's essay, "Berkshire -- Past,
Present and Future," began on page 24; Munger's, "Vice Chairman's
Thoughts -- Past and Future," began on page 39.

Buffett's retrospective stated that Berkshire's gain in net worth during
2014 was $18.3 billion, increasing per-share book value by 8.3 percent.
Over the 50 years since present management took over, per-share book
value grew from $19 to $146,186, a rate of 19.4 percent compounded
annually. Buffett and Munger believed that the increase in Berkshire's
per-share intrinsic value over the prior 50 years was roughly equal to
the 1,826,163 percent gain in market price of the company's shares --
a statement that book value was a crude but useful tracking device, and
that intrinsic value, not book value, was the number that really
counted.

Munger's retrospective provided the clearest single-document statement
of the Berkshire system. He identified the management system and
policies that caused a small, unfixably-doomed commodity textile
business to morph into the present Berkshire. The elements included:
Berkshire as a diffuse conglomerate averse only to activities it could
not predict; extreme operating autonomy for subsidiary CEOs; a tiny
headquarters with only a chairman, CFO, and a few assistants; the
prominent inclusion of casualty insurers expected to produce
underwriting gains plus float for investment; no significant
system-wide personnel, option, or retirement systems; the chairman
reserving only a few activities; almost never selling a subsidiary;
almost never transferring a CEO to an unrelated subsidiary; never
forcing a CEO to retire on account of mere age; little debt to maintain
near-perfect creditworthiness; and a user-friendly posture toward
prospective sellers of large businesses.

Munger diagnosed why Berkshire did well under the system: the
constructive peculiarities of Buffett, the constructive peculiarities
of the Berkshire system, good luck, and the weirdly intense, contagious
devotion of some shareholders and admirers. He predicted that
abnormally good results would continue if Buffett departed, because
Berkshire had in place much business momentum grounded in durable
competitive advantage, and its railroad and utility businesses provided
desirable opportunity to invest large sums in new fixed assets. The
retrospective was both a celebration and a succession-planning document
-- a statement that the system, not the man alone, produced the
results.

### Judging Performance Per Share

A recurring theme across the 2006-2015 reports is the distinction
between aggregate growth and per-share growth. Buffett repeatedly
emphasized that the relevant metric was not total earnings or total
book value but per-share intrinsic value. The 2014 retrospective made
this explicit: per-share book value grew from $19 to $146,186 over 50
years, but the percentage gains could not continue at past rates
because "the numbers have become too big." Buffett predicted that
Berkshire would outperform the average American company but that the
advantage, if any, would not be great.

The per-share discipline connects to several capital allocation
decisions in the decade. The BNSF acquisition required issuing Berkshire
shares, which diluted existing shareholders -- Buffett acknowledged
this cost but judged the railroad's earning power sufficient to
overcome the dilution. The Kraft Heinz deal further diluted Berkshire's
effective ownership from 52.5 percent to 26.8 percent, though the
accounting gain softened the optics. The buyback policy, unchanged
since 1999 and restricted to prices below 120 percent of book value,
remained inactive through this decade because Berkshire's stock did not
fall below that threshold -- the policy would not be relaxed until
mid-2018, after this decade closed.

The per-share discipline also explains Buffett's hostility to issuing
shares casually. Throughout the decade, he treated Berkshire stock as
the most valuable currency the company possessed -- more valuable than
cash, because cash could be replenished by insurance float and
operating earnings, but shares, once issued, permanently diluted
existing owners. The BNSF deal and the Class B split were undertaken
because the railroad's scale and durability justified the dilution.
The IBM investment, by contrast, used cash, not shares -- Buffett was
willing to risk $10.7 billion of cash on a thesis he turned out to be
wrong about, but he did not compound the error by issuing shares.

### Liquidity as a Strategic Asset

The crisis-era deals demonstrated that Berkshire's large cash position
was not idle capital waiting for deployment but a strategic asset whose
value increased precisely when other capital was unavailable. At the
end of 2007, Berkshire held approximately $44 billion in cash and
equivalents. By the time the crisis peaked in September-October 2008,
Berkshire had the liquidity to fund $5 billion to Goldman Sachs, $3
billion to GE, and $6.5 billion to the Mars-Wrigley transaction in the
span of three weeks -- approximately $14.5 billion deployed -- while
maintaining sufficient reserves to operate the insurance businesses
and absorb catastrophe losses.

Buffett's 2014 retrospective stated the principle explicitly: it is
entirely predictable that people will occasionally panic, but not at
all predictable when this will happen. Though practically all days are
relatively uneventful, tomorrow is always uncertain. If you cannot
predict what tomorrow will bring, you must be prepared for whatever it
does. He described Berkshire's conservatism as potentially extreme to
some, but grounded in the conviction that it was madness to risk losing
what you need in pursuing what you simply desire. The retrospective
noted that Berkshire would maintain virtually perfect creditworthiness
under all conditions and easy availability of cash and credit for
deployment in times presenting unusual opportunities.

The liquidity discipline has an opportunity cost: cash held during
non-crisis periods earns modest returns while equity markets compound.
The decade 2006-2015 demonstrated the payoff: the crisis-era
preferred-stock deals produced billions in dividends and warrant gains
that would not have been available to a company that had deployed all
its cash during the preceding boom. The BNSF acquisition, requiring
approximately $26 billion in cash and stock, was possible because
Berkshire had not exhausted its reserves. The lesson is that liquidity
is not merely a risk-management tool but a source of countercyclical
returns -- its value is asymmetric, high in crisis and low in calm,
and it must be preserved during calm periods to capture the crisis
premium.

## Evidence

### The 2008-2009 Financial Crisis Deals

The financial crisis deals provide the clearest empirical evidence for
Berkshire's capital allocation discipline under stress. The four major
crisis-era investments -- Goldman Sachs, General Electric, Mars-Wrigley,
and Bank of America -- shared a common structure (preferred stock plus
warrants) but differed in timing, terms, and outcome.

The Goldman Sachs deal was announced September 23, 2008, per a joint
press release filed with the SEC. Berkshire purchased $5 billion of
perpetual preferred stock with a 10 percent dividend, callable at a 10
percent premium, plus warrants to buy $5 billion of common stock at
$115 per share exercisable over five years. Goldman simultaneously
raised at least $2.5 billion in common equity, bringing total capital
to $10 billion. The deal was struck eight days after Lehman Brothers
filed for bankruptcy, when credit markets had effectively stopped
functioning. Goldman redeemed the preferred stock in April 2011,
returning Berkshire's $5 billion principal plus the 10 percent premium
and accrued dividends. In March 2013, the warrants were renegotiated
into a net-share settlement, leaving Berkshire with approximately 2
percent of Goldman's common stock without requiring the $5 billion
exercise payment.

The General Electric deal followed on October 1, 2008. GE agreed to
sell Berkshire $3 billion of preferred stock paying a 10 percent
dividend ($300 million per year), redeemable at GE's option at 110
percent of liquidation value, with warrants to buy $3 billion of common
stock at $22.25 per share, per GE's 8-K filing with the SEC. GE redeemed
the preferred stock in 2011, and the warrants were later settled for a
smaller equity stake than the Goldman warrants produced.

The Mars-Wrigley financing, funded October 6, 2008, was structurally
different. The terms were set in April 2008 when Mars announced its
intent to acquire Wrigley -- months before the September panic. Berkshire
invested $6.5 billion to help fund the closing, structured as $4.4
billion of 11.45 percent subordinated notes due 2018 and $2.1 billion of
preferred stock paying 5 percent. The subordinated notes were redeemed
in October 2013 at 115.45 percent of par ($5.08 billion), producing an
estimated internal rate of return of 14.4 percent. The preferred stock
was repurchased in September 2016 at 115.45 percent of par, producing an
estimated IRR of 14.1 percent. An additional $1 billion of 5 percent
senior notes was invested in December 2009, redeemed on schedule in
2013-2014 at approximately 5 percent IRR.

The Bank of America deal, announced August 25, 2011, was the last and
most profitable of the crisis-era investments. Berkshire invested $5
billion in 6 percent preferred stock (redeemable at a 5 percent premium)
plus warrants on 700 million shares at $7.142857 each over ten years.
Berkshire exercised the warrants on August 29, 2017, converting the $5
billion investment into a large common-stock stake. University of
Maryland professor David Kass calculated a total profit of approximately
$13 billion, comprising roughly $11.5 billion in capital gains plus
approximately $1.5 billion in preferred dividends.

The aggregate evidence across the four deals supports several
conclusions. First, the preferred-stock-plus-warrant structure produced
positive returns in every case, even when the underlying common stock
performed poorly (GE). Second, the warrants provided asymmetric upside
that exceeded the fixed-income component in the two best cases (Goldman,
Bank of America). Third, the timing was driven by counterparty
desperation, not by Berkshire's predictions about market direction --
Buffett stated repeatedly that he could not time markets, but he could
recognize when capital was scarce and price it accordingly.

### The BNSF Acquisition Outcome

The BNSF acquisition provides the strongest evidence for Berkshire's
willingness to accept adequate returns when scale demands it. The deal
was announced November 3, 2009, per a joint press release filed with
the SEC. Berkshire offered $100 per share in cash or stock for the
remaining 77.4 percent of BNI shares, with consideration split
approximately 60 percent cash and 40 percent stock. The transaction
required approval by holders of two-thirds of BNI's outstanding shares
(other than shares held by Berkshire) and customary closing conditions
including Department of Justice review. The deal closed February 12,
2010.

The financial terms were documented in detail. The total deal value
reached approximately $44 billion, comprising $8 billion in BNSF shares
Berkshire already owned, $26 billion in cash paid for the remaining
shares, and $10 billion in assumed BNSF debt. The cash component
included $15.9 billion in cash and $10.6 billion in newly issued
Berkshire stock. The stock issuance required a 50-to-1 split of the
Class B shares to make the consideration acceptable to BNSF
shareholders.

The post-acquisition performance validated the thesis. Buffett's 2010
letter stated that the BNSF acquisition was working out better than
expected and would increase Berkshire's normal earning power by nearly
40 percent pre-tax and by well over 30 percent after-tax. By March
2011, 13 months after closing, BNSF had paid Berkshire $2.25 billion in
dividends -- nearly triple what BNSF paid in a comparable pre-acquisition
period. BNSF's top-line growth in the decade following the deal was
modest (revenue increased approximately 25 percent), but the cash
generation and the strategic position (operating 32,500 route miles
across 28 U.S. states and three Canadian provinces) confirmed the
infrastructure thesis. BNSF reported approximately $23.3 billion in
railroad transportation revenues for 2025, according to Berkshire's
2025 annual report.

The BNSF evidence tests the distinction between capital-light franchise
investing and capital-intensive infrastructure investing. See's
Candies (acquired 1972) required minimal incremental capital and
compounded returns for decades. BNSF required sustained capital
expenditure -- Buffett told BNSF CEO Matt Rose that Berkshire would be
investing more in the near term to build for the future. The railroad's
returns depended on volume growth, operating efficiency, and pricing
power in a regulated industry, not on brand strength or customer
stickiness. The lesson is that at Berkshire's scale, the universe of
capital-light opportunities becomes too small to deploy the available
capital, and the company must accept lower returns on larger amounts
to keep compounding.

### The Kraft Heinz Merger and Dilution Accounting

The Kraft Heinz merger provides empirical evidence for the cost of
issuing equity and the accounting treatment of dilution. The 2015
Berkshire annual report, filed with the SEC, documented the transaction
in detail. On July 1, 2015, Berkshire acquired 262.9 million shares of
newly issued Heinz Holding common stock for $5.26 billion, and 3G
acquired 237.1 million shares for $4.74 billion. After the merger
closed on July 2, 2015, Berkshire owned approximately 26.8 percent of
the combined company (renamed The Kraft Heinz Company) and 3G owned
approximately 24.2 percent, together controlling approximately 51
percent.

The accounting treatment is instructive. The issuance of new common
stock by Kraft Heinz for the Kraft common stock reduced Berkshire's
ownership from approximately 52.5 percent to 26.8 percent. Under the
equity method, the issuance of shares by an investee is accounted for
by the investor as if the investor had sold a proportionate share of
its investment. Berkshire recorded a non-cash pre-tax holding gain of
approximately $6.8 billion in the third quarter of 2015, representing
the excess of the fair value of Kraft Heinz common stock at the date of
the merger over Berkshire's carrying value associated with the
reduction in ownership.

The preferred stock component carried a 9 percent annual dividend,
senior to common stock, callable after June 7, 2016 at liquidation
value plus applicable premium and accrued dividends. Kraft Heinz
announced its intention to call the preferred stock after June 7, 2016
for approximately $8.3 billion. The preferred dividends produced $728
million in 2015, $694 million in 2014, and $146 million in 2013, per
Berkshire's consolidated statements. The structure provided Berkshire
with a fixed return during the operating transition while 3G managed
cost reduction and integration.

The Kraft Heinz evidence illustrates a tension in Berkshire's capital
allocation: the willingness to deploy large sums in partnership with an
operating manager (3G) who applied a cost-cutting playbook that
Berkshire would not run itself. Buffett praised the merger as "my kind
of transaction, uniting two world-class organizations and delivering
shareholder value." The longer-term outcome (Kraft Heinz struggled in
subsequent years with debt, dividend cuts, and an accounting
restatement) was not yet visible in the 2006-2015 window, but the
structure -- passive minority co-ownership with preferred downside
protection -- reveals Berkshire's approach to deals where it cannot or
will not manage operations directly.

### The IBM Value Trap

The IBM investment provides the clearest evidence for the value trap
concept within the Berkshire record. Buffett disclosed the $10.7
billion position in March 2011, having purchased approximately 64
million shares at an average price of $170. IBM traded at approximately
13 times 2011 earnings, which appeared reasonable for a company with
sticky enterprise relationships and aggressive share repurchases.
Buffett argued that IBM was more than a technology company, with
roughly 50 percent of revenue from services, and that it was a "big
deal" for a large company to change IT support -- creating a
"presumption" that enterprise customers stayed with IBM.

The evidence against the thesis accumulated over the following years.
IBM's revenue declined as the technology market shifted from on-premise
servers and mainframes to cloud computing. Amazon Web Services,
Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud Platform captured enterprise
workloads, and IBM's myopic focus on near-term profits (heavy buybacks
and dividends) masked the deteriorating underlying business economics.
By mid-2018, IBM's stock had fallen to approximately $140 per share, and
Buffett had sold the entire position, incurring approximately a 20
percent loss on the cost basis.

The IBM case provides empirical support for several propositions. A
company can be cheap on current earnings and expensive on permanent
earnings power if the business faces secular decline rather than
cyclical weakness. Capital allocation discipline (buybacks) can
mechanically increase per-share metrics while the underlying business
deteriorates -- a value trap mechanism where the per-share optics
disguise the aggregate reality. And a careful analysis of moat and
stickiness can still be wrong when the competitive landscape shifts in
ways that the analyst did not predict. The lesson is not that Buffett
was careless but that secular decline can persist for years, rendering
"cheap" stocks increasingly less valuable as earnings contract.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocators: When to Deploy Cash and When to Hold It

The 2006-2015 decade offers the clearest case study in the value of
holding cash for countercyclical deployment. Berkshire entered the
financial crisis with approximately $44 billion in cash and equivalents,
having accumulated this reserve during the 2003-2007 boom when
attractive opportunities were scarce. When the crisis hit, this cash
became the most valuable asset on Berkshire's balance sheet -- not
because of its intrinsic return but because of its scarcity value. The
crisis-era preferred-stock deals (Goldman, GE, Wrigley, and later Bank
of America) deployed approximately $19.5 billion of this cash at terms
that would have been unavailable in normal markets: 10 percent
dividends on perpetual preferred, warrants struck below market, and
call premiums that guaranteed a minimum return.

The implication for capital allocators is that the opportunity cost of
holding cash during calm periods must be measured against the
asymmetric returns available during crises. A capital allocator who
deploys all available cash during a bull market forfeits the ability to
act as a lender of last resort when credit markets freeze. The
discipline required is significant: during 2003-2007, Berkshire
underperformed a fully-invested benchmark, and shareholders and
commentators questioned whether the cash drag was justified. The
crisis vindicated the patience, but the vindication came only because
Berkshire had maintained the discipline through years of underwhelming
opportunity cost.

The BNSF acquisition extends this lesson. The railroad was not a
crisis-era distressed asset, but it required approximately $26 billion
in cash plus stock, a deployment possible only because Berkshire had
not exhausted its reserves in the crisis deals. The sequence --
crisis deals in 2008, BNSF in 2009-2010, Lubrizol in 2011 -- shows that
a large cash position can fund multiple major deployments in sequence,
provided the allocator does not overcommit to the first opportunity.

### For Investors Evaluating Conglomerate Capital Allocation

The decade provides a framework for evaluating how a conglomerate
allocates capital across its portfolio of businesses and new
opportunities. Berkshire's capital allocation in 2006-2015 fell into
four distinct modes: (1) crisis lending through structured preferred
investments, (2) full acquisition of capital-intensive businesses
(BNSF, Lubrizol), (3) passive minority co-ownership with an operating
partner (Kraft Heinz with 3G), and (4) public equity stakes (IBM, Wells
Fargo, American Express, Coca-Cola). Each mode had different
risk-return characteristics, different capital requirements, and
different implications for the per-share metric.

An investor evaluating a conglomerate should ask which mode the
allocator is using and whether the mode matches the opportunity.
Crisis lending requires a large cash reserve and the willingness to
demand favorable terms when counterparties are desperate. Full
acquisition of capital-intensive businesses requires accepting lower
returns on larger amounts and the willingness to commit to sustained
capital expenditure. Passive minority co-ownership requires trust in
an operating partner and structural downside protection (preferred
stock). Public equity stakes require the ability to identify durable
businesses at reasonable prices and the temperament to hold through
volatility.

The decade also demonstrates the cost of issuing equity as acquisition
currency. The BNSF deal required issuing approximately $10.6 billion in
Berkshire stock, permanently diluting existing shareholders. The Kraft
Heinz deal reduced Berkshire's ownership from 52.5 percent to 26.8
percent. An investor should track not just the aggregate value of
deployments but the per-share impact -- a conglomerate that grows total
earnings by issuing shares may not grow per-share value at all.

### For Students of the Buffett-Munger Reporting Philosophy

The 2006-2015 reports, particularly the 2008 letter and the 2014
fifty-year retrospective, are primary documents for studying the
Buffett-Munger reporting philosophy. Three principles emerge. First,
candor about errors is a fiduciary obligation, not a public-relations
risk. Buffett wrote openly about mistakes (IBM, the ConocoPhillips
timing, earlier errors) and treated the admission as a teaching
opportunity. Second, the reports are educational documents, not merely
disclosure documents. The letters explain the reasoning behind each
capital allocation decision, the framework used, and the lessons
learned -- providing a curriculum in capital allocation that no business
school course replicates. Third, the reports build a relationship of
trust with shareholders based on the assumption that shareholders are
owners, not counterparties.

The 2014 retrospective is particularly valuable because it is the only
document in which both Buffett and Munger independently assessed the
Berkshire system and its future. Munger's essay identified the system
elements (extreme decentralization, tiny headquarters, insurance float,
no system-wide incentives, never selling subsidiaries, never forcing
retirement) and diagnosed why they produced results (constructive
peculiarities of Buffett and the system, good luck, shareholder
devotion). Buffett's essay stated the growth constraint (numbers too
large for past rates) and the succession logic (the system, not the
man alone, produces results). For students of corporate governance and
capital allocation, these two essays are the most concentrated source of
operating philosophy in the Berkshire record.

### For Understanding Scale Constraints on Compounding

The decade provides direct evidence for the scale constraint that
Buffett identified in the 2014 retrospective: "Berkshire's long-term
gains -- measured by percentages, not by dollars -- cannot be
dramatic and will not come close to those achieved in the past 50
years. The numbers have become too big." The evidence is visible in the
capital allocation choices. BNSF, a $44 billion acquisition, increased
Berkshire's normal earning power by approximately 40 percent pre-tax --
a significant absolute gain but a modest percentage gain for a company
with Berkshire's market capitalization. The crisis-era deals produced
billions in profit but moved Berkshire's per-share value by single
digits. The IBM investment, at $10.7 billion, was one of the largest
public equity positions Berkshire had ever taken, yet it represented
only a modest percentage of total equity portfolio.

The implication for investors analyzing large compounds is that
percentage returns necessarily decline as the capital base grows, and
the allocator must shift from searching for multi-baggers to deploying
large sums at adequate returns. The BNSF acquisition is the paradigm
case: a railroad earning a regulated return on capital-intensive assets,
accepted because the alternative (finding multiple capital-light
businesses large enough to move the needle) was no longer feasible at
Berkshire's scale. Investors who expect a large compounder to maintain
its early-stage percentage growth are misunderstanding the mathematics
of scale, not the skill of the allocator.

### For Evaluating Acquisition Currency and Dilution

The BNSF and Kraft Heinz transactions provide a framework for evaluating
when issuing shares as acquisition currency is justified and when it is
destructive. The BNSF deal required issuing Berkshire stock because the
railroad's size made an all-cash transaction impractical, and Buffett
judged that the railroad's earning power would overcome the dilution.
The Kraft Heinz deal reduced Berkshire's ownership from a controlling
stake to a minority stake, and the accounting gain ($6.8 billion) did
not change the fundamental fact that Berkshire owned less of the
combined company than it owned of Heinz alone.

The framework: issuing shares is justified when (1) the acquired
business has durable earning power sufficient to overcome the dilution,
(2) the currency (Berkshire stock) is not significantly undervalued at
the time of issuance, and (3) no practical cash alternative exists.
Issuing shares is destructive when any of these conditions fail. The
IBM investment, by contrast, used cash rather than shares -- Buffett was
willing to risk a large cash sum on a thesis that proved wrong, but he
did not compound the error by permanently diluting existing
shareholders. The distinction between cash and stock as acquisition
currency is one of the most important capital allocation decisions a
conglomerate makes, and the 2006-2015 reports provide the clearest
Berkshire-era evidence for the framework.

## Common Pitfalls

The 2006-2015 reports illustrate several pitfalls that recur in capital
allocation analysis. First, conflating aggregate growth with per-share
growth: a conglomerate that issues shares to fund acquisitions may grow
total earnings while shrinking per-share value. The Kraft Heinz
dilution from 52.5 percent to 26.8 percent is the clearest example.
Second, misidentifying a value trap as a value opportunity: the IBM
investment looked cheap on current earnings and sticky on customer
relationships, but the secular shift to cloud computing rendered the
"cheap" valuation expensive relative to permanent earnings power.
Third, underestimating the opportunity cost of cash: a capital
allocator who deploys all cash during calm periods forfeits the
asymmetric returns available during crises, but a capital allocator
who holds too much cash for too long drags returns during extended bull
markets. The balance is the discipline. Fourth, assuming that past
percentage returns can continue at scale: the 2014 retrospective
explicitly warned that the numbers had become too large for past
rates, and the decade's capital allocation choices (BNSF, Kraft Heinz,
Lubrizol) confirm that Berkshire had shifted from seeking multi-baggers
to deploying large sums at adequate returns.
