# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 2006-2015 -- Liquidity Enabled Crisis Deployment, BNSF Expanded Scale, and Per-Share Value Remained the Scorecard

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 2006 through 2015 document a
decade in which the company deployed cash through negotiated crisis-era
securities, completed the largest acquisition in its history, partnered
with 3G Capital to build a packaged-food giant, and produced a
fifty-year retrospective that framed the entire Berkshire enterprise.
Read as a sequence, the ten reports reveal a capital allocation
discipline that swung from opportunistic distress financing to
capital-intensive infrastructure to passive minority stakes, and a
reporting philosophy that used candor about mistakes as a teaching
tool rather than a liability to be managed.

## Background

The decade 2006-2015 is a natural unit of analysis because it brackets
three of the most consequential events in Berkshire's post-1965 history.
First, the 2008-2009 financial crisis tested whether Berkshire's
liquidity and balance-sheet capacity could fund countercyclical
investments while credit markets were severely impaired. Second, the
November 2009 announcement and February 2010
closing of the BNSF acquisition -- valued at approximately $44 billion
including assumed debt -- represented Berkshire's largest transaction
ever and materially expanded its controlled-business base into
capital-intensive, regulated infrastructure. Third, the 2014 annual
report contained a fifty-year retrospective in which both Warren Buffett
and Charlie Munger independently wrote about what had happened at
Berkshire over the prior half-century and what each expected in the
next fifty years, providing an unusually concentrated statement of the
Berkshire system and its operating philosophy.

Berkshire's shareholder letters supplement the audited financial
statements with management's explanation of capital allocation,
operating economics, and errors. In the 2014 report, Buffett described
the reporting objective as giving owners the information he and Munger
would want if their positions were reversed. The same report discussed
the delayed sale of Tesco as a mistake, revisited the failed Dexter Shoe
acquisition, and distinguished accounting earnings from economic
reality. Candor therefore functions as part of Berkshire's owner-oriented
reporting policy rather than as a claim that every allocation decision
worked.

The decade opens with Berkshire already a diversified holding company
with major insurance operations (GEICO, General Re, National
Indemnity), regulated utilities (MidAmerican Energy, later Berkshire
Hathaway Energy), manufacturing and housing businesses, and large
equity stakes in American Express, Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, Wells
Fargo, and The Washington Post Company. The portfolio and controlled
business mix then changed materially. The Washington Post Company was
renamed Graham Holdings, and in 2014 Berkshire exchanged its Graham
shares for WPLG, a Miami television station, Berkshire shares held by
Graham, and cash. By the end of 2015, Kraft Heinz, IBM, and Wells Fargo
were major investments, while BNSF, Lubrizol, IMC (International
Metalworking Companies), and a larger Marmon stake had expanded the
controlled-business base.

Insurance float -- net policyholder funds held for investment -- grew
from approximately $49.3 billion at the end of 2005 to approximately
$88 billion at the end of 2015. That is a 78.5 percent increase, or
approximately 6.0 percent compounded annually over ten years. The 2015
letter's separate comparison of $41 billion with $88 billion covers the
thirteen-year run beginning at year-end 2002, not year-end 2005.
Buffett described float as money that does not belong to Berkshire but
can be invested for Berkshire's benefit while claims remain unpaid. The
author's synthesis is that growing float, retained earnings, and other
funding sources together supported the decade's large deployments; the
reports do not earmark particular dollars of float to particular deals.

The second half of the decade brought a different pattern: partnership
with 3G Capital, first on the 2013 acquisition of H.J. Heinz Company
and then on the 2015 merger of Heinz with Kraft Foods Group. Berkshire
acted as a capital partner while 3G led operations. This represented a
structural departure from the BNSF model, where Berkshire took full
ownership and retained the railroad's management. Through July 2015,
Berkshire had invested $12.25 billion in the original Heinz securities
and another $5.26 billion in Heinz Holding common stock. Kraft Heinz,
not Berkshire, then issued shares to former Kraft shareholders; that
investee-level issuance reduced Berkshire's ownership of the combined
company from 52.5 percent immediately before the merger to 26.8 percent
after it.

The decade also produced two especially useful teaching documents in the
Berkshire corpus. The 2008 letter, written during the depths of the
financial crisis, explained the preferred-stock investment structure
and the principle of treating capital as a scarce resource deployed
only when terms were favorable. The 2014 retrospective letter explained
why Berkshire's long-term percentage gains could not continue at past
rates, why the company would eventually need to consider dividends or
repurchases, and why the chance of permanent capital loss for patient
shareholders was "as low as can be found among single-company
investments."

## Core Concepts

### Pre-Crisis Positioning: Operating Acquisitions and an Initial BNSF Stake

The 2006 and 2007 reports show that the decade did not begin with
Berkshire waiting passively for a financial panic. In 2006 it completed
three previously pending acquisitions -- PacifiCorp, Business Wire, and
Applied Underwriters -- for about $6 billion in total, and it bought 80
percent of Israel-based ISCAR for $4 billion. The ISCAR transaction was
important both as Berkshire's first purchase of an operating business
based outside the United States and as another example of a family owner
selecting Berkshire as a permanent home. These transactions expanded
the controlled-business base before the crisis.

The 2007 report documents two other allocation modes. Berkshire agreed
to buy 60 percent of Marmon for $4.5 billion, with virtually all of the
balance to be purchased within six years under an earnings-based formula.
It also sold its 1.3 percent PetroChina interest for $4 billion after the
market value of the whole company reached about $275 billion, close to
Berkshire's estimate of value relative to other large oil companies.
The PetroChina sale is evidence that Buffett's stated preference for
long holding periods did not prohibit a sale when price reached his
estimate of value.

Berkshire also built the position that preceded the full BNSF purchase.
At year-end 2007 it owned 60,828,818 BNSF shares, or 17.5 percent, at a
cost of $4.731 billion and a market value of $5.063 billion. By the time
the 2010 merger closed, Berkshire already owned 22.5 percent of BNSF.
That sequence matters: the $44 billion transaction value later reported
for the whole company included the BNSF shares Berkshire already owned,
whereas the consideration paid in the 2010 merger covered only the
remaining interest.

The pre-crisis balance sheet explains Berkshire's capacity to act in
2008. Total cash and cash equivalents across its three reporting groups
were $44.329 billion at year-end 2007. This was not all freely deployable
parent cash -- regulated and operating subsidiaries had their own needs
-- but it establishes the consolidated liquidity base before Berkshire
committed $14.5 billion to Goldman Sachs, General Electric, and
Mars-Wrigley during the following year.

### The Preferred Stock Crisis Structure

One distinctive capital allocation pattern of the decade was Berkshire's
use of negotiated securities when capital was scarce. The transactions
were not all the same. Goldman Sachs and General Electric issued
high-coupon preferred stock with common-stock warrants; Mars-Wrigley
used subordinated notes and preferred stock without warrants; and the
2011 Bank of America package paired lower-coupon preferred stock with
long-dated warrants. Grouping them as a single preferred-plus-warrant
formula would therefore obscure material differences in seniority,
coupon, redemption rights, and upside participation.

Goldman announced on September 23, 2008 that Berkshire would buy $5
billion of perpetual preferred stock carrying a 10 percent dividend and
receive warrants to buy $5 billion of common stock at $115 per share for
five years. The preferred was callable at a 10 percent premium. Goldman
initially announced a public common-stock offering of at least $2.5
billion; it priced approximately $5 billion the next day, bringing the
combined capital raised from Berkshire and the public offering to $10
billion. GE announced a similar $3 billion package on October 1, and
Berkshire acquired the 10 percent preferred and warrants on October 16,
2008. The GE warrants covered 134,831,460 shares at $22.25 per share.

Mars-Wrigley was different. On October 6, 2008, Berkshire acquired $4.4
billion principal amount of 11.45 percent subordinated notes due in 2018
and $2.1 billion of 5 percent Wrigley preferred stock to help finance
Mars's acquisition. The terms had been negotiated before the September
panic, and the package included no common-stock warrant. On August 25,
2011, Bank of America announced a $5 billion Berkshire investment in 6
percent preferred stock plus warrants on 700 million common shares at
$7.142857 per share through September 2021.

The structures illustrate three principles. First, Berkshire obtained
contractual income while waiting: the Goldman and GE preferred issues
paid $500 million and $300 million annually while outstanding. Second,
warrants created optional equity upside, but exercising them normally
required additional consideration; the later Goldman and GE cashless
settlements and the Bank of America surrender of preferred stock avoided
a separate cash payment only because the terms were amended or the
exercise consideration was otherwise delivered. Third, the issuers
publicly described Berkshire's participation as a validation of their
franchises. The author's assessment is that this signaling value helped
Berkshire negotiate terms unavailable to ordinary public-market buyers.

The realized outcomes also differed. Goldman redeemed its preferred on
April 18, 2011 for $5.5 billion, and GE redeemed its preferred on October
17, 2011 for $3.3 billion. Cashless warrant exercises in October 2013
left Berkshire with 13,062,594 Goldman shares and 10,710,644 GE shares.
Mars repurchased the $4.4 billion Wrigley subordinated notes in October
2013 for $5.08 billion plus accrued interest. In September 2016, Mars
purchased Berkshire's Wrigley preferred for approximately $4.56 billion,
including a prorated dividend. On August 24, 2017, Berkshire exercised all Bank of
America warrants for 700 million shares and surrendered substantially
all of the preferred stock to satisfy the $5 billion exercise cost. At
year-end 2017 those common shares had a reported cost of $5.007 billion
and market value of $20.664 billion.

### BNSF as a Bet on Regulated, Capital-Intensive Infrastructure

The BNSF acquisition, announced November 3, 2009 and closed February 12,
2010, represented the largest transaction in Berkshire's history and a
structural pivot. The announcement valued the whole BNSF enterprise at
approximately $44 billion: about $34 billion of equity value, including
the shares Berkshire already owned, plus $10 billion of outstanding BNSF
debt. Berkshire offered $100 per share in cash or stock for the remaining
77.4 percent. At closing, the remaining interest was 264.5 million shares,
or 77.5 percent, and the actual merger consideration was $26.5 billion:
approximately $15.9 billion in cash plus 80,931 Berkshire Class A shares
and 20,976,621 Class B shares. The announced 60/40 cash-stock mix applied
to that merger consideration, not to the $44 billion enterprise value.

Buffett explained the thesis in the 2010 letter: the acquisition was
working out better than expected and appeared likely to increase
Berkshire's "normal" earning power by nearly 40 percent pre-tax and well
over 30 percent after tax. Railroads move a ton of freight roughly 500
miles on a gallon of diesel fuel, according to that report, giving rail a
cost and emissions advantage over trucking. The network nevertheless
requires continuing investment in track, equipment, and technology.
Buffett expected U.S. freight volumes to grow over time and stated that
Berkshire could supply the large amounts of capital BNSF would require.

The transaction tested several value-investing principles. The railroad
was regulated and capital-intensive, unlike capital-light businesses
such as See's Candies. At Berkshire's scale, however, opportunities able
to absorb tens of billions of dollars necessarily included businesses
with large reinvestment requirements and adequate rather than
extraordinary returns on incremental capital. The deal also required
issuing Berkshire shares, a currency Buffett generally tried to avoid.
The Class B stock was split 50-for-1 to facilitate the merger, and the
shares issued increased Berkshire's share count by about 6.1 percent.
Buffett later summarized the overall funding as about 70 percent cash and
30 percent Berkshire shares. He also said the purchase used $22 billion
of cash and increased Berkshire's share count by about 6 percent.

BNSF's filings provide an early cash-flow check. During the period from
February 13 through December 31, 2010, BNSF paid $1.25 billion of
dividends; in February 2011 it declared and paid another $1 billion to
its parent. The $2.25 billion total in the first thirteen months under
Berkshire compared with $772 million paid in the preceding 2009 and
pre-merger 2010 periods. That transfer did not by itself measure value
creation -- dividends can be financed by operating cash, asset sales, or
debt -- but BNSF also continued heavy capital investment. By 2015 BNSF
reported $21.967 billion of revenue and record pre-tax earnings of $6.8
billion. As a current outcome check, Berkshire's 2025 report listed about
$23.35 billion of railroad operating revenue, $5.476 billion of net
earnings, and $4.4 billion of dividends returned to Berkshire.

### The Kraft Heinz Partnership with 3G Capital

The 2013 Heinz acquisition introduced a partnership model. Berkshire and
3G each paid $4.25 billion for half of Heinz Holding's common stock, while
Berkshire alone bought $8 billion of 9 percent cumulative compounding
preferred stock. The common shares and near-zero-price warrants later
exercised by Berkshire cost $4.25 billion; the preferred brought its
initial Heinz investment to $12.25 billion. Buffett called Berkshire the
financing partner and 3G the operating partner. He estimated that the 9
percent coupon and other preferred-stock features should produce an
annual return of about 12 percent.

The 2015 Kraft transaction changed the economics. Berkshire invested an
additional $5.26 billion and 3G invested $4.74 billion in newly issued
Heinz Holding common stock. Immediately after a reverse split and before
the merger, Berkshire owned 52.5 percent of Heinz Holding. Heinz then
acquired Kraft Foods Group and was renamed Kraft Heinz. Each former Kraft
share became one Kraft Heinz share, and former Kraft shareholders
received a $16.50 special cash dividend funded by the $10 billion equity
contribution from Berkshire and 3G. After Kraft Heinz issued shares to
former Kraft holders, Berkshire owned 325,442,152 shares, or 26.8 percent
of the combined company. Berkshire did not issue its own shares in this
transaction.

Because Berkshire and 3G retained effective joint control, Berkshire
continued to apply equity-method accounting. The merger and related
investee share issuance produced a non-cash holding gain of approximately
$6.8 billion in Berkshire's 2015 accounts. That accounting gain was not
cash proceeds and did not, by itself, establish that value had been
created; it largely reflected remeasurement of the retained interest as
ownership moved from a majority stake in Heinz to a minority stake in the
larger combined company.

The preferred security remained separate from the common-stock merger.
It was entitled to 9 percent cumulative dividends, and Berkshire reported
preferred income of $720 million in 2014 and $852 million in 2015. Kraft
Heinz redeemed it in June 2016 for $8.32 billion; Berkshire reported $180
million of 2016 dividends before redemption and a $610 million redemption
gain. The structure therefore combined a contractual preferred return
with common-equity exposure, but only the preferred had senior contractual
protection. The common investment remained exposed to operating and
valuation risk.

### The IBM Investment and the Concept of a Value Trap

Berkshire accumulated 63,905,931 IBM shares during 2011 for $10.856
billion, an average cost of approximately $169.87 per share. Buffett's
stated thesis was
that IBM had become deeply embedded in large customers, had articulated a
per-share financial "road map," and used repurchases to increase each
remaining shareholder's interest. At year-end 2011 the stake represented
5.5 percent of IBM and had a market value of $11.751 billion.

The outcome supplies a useful but qualified value-trap case. In May 2017,
Buffett said that he had revalued IBM somewhat downward after seeing the
business perform and had sold about one-third of Berkshire's position.
He also pointed to stronger-than-expected competition. By the end of the
first quarter of 2018, Berkshire had sold the remaining shares. The later
sales establish that the original business assessment changed; they do
not establish the precise investment return. Berkshire's annual reports
do not disclose a complete lot-by-lot schedule of sale proceeds, and a
loss percentage inferred from one market price would omit the timing of
sales and dividends received.

As used here, a value trap is a security that looks inexpensive on historical earnings
or assets but whose future earning power is deteriorating. The author's
retrospective assessment is that IBM fits the analytical warning better
than the most categorical version of the label. The original thesis
relied partly on customer retention, management's earnings targets, and
repurchases. The subsequent reassessment showed that those factors did
not remove competitive and technological risk. The evidence supports the
lesson that a low multiple and shrinking share count cannot substitute
for a defensible estimate of normalized future cash earning power.

### The Fifty-Year Retrospective and the Berkshire System

The 2014 annual report contained a fifty-year retrospective in which
Buffett and Munger each independently wrote about what had happened at
Berkshire over the prior half-century and what each expected in the
next fifty years. Neither changed a word of his commentary after
reading what the other wrote. Buffett's essay, "Berkshire -- Past,
Present and Future," began on page 24; Munger's, "Vice Chairman's
Thoughts -- Past and Future," began on page 39.

Buffett's retrospective stated that Berkshire's gain in net worth during
2014 was $18.3 billion, increasing per-share book value by 8.3 percent.
Over the 50 years since present management took over, per-share book
value grew from $19 to $146,186, a rate of 19.4 percent compounded
annually. Buffett and Munger believed that the increase in Berkshire's
per-share intrinsic value over the prior 50 years was roughly equal to
the 1,826,163 percent gain in market price of the company's shares --
a statement that book value was a crude but useful tracking device, and
that intrinsic value, not book value, was the number that really
counted.

Munger's retrospective provided a detailed statement
of the Berkshire system. He identified the management system and
policies that caused a small, unfixably-doomed commodity textile
business to morph into the present Berkshire. The elements included:
Berkshire as a diffuse conglomerate averse only to activities it could
not predict; extreme operating autonomy for subsidiary CEOs; a tiny
headquarters with only a chairman, CFO, and a few assistants; the
prominent inclusion of casualty insurers expected to produce
underwriting gains plus float for investment; no significant
system-wide personnel, option, or retirement systems; the chairman
reserving only a few activities; almost never selling a subsidiary;
almost never transferring a CEO to an unrelated subsidiary; never
forcing a CEO to retire on account of mere age; little debt to maintain
near-perfect creditworthiness; and a user-friendly posture toward
prospective sellers of large businesses.

Munger diagnosed why Berkshire did well under the system: the
constructive peculiarities of Buffett, the constructive peculiarities
of the Berkshire system, good luck, and the weirdly intense, contagious
devotion of some shareholders and admirers. He predicted that
abnormally good results would continue if Buffett departed, because
Berkshire had in place much business momentum grounded in durable
competitive advantage, and its railroad and utility businesses provided
desirable opportunity to invest large sums in new fixed assets. The
author's assessment is that the retrospective also served a
succession-planning purpose by arguing that the system, rather than one
individual alone, produced the results.

### Judging Performance Per Share

A recurring theme across the 2006-2015 reports is the distinction
between aggregate growth and per-share growth. Buffett repeatedly
emphasized that the relevant metric was not total earnings or total
book value but per-share intrinsic value. The 2014 retrospective made
this explicit: per-share book value grew from $19 to $146,186 over 50
years, but the percentage gains could not continue at past rates
because "the numbers have become too big." Buffett predicted that
Berkshire would outperform the average American company but that the
advantage, if any, would not be great.

The per-share discipline connects to several capital allocation
decisions in the decade. The BNSF acquisition increased Berkshire's
share count by about 6.1 percent, but Buffett judged the railroad's
earning power sufficient to make continuing shareholders wealthier per
share. Kraft Heinz presented a different issue: Berkshire issued no
stock, but new investee shares reduced its percentage ownership of the
combined food company. Those two forms of dilution should not be
conflated.

Berkshire announced a repurchase program in September 2011. It authorized
purchases at no more than 110 percent of book
value while requiring at least $20 billion of cash equivalents. Berkshire
bought $67 million of stock before the market price moved above the
limit. In December 2012, the board raised the ceiling to 120 percent when
a large block became available at about 116 percent; total 2012
repurchases were $1.3 billion. The 120 percent limit remained in place
through 2015, so the policy was active during the decade.

The per-share discipline also explains Buffett's reluctance to issue
shares casually. Cash can be replenished by future retained earnings;
insurance float is a liability and is not an interchangeable substitute
for equity. Shares, once issued, permanently change each continuing
owner's claim. The BNSF deal and the Class B split were undertaken because
management judged the railroad's scale and durability worth that cost.
The IBM investment, by contrast, used cash rather than Berkshire shares,
so the later thesis revision reduced investment value but did not create
additional share-count dilution.

### Liquidity as a Strategic Asset

The crisis-era deals demonstrated an option-like benefit of Berkshire's
large liquidity position. At the end of 2007, Berkshire reported $44.329
billion in cash and equivalents across its reporting groups. By the time
the crisis peaked in September-October 2008, it could fund $5 billion to
Goldman Sachs, $3 billion to GE, and $6.5 billion to Mars-Wrigley within
about two weeks -- $14.5 billion in total -- while preserving resources
for insurance and operating obligations. Not all consolidated cash was
freely deployable, and liquidity also served solvency rather than only
investment opportunity.

Buffett's 2014 retrospective stated the principle explicitly: it is
entirely predictable that people will occasionally panic, but not at
all predictable when this will happen. Though practically all days are
relatively uneventful, tomorrow is always uncertain. If you cannot
predict what tomorrow will bring, you must be prepared for whatever it
does. He described Berkshire's conservatism as potentially extreme to
some, but grounded in the conviction that it was madness to risk losing
what you need in pursuing what you simply desire. The retrospective
noted that Berkshire would maintain virtually perfect creditworthiness
under all conditions and easy availability of cash and credit for
deployment in times presenting unusual opportunities.

Liquidity has an opportunity cost because short-term instruments may
earn less than attractive operating assets or equities. The offset is
the ability to meet claims without forced sales and to commit capital
when financing is scarce. The crisis securities generated contractual
dividends and redemption premiums, while some warrants later produced
large equity gains. The author's synthesis is that liquidity supplied
both resilience and countercyclical capacity; the annual reports do not
permit a precise calculation of a single "liquidity return," because the
same cash pool also protected policyholders and operating subsidiaries.

## Evidence

### The 2008-2011 Negotiated Securities

Primary filings reconcile the timing, terms, and outcomes of the four
negotiated packages. They should not all be described as 2008 crisis
investments or as preferred-stock-plus-warrant transactions: Bank of
America was negotiated in 2011, and Mars-Wrigley included no warrant.

The Goldman announcement was filed with the SEC on September 23, 2008,
eight days after Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. Berkshire agreed
to buy $5 billion of 10 percent perpetual preferred stock, callable at
$5.5 billion, and received five-year warrants to buy $5 billion of common
stock at $115 per share. The release initially described a public common
offering of at least $2.5 billion; Goldman's next-day pricing release
reported 40.65 million shares at $123, or about $5 billion gross. The two
financings therefore totaled about $10 billion only after that offering
was priced. Goldman paid $5.5 billion to redeem the preferred on April 18,
2011. A later amendment replaced the original cash exercise with a
cashless settlement, and on October 1, 2013 Berkshire received 13,062,594
shares. Berkshire reported those shares as a 2.8 percent interest at
2013 year-end.

GE announced its package on October 1, 2008, but Berkshire's annual
report records the acquisition date as October 16. Berkshire paid $3
billion for 10 percent perpetual preferred stock redeemable at $3.3
billion and received warrants for 134,831,460 shares at $22.25. GE
redeemed the preferred on October 17, 2011 for $3.3 billion. Following an
amendment, Berkshire made a cashless warrant exercise on October 16, 2013
and received 10,710,644 shares. These facts support a positive contractual
outcome without relying on an imprecise estimate of total return.

Mars-Wrigley closed on October 6, 2008 under terms negotiated in April.
Berkshire bought $4.4 billion of 11.45 percent subordinated notes and $2.1
billion of 5 percent preferred stock. Mars repurchased the subordinated
notes for $5.08 billion plus accrued interest in October 2013. Berkshire's
2016 report records the later preferred-stock disposition: Mars paid
approximately $4.56 billion, including a prorated dividend, and Berkshire
recognized an approximately $2.4 billion gain. A complete return
calculation would also require the timing and amount of every dividend.

Bank of America announced on August 25, 2011 that Berkshire would buy $5
billion of 6 percent preferred stock and receive ten-year warrants on 700
million shares at $7.142857. On August 24, 2017,
Berkshire exercised the warrants and used substantially all of the
preferred stock as the $5 billion consideration. The year-end 2017
annual report supplies a directly comparable result: 700 million common
shares, a $5.007 billion cost basis, and $20.664 billion market value.
That unrealized difference does not equal total profit because Berkshire
had also received preferred dividends and had not sold the common shares.

Across the cases, the evidence supports three bounded conclusions. High
coupons and redemption premiums compensated Berkshire while capital was
committed. Warrants added equity upside in the Goldman, GE, and Bank of
America cases but required exercise consideration unless amended or
settled net. Finally, the security-by-security terms, rather than a
single formula, determined each payoff.

### The BNSF Acquisition Outcome

The November 3, 2009 announcement and the acquisition note in Berkshire's
2010 financial statements resolve several figures that are easy to mix.
Berkshire already owned 76,777,029 shares, or 22.5 percent. The $44
billion announced transaction value covered the whole company and
included $10 billion of outstanding BNSF debt. The closing-date
consideration for the other 264.5 million shares was $26.5 billion,
consisting of $15.9 billion cash, 80,931 Berkshire Class A shares, and
20,976,621 Class B shares. The stock was part of, rather than additional
to, the $26.5 billion consideration.

The operating evidence through 2015 supports the original earning-power
thesis while showing its capital intensity. BNSF's 2015 revenue was
$21.967 billion, pre-tax earnings were a record $6.8 billion, and net
earnings applicable to Berkshire were $4.248 billion. BNSF operated
approximately 32,500 route miles in 28 states and three Canadian
provinces. From the February 2010 acquisition date through 2015, reported
capital expenditures ranged from $1.829 billion for the partial 2010
period to $5.651 billion in 2015 and totaled $23.514 billion. Earnings
therefore cannot be evaluated independently of the large reinvestment
required to maintain and expand the network.

The 2010 BNSF filing also verifies the early dividend comparison. It
reported $1.25 billion of dividends after the merger during 2010 and a
$1 billion dividend in February 2011. The comparable 2009 and pre-merger
2010 dividends were $546 million and $226 million, a combined $772
million. The higher distributions demonstrate that Berkshire received
cash from the subsidiary; they do not alone prove that the acquisition
created value, because dividends are transfers within the consolidated
group.

The current outcome remains economically material. Berkshire's 2025
report records BNSF railroad operating revenue of $23.35 billion, net
earnings of $5.476 billion, and dividends of $4.4 billion. The author's
assessment is that BNSF broadened Berkshire's opportunity set from
capital-light franchises to large regulated infrastructure. The price
of that scale was continuous reinvestment and a 6.1 percent increase in
Berkshire's share count, so performance should be judged on incremental
cash earning power per Berkshire share rather than revenue growth or
subsidiary dividends alone.

### The Kraft Heinz Merger and Dilution Accounting

The 2015 financial-statement note distinguishes Berkshire's securities
from the merger mechanics. Before the merger, Berkshire's original Heinz
investment consisted of 425 million common shares, warrants for about 46
million additional shares, and $8 billion of preferred stock. Berkshire
exercised the warrants in June 2015. It then bought 262.9 million newly
issued Heinz Holding shares for $5.26 billion while 3G bought 237.1
million for $4.74 billion. A 0.443332-for-one reverse split preceded the
Kraft acquisition. Immediately before that acquisition Berkshire owned
52.5 percent of Heinz Holding; after Kraft shareholders received shares,
Berkshire owned 325,442,152 Kraft Heinz shares, or 26.8 percent.

The dilution occurred at Kraft Heinz, not at Berkshire. Because Berkshire
and 3G together retained effective control, Berkshire continued to use
the equity method and recognized a non-cash holding gain of approximately
$6.8 billion. The annual report explains that an investee's share
issuance is treated as though the investor sold a proportionate interest.
The gain represented the excess of the fair value associated with the
interest deemed sold over its carrying value. It was an accounting
consequence of combining Heinz and Kraft, not cash received by Berkshire.

The preferred stock remained outside that common-stock ownership change.
It carried a 9 percent cumulative dividend. Berkshire later reported $720
million of preferred income in 2014, $852 million in 2015, and $180
million in 2016 before Kraft Heinz redeemed the security for $8.32
billion in June 2016. Berkshire also recognized a $610 million redemption
gain.

The case supports a precise distinction. BNSF issuance diluted each
Berkshire share because Berkshire itself issued stock. Kraft Heinz
issuance reduced Berkshire's percentage ownership of an investee but did
not change Berkshire's own share count. Evaluating either transaction
requires comparing the economic interest acquired with the interest or
currency surrendered; the $6.8 billion accounting gain alone does not
answer that question.

### The IBM Thesis Revision

Berkshire's annual reports verify the initial position and its later
expansion. It bought 63,905,931 IBM shares in 2011 for $10.856 billion,
an average of $169.87 per share; those shares were worth $11.751 billion
at year-end. By year-end 2016, Berkshire held 81,232,303 shares at a
reported cost of $13.815 billion and market value of $13.484 billion.
Thus the position was slightly below cost at that date before considering
dividends, but that snapshot is not a complete investment return.

The later evidence includes Buffett's own CNBC explanation. On May
4, 2017 he said he had revalued IBM downward and sold about one-third of
the position after seeing the business perform and observing stronger
competition. On May 4, 2018 he said Berkshire had sold the rest during
the first quarter. Those statements establish a changed thesis and a
complete exit. Calculating the exact return would require the prices and
dates of all purchases, sales, and dividends.

The analytical implication is narrower than hindsight makes it appear.
Repurchases add value only when made below intrinsic value and when the
business retains adequate future earning power. IBM's shrinking share
count could not validate the original estimate of that earning power.
The author's assessment is that this is the value-trap mechanism shown by
the case: apparently favorable historical multiples and buybacks did not
eliminate secular competitive risk.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocators: When to Deploy Cash and When to Hold It

The decade provides a case study in liquidity's dual role. Berkshire
reported $44.329 billion of consolidated cash and equivalents at year-end
2007. This balance supported policyholder and operating obligations, but
it also let Berkshire commit $14.5 billion to Goldman, GE, and
Mars-Wrigley in 2008. The 2011 Bank of America transaction added another
$5 billion later. The exact terms differed: Goldman and GE offered 10
percent preferred dividends, call premiums, and warrants; Wrigley offered
notes and preferred stock without warrants; Bank of America offered a 6
percent preferred dividend and long-dated warrants. None was risk-free,
because the contractual payments still depended on issuer solvency.

The implication is not that maximum cash always produces maximum value.
Cash and short-term Treasury instruments may lag attractive productive
assets for long periods. The relevant comparison is between that carrying
cost and the combined value of solvency, avoidance of forced sales, and
the ability to transact when other capital is scarce. Berkshire's record
does not isolate those benefits into a single return number. It does show
that a liquidity reserve can serve insurance needs and create investment
optionality at the same time.

The BNSF financing extends the lesson but also limits it. The closing
consideration for the remaining BNSF interest was $26.5 billion: $15.9
billion cash and $10.6 billion of Berkshire stock. Of the cash, $8 billion
came from newly issued parent debt and $7.9 billion from cash on hand.
Berkshire therefore did not fund the deal solely from an untouched cash
reserve. The sequence -- 2008 negotiated securities, BNSF in 2010, and
Lubrizol in 2011 -- instead shows access to multiple funding sources and
the need to preserve capacity rather than simply preserve cash.

### For Investors Evaluating Conglomerate Capital Allocation

The decade provides a framework for evaluating how a conglomerate
allocates capital. Berkshire used at least five modes: negotiated senior
securities; full acquisitions such as BNSF and Lubrizol; partnership
investments such as Heinz with 3G; public equity holdings; and repurchases
of Berkshire's own stock. Earlier operating acquisitions such as ISCAR
and Marmon also show that a full acquisition can be capital-light or
capital-intensive. Each mode has different control rights, funding needs,
and effects on per-share value.

An investor evaluating a conglomerate should ask which mode the
allocator is using and whether the mode matches the opportunity.
Crisis lending requires liquidity and the ability to analyze issuer
solvency under pressure. Full acquisition of capital-intensive businesses
requires funding sustained reinvestment. Partnership investing requires
clear control, governance, and incentive expectations. The Heinz package
also shows that securities within one deal can carry different risks:
the preferred stock had contractual seniority; the common stock did not.
Public equities require an estimate of durable future economics rather
than reliance on historical multiples. Repurchases require both surplus
liquidity and a price below conservatively estimated intrinsic value.

The decade also distinguishes two forms of dilution. BNSF required about
$10.6 billion of Berkshire stock and increased Berkshire's share count
by 6.1 percent. Kraft Heinz reduced Berkshire's ownership of an investee
from 52.5 percent to 26.8 percent when the investee issued shares, but it
did not increase Berkshire's share count. An analyst should track both
the parent's per-share claim and the parent's percentage interest in each
investee's expanded asset and earnings base.

### For Students of the Buffett-Munger Reporting Philosophy

The 2006-2015 reports, particularly the 2008 letter and the 2014
fifty-year retrospective, are primary documents for studying the
Buffett-Munger reporting philosophy. Three principles emerge. First,
Buffett identified allocation errors such as the ill-timed ConocoPhillips
purchase and the delayed Tesco sale rather than presenting a uniformly
successful record. The IBM thesis was revised publicly only after this
ten-year period. Second, the letters explain why management chose a form
of financing or an operating structure, supplementing mandatory
disclosures. Third, they address shareholders as owners who need enough
information to evaluate both the businesses and management's reasoning.

The 2014 retrospective is particularly valuable because both Buffett and
Munger independently assessed the Berkshire system and its future in the
same report. Munger's essay identified the system
elements (extreme decentralization, tiny headquarters, insurance float,
no system-wide incentives, never selling subsidiaries, never forcing
retirement) and diagnosed why they produced results (constructive
peculiarities of Buffett and the system, good luck, shareholder
devotion). Buffett's essay stated the growth constraint (numbers too
large for past rates) and the succession logic (the system, not the
man alone, produces results). For students of corporate governance and
capital allocation, the author's assessment is that these two essays are
the most concentrated source of operating philosophy in this ten-year
record.

### For Understanding Scale Constraints on Compounding

The 2014 report states the scale constraint directly: Berkshire's future
percentage gains could not approach those of its first fifty years
because the capital base had become too large. The decade's transactions
show both the constraint and management's responses. The BNSF purchase
was unusually consequential -- Buffett estimated a nearly 40 percent
increase in normal pre-tax earning power -- but it required $22 billion
of cash in aggregate, a 6.1 percent share-count increase, and continuing
multi-billion-dollar annual capital expenditures. Transactions of that
size and suitability are rare.

Scale also changes what counts as material without making every large
investment immaterial. The initial IBM position was $10.856 billion and
15.3 percent of Berkshire's year-end 2011 quoted common-stock portfolio,
so describing it as only a modest portfolio position understates its
weight. Yet even a favorable outcome on $10 billion would affect a
company with a far larger equity base less than the same outcome would
have affected early Berkshire. The mathematical claim concerns
percentage impact on an expanding base, not the analytical importance of
an individual investment.

The practical response was diversification of deployment channels:
regulated infrastructure that can reinvest internally, very large full
acquisitions, public equities, negotiated securities, partner-led deals,
and many subsidiary bolt-ons. An investor should therefore test a large
compounder on the return and durability of incremental deployments, the
amount that can be deployed, and the financing cost. Assuming either that
historic percentage growth must continue or that scale makes every large
deal irrelevant would contradict the evidence in these reports.

### For Evaluating Acquisition Currency and Dilution

BNSF and Kraft Heinz should be analyzed with related but distinct
questions. In BNSF, Berkshire issued its own stock. A continuing
Berkshire owner surrendered part of the percentage claim on all existing
Berkshire assets in exchange for the railroad. The test is whether the
value and earning power received per new Berkshire share exceeded the
intrinsic value transferred. Cash availability alone does not answer the
question, because issuing undervalued shares can make even a good
acquisition costly.

In Kraft Heinz, Berkshire did not issue acquisition currency. Kraft Heinz
issued investee shares to Kraft owners and received the Kraft business,
while Berkshire's interest moved from 52.5 percent of Heinz to 26.8
percent of the larger combined entity. The correct test compares
Berkshire's share of the combined business with its former share of Heinz
and the additional $5.26 billion invested. The non-cash accounting gain
measured a revaluation under the equity method, not that economic result.

IBM supplies a third contrast: an open-market investment financed with
cash. A mistaken cash-funded investment can destroy value, but it does
not alter the ownership percentage of every Berkshire shareholder. These
cases produce a general sequence for analysis: identify who issued the
shares, value the interest surrendered, value the assets and earning
power received, include debt and cash funding, and calculate the result
per parent share. Treating all percentage ownership reductions as parent
share dilution obscures the economically relevant exchange.

## Common Pitfalls

Several analytical errors recur in this material. First, do not equate
all ownership dilution. BNSF increased Berkshire's own share count;
Kraft Heinz increased the investee's share count. Both require a
per-share analysis, but the exchanged claims differ. Second, do not turn
a changed thesis into a fabricated return. IBM was fully sold after
Buffett revised his view, but an exact gain or loss requires complete
cash flows, not one closing price.

Third, do not call every negotiated security a preferred-plus-warrant
package. Wrigley had notes and preferred stock without warrants, and Bank
of America occurred in 2011 rather than during the September 2008 panic.
Fourth, do not treat a subsidiary dividend as independent value creation:
a dividend is a transfer within Berkshire and must be considered with
subsidiary debt, reinvestment, and earning power. Fifth, do not treat cash
as either wholly idle or wholly available. Consolidated cash serves
insurance and operating obligations as well as investment optionality.

Finally, do not extrapolate Berkshire's early percentage returns onto a
much larger capital base. Scale does not prove that every large deal is
low-return, as BNSF's estimated 40 percent initial earning-power increase
shows. It does reduce the number of suitable opportunities and the
percentage impact of a given dollar gain. The correct focus is durable
incremental earning power per Berkshire share after financing costs.
