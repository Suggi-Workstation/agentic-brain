# Value vs Growth: The False Dichotomy -- Why the Style Split Is an Artifact of Labels, Not Philosophy

The division between "value" and "growth" investing is a classification
invention of index providers and factor researchers, not a genuine
philosophical split. Warren Buffett stated it plainly in his 1992
Berkshire Hathaway chairman's letter: the two approaches are "joined at
the hip," and growth is always a component in the calculation of value.
The dichotomy arose because the investment industry needed labels to
sort stocks into mutual fund categories and quantitative factor
portfolios, not because the underlying mathematics of intrinsic value
change depending on a company's growth rate. Understanding why the
split is false is essential to avoiding the traps it creates: value
traps on one side, growth traps on the other, both rooted in confusing
a price multiple with a valuation methodology.

## Background

The intellectual history of the value-growth dichotomy begins with
Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, whose 1934 work Security Analysis
established the framework of buying securities at prices below their
intrinsic value. Graham's approach was deliberately quantitative and
statistical: he sought companies trading below their net current asset
value, what he called "net-nets," and his method was designed to work
without making qualitative judgments about a company's future growth
prospects. The margin of safety came from the price discount itself,
not from confidence in the business's durability. This approach, which
Buffett later termed "cigar-butt investing," involved finding a
discarded cigar on the ground with one free puff left -- cheap enough
that the residual value justified the purchase regardless of the
business's long-term prospects (Source 1, Source 7).

Graham's framework dominated investment thinking for decades, but it
contained an implicit assumption that would later drive the value-growth
split: if value investing meant buying cheap statistical bargains, then
companies with high growth rates and high valuation multiples were, by
definition, not value investments. The logic was circular. Growth
companies traded at high price-to-earnings ratios and low dividend
yields, so they looked expensive by Graham's metrics. Value companies
traded at low multiples and high yields, so they looked cheap. The
labels were derived from the price characteristics, not from any
assessment of whether the price reflected the intrinsic value of the
business.

The formal academic codification of the split came in 1992 and 1993,
when Eugene Fama and Kenneth French published their seminal papers on
stock returns. In "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns" (1992)
and "Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds" (1993),
Fama and French identified three factors that explained the cross-section
of average stock returns: market risk, firm size, and book-to-market
equity. The book-to-market factor (HML, "high minus low") became the
academic definition of the value premium: stocks with high
book-to-market ratios (value stocks) earned higher average returns than
stocks with low book-to-market ratios (growth stocks), after controlling
for market risk and size (Source 2, Source 3).

The Fama-French model was a breakthrough for asset pricing theory, but
it had an unintended consequence for investment practice. By defining
"value" as high book-to-market and "growth" as low book-to-market, the
academic literature gave the style split a quantitative rigor it had
previously lacked. Index providers adopted the framework: Russell and
S&P created value indices and growth indices, mutual fund companies
launched value funds and growth funds, and the investment industry
built an entire product architecture around the dichotomy. The
style-box grid -- large-cap value, large-cap growth, mid-cap value,
mid-cap growth -- became the organizing principle of fund selection
and portfolio construction.

Buffett's objection, articulated most clearly in the 1992 Berkshire
letter, was that this entire framework rested on a category error. The
Fama-French factor model measured the returns of portfolios sorted by
accounting ratios. It did not measure whether investors who bought
companies below their intrinsic value earned different returns from
investors who bought companies above their intrinsic value. A company
with a high price-to-book ratio could be undervalued if its future cash
flows justified the price. A company with a low price-to-book ratio
could be overvalued if its business was deteriorating. The accounting
ratio was a proxy for valuation, and like all proxies, it was
imperfect.

The tension between the academic definition of value (a accounting
ratio) and the philosophical definition (buying below intrinsic value)
is the root of the false dichotomy. Graham and Buffett defined value
investing as a methodology: estimate intrinsic value, buy at a
discount, require a margin of safety. Fama-French defined value as a
characteristic: high book-to-market. These are not the same thing, and
conflating them produces both the value trap (buying a statistically
cheap company that is cheap for good reason) and the growth trap
(buying a statistically expensive company whose growth does not justify
the price).

Meanwhile, Buffett's own evolution told the story. Under the influence
of Charlie Munger, beginning in the late 1950s and crystallizing by the
late 1970s, Buffett moved from Graham's cigar-butt approach to buying
high-quality businesses with durable competitive advantages at fair
prices. The 1989 Berkshire letter captured the shift: "It is far better
to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a
wonderful price." This was not a move from value to growth. It was a
recognition that growth, when deployed at high rates of return on
incremental capital, is a component of value, not an alternative to it
(Source 7, Source 8).

Peter Lynch, managing the Fidelity Magellan Fund from 1977 to 1990,
provided a parallel data point. Lynch popularized the GARP approach --
Growth at a Reasonable Price -- which used the PEG ratio
(price-to-earnings divided by earnings growth rate) to identify
companies whose growth rates justified their valuation multiples. Lynch
described his approach in his 1989 book One Up on Wall Street, arguing
that slow-growing companies, stalwarts, fast growers, cyclical, turnarounds,
and asset plays each required different analytical frameworks, but the
common thread was paying a price that made sense relative to the
business's earnings power and growth trajectory (Source 6).

The GMO research firm, in its analysis of value traps versus growth
traps published in 2021, provided the empirical capstone. Over 25 years
of data, value traps -- statistically cheap companies that
underperformed -- lagged their benchmark by 9.5 percent annualized.
Growth traps -- statistically expensive companies that underperformed --
lagged their benchmark by 13.0 percent annualized. The asymmetry is
instructive: both traps exist, but the growth trap is more painful
because high expectations leave no margin for error, whereas low
expectations in value traps at least provide some downside cushion
(Source 4).

The history reveals that the value-growth dichotomy was always more
about product categorization than investment philosophy. The
practitioners who are most often cited as exemplars of each style --
Buffett for value, Lynch for growth -- both operated from the same
first principle: buy businesses at prices below their intrinsic value,
where intrinsic value incorporates growth as a variable. The split is
an artifact of how the industry chose to label stocks, not of how
successful investors actually think about valuation.

## Core Concepts

### Growth Is a Variable in the Value Equation

The foundational concept is that growth is not an alternative to value
-- it is an input to value. Intrinsic value, whether estimated by
discounted cash flow, earnings power value, or any other methodology,
is a function of the cash flows a business will generate over its
remaining life and the rate at which those cash flows are discounted
back to the present. Growth enters this equation directly: higher
growth means higher future cash flows, which means higher intrinsic
value. Buffett's 1992 letter made this explicit: "Growth is always a
component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose
importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can
be negative as well as positive" (Source 1).

The critical nuance is that growth's impact on value can be negative.
A company that grows revenues while destroying shareholder value --
deploying capital at returns below its cost of capital -- is worth less
as it grows, not more. Buffett's example was the airline industry:
investors poured capital into airlines for decades, the industry grew
enormously, and the growth was "profitless or worse" for shareholders.
Growth funded at low returns on incremental capital reduces intrinsic
value, which is why a high growth rate alone says nothing about whether
a stock is undervalued or overvalued (Source 1, Source 7).

This concept dissolves the dichotomy at its root. If growth is a
variable in the value equation, then there is no separate "growth
investing" that stands apart from "value investing." There is only
investing: estimating intrinsic value, accounting for growth as one
input among several, and buying at a discount to that estimate. The
label "growth investing" describes companies whose growth variable is
large and positive. The label "value investing" describes companies
whose price-to-intrinsic-value ratio is low. These labels measure
different things and can apply to the same company simultaneously.

### Cheapness Is Not Value

A second core concept is the distinction between cheapness and value.
Cheapness is a price characteristic: a low price-to-earnings ratio, a
low price-to-book ratio, a high dividend yield. Value is a valuation
assessment: a market price below the intrinsic value of the business.
Cheapness can be measured mechanically from financial statements. Value
requires estimating intrinsic value, which requires judgment about
future cash flows, growth rates, and discount rates.

The confusion of cheapness with value is the source of the value trap.
A company trading at a price-to-earnings ratio of 5 looks cheap by any
statistical measure. But if earnings are about to collapse -- because
the business model is obsolete, the competitive advantage has eroded,
or the industry is in structural decline -- the stock is not
undervalued. It is overvalued, because the current earnings are not
sustainable and the low multiple reflects the market's expectation of
decline. Graham's net-net approach worked because the margin of safety
came from the balance sheet, not from earnings sustainability. But when
investors apply Graham's statistical screens to companies without
balance-sheet protection, they conflate cheapness with value and fall
into the trap (Source 8, Source 5).

Conversely, a company trading at a price-to-earnings ratio of 30 looks
expensive by statistical measures. But if the company is compounding
capital at 25 percent returns on equity and has a durable competitive
advantage, the current earnings may understate the business's long-term
cash-generating capacity. In this case, the high multiple may reflect
growth that is real and value-accretive, and the stock may be
undervalued despite appearing expensive. Buffett's purchase of
Coca-Cola in 1988 is the canonical example: he paid what seemed like a
high multiple for a business with extraordinary growth economics, and
the investment compounded at rates that made the entry price look cheap
in retrospect (Source 7, Source 9).

### The Value Factor Is a Factor, Not a Philosophy

The Fama-French value factor (HML) measures the return spread between
portfolios of high book-to-market stocks and low book-to-market stocks.
It is an empirical observation about average returns, not a
prescription for how to invest. The value premium -- the tendency for
high book-to-market stocks to outperform low book-to-market stocks over
long periods -- is one of the most documented phenomena in finance, but
its interpretation is contested.

Fama and French themselves noted that the value premium could reflect
either risk or mispricing. Under the risk interpretation, value stocks
are riskier (more financially distressed, more exposed to economic
downturns) and the premium is compensation for bearing that risk. Under
the mispricing interpretation, investors systematically overpay for
glamorous growth stocks and underpay for boring value stocks, creating
an exploitable anomaly. The distinction matters because if the premium
is risk compensation, it is not an arbitrage opportunity -- it is a
fair return for bearing risk (Source 2, Source 3).

Buffett's critique of the factor approach is that it measures the wrong
thing. The Fama-French model sorts stocks by an accounting ratio and
measures the returns of the resulting portfolios. It does not measure
whether investors who carefully estimate intrinsic value and buy at a
discount outperform investors who do not. The value factor is a
statistical average across thousands of stocks, many of which are cheap
for good reasons (value traps). The value investing philosophy, as
practiced by Buffett and Munger, involves deep analysis of individual
businesses to distinguish undervalued companies from value traps. The
factor captures the average; the philosophy seeks the outliers (Source 1,
Source 5).

### Quality Bridges Value and Growth

The concept of quality -- durable competitive advantages, high returns
on invested capital, and reinvestment capacity -- is what bridges the
value and growth labels. A quality company with a strong economic moat
can grow at high rates for long periods because its competitive
advantage protects it from the forces that would otherwise erode its
returns. When such a company trades at a reasonable price, it satisfies
both the value criterion (price below intrinsic value) and the growth
criterion (high growth rate). The style-box framework cannot classify
it because it sits in both boxes simultaneously.

Munger's influence on Buffett was precisely this insight: that quality
is a form of value. A wonderful company at a fair price can compound
capital for decades, generating intrinsic value growth that makes the
initial entry price irrelevant in the long run. The See's Candies
acquisition in 1972 was the turning point. Berkshire paid what seemed
like a premium price -- 25 million dollars for a business with 4 million
in pre-tax earnings, a price-to-earnings ratio of about 12.5 -- but
See's generated over 2 billion in pre-tax earnings over the following
decades while requiring minimal reinvestment. The growth was
value-accretive because it required little incremental capital, and the
quality of the brand allowed pricing power that sustained margins
indefinitely (Source 7, Source 8).

The quality bridge also explains why the style-box framework can be
actively misleading. A quality compounder that has grown earnings at 20
percent annually for a decade may trade at a price-to-earnings ratio of
25, placing it firmly in the "growth" box. But if the business can
sustain that growth for another decade, the current price may represent
a deep discount to intrinsic value. An investor who refuses to buy
because the multiple is "too high" is applying a cheapness screen, not
a value assessment, and will miss the compounding that generates the
real return.

### GARP: Growth at a Reasonable Price

Peter Lynch's GARP framework provides a practical operationalization of
the value-growth synthesis. The PEG ratio -- price-to-earnings divided
by earnings growth rate -- normalizes the valuation multiple by the
growth rate, allowing comparison across companies with different growth
profiles. A company with a price-to-earnings ratio of 20 and earnings
growth of 20 percent has a PEG of 1.0. A company with a
price-to-earnings ratio of 10 and earnings growth of 5 percent has a
PEG of 2.0. By the GARP framework, the first company is cheaper despite
the higher multiple, because the growth rate justifies the price.

Lynch outlined his approach in One Up on Wall Street (1989),
categorizing companies into six types -- slow growers, stalwarts, fast
growers, cyclicals, turnarounds, and asset plays -- each with different
valuation logic. The common thread was that the price paid should make
sense relative to the business's earnings trajectory and growth
prospects. Lynch did not consider himself a "growth investor" or a
"value investor." He was an investor who looked for mispriced growth,
wherever it appeared in the style spectrum (Source 6).

The PEG ratio is an imperfect tool -- it relies on a single year's
growth rate, ignores the durability of growth, and says nothing about
the return on capital that funds the growth. But its conceptual
contribution is significant: it demonstrates that valuation and growth
are not opposing forces but inputs to the same calculation. A high
multiple is not inherently expensive if the growth rate is high enough.
A low multiple is not inherently cheap if the growth rate is low or
negative. The ratio forces the investor to consider both variables
simultaneously, which is what the intrinsic value calculation does in
full.

### The Evolution from Cigar Butts to Compounders

Buffett's intellectual journey from Graham's cigar-butt approach to
Munger's quality-compounding approach is itself a core concept, because
it illustrates how the value-growth distinction dissolves when the
investor focuses on intrinsic value rather than price multiples. The
cigar-butt phase (roughly 1950-1969) was pure Graham: buy companies
trading below their working capital liquidation value, extract the
residual value, and move on. The approach was statistical, did not
require growth assumptions, and generated high returns on small capital
amounts (Source 7).

The transition began in the late 1950s when Buffett met Munger.
Munger's argument was subtle: Graham's approach worked, but it was not
optimal. If you could identify a business with durable competitive
advantages, strong management, and excellent growth prospects, paying a
reasonable price for such a business would outperform buying a mediocre
business at a deep discount. The reasoning was mathematical: a cigar
butt bought at 50 cents on the dollar and sold at par generates a 100
percent return, but the capital must be redeployed, and taxes erode the
gain. A quality business bought at fair value and held for decades
compounds at 20 percent annually without selling, and the tax deferral
amplifies the after-tax return. Over 20 years, the compounding path
generates roughly 20 times more wealth than the cigar-butt path, even
with similar pre-tax returns (Source 7, Source 8).

The See's Candies acquisition in 1972 was the proof of concept. Buffett
later said that See's taught him the value of a durable franchise. The
business required minimal reinvestment to grow, generated enormous free
cash flow, and used its brand power to raise prices annually. The
growth was organic and capital-light, meaning each dollar of growth
produced nearly a dollar of distributable cash. This is growth that is
unambiguously value-accretive, and it cannot be classified as either
"value" or "growth" in the style-box framework. It is simply a good
business bought at a price that underestimated its long-term earning
power.

### The Index Label Problem

The final core concept is the mechanical problem with index-based
style classification. Russell and S&P sort stocks into value and growth
indices using composite scores based on price-to-book, price-to-earnings,
price-to-sales, dividend yield, and growth metrics. A stock that scores
high on value characteristics goes into the value index. A stock that
scores high on growth characteristics goes into the growth index. Some
indices split stocks between the two with a probability weighting.

The problem is that this sorting is backward-looking. It uses trailing
financial data to classify a company's "style," but intrinsic value
depends on forward-looking cash flows. A company that has been a growth
stock for a decade may be approaching maturity, with growth slowing and
the multiple contracting. At the moment the index reclassifies it as a
value stock, its growth may be ending, and the low multiple may reflect
the market's correct assessment that the best years are behind it.
Conversely, a company that has been a value stock may be on the cusp of
a growth reacceleration driven by a new product or market, and the low
multiple may represent a buying opportunity that the index label
obscures.

The index labels also create herding behavior. Fund managers benchmarked
against a value index must hold value stocks, defined by the index
methodology. They cannot buy a high-multiple growth stock even if their
analysis shows it is undervalued, because doing so would create tracking
error against their benchmark. This constraint embeds the false
dichotomy into the structure of the asset management industry, making
it self-reinforcing regardless of what individual investors believe
about the relationship between value and growth (Source 5, Source 9).

## Evidence

### The Fama-French Value Premium

The most cited evidence for the existence of a distinct value factor is
the Fama-French three-factor model, published in 1993. Fama and French
constructed mimicking portfolios for size (SMB, "small minus big") and
book-to-market (HML, "high minus low") factors using NYSE, Amex, and
NASDAQ stocks from 1963 to 1990. They found that the three-factor model
-- market risk, size, and book-to-market -- explained the cross-section
of average stock returns far better than the single-factor CAPM, with
intercepts from the three-factor regressions close to zero across 25
stock portfolios sorted by size and book-to-market (Source 2).

The value premium documented by Fama and French was substantial: high
book-to-market stocks earned average returns roughly 4-5 percentage
points higher than low book-to-market stocks annually over the sample
period. But the interpretation remained contested. In their 2006 paper
"The Value Premium and the CAPM," Fama and French showed that the CAPM
could not explain value premiums and that variation in market beta
unrelated to size and the value-growth characteristic went unrewarded
throughout 1926 to 2004. The evidence was consistent with the value
premium being a risk factor, a mispricing phenomenon, or both -- the
data could not distinguish between these explanations (Source 3).

The critical point for the false-dichotomy argument is what the
Fama-French evidence does not show. It shows that portfolios sorted by
book-to-market ratios have different average returns. It does not show
that investors who estimate intrinsic value and buy at a discount earn
different returns from investors who buy growth. The value factor
measures the return to a mechanical sorting rule, not the return to a
valuation methodology. Buffett's argument is not that the value premium
does not exist -- it is that the factor and the philosophy measure
different things, and conflating them leads to errors (Source 1).

### The GMO Trap Analysis

GMO's 2021 research on value traps versus growth traps provided direct
empirical evidence for the cost of confusing cheapness with value and
expensiveness with growth. GMO tracked baskets of value trap and growth
trap stocks over 25 years, comparing their returns to relevant MSCI US
style universes. Value traps -- stocks that appeared cheap by
book-to-market and other value metrics but subsequently underperformed --
lagged their benchmark by 9.5 percent annualized. Growth traps -- stocks
that appeared to have strong growth characteristics but subsequently
underperformed -- lagged their benchmark by 13.0 percent annualized
(Source 4).

The asymmetry between the two trap types is informative for the
false-dichotomy argument. Both traps are real, but they have different
mechanisms. The value trap occurs when a stock is cheap because the
market correctly anticipates deteriorating fundamentals. The investor
who buys based on the low multiple is confusing cheapness with value.
The growth trap occurs when a stock is expensive because the market
overprices growth expectations, and the company fails to deliver. The
investor who buys based on the high growth rate is confusing growth
with value. In both cases, the error is the same: using a
classification label (value or growth) as a substitute for estimating
intrinsic value.

GMO's finding that growth traps underperform more severely than value
traps (13.0 percent versus 9.5 percent annualized) reflects the
asymmetry of expectations. When a value stock disappoints, expectations
were already low, so the downside is limited. When a growth stock
disappoints, expectations were high, and the repricing is severe. This
is consistent with Buffett's warning that growth can have negative
impact on value: growth that fails to materialize, or that materializes
at substandard returns on capital, destroys value, and the destruction
is larger when the market had priced in high growth (Source 1, Source 4).

### Buffett's Track Record and the Style Classification Problem

Buffett's own investment record provides the most direct evidence that
the value-growth dichotomy is false in practice. Over the period
1965-2023, Berkshire Hathaway's compounded annual return was
approximately 19.8 percent, far exceeding the S&P 500's approximately
10.2 percent. Berkshire's portfolio has included companies that would
be classified as value stocks by any metric (American Express, GEICO,
Wells Fargo in its early Berkshire years), companies that would be
classified as growth stocks (Apple, Amazon), and companies that sit in
neither box comfortably (See's Candies, BNSF Railway, MidAmerican
Energy).

The Apple position is the most instructive case. Berkshire began buying
Apple in 2016, when the stock traded at a price-to-earnings ratio of
approximately 10-12, a level that would place it in the value box by
most definitions. By the time Berkshire's position reached its peak
value, Apple had been reclassified by many style indices as a growth
stock, trading at a much higher multiple. Buffett did not change his
analysis. He continued to hold because the intrinsic value of the
business, driven by its ecosystem lock-in, services revenue growth, and
capital return program, continued to exceed the market price. The style
label changed; the valuation logic did not (Source 1, Source 5, Source 9).

The broader evidence from long-term factor returns also supports the
synthesis view. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 2022 analysis showed
that prolonged periods of growth dominance are usually followed by long
periods of value outperformance, using Russell 1000 data from 1927 to
2022. The cyclicality of the value-growth return spread is consistent
with the hypothesis that both styles mean-revert because both are
subject to the same fundamental driver: the relationship between price
and intrinsic value. When growth stocks become sufficiently overpriced
relative to their intrinsic value, value stocks outperform. When value
stocks become sufficiently underpriced, they outperform. The driver is
valuation, not style (Source 5).

### The See's Candies Case Study

The See's Candies acquisition in 1972 is the single most cited example
of the value-growth synthesis in practice. Berkshire paid 25 million
dollars for See's, which had approximately 4 million in pre-tax earnings
and approximately 8 million in net tangible assets. The price-to-earnings
multiple was approximately 12.5, and the return on tangible capital was
approximately 50 percent. By Graham's standards, the price was not
cheap -- it was above the net-net threshold and required paying for
intangible brand value (Source 7, Source 8).

Over the next four decades, See's generated over 2 billion in pre-tax
earnings while requiring only approximately 32 million in incremental
capital reinvestment. The business grew earnings at roughly 8-10 percent
annually, driven primarily by pricing power on its brand rather than
unit volume expansion. The economics were extraordinary: nearly every
dollar of earnings was distributable free cash flow that Berkshire could
redeploy into other investments. The compound return on the original 25
million dollar investment was astronomical.

The See's case demonstrates the core thesis. The business had growth
(earnings compounding at 8-10 percent), it had value (bought at a
reasonable price with extraordinary returns on capital), and it had
quality (a durable brand moat that enabled sustained pricing power).
No style-box classification captures this. It was not a value stock by
Graham's statistical criteria, and it was not a growth stock by the
high-multiple definition. It was a good business bought below its
intrinsic value, where the intrinsic value was driven by the interaction
of growth, returns on capital, and competitive durability. The lesson
Buffett drew from See's was that the quality of the business matters
more than the statistical cheapness of the price, and that growth, when
deployed at high returns on capital with minimal reinvestment needs, is
the most powerful driver of intrinsic value (Source 7, Source 8).

## Implications

### For Individual Investors

The false dichotomy has direct practical consequences for individual
investors. The most common error is rejecting a quality company because
its valuation multiple appears high, or buying a deteriorating company
because its multiple appears low. Both errors stem from using the style
label as a proxy for valuation analysis. The antidote is to return to
first principles: estimate the intrinsic value of the business, account
for growth as a variable in that estimation, and buy only when the
market price offers a margin of safety relative to the estimate.

For individual investors, the implication is that screening tools based
on style metrics (price-to-book, price-to-earnings, dividend yield) are
starting points, not conclusions. A low price-to-earnings ratio is a
flag that warrants investigation, not a signal to buy. The investigation
must answer whether the low multiple reflects a temporary dislocation
(the market is wrong) or a permanent deterioration (the market is
right). Similarly, a high price-to-earnings ratio is a flag that the
market expects significant growth, and the investigation must assess
whether those growth expectations are justified by the business's
competitive position, reinvestment economics, and addressable market
(Source 6, Source 8).

The GARP framework provides a practical bridge for individual investors
who are not prepared to build full discounted cash flow models. The PEG
ratio normalizes the valuation multiple by the growth rate, providing a
quick screen for whether a high-multiple stock is reasonably priced
given its growth. The limitations of PEG -- it uses a single growth
estimate, ignores capital intensity, and says nothing about competitive
durability -- mean it should be supplemented with qualitative
assessment of the business's moat, management quality, and reinvestment
opportunities. But the conceptual contribution remains: it forces the
investor to consider valuation and growth simultaneously rather than
treating them as alternatives (Source 6).

### For Institutional Investors and Fund Managers

For institutional investors, the false dichotomy is structurally
embedded in the asset management industry. Fund mandates, benchmark
construction, and performance attribution all rely on the style-box
framework. A large-cap value fund manager who buys a high-multiple
growth stock faces career risk: if the position underperforms, the
decision is indefensible against the benchmark, even if the analysis was
correct. This structural constraint means that the industry's
classification system can prevent the very analysis that the value
investing philosophy calls for.

The implication for institutional investors is that benchmark-relative
constraints can be value-destroying. An unconstrained investor who
follows intrinsic value wherever it leads -- whether the resulting
portfolio looks like a value portfolio, a growth portfolio, or a mix --
will outperform a constrained investor who must stay within a style box,
provided the unconstrained investor's analysis is sound. This is the
argument for concentrated, benchmark-agnostic investing as practiced by
Buffett and Munger: the benchmark is a constraint that forces style
adherence at the cost of valuation discipline (Source 1, Source 5).

The evidence from GMO's trap analysis reinforces this implication.
Value traps and growth traps both occur when investors apply style
labels as decision rules. The institutional framework that sorts
managers into value and growth buckets, benchmarks them against style
indices, and evaluates them on tracking error is the same framework
that produces the traps. An institutional structure that evaluated
managers on absolute return and valuation discipline rather than style
adherence would reduce the trap frequency (Source 4).

### For Portfolio Construction

The false dichotomy also has implications for how portfolios are
constructed. The conventional approach allocates capital across style
boxes: a portion to large-cap value, a portion to large-cap growth, and
so on, with the allocation based on expected style returns and
correlations. If the dichotomy is false -- if value and growth are both
proxies for the same underlying variable (price relative to intrinsic
value) -- then style-based diversification provides less protection
than it appears to.

The alternative is valuation-based portfolio construction: allocate
capital to the securities that offer the largest discount to intrinsic
value, regardless of their style classification. This approach
naturally diversifies across sectors, market caps, and growth profiles,
because undervalued businesses can be found anywhere in the market. It
also avoids the style rotation problem -- the need to predict whether
value or growth will outperform in a given period -- because the
portfolio is always positioned toward undervalued securities, however
the market chooses to label them (Source 1, Source 5, Source 9).

### For Factor Investing and Quantitative Strategies

For factor investors, the false dichotomy raises the question of
whether the value factor, as defined by Fama-French, captures the same
phenomenon that value investors exploit. The evidence suggests it does
not entirely. The value factor captures the return to buying high
book-to-market stocks in aggregate, including the value traps. The
value philosophy captures the return to buying individual businesses
below their intrinsic value after careful analysis, excluding the traps.
The factor's return is diluted by the traps that the philosophy would
filter out (Source 2, Source 4).

This has driven the development of "quality value" factor strategies
that combine the value factor with quality screens (profitability,
return on equity, low debt) to reduce the trap frequency. Fama and
French's own five-factor model, published in 2015, added profitability
and investment factors, acknowledging that book-to-market alone did not
fully capture the drivers of average returns. The evolution of factor
models toward incorporating quality is consistent with Buffett's
argument: value without quality assessment is an incomplete framework,
and the most productive approach integrates growth, profitability, and
competitive durability into a single valuation (Source 2, Source 3).

The implication for quantitative strategists is that the value factor
should not be used in isolation. A multi-factor approach that combines
value with quality, momentum, and profitability signals better reflects
the philosophy of successful value investors than a single-factor value
screen. The false dichotomy, at the factor level, manifests as the
tendency to treat value and growth as opposite ends of a single
spectrum, when in fact they are partially independent dimensions that
interact through the intrinsic value calculation (Source 2, Source 9).

## Common Pitfalls

### Confusing the Factor with the Philosophy

The most common pitfall is assuming that because the Fama-French value
factor exists and generates a premium, value investing as a philosophy
is validated by the factor evidence. The factor and the philosophy
measure different things. The factor measures the return to a
mechanical sorting rule. The philosophy describes a method of analysis
and decision-making. Validating the factor does not validate the
philosophy, and the philosophy can succeed even in periods when the
factor fails, because the philosophy excludes the traps that the factor
includes.

### Using PEG as a Complete Valuation

The PEG ratio is a useful normalization tool, but it is not a valuation.
It uses a single year's growth rate, ignores the durability of growth,
says nothing about the return on invested capital that funds the growth,
and assumes that the earnings base is sustainable. An investor who uses
PEG as the primary valuation metric will overpay for cyclical companies
at the peak of their cycle (when trailing growth is high but
unsustainable) and underpay for companies with accelerating but
currently low growth. PEG should be a screening input, not a
decision-making output.

### Ignoring Capital Intensity

Growth that requires large amounts of capital reinvestment is less
valuable than growth that requires little. A company growing earnings
at 20 percent but consuming all its free cash flow to fund that growth
is worth less than a company growing at 10 percent while distributing
half its earnings as free cash flow. The style-box framework captures
the growth rate but not the capital intensity, which is why two
companies with identical growth rates and identical multiples can have
vastly different intrinsic values. See's Candies grew at 8-10 percent
with minimal reinvestment; an airline might grow at 8-10 percent while
consuming billions in capital. The growth rate is the same; the value
is radically different (Source 7, Source 8).
