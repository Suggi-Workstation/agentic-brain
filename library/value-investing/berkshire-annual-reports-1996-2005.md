# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 1996-2005 -- Scale Raised the Cost of Acquisition Errors

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 1996 through 2005 document a
decade in which the company moved toward whole-business acquisitions
at much greater scale. It acquired the remaining 49 percent of GEICO,
bought General Re in an approximately $22 billion stock transaction,
and increased year-end insurance float from $7.4 billion in 1997 to
$22.8 billion in 1998. The author's synthesis is that the ten reports
show how scale narrowed Berkshire's opportunity set, how acquisition
errors tested its capital-allocation discipline, and how the letters
recorded both those errors and the subsequent corrections.

## Background

The decade 1996-2005 is a natural unit of analysis because it
brackets the transition from an insurance holding company with large
minority equity stakes toward a more diversified operating
conglomerate. This topic uses three events to frame the period. First,
on January 2,
1996, Berkshire paid approximately $2.3 billion for the remaining 49
percent of GEICO, completing ownership of an insurer in which
Berkshire had accumulated shares before 1981 after GEICO's 1976
crisis. Second, Berkshire announced the General Re merger on June 19,
1998 and closed it on December 21, 1998; the all-stock transaction was
valued at approximately $22 billion. Third, Berkshire's 0.5 percent
per-share book-value gain in 1999 trailed the S&P 500 by 20.5
percentage points while Buffett
and Munger declined to value fast-changing technology businesses
outside their stated circle of competence.

Buffett used the annual shareholder letter to teach capital-allocation
logic and explain mistakes alongside required corporate reporting. The
1998 letter updated a two-column intrinsic-value guide that, it said,
had appeared in the prior three annual reports: per-share investments
in one column and per-share pre-tax operating earnings in the other.
It also warned that most of 1998's 48.3 percent per-share book-value
gain came from issuing shares in acquisitions and did not imply an
equivalent gain in intrinsic value. The 1999 letter extended this
candor to performance: Buffett called his 1999 grade a "D" and named
himself the guilty party, writing that his "one subject" was capital
allocation. The author's assessment is that this combination of
measurement and explicit error attribution made the letters useful as
capital-allocation records rather than promotional summaries.

The decade opened with Berkshire already a diversified holding company
with major insurance operations, including National Indemnity,
Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group, and GEICO, which became wholly
owned on January 2, 1996. It also held large equity stakes
in American Express, Coca-Cola, the Washington Post, Gillette, and
Capital Cities/ABC, and operating subsidiaries including See's Candies,
the Buffalo News, Nebraska Furniture Mart, and the Pampered Chef (added
in 2002). By the end of 2005, the company had acquired General Re,
FlightSafety International, Executive Jet (later NetJets), Dairy
Queen, and a cluster of building-products and home-construction
businesses (Shaw Industries, Johns Manville, MiTek, Acme Brick,
Benjamin Moore, Clayton Homes). The equity portfolio included large
positions in financial companies such as American Express, Wells
Fargo, Moody's, and H&R Block. The author's synthesis is that this was
not merely a change in holdings: operating subsidiaries became a
larger part of the enterprise's economics as Berkshire's capacity for
marketable-security investments grew harder to deploy at scale.

The author's assessment is that the intellectual context is Buffett's
established preference for durable franchises and owner-oriented
capital allocation. The
1996-2005 reports test that philosophy at a larger scale. General Re,
in particular, asked whether the discipline
used for minority equity stakes could survive ownership of a global
reinsurer whose underwriting culture had deteriorated and whose
derivatives operation proved difficult to exit. When Berkshire began
liquidating General Re Securities in early 2002, that operation had
23,218 outstanding contracts with 884 counterparties. The author's
assessment is that the eventual correction did not validate the
original purchase price; it showed that a useful strategic asset can
still be acquired on terms that destroy per-share value.

The author's assessment is that the 2002 letter widened company
reporting into institutional critique. Its warning that derivatives
were "financial weapons of mass
destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially
lethal" arose directly from Berkshire's experience with General Re
Securities and then extended to accounting incentives, collateral
spirals, counterparty chains, and systemic risk. Berkshire had begun
terminating the operation in early 2002; after ten months, 14,384
contracts with 672 counterparties remained. The letter's statement
that reinsurance and derivatives were "easy to enter and almost
impossible to exit" was therefore an operating observation as well as
a policy argument.

## Core Concepts

### The Class B Share Issuance and the Problem of Scale

In May 1996, Berkshire issued 517,500 Class B shares, each with an
economic interest equal to one-thirtieth of a Class A share. The sale
generated net proceeds of $565 million, but the 1996 letter said its
purpose was to arrest proposed unit trusts that would market
Berkshire-like interests to small investors while charging high fees.
Berkshire instead supplied a direct, low-cost entry point and called
its prior record "definitely nonrepeatable." In 1998, Buffett made the
scale constraint quantitative: a 15 percent average increase in
intrinsic value was, in his words, "at the very peak of possible
outcomes," and achieving it for five years would require adding $58
billion to net worth. The warning linked the share issuance to a theme
that would dominate the decade: a larger capital base required much
larger ideas.

The scale problem is structural. As Berkshire grew, the universe of
investments large enough to matter shrank. Buffett wrote in 2003 that
the number of stocks purchasable in quantities large enough to move
Berkshire's performance had become a small fraction of the number
available a decade earlier. The 1998 letter provided an accounting
example: Berkshire's per-share book value gain of
48.3 percent that year looked impressive but most of it came from
issuing shares in acquisitions, not from operating performance. The
book-value increase was arithmetic rather than evidence of matching
intrinsic-value growth, and the distinction between
per-share book value growth from operations and per-share book value
growth from issuing stock above book value became a recurring analytical
thread. The author's synthesis is that the Class B issuance shows how
scale changes the set of attractive opportunities: big pools of
capital need big, reliable businesses to move the needle, and the
patience to wait for them becomes the dominant operational discipline.

### GEICO and the Logic of Full Ownership

Berkshire's purchase of the remaining 49 percent of GEICO in January
1996 for $2.3 billion completed a relationship that began with
Buffett's 1951 visit to GEICO vice president Lorimer Davidson. At the
merger date, Berkshire subsidiaries owned 34.25 million GEICO shares,
acquired before 1981 at an aggregate cost of $45.7 million. GEICO's
repurchases had raised that block from about 33 percent of the company
in 1980 to approximately 51 percent by year-end 1995. The cash paid
for the remaining interest was therefore about fifty times the
historical cost of Berkshire's existing block, although the ownership
percentages were not the same. Buffett accepted the price because
GEICO had become a low-cost direct-marketing insurer with a structural
cost advantage under Tony Nicely.

The GEICO acquisition illustrates the distinction between accounting
net worth and economic value when float is involved. Berkshire's 2010
letter retrospectively put GEICO's implied 1996 value at approximately
$4.6 billion and its tangible net worth at $1.9 billion, a multiple of
about 2.4 times tangible net worth. The 1996 annual report said the
acquisition added approximately $2.6 billion of float. A simple
analytical heuristic -- tangible net worth plus float -- therefore
produces about $4.5 billion, close to the implied purchase value; this
is not a GAAP valuation and does not credit future growth. The author's
interpretation is that low-cost float can have economic value not
captured by tangible net worth alone. From 1996 through 2005, GEICO
reported an
underwriting loss only in 2000; by 2005 its year-end float was $6.7
billion and its underwriting profit was $1.2 billion. The operating
record, rather than the book multiple alone, explains why Berkshire
accepted a price Buffett described as steep.

### General Re and the Cost of Stock Currency

The June 1998 General Re merger was an all-stock transaction valued
at approximately $22 billion.
General Re shareholders received 0.0035 Class A shares or 0.105
Class B shares per General Re share, at an implied price of
approximately $276.50 per General Re share. Buffett's stated rationale
was the acquisition of General Re's approximately $14.9 billion of
reinsurance float, its global distribution network across 124
countries, and its reputation for quality under Ron Ferguson's
leadership. The 1998 letter praised General Re as "first-class in
every way" and predicted that its earnings a decade hence would
materially exceed what it could have achieved as an independent
company.

The transaction's structure -- all stock -- is the central lesson. The
1998 letter explained that issuing shares in acquisitions produced
most of that year's 48.3 percent per-share book-value gain even though
the transactions created no immediate per-share intrinsic-value gain:
what Berkshire gave and received were then judged roughly equal. The
later record changed that judgment. In 2016, Buffett called issuing
272,200 Berkshire shares for General Re a "terrible mistake" that
increased shares outstanding by 21.8 percent and caused Berkshire
shareholders to give far more than they received. That retrospective
measurement makes the opportunity cost of stock currency explicit.

The concept is that acquisition currency matters. The relevant
comparison is the per-share economic value surrendered against the
value received, not the accounting gain created by issuing stock above
book value. General Re's underwriting deterioration, under-reserving,
and risk aggregation were problems Buffett said he failed to detect;
the 23,218 derivatives contracts were the outstanding inventory when
liquidation began in early 2002, not a disclosed acquisition-date
count. Buffett wrote in 2002 that he had been "dead wrong" about
General Re's underwriting culture. The author's synthesis is not that
all stock-for-stock deals are bad, but that using shares transfers a
permanent fraction of future value and therefore demands the same
margin-of-safety discipline as the target valuation.

### Insurance Float and the Cost of Capital

Insurance float is the core economic engine of Berkshire, and the
1996-2005 reports develop the concept with increasing rigor. Float is
the money an insurer holds between collecting premiums and paying
claims; if underwriting is profitable, float is negative-cost
capital -- Berkshire is paid to hold it. Year-end float grew from
$6.7 billion in 1996 to $49.3 billion in 2005, driven by the GEICO and
General Re acquisitions and by Ajit Jain's reinsurance operation at
National Indemnity. The 1996 letter stressed that float was a major
component of intrinsic value not shown in book value, but it also
defined the limiting condition: an insurance business becomes an
economic burden when float costs more than market-rate funding.

The cost of float is the underwriting result. If an insurer pays out
more in claims and expenses than it collects in premiums, the float
has a positive cost; if less, the float is costless or negative-cost.
The decade's worst float-cost year was 2001, when Berkshire's cost
reached 12.8 percent; the 2002 letter attributed about half of that
cost to World Trade Center losses. The 2002 letter also documented the
repair under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross: General Re recorded $1.31
billion of additional prior-year losses to correct under-reserving,
while Berkshire's total float cost fell to 1 percent. By 2005, despite
$3.4 billion in losses from Katrina, Rita, and Wilma, Berkshire's
aggregate underwriting result was a $53 million profit, making float
better than costless for the year.

The author's synthesis is that float is valuable only when underwriting
discipline is maintained. The General Re experience
showed what happens when discipline lapses: float remains available,
but its cost can exceed the return available on it. Buffett's 2002
statement that "size simply doesn't count" in underwriting meant that
volume was subordinate to properly priced business. General Re's
discipline had failed before and during 2001; it was restored by
shrinking or repricing business, correcting reserves, and changing
management accountability. GEICO and the other primary insurers then
offset 2005's catastrophe-exposed reinsurance losses, demonstrating
that low-cost float depended on operating discipline and portfolio
structure rather than float growth alone.

### The Dot-Com Refusal and Circle of Competence

The author's assessment is that the 1999 letter is the clearest single
document of Berkshire's refusal to chase the dot-com boom. Berkshire's
per-share book value
gain in 1999 was 0.5 percent, then the worst absolute and relative
performance of Buffett's tenure. The S&P 500 returned 21 percent
including dividends. Buffett named the cause directly: the inferior
performance of Berkshire's equity portfolio, for which he took full
responsibility. Several of Berkshire's major holdings (American
Express, Coca-Cola, Gillette, Disney) had disappointing operating
results. But the deeper reason was philosophical: Buffett and Munger
could not identify which technology companies had "a truly durable
competitive advantage," and they refused to invest in businesses they
could not value.

The 1999 letter stated the principle plainly: "Our lack of tech
insights, we should add, does not distress us. After all, there are
a great many business areas in which Charlie and I have no special
capital-allocation expertise." The strength, Buffett argued, was in
"recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of
competence and when we are approaching the perimeter." Predicting
the long-term economics of companies in fast-changing industries was
"simply far beyond" their perimeter. The Fortune magazine article in
late 1999, in which Buffett argued that the public's expectations
for equity returns were unrealistically high and that few people had
gotten rich from two transforming industries (automobiles and
aviation), extended this analysis to the general market level.

The subsequent record supplies a measured comparison. Berkshire's
per-share book value changed by 6.5 percent, negative 6.2 percent, and
10.0 percent in 2000, 2001, and 2002; the S&P 500 total return was
negative 9.1 percent, negative 11.9 percent, and negative 22.1 percent
in those years. In 2002 alone, Berkshire gained $6.1 billion in net
worth and outperformed the index by 32.1 percentage points, a result
Buffett explicitly called "aberrational." The method is a full-cycle
comparison rather than a claim that short-term underperformance proves
or disproves competence. The author's synthesis is that a circle of
competence has value only when its boundary is maintained during a
period in which the excluded area is outperforming.

### Derivatives as Systemic Risk

The 2002 letter stated its derivatives warning directly: "In our view,
however, derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction,
carrying dangers that, while
now latent, are potentially lethal." The warning was not abstract.
It was grounded in Berkshire's direct experience trying to unwind
General Re's derivatives book. The inventory fell from 23,218
contracts when liquidation began in early 2002 to 14,384 at year-end
2002, 7,580 at year-end 2003, 2,890 at year-end 2004, and 741 at
year-end 2005. The unwind had generated $404 million of cumulative
pre-tax losses by year-end 2005. The 2005 letter compared the
experience to carrying a cat by its tail: after trying to wind up a
derivatives business, the carrier would choose the cat.

The diagnosis had three components. First, mark-to-model accounting:
derivatives contracts, especially long-dated or multi-variable ones,
often lack observable market prices. "Mark-to-market" becomes
"mark-to-model" becomes "mark-to-myth." Because traders are paid on
mark-to-market earnings, the incentives for overstatement are
enormous. Second, the chain-reaction problem: derivatives create
counterparty interconnections invisible in normal times and
dangerous in stressed ones. In 1998, Long-Term Capital Management,
which employed only a few hundred people, prompted a Federal
Reserve-orchestrated rescue because officials believed its outstanding
trades could threaten the stability of American markets. The 2002
letter contrasted the Federal Reserve's bank-liquidity role with the
absence of a central bank charged with stopping domino effects in
insurance or derivatives. Third, the exit is hell: "Like Hell, both
[derivatives and reinsurance] are easy to enter and almost impossible
to exit." The General Re experience showed that a complex derivatives
book could require active management for years before termination,
consuming capital and management attention.

The author's synthesis is that systemic risk can hide in instruments
that appear to distribute risk but in fact concentrate it through opaque
interconnections. The warning itself was more qualified than a
categorical ban. Buffett wrote that he sometimes used large
derivatives to facilitate
investment strategies, while arguing that aggregate counterparty
concentration, collateral triggers, and valuation discretion made the
system more dangerous. For Berkshire, the operational lesson was to
limit exposures it could not measure and to exit an inherited activity
whose risks it judged difficult to value. The 2002 letter extended the
same transparency logic to governance: it said the audit committee's
key job was to get outside auditors to divulge what they knew, and it
gave committees four questions covering accounting choices,
plain-English disclosure, internal-audit procedure, and the shifting
of revenues or expenses between reporting periods.

### Acquisition Discipline and the Admission of Error

The 1996-2005 reports repeatedly identify acquisition errors and assign
responsibility. In 2001, Buffett listed three Dexter Shoe decisions
that hurt shareholders: buying the company, paying with Berkshire
stock, and delaying necessary operating changes. He said he had been
wrong that Dexter could continue to withstand low-cost foreign
competition. A 2016 retrospective quantified the stock cost: the
25,203 Berkshire shares issued for Dexter, acquired for $434 million
in 1993, were then worth more than $6 billion. General Re was the much
larger parallel case. Buffett wrote in 2002 that he was "dead wrong"
about its underwriting culture, and in 2016 called the 21.8 percent
dilution from its acquisition a "terrible mistake."

The author's synthesis is that public error attribution is useful only
when paired with diagnosis and changed behavior. General Re's repair
under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross included recognizing $1.31 billion
of prior-year losses, judging performance by underwriting
profitability rather than volume, and declaring that "size simply
doesn't count." By year-end 2003, Buffett wrote that General Re was
"fixed," while warning that reinsurance would still produce setbacks.
The lesson is not that candor reverses an acquisition loss; it is that
candor creates a testable record of the error, the correction, and the
subsequent operating result.

## Evidence

### The 1996 GEICO Acquisition and Its Long-Run Validation

The author's assessment is that the GEICO acquisition provides the
decade's clearest evidence of the float-as-capital thesis. Berkshire
purchased the remaining 49 percent
of GEICO in January 1996 for $2.3 billion, implying a total company
valuation of approximately $4.6 billion -- roughly 2.4 times GEICO's
$1.9 billion of tangible net worth. The price appeared steep. The
validation came through operating performance: from 1996 through
2005, GEICO had only one year of underwriting loss (2000). Its
advertising expenditure grew from $31 million in 1996 to $502 million
in 2005, and its market share of U.S. private passenger auto insurance
grew from approximately 5.6 percent to 6.1 percent between 2004 and
2005 alone. The 2005 letter reported that GEICO's auto policies in
force grew 12.1 percent and that the company achieved its highest-ever
conversion rate of internet and phone quotes into sales. The
method of the evidence is the operating record: GEICO's underwriting
profit grew from $970 million in 2004 to $1,221 million in 2005, and
its float grew from $5,960 million to $6,692 million. The finding is
that a low-cost direct-marketing model can grow policies and float
while producing underwriting profits; the 2000 loss also shows that
the result was not automatic. The 2004 statement that
Berkshire would be "lucky to be worth half" without an insurance
acquisition referred to National Indemnity, not GEICO or General Re,
and is therefore not evidence for the GEICO purchase.

### The 1998 General Re Merger and the Multi-Year Correction

The author's assessment is that the General Re merger is the decade's
clearest case study in acquisition error and recovery. Announced June
19, 1998 and closed December 21, 1998, the all-stock deal was valued
at approximately $22
billion. General Re contributed $14.9 billion of year-end float, a
global distribution network across 124 countries through General Re
and Cologne Re, and an inherited derivatives dealer. The 23,218
outstanding derivatives contracts were measured when liquidation
began in early 2002, not at the acquisition date. The 1998 letter
praised the acquisition as "first-class in every way" and predicted
materially higher earnings within a decade.

The 1999 letter reported a significant underwriting loss at General Re
due to underpricing, and noted that Berkshire's overall underwriting
loss of $1.4 billion produced a float
cost of 5.8 percent. The 2001 letter, written after the September 11
attacks, stated directly: "Gen Re's underwriting has been far below
the standard I expect of our insurance operations." Berkshire's total
float cost reached 12.8 percent in 2001, about half of which the 2002
letter attributed to World Trade Center losses. The 2002 letter then
documented the correction: under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross,
General Re recognized $1.31 billion in additional prior-year losses to
correct under-reserving and established a new standard: "The entire
organization now understands that we wish to write only properly-priced
business, whatever the effect on volume." Berkshire's total float cost
fell to 1 percent in 2002.

The derivatives unwind ran in parallel. The 2002 letter issued the
"financial weapons of mass destruction" warning. By the start of 2005,
the book was down from 23,218 contracts to 2,890, and by year-end 2005
to 741, at an aggregate cost
of $404 million in losses. The 2005 letter noted that "the blood has
kept flowing" even at reduced inventory levels. The finding of this
evidence is that a sound strategic category (acquiring global
reinsurance capacity and float) can be a poor purchase if the buyer
fails to detect underwriting deterioration, reserving inadequacy, and
hard-to-exit derivatives exposure. The recovery required management
change, cultural repair, reserve corrections, and acceptance of lower
volume before profitability returned. General Re's year-end float
rose from $14.9 billion in 1998 to $22.9 billion in 2005, but the 2016
letter still judged the stock consideration a per-share value error.
The measured conclusion is therefore mixed: the operation recovered
and supplied more float, while the acquisition currency remained too
costly.

### The 1999 Underperformance and the Dot-Com Refusal

The 1999 letter provides direct evidence of Berkshire's circle-of-
competence discipline under pressure. Per-share book value grew 0.5
percent, the worst performance of Buffett's tenure. The S&P 500
returned 21 percent including dividends. Buffett called his 1999 grade
a "D" and named himself the guilty party. Several major holdings
(American Express, Coca-Cola,
Gillette, Disney) had operating shortfalls. The deeper cause was
philosophical: Buffett and Munger could not identify which technology
companies had durable competitive advantages, and refused to invest
in businesses they could not value.

The comparative evidence arrived over the next three years.
Berkshire's per-share book value beat the S&P 500 total return in
2000, 2001, and 2002, including a 32.1-point advantage in 2002 that
Buffett called "aberrational." The method is the published annual
performance series across both the 1999 boom and the 2000-2002 decline,
not a selected endpoint from the recovery alone. The finding is
narrower than claiming that every excluded technology company was a
bad investment: refusing to value businesses outside the stated
circle prevented Berkshire from changing method in response to one
year of severe relative underperformance. The 1999 Fortune article
provided the analytical framework by showing that transformative
industries such as automobiles and aviation had not necessarily
produced attractive aggregate returns for their equity owners.

### Hurricane Katrina and the Test of Float Cost

The 2005 letter provides evidence of float-cost discipline under
extreme insurance losses. Hurricane Katrina inflicted an estimated
$2.5 billion in losses on Berkshire; Rita and Wilma added $0.9
billion, for total hurricane losses of $3.4 billion. General Re
posted an underwriting loss of $334 million; Berkshire Hathaway
Reinsurance Group lost $1,069 million. Yet Berkshire's total
underwriting result was a $53 million profit, and the float was
costless for the year. The offset came from GEICO ($1,221 million
underwriting profit) and the other primary companies ($235 million).
Total float grew from $46,094 million to $49,287 million.

The method of the evidence is the segment-level breakdown. The
author's finding is that diversification within the insurance
portfolio -- combining a catastrophe-exposed reinsurer with a primary
auto insurer having a different loss profile -- allowed Berkshire to
absorb the largest insurance losses in company history without
impairing the cost of capital. The 2005 letter framed the broader
question: whether the frequency and intensity of hurricanes had
changed due to climate factors, and whether the ignorance meant
Berkshire should follow Pascal's wager and assume the worst. The
letter noted that "surprises in insurance are far from symmetrical"
-- you are lucky to get one pleasant surprise for every ten that go
the other way. Berkshire therefore said future mega-catastrophe
coverage would require much higher prices and aggregate exposure that
would remain tolerable if storm behavior had changed.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocators: Scale Narrows the Opportunity Set

The 1996-2005 reports carry a direct implication for capital
allocators at any scale: as assets grow, the universe of investments
large enough to matter shrinks, and the discipline of waiting for
the right opportunity becomes the dominant operational skill. The
1996 letter called Berkshire's prior record nonrepeatable, and the
1998 letter described 15 percent average intrinsic-value growth as the
peak of possible outcomes. In that same year, the
48.3 percent book value gain was largely a function of issuing shares
at prices well above book value, not of operating performance. By
the end of the decade, Berkshire's acquisitions had shifted toward
capital-intensive regulated utilities through MidAmerican Energy and
large operating businesses such as Shaw, Johns Manville, and Clayton
Homes because smaller minority-equity positions could no longer move
the needle.

The practical application is that capital allocators must adjust
their strategy to their scale. Small pools can build portfolios from
small bets; large pools need big, reliable businesses. The
temptation at large scale is to lower standards -- to buy
mediocre businesses because they are large enough to matter. The
author's synthesis is that standards must rise as scale increases,
because the cost of an acquisition error grows with both the size of
the check and the permanence of any shares issued. General Re is the
cautionary case: a $22 billion deal entered with an incorrect
assessment of underwriting culture required years of correction,
produced $404 million of derivatives-unwind losses by 2005, and
increased Berkshire's share count by 21.8 percent. The
reversible response to excess cash is waiting; issuing permanent
equity for a misjudged asset is not reversible.

### For Insurance Investors: Float Is Only Valuable at Low Cost

The decade's insurance results carry a direct implication for
investors in insurance businesses: float is an economic asset only
when generated at low cost, and the cost of float is the underwriting
result. The 2001 letter documented the worst case: a 12.8 percent
float cost after September 11 and a breakdown in General Re's
underwriting discipline. The 2002 letter documented the repair: $1.31
billion in prior-year loss recognition, a new standard of
profitability over volume, and a 1 percent total float cost. The 2005
letter documented a different test: $3.4 billion in hurricane losses
offset by GEICO and other operations, producing a $53 million aggregate
underwriting profit in a record-loss year for Berkshire.

The practical application for investors evaluating insurance
companies is to focus on the underwriting cycle, not the investment
gains. An insurer that grows float by underpricing to win business is
destroying value, not creating it, because the float's cost exceeds
the return available on it. An insurer that maintains underwriting
discipline through cycles -- shrinking when prices are inadequate,
growing when prices are adequate -- generates float that is
genuinely cheap capital. The 1996-2005 reports provide the
analytical tools: the two-column intrinsic value guide separates
investment assets from operating earnings; the float cost table
tracks underwriting results against float growth; the segment
breakdown shows which units generate costless float and which
consume it. Investors who apply these tools can distinguish insurers
that compound from insurers that merely appear to.

### For Students of Reporting: Candor as a Competitive Advantage

The 1996-2005 reports carry an implication that extends beyond
investing into corporate governance and reporting practice. Buffett's
identification of the three Dexter Shoe errors, his acknowledgment of
being "dead wrong" about General Re's underwriting culture, and his
willingness to grade his own 1999 performance a "D" are paired with
specific diagnoses and corrective actions. The 2002 letter applied the
same standard to audit committees: make outside auditors state what
they would report differently, what an investor needs in plain
English, whether internal-audit procedures are sufficient, and whether
revenues or expenses have been shifted between periods.

The practical application for managers and boards is to make an error
falsifiable: identify the original assumption, quantify the damage,
state the corrective action, and publish a measure by which the repair
can be judged. The author's assessment is that such reporting can
support a patient shareholder base because owners can distinguish a
recognized error under repair from an unmeasured problem. This does
not mean candor erases loss; Dexter's stock cost and General Re's
dilution remained real. The author's assessment is that transparent
reporting can improve the conditions for organizational learning and
long-horizon capital allocation.

### For Value Investors: The Circle of Competence Under Pressure

The 1999 letter and the dot-com refusal carry a direct implication
for value investors: the circle of competence is a discipline
tested under social pressure, and its value is realized only by
those who maintain it when maintenance looks foolish. Buffett was
responsible for a 0.5 percent per-share book-value gain while the S&P
500 returned 21 percent in 1999. He nevertheless wrote that predicting
the long-term economics of fast-changing industries was beyond
Berkshire's perimeter. The following three years produced relative
outperformance, but Buffett called the 32.1-point 2002 gap
"aberrational" rather than treating it as a repeatable forecast.

The practical application is that the circle of competence is not a
static list of industries but a repeated decision process. The
author's synthesis is that individual investors face liquidity needs
and behavioral pressure, while fund managers may also face client
redemptions and career risk. Investment structure should therefore be
aligned with the claimed time horizon: explain the method before
underperformance,
avoid promises that require short-term relative success, and maintain
liquidity sufficient to avoid forced sales. Under those conditions,
the circle of competence can operate as an organizational discipline
rather than as a slogan applied after the outcome is known.
