# Scale Economies Shared -- Why Passing Efficiency Gains to Customers Can Deepen a Moat

Scale economies shared is a business model in which a company
deliberately returns its growing cost advantage to customers as lower
prices, better service, or wider selection rather than retaining it as
profit margin. The customer reciprocates with more business, which
creates greater scale, which creates a larger cost advantage, which
is returned again. The result is a self-reinforcing loop that widens
the competitive moat every year it operates. The model was identified
and named by Nick Sleep of the Nomad Investment Partnership, who used
it as the primary lens through which he analyzed and held Costco,
Amazon, and Berkshire Hathaway -- compounding at 20.8 percent
annually over thirteen years against 6.5 percent for the MSCI World
Index.

## Background

### The Investor Who Named the Model

Nick Sleep partnered with Qais Zakaria in 2001 to form the Nomad
Investvement Partnership, initially under Marathon Asset Management
and later as an independent entity. Over thirteen years, from
September 2001 through December 2013, Nomad compounded capital at
20.8 percent per year net of fees, against 6.5 percent for the MSCI
World Index over the same period. One dollar invested with Nomad
became roughly ten; one dollar in the index became roughly two. The
partnership achieved this with almost no turnover, a handful of
holdings, no leverage, no short-selling, and shareholder letters
typed in Courier font and mailed to partners a couple of times a
year. By the time Sleep and Zakaria closed the fund in 2014 to pursue
philanthropic endeavors, the portfolio was concentrated in Costco,
Amazon, and Berkshire Hathaway.

Sleep was not a public figure. He did not give interviews, publish
books, or manage money for institutions. His shareholder letters --
approximately 200 pages over the partnership's life -- circulated
quietly among a small community of serious investors. They were
described as the "dead sea scrolls of investing" by those who studied
them. The letters were not performance reports; they were working
documents in which Sleep and Zakaria documented their evolving
philosophy, their mistakes, their mental models, and their deepest
thinking about how great businesses actually work. The scale
economies shared concept was the most important idea to emerge from
those letters, and it was the framework that justified Nomad's
concentration in a small number of companies held for a decade or
more.

### The Intellectual Lineage

The phrase belongs to Sleep, but the lineage runs deeper. The model
has roots in Buffett's discussion of GEICO's low-cost insurance
advantage and in the broader value-investing tradition of identifying
businesses that compound shareholder value through structural cost
advantages rather than through pricing power alone. Sleep read
Buffett's 2004 Berkshire Hathaway annual report and noted that GEICO
delivered real money to three groups simultaneously: customers saved
roughly one billion dollars compared to what they would have paid
elsewhere, employees earned a large profit-sharing bonus, and owners
earned an excellent return. Those benefits existed only because of
scale. Sleep asked a sharper question: if scale produces a pool of
benefits, who gets it, and what does the split tell you about how
hard the business is to attack?

That question became the robustness ratio, and the company that
answered it most elegantly was Costco. Sleep made Costco Nomad's
largest holding and never stopped writing about it. In his 2004
letter he wrote: "In the office we have a white board on which we
have listed the (very few) investment models that work and that we
can understand. Costco is the best example we can find of one of
them: scale efficiencies shared. Most companies pursue scale
efficiencies, but few share them. It's the sharing that makes the
model so powerful."

### The Problem the Model Solves

Traditional moat analysis identifies competitive advantages that
defend a position: a brand, a patent, a network effect, a regulatory
license. These moats are defensive. They protect what a company
already has, and they erode over time as competitors find ways around
them. Scale economies shared is different. It is not a defensive
moat; it is an offensive one. The moat does not merely persist -- it
widens. Every year the business operates, the cost gap between it
and competitors grows, prices fall further, the customer proposition
improves, and the business becomes harder to dislodge than it was the
year before. Sleep borrowed Peter Bernstein's image of a cone of
uncertainty that widens the further out you look. For most
businesses, the future becomes less predictable the further out you
project. For a scale-economies-shared business, the future becomes
more predictable, not less -- which is precisely backwards from how
most businesses age.

## Core Concepts

### The Reciprocation Loop

The central mechanism of scale economies shared is the reciprocation
loop. The loop operates as follows: a company with a cost advantage
lowers its prices. The customer notices and consolidates spending
with the company because it offers the best value. More sales means
more volume, which means better buying power and lower costs per
unit. The company returns those savings to the customer as still
lower prices. The customer reciprocates with still more business.
The loop turns on itself, and it gets harder to attack every year it
runs.

Sleep called customer reciprocation a "super-factor" in business
performance, and he meant it literally. The model does not work
without reciprocation. If a company lowers prices and customers do
not respond with more volume, the loop stalls. The company has given
away margin without receiving the volume that would justify it. The
reciprocation loop is what separates a genuine scale-economies-shared
business from a company that is simply pricing aggressively to gain
market share. The former has a self-reinforcing engine; the latter
has a one-way drain.

The loop also creates a fundamentally different customer relationship
than the standard retail model. In high-low retailing, customers are
trained to buy on a deal, shop around, and remain disloyal. The
incentives are misaligned: the retailer trains the customer to wait
for the next promotion, which raises operating costs and erodes
efficiency. In a scale-economies-shared model, the customer is
trained to trust the price every day and to consolidate spending
with the one player who is always the cheapest. The customer is not
loyal out of affection; the customer is loyal because the economic
incentive to consolidate spending is structural and persistent.

### Economies of Scale vs. Scale Economies Shared

A common confusion conflates scale economies shared with ordinary
economies of scale. The two are related but structurally different.
Economies of scale means costs per unit fall as a business grows.
This is common. A larger manufacturer negotiates better input prices,
spreads fixed costs over more units, and achieves a lower cost per
unit than a smaller competitor. On its own, this produces a fatter
margin. The company keeps the savings as profit. A fatter margin,
however, invites competition. If a competitor can replicate the
scale advantage, or if the margin is large enough to attract
new entrants willing to accept lower returns, the advantage erodes.

Scale economies shared is the deliberate decision to give most of
that cost saving back to the customer as lower prices or a better
deal, which triggers the reciprocation loop. The sharing is the part
that builds the moat. A company that keeps its scale savings as
margin has a cost advantage that competitors can attack. A company
that returns its scale savings to customers has a cost advantage
that compounds, because the customer reciprocation drives still more
scale, which drives still lower costs, which drives still lower
prices. The first model is a snapshot; the second is a flywheel.

The distinction has a direct investment implication. A company
pursuing ordinary economies of scale is optimizing for current
profitability. A company pursuing scale economies shared is
optimizing for the durability and growth of its competitive position
over a long horizon. The former may show higher margins and higher
current earnings; the latter may show thinner margins and lower
current earnings but a moat that widens with every year of
operation. This is why the model is so often misread by conventional
valuation heuristics that focus on margin and earnings multiples.

### The Robustness Ratio

Sleep built a specific tool to measure the depth of a
scale-economies-shared moat: the robustness ratio. In its full form,
the robustness ratio is the combined distribution to customers and
employees (through lower prices, better service, profit sharing, and
similar mechanisms) divided by the distribution to shareholders. In
its simpler and more usable form, it is the amount of money customers
save divided by the amount shareholders earn. In one line: dollars
saved by customers for every dollar kept by owners.

The ratio at GEICO was approximately 1:1. GEICO policyholders saved
about one billion dollars compared to the next cheapest carrier, and
GEICO earned roughly one billion dollars in underwriting profit. The
savings and the profits were roughly equal. The ratio at Costco was
approximately 5:1. A Costco member saved about five dollars compared
to shopping the same basket at an ordinary supermarket, for every one
dollar Costco kept as profit. The arithmetic ran through Costco's
gross margin of about 11 percent against roughly 23 to 26 percent at
a Walmart or a Kroger, adjusted for the membership fee.

The interpretation of the ratio is not "higher is always better." A
higher ratio means a wider moat, because a rival trying to take
share from the business must find the dollars of customer value that
the incumbent is already giving away before it even reaches the
incumbent's own thin profit. But a higher ratio can also mean a less
equitable split, where the business is handing customers so much that
shareholders take very little. Costco's 5:1 and GEICO's 1:1 may be
equally effective barriers against competition. The ratio tells you
how hard the business is to attack. It does not, on its own, tell you
whether it is the better investment.

The ratio has a critical boundary. It is appropriate only when the
customer proposition is built on price. Costco, GEICO, a discount
retailer, a low-fee financial product -- these are businesses where
the value delivered to the customer is measurable as a price
saving. The ratio is not appropriate for a proposition built on
advertising, aspiration, or status. Sleep used the example of Nike:
nobody buys Nike to save money, so measuring a price-based give-back
tells you nothing about that moat. If the value a business delivers
is mostly status, brand, or experience rather than price, the
robustness ratio is the wrong instrument.

### The Widening Moat

The single most important property of scale economies shared is
that the moat widens over time rather than eroding. Most moats
defend a position. A patent defends until it expires. A brand defends
until taste shifts. A network effect defends until a larger network
emerges. A scale-economies-shared business does not sit and defend.
Every year it operates, it grows, the cost gap widens, prices fall
further, the customer proposition improves, and the business becomes
harder to dislodge than it was the year before. Size is not a
liability that invites competition, bureaucracy, and decay; size is
the mechanism that lowers prices, deepens customer loyalty, and
raises the wall. For most businesses, getting big invites the
problems of scale. For this model, getting big is the solution.

This property has a direct consequence for predictability. Sleep
argued that Costco's obsession with sharing savings narrowed the
cone of uncertainty around its future. The future of a
scale-economies-shared business becomes more predictable, not less,
the longer the loop runs. This is the opposite of most businesses,
where the future becomes less certain as competitive landscapes
shift, technologies change, and consumer preferences evolve. The
self-reinforcing nature of the loop means that the business's
destination becomes clearer with every year of operation, provided
management maintains the discipline of returning savings to
customers rather than capturing them as margin.

### The Low-Margin Paradox

Scale economies shared produces a counterintuitive financial
profile: the business often appears weak by conventional metrics
while being structurally dominant. Costco operated with gross
margins near 11 to 13 percent, roughly half those of Walmart, and
operating margins near 3 percent. On a price-to-earnings basis,
Costco traded at 24x earnings when Sleep was buying -- expensive for
a big-box retailer. Amazon operated at thin or negative margins for
years, reinvesting every dollar of scale advantage into lower prices.
Conventional valuation heuristics -- which reward high margins, high
returns on equity, and low earnings multiples -- systematically
misread these businesses. The margins are low by design, not by
weakness. The low margin is the mechanism that feeds the loop.

The paradox resolves when the investor shifts from asking "what does
this earn today" to "what does this look like as a mature franchise in
fifteen to twenty years, and is that destination secured by the loop
itself." If the loop is genuine and management maintains the
discipline of sharing savings, the destination is a business with a
cost structure competitors cannot match, a customer base that
consolidates spending with the company by structural incentive, and
a moat that has been widening for over a decade. The current margin
is not the output that matters; the destination is.

### The Membership Fee as Alignment Mechanism

Costco's membership fee is not a minor revenue line; it is the
structural mechanism that aligns the company's incentives with the
scale-economies-shared model. Membership fees account for
approximately 70 percent of Costco's operating profit. Because the
fee is the primary profit source, Costco has no incentive to mark up
merchandise. The company sells goods at barely above cost, which
maximizes the value proposition for the member, which drives
membership renewals, which produces the recurring, high-margin
revenue that funds the next round of expansion. The membership fee
solves the alignment problem that plagues conventional retail: a
retailer that profits on product markup is incentivized to charge as
much as the market will bear, which conflicts with the customer's
interest. A retailer that profits on the membership fee is
incentivized to deliver the best possible value, because the member's
renewal is the economic engine.

Sleep understood this. He wrote: "This is a very simple and honest
consumer proposition in the sense that the membership fee buys the
customer's loyalty (and is almost all profit) and Costco in exchange
sells goods whilst just covering operating costs. By sticking to a
standard mark-up, savings achieved through purchasing or scale are
returned to the customer in the form of lower prices, which in turn
encourages growth and extends scale advantages." The membership fee
is what makes the loop self-sustaining: it provides the profit that
justifies the low margins, and the low margins provide the value that
justifies the membership renewal.

## The Flywheel in Practice: Costco and Amazon

### Costco: The Canonical Case

Costco is the example Sleep returned to most often and the holding
that best illustrates the model. The company operates warehouse-club
stores with a limited SKU count of approximately 3,700 items --
roughly one-tenth of a conventional supermarket. The limited SKU
count creates concentration of purchasing power: with fewer products
and higher volume per product, Costco negotiates lower input prices
than competitors who spread their purchasing across tens of thousands
of SKUs. The savings are passed to the customer as lower prices. The
customer reciprocates with higher revenue per square foot. Sleep
noted that Costco's revenue per foot of retailing space exceeded
that of Walmart's Sam's Club by approximately 50 percent, and was
roughly four times that of run-of-the-mill supermarkets.

The competitive gap is structural, not tactical. Sleep estimated
that matching Costco's prices would cost Sam's Club somewhere in the
region of 1.4 billion dollars annually, and matching Costco's pay
scales would cost another 750 million dollars. These are not
trivial numbers even for Walmart, which earned approximately nine
billion dollars in net profit at the time. The chasm of
competitiveness is difficult to capture using traditional analytical
tools: both Costco and Sam's Club generate thin margins, both are
growing revenues, and both are pressuring traditional supermarkets.
But the long-term outcome is significantly different, because
Costco's business model dictates that the competitive gap should
expand over time. As Costco grows, its relative asset intensity
falls and its buying power rises, leading to greater scale
efficiencies, which are returned to the customer as lower prices.

### Amazon: The Digital Extension

Sleep recognized that the scale-economies-shared model applied to
internet retailers as well, and Amazon was the case he identified
early. In 2005, Jeff Bezos wrote in his shareholder letter: "We
have made a decision to continuously and significantly lower prices
for customers year after year as our efficiency and scale make it
possible. This is an example of a very important decision that
cannot be made in a math-based way. In fact, when we lower prices, we
go against the math that we can do, which always says that the smart
move is to raise prices." Bezos explicitly described the
short-term/long-term tension: the volume increase from a price
reduction is never enough to pay for the price decrease in the
short term. But the long-term effect of consistently lowering prices
creates a virtuous cycle that leads to a much larger dollar amount of
free cash flow over five or ten years.

Amazon's flywheel, as documented by Brad Stone in "The Everything
Store," was sketched around 2001, inspired by Jim Collins' flywheel
concept from "Good to Great." The mechanism: lower prices lead to
more customer visits, more visits attract more sellers, more sellers
widen the selection, wider selection improves the customer
experience, improved customer experience drives more traffic, more
traffic allows Amazon to lower its cost structure further, and the
lower cost structure allows still lower prices. Unlike Costco, which
must spend approximately 15 million dollars per incremental store to
serve a radius of perhaps thirty miles, Amazon can add scale with
near-zero incremental capital. This makes the digital version of the
flywheel potentially more powerful than the physical one.

Nomad held 55.2 million dollars of Amazon shares in 2007, when the
stock traded at 82x earnings. The partnership more than doubled the
position by the time it closed in 2014, when the holding was worth
1.2 billion dollars. Sleep argued that the biggest error an investor
can make is the sale of a Walmart or a Microsoft in the early stages
of growth -- an error that is mathematically far greater than the
equivalent sum invested in a firm that goes bankrupt, because
opportunity costs go unrecorded in performance records.

## Evidence

### Nomad's Track Record: 921 Percent Over Thirteen Years

The most direct evidence for the model's investment power is Nomad's
own performance. From September 2001 through December 2013, the
partnership compounded at 20.8 percent per year net of fees. The MSCI
World Index returned 6.5 percent over the same period. The aggregate
result was a 921 percent return for Nomad versus 117 percent for the
index. One dollar invested with Nomad became approximately ten; one
dollar in the index became approximately two. The performance was
achieved with a concentrated portfolio -- by the end, dominated by
Costco, Amazon, and Berkshire Hathaway -- almost no turnover, and no
leverage. The strategy was not diversified across hundreds of
names; it was a bet on a small number of businesses that exhibited
the scale-economies-shared property, held for as long as the loop
continued to turn.

The performance is not attributable to market timing or sector
rotation. Nomad held through the 2008 financial crisis without
selling. Sleep and Zakaria argued that volatility was not risk but
opportunity, and that the worst error was not buying a bad business
but selling a great one too early. The fund's discipline was
structural: the partnership agreement charged only 10 basis points
as a management fee (far below the industry standard of 1 to 2
percent), and the 20 percent performance fee was earned only after a
6 percent annual hurdle. Sleep and Zakaria had 100 percent of their
personal investments in the fund, aligning their interests
completely with their partners.

### GEICO: The Berkshire Connection

The seed of the scale-economies-shared framework was Buffett's 2004
Berkshire Hathaway annual report discussion of GEICO. Buffett pointed
out that GEICO delivered real value to three groups simultaneously:
customers saved approximately one billion dollars compared to what
they would have paid elsewhere, employees earned a large
profit-sharing bonus (averaging 24.3 percent of annual salaries, or
191 million dollars), and owners earned an excellent return
(approximately one billion dollars in underwriting profit). Those
benefits existed only because of GEICO's scale. A smaller insurer
could not match GEICO's cost structure and therefore could not
deliver the same customer savings, the same employee bonuses, and
the same shareholder returns simultaneously.

Sleep read this and generalized it. If scale produces a pool of
benefits, the question is not just whether the pool exists but how it
is distributed. GEICO's roughly 1:1 split between customer savings
and shareholder profits was the baseline. Costco's 5:1 split
represented a more extreme version: the business was giving away
five dollars of customer value for every dollar of profit retained.
The robustness ratio emerged from this analysis as a tool to
compare the depth of the moat across businesses that compete on
price.

### Costco's Financial Evidence

Costco's financial performance validates the model over decades.
The company's membership renewal rate has consistently exceeded 90
percent in North America, reflecting the structural loyalty that
the low-price, membership-fee model produces. Membership fee
revenue has grown from 1.7 billion dollars in 2010 to over 4.6
billion dollars by 2024, accounting for approximately 70 percent of
operating profit. Because the marginal cost of servicing an
additional membership is near zero, this revenue drops almost
entirely to the bottom line. The stock has traded at a premium
valuation relative to other retailers -- typically 35 to 40x
earnings -- because investors recognize that the membership revenue
is recurring, high-margin, and predictable.

Costco's revenue per square foot has consistently exceeded
competitors. Sleep noted in his letters that Costco's sales per foot
of retailing space were approximately four times those of ordinary
supermarkets and approximately 50 percent above Sam's Club. The gap
has not narrowed over time; it has widened, consistent with the
model's prediction that the competitive advantage compounds rather
than erodes. The limited SKU count -- approximately 3,700 items
versus 40,000-plus at a conventional supermarket -- creates
purchasing concentration that produces lower input costs, which are
returned to the customer as lower prices, which drives higher
volume per SKU, which produces still lower input costs.

### Amazon's Long-Term Validation

Amazon's trajectory provides the strongest evidence that the model
works in a digital context. The company operated at thin or negative
margins for years, reinvesting scale advantages into lower prices,
free shipping (Amazon Prime), and infrastructure. Bezos
explicitly stated in his 2005 letter that the short-term volume
increase from a price reduction was never enough to pay for the
price decrease, but that the long-term effect of consistently
lowering prices would create a virtuous cycle leading to much larger
free cash flow over five to ten years. Amazon's market
capitalization has grown from under one billion dollars at its IPO
to over a trillion dollars, validating the thesis that the loop, if
maintained, produces extraordinary long-term value.

Nomad's Amazon position illustrates the cost of premature selling.
Sleep and Zakaria held Amazon from 2006 through the partnership's
closure in 2014, more than doubling the position over that period.
The 55.2 million dollar holding in 2007 grew to 1.2 billion dollars
by 2014. Sleep argued that selling a compounder in its early growth
stage was the largest error an investor could make -- larger, in
dollar terms, than investing the same amount in a company that goes
bankrupt, because the opportunity cost of missing decades of
compounding dwarfs the capital loss from a single failure.

### AirAsia: The Model's Limits

Not every application of the model succeeds. Nomad also invested in
AirAsia, the low-cost Malaysian carrier, which Sleep grouped with
the other major holdings in the letters. AirAsia's cost per seat per
kilometer was approximately 3 cents, compared to 4.5 cents at
Ryanair and 5.5 cents at Southwest. The low-cost model filled seats
that high-fare flag carriers left empty. On paper, the business
exhibited the scale-economies-shared property: lower costs passed to
customers as lower fares, which drove volume, which drove scale.
But AirAsia did not compound as an investment the way Costco and
Amazon did. The reasons are not entirely clear from the available
letters, but the case illustrates that the model identifies a
mechanism, not a guarantee. The reciprocation loop can stall if the
cost advantage is not durable, if regulation or fuel prices erode
the savings, or if the customer does not reciprocate at the scale
required to sustain the flywheel. The model is a framework for
identifying candidate businesses, not a formula that ensures
success.

## Implications

### For Value Investors: A Specific Moat Test

Scale economies shared gives value investors a specific, testable
framework for identifying businesses that compound competitive
advantage over time rather than merely defending it. The
robustness ratio provides a quantitative lens: how many dollars of
customer value does the business give away for every dollar of profit
it retains? A high ratio signals a deep moat for a price-based
business. The test is not universal -- it fails for businesses that
compete on brand, status, or experience rather than price -- but
within its domain, it is a sharper instrument than generic moat
analysis because it measures the mechanism by which the moat widens,
not just whether a moat exists.

For investors following the Buffett and Munger tradition, the model
refines the concept of a "wonderful business." Buffett has said that
the ideal business is one that can raise prices without losing
customers. Scale economies shared inverts this: the ideal business
is one that lowers prices and gains customers. Both produce
durable competitive advantages, but they operate through different
mechanisms. The pricing-power business extracts more value from each
transaction; the scale-economies-shared business creates more value
per transaction and captures a smaller share of it, relying on
volume growth and compounding scale to produce shareholder returns
over the long term. The investor must recognize both patterns and
understand when each applies.

### For Business Operators: The Discipline of Sharing

The model carries a direct implication for business strategy: the
decision to share scale savings with customers rather than retaining
them as margin is a strategic choice that determines the long-term
trajectory of the business. A company that captures scale savings as
profit maximizes current earnings but invites competition, because
the margin gap attracts entrants who can replicate the scale
advantage or accept lower returns. A company that returns scale
savings to customers minimizes current earnings but builds a moat
that compounds, because the customer reciprocation drives still more
scale, which drives still lower costs, which drives still lower
prices. The choice is between a snapshot of high profitability and a
flywheel of compounding advantage.

The discipline required is significant. Management must resist the
pressure from analysts and short-term shareholders who reward high
margins and high earnings. Costco has held its hot-dog-and-soda
combination at 1.50 dollars since the mid-1980s, reportedly at a
loss, under a standing order from founder Jim Sinegal never to raise
the price. Amazon operated for years at margins that conventional
analysis deemed unsustainable. The discipline is not occasional; it
is continuous. Every quarter, the question recurs: do we keep the
savings or give them back? The answer determines whether the loop
turns or stalls.

### For Valuation: Why Conventional Heuristics Fail

Scale-economies-shared businesses are systematically misvalued by
conventional heuristics. Price-to-earnings multiples appear high
because earnings are depressed by the deliberate margin compression.
Return on equity appears low because the business reinvests
aggressively rather than distributing. Profit margins appear thin
because the business is designed to be thin. An investor who
screens for low P/E, high ROE, and fat margins will filter these
businesses out -- and will miss the companies that compound the
fastest over long horizons.

Sleep recognized this and argued that the right question was not
"what does this earn today" but "what does this look like as a mature
franchise, and is that destination secured by the loop itself." If
the loop is genuine and management maintains the discipline of
sharing, the destination is a business with an unassailable cost
structure, a customer base that consolidates spending by structural
incentive, and a moat that has been widening for a decade. The
current earnings are a small residual of a much larger economic
engine. Costco could earn Walmart-level margins by raising prices
slightly, in which case it would trade at 11x earnings instead of
24x -- but it would be a weaker business, because the price
increase would allow competitors to close the gap. The low multiple
would reflect a narrowing moat, not a bargain.

### For Portfolio Construction: Concentration and Patience

The model has direct implications for how a portfolio is
constructed. If scale-economies-shared businesses compound
advantage over decades, and if the moat widens rather than erodes,
then the optimal strategy is concentration and patience: identify
the small number of businesses that exhibit the property, hold them
for as long as the loop continues to turn, and resist the impulse
to sell on valuation grounds alone. Nomad's portfolio was
concentrated in three to five names. Sleep argued that the biggest
error was not buying a bad business but selling a great one too
early, because the opportunity cost of missing decades of
compounding is mathematically larger than the capital loss from a
single failure.

This contrasts with diversification strategies that spread risk
across many positions. Diversification reduces the impact of any
single failure, but it also reduces the impact of any single
success. For a business whose moat widens with time, the cost of
under-allocating to it may exceed the cost of allocating to
businesses that fail. The investor who recognized Costco and Amazon
early and held for a decade captured returns that no diversified
portfolio could match, because the compounding of a single
widening-moat business over twenty years produces multiples that
dilute the impact of every other position in the portfolio.

### For Identifying New Candidates: The Test Questions

The model gives investors a set of test questions for identifying
new scale-economies-shared candidates beyond the canonical cases.
First: does the business have a structural cost advantage that
grows with scale? Second: does management systematically return that
advantage to customers rather than retaining it as margin? Third:
do customers reciprocate with more business when prices fall --
is the volume response structural, not just transactional? Fourth:
is the customer proposition built on price, making the robustness
ratio an appropriate instrument? Fifth: does the moat widen with
time rather than erode, or is the cost advantage static? A business
that passes all five tests is a candidate for the model. A business
that fails any one of them is not necessarily a bad business, but it
is not a scale-economies-shared business, and the framework does not
apply.

Modern candidates that analysts have identified using these tests
include Nu Holdings and MercadoLibre in Latin American financial
services, and Toast in restaurant technology. Each exhibits some
properties of the model: a cost advantage that can be returned to
customers, a price-based value proposition, and a potential
reciprocation loop. Whether these businesses sustain the discipline
over decades, as Costco and Amazon have, remains to be seen. The
model is a framework for asking the right questions, not a
guarantee that the answers will be affirmative.

## Common Pitfalls

### Misidentifying the Model

Not every low-margin business is a scale-economies-shared business.
A company may have low margins because it is inefficient, because
it competes in a commoditized market with no structural advantage,
or because it is losing money to gain share without a reciprocation
loop. The test is not the margin level but the mechanism: does the
business systematically return its scale advantage to customers in
a way that they reward with more business, and does that
reciprocation drive still more scale? A company that lowers prices
without triggering customer reciprocation is bleeding margin, not
building a moat.

### Applying the Robustness Ratio Outside Its Domain

The robustness ratio is appropriate only for businesses where the
customer proposition is based on price. Applying it to a
brand-driven business (Nike, Apple, luxury goods) produces a
meaningless number, because customers are not seeking savings and
the price give-back is not the mechanism that drives loyalty. The
ratio measures the depth of a price-based moat. Using it to
evaluate a non-price-based moat is like using a thermometer to
measure distance -- the instrument is not wrong, it is the wrong
instrument.

### Confusing the Loop With Growth

A company can grow without a reciprocation loop. Growth through
acquisition, growth through new market entry, or growth through
product innovation may produce revenue increases without the
self-reinforcing cost advantage that characterizes scale economies
shared. The loop is specific: scale produces cost savings, which are
returned to customers, which produces more volume, which produces
more scale. Growth that does not pass through this cycle is not the
model, regardless of how fast the revenue line moves.
