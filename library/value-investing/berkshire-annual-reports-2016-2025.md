# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 2016-2025 -- A Decade That Tested Capital Allocation Discipline and Completed a Succession

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 2016 through 2025 document a
decade in which the company made its largest acquisition, admitted its
costliest mistake, executed roughly $78 billion in buybacks, built and
then halved its largest equity position, and completed the leadership
transition from Warren Buffett to Greg Abel. Read as a sequence, the
ten reports reveal a capital allocation discipline that held through
market dislocation, a pandemic, and the death of Charlie Munger --
and a reporting philosophy that treated candor about errors as a
fiduciary obligation rather than a public-relations risk.

## Background

Berkshire Hathaway's annual shareholder letter is unlike any other
document in corporate America. Since taking control of the failing
textile manufacturer in 1965, Warren Buffett used the letter not merely
to satisfy SEC disclosure requirements but to teach, to explain his
capital allocation logic, and to build a relationship of trust with
shareholders based on radical candor. Buffett stated the principle
explicitly in the 2024 letter: "report" implies a greater
responsibility than merely providing mandated data; Berkshire owes
shareholders additional commentary about what they own and how
management thinks. He contrasted this with companies where "mistake"
or "wrong" were forbidden words at board meetings -- a taboo that, in
his view, implied a false managerial perfection that always made him
nervous.

The decade 2016-2025 is a natural unit of analysis for several reasons.
It opens with the closing of Berkshire's largest-ever acquisition --
Precision Castparts Corp. (PCC) on January 29, 2016, at approximately
$37 billion including debt -- and it closes with Greg Abel's first
shareholder letter as CEO, released February 28, 2026, covering fiscal
year 2025. Within this window, Berkshire navigated the Tax Cuts and
Jobs Act of 2017 (which produced a one-time $29 billion deferred-tax
benefit), the COVID-19 disruption of 2020, the death of Charlie
Munger on November 28, 2023 at age 99, and Buffett's surprise
announcement on May 3, 2025 that he would recommend Greg Abel as his
successor effective January 1, 2026.

The decade also brackets a fundamental shift in Berkshire's capital
allocation posture. At the start of 2016, Berkshire had not repurchased
a single share in over five years; its buyback policy, unchanged since
1999, restricted repurchases to prices below 120 percent of book
value -- a threshold the stock had not touched since the financial
crisis. By mid-2018, the board relaxed the threshold to allow
repurchases whenever Buffett and Munger judged the price to be below
intrinsic value. The result was approximately $78 billion in buybacks
from 2018 through 2024, shrinking the Class A-equivalent share count
from roughly 1.64 million to under 1.4 million. Then in the third
quarter of 2024, buybacks stopped entirely -- they remained at zero
through all of 2025 -- before resuming modestly in the first quarter
of 2026 with $235 million in repurchases.

Insurance float, the engine of Berkshire's compounding since the 1967
acquisition of National Indemnity for $8.6 million, grew from
approximately $91.6 billion at the end of 2016 to $176 billion by the
end of 2025. Buffett described float as a "revolving fund" rather
than a typical liability: as old claims are paid, new premiums flow
in to replace them, and as the business grows, the pool grows with
it. Since 1967, the float compounded at an annual rate of 21.7
percent. The decade 2016-2025 nearly doubled the float, providing
Berkshire with an ever-larger pool of cost-free (or negative-cost)
capital to deploy.

Operating earnings -- Buffett's preferred metric, which excludes
investment gains and losses -- grew from $17.6 billion in 2016 to
$47.4 billion in 2024, then slipped to $44.5 billion in 2025. Per
share, the growth was faster because of buybacks. The stock roughly
tripled over the decade, with Class B shares rising from $148.64 in
August 2016 to approximately $504 by August 2026, a compound annual
return of about 13 percent.

The succession question -- how to replace the world's most celebrated
investor -- had preoccupied shareholders and the board for decades.
In 2018, Buffett began publicly handing over operating reins when he
appointed Greg Abel and Ajit Jain as vice chairmen, with Abel
responsible for all non-insurance operations. The board devoted part
of every meeting to the succession question. In 2021, Charlie Munger
inadvertently revealed that Abel was the designated successor at a
shareholder meeting. The well-telegraphed move gave Abel years to win
over shareholders and stakeholders. Yet when Buffett made the formal
announcement at the end of the May 3, 2025 annual meeting, it
surprised even Abel -- and the meeting ended with a standing ovation
for Buffett.

## Core Concepts

### The Annual Letter as a Capital Allocation Report

Buffett's letters are not press releases; they are capital allocation
reports written by the person making the decisions. Each letter
explains not just what Berkshire did with its capital but why, what
worked, what failed, and what the management team learned. This
transparency serves two functions: it educates shareholders so they
can make informed decisions about whether to remain invested, and it
creates a public accountability mechanism. When Buffett admitted in
the 2020 letter that he "paid too much" for Precision Castparts, he
was not managing optics; he was fulfilling what he saw as a fiduciary
duty to be honest about his own judgment errors. He wrote that he was
"simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential" and
that the $11 billion write-down was "almost entirely the
quantification of a mistake I made in 2016." This level of candor
from a CEO about a specific dollar amount tied to a specific decision
is rare in corporate reporting and is a defining feature of the
Berkshire letter.

### Insurance Float as Permanent, Growing Capital

Float is the premium money Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries hold
and invest before claims are paid. Buffett called it a "revolving
fund" rather than a liability because individual dollars of float are
temporary (claims eventually get paid) but the aggregate pool is
permanent and growing. The cost of float -- the underwriting loss (or
gain) relative to the amount of float held -- determines whether the
capital is cheap or expensive. In good years, Berkshire's
underwriting is profitable, making the float negative-cost: Berkshire
is paid to hold other people's money. In catastrophe-heavy years, the
float becomes expensive. The 2020 letter warned that insurance "could
in certain years be an albatross" if underwriting discipline lapsed.
Float grew from $91.6 billion (end of 2016) to $171 billion (end of
2024) to $176 billion (end of 2025), roughly doubling over the decade.
Even the strong 2024 underwriting result -- a $9.02 billion gain
across all insurance operations -- came after absorbing approximately
$1.2 billion in estimated claims from Hurricanes Helene and Milton.

### The Buyback Decision Framework

For most of Berkshire's history, buybacks were nonexistent. The 1999
policy restricted repurchases to prices below 120 percent of book
value per share. In July 2018, the board eliminated the book-value
threshold and replaced it with a judgment-based test: repurchases
were authorized whenever Buffett and Munger concluded that the stock
price was below "conservatively determined" intrinsic value. This
change reflected a philosophical evolution. Book value had become a
less relevant metric as Berkshire's intrinsic value diverged from its
accounting book value -- the gap widened as the portfolio of
high-return businesses grew and as GAAP accounting failed to capture
the economic value of wholly-owned subsidiaries. The result was
$78 billion in buybacks over 2018-2024, with peak years in 2020
($24.7 billion) and 2021 ($27.1 billion). Buybacks then stopped in
Q3 2024 and stayed at zero through 2025, signaling that Buffett no
longer saw the stock as undervalued at prevailing prices. The pause
is itself a capital allocation decision: refusing to buy back shares
at a price above intrinsic value is as disciplined as buying them
below it.

### Elephant Hunting and Acquisition Discipline

Buffett described large acquisitions as "elephant hunting" -- a
patient pursuit of rare opportunities to deploy capital in outstanding
businesses at sensible prices. The decade produced three major
elephants: Precision Castparts ($37 billion, closed 2016), the Pilot
Travel Centers staged acquisition (total approximately $13.6 billion
across 2017, 2023, and 2024 tranches), and the Occidental Petroleum
financing ($10 billion preferred stock in 2019, plus a multi-year
common-stock accumulation beginning 2022). Beyond these, Berkshire
made smaller acquisitions in 2025 -- OxyChem and Bell Laboratories --
under Abel's leadership. The discipline of elephant hunting is not
just about finding targets; it is about refusing to settle for
mediocre deals when prices are high. Buffett's 2024 letter explained
the record $321 billion cash pile not as fear or pessimism but as
the absence of opportunities meeting Berkshire's criteria. The
willingness to hold cash -- what Abel called "strategic ammunition"
-- is itself a capital allocation skill that most managers lack,
because the institutional pressure to "do something" is relentless.

### Concentration, Position Sizing, and the Apple Trajectory

Berkshire's Apple position is the most profitable investment in
Buffett's career. Buying began in 2016 under the influence of
investment lieutenants Ted Weschler and Todd Combs, starting with
about $1 billion. The position grew to approximately $40 billion in
cost basis and peaked above $150 billion in market value by late
2023, at one point comprising roughly half of Berkshire's equity
portfolio. Buffett called Apple one of the "four giants" of
Berkshire's business alongside the insurance operations, BNSF
railroad, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy. Then in 2024, Berkshire
sold over 515 million shares, reducing the position by roughly 56
percent between the third quarter of 2023 and mid-2024, and trimming
further in 2025. Buffett hinted at the 2024 annual meeting that tax
considerations played a role -- anticipating potential capital gains
tax increases -- but the reduction also addressed concentration
risk. The Apple trajectory illustrates a capital allocation
principle: even a conviction holding must be managed relative to
portfolio weight, tax timing, and the opportunity cost of holding
a position that has grown to dominate the portfolio.

### The Succession Architecture

Berkshire's succession plan was built over years, not announced in a
day. Abel joined Berkshire in 2000 when the company acquired a
controlling stake in MidAmerican Energy (later renamed Berkshire
Hathaway Energy), where he eventually became CEO in 2008. He was
appointed to Berkshire's board in 2018 and given responsibility for
all non-insurance operations. Munger's 2021 disclosure identified him
as the heir apparent. Buffett's May 2025 announcement formalized the
transition: Abel would become CEO on January 1, 2026, while Buffett
would remain chairman. The architecture separates the CEO role
(capital deployment, operations) from the chairman role (culture,
governance), and it gave Abel years of on-the-job training before
formal authority transferred. Buffett told shareholders that Abel's
hands-on managerial style was "working way better" than his own
because Abel worked harder at operations than Buffett wanted to.
The board's decision was unanimous.

### Cash as Strategic Optionality

Berkshire's cash pile grew from roughly $90 billion at the start of
the decade to over $370 billion by the end of 2025. This is not
hoarding; it is the deliberate accumulation of optionality.
Buffett's 2024 letter was explicit: the "great majority" of
investor money remains in equities, and the cash is waiting for an
opportunity that meets Berkshire's criteria. Abel's 2025 letter
called the cash "strategic ammunition" and stated that Berkshire's
purpose is to "own equity in businesses with enduring earning power,
not simply to hold U.S. Treasuries." The willingness to hold
extraordinary cash balances while equity markets rise is a test of
discipline that most institutional investors fail, because the
opportunity cost of non-invested capital is visible every quarter
while the cost of a bad acquisition is amortized over years. The
decade's reports document Berkshire passing that test repeatedly,
year after year, regardless of the criticism that accumulated cash
is unproductive. Buffett's response was consistent: the cost of a
bad deal is permanent, while the cost of patience is temporary.
Cash earns Treasury yields while waiting; a bad acquisition destroys
capital forever. The asymmetry favors patience, and the reports
show a management team that understood this and acted on it.

## The Transition: From Buffett to Abel

The 2025 annual letter, released February 28, 2026, was the first
authored by Greg Abel. It confirmed that 95-year-old Buffett remained
chairman and was "at his post five days a week." Abel signaled
stability: the no-dividend policy was unchanged (retained earnings
must generate more than a dollar of market value per dollar retained),
the concentrated holdings in core U.S. equities and Japan's five
trading houses would continue, and Abel would personally manage the
nearly $300 billion equity portfolio.

The tone shifted. Abel was more operationally specific and more
direct about performance gaps. He called out BNSF Railway, noting
that its operating ratio had improved to 65.5 percent in 2025 but
that "the gap with industry leaders remains significant." He warned
that if substantial improvement was not achieved in coming years,
"we will be deeply disappointed." He was candid about Pilot Travel
Centers' disappointing earnings. He addressed Berkshire Hathaway
Energy and the surging power demand from AI-driven data centers with
an emphasis on commercial discipline.

The transition raises a structural question that the decade's
reports only begin to answer: can a single individual replicate the
judgment that Buffett and Munger applied across insurance, equity
selection, and acquisition decisions? Abel's letter suggested an
approach of delegation within a framework: he stated the principles
(value, patience, long-term holding, concentration) and then held
subsidiary managers accountable for operational execution within
those principles. Whether this produces the same quality of capital
allocation decisions as Buffett's individual judgment is the
central question for the next decade.

## Evidence

### Precision Castparts: The $11 Billion Admission

Berkshire closed its acquisition of Precision Castparts Corp. on
January 29, 2016, paying $235 per share in cash -- approximately
$32.7 billion in equity value and roughly $37 billion including
assumed debt. It was the largest check Berkshire had ever written.
Buffett conceded at the time that the price was "right there at
the top," reflecting a 21 percent premium and roughly 19 times
expected earnings. PCC manufactures large metal components for
aircraft, electrical generators, and heavy industry.

By the 2020 annual letter, Buffett acknowledged an $11 billion
write-down, almost entirely attributable to the PCC acquisition.
His language was unusually direct: "I paid too much for the
company. No one misled me in any way -- I was simply too optimistic
about PCC's normalized profit potential." The aerospace downturn --
Boeing's 737 MAX was grounded for 19 months after two fatal crashes
-- exposed the overpayment. Buffett wrote: "PCC is far from my
first error of that sort. But it's a big one."

The admission matters as evidence because it demonstrates that
Berkshire's capital allocation process includes a feedback loop:
mistakes are identified, quantified, and disclosed. The write-down
did not trigger a sale of the business. PCC's order book recovered
as aerospace demand returned, and by 2025 the subsidiary was
generating stronger operating cash flow. Whether Buffett was wrong
or merely early remains debated, but the evidence of the 2020
letter is clear: the process worked as designed, and shareholders
were told the truth.

### The Buyback Decade: $78 Billion in Repurchases

Berkshire repurchased zero shares from 2012 through mid-2018. After
the July 2018 policy change, buybacks scaled rapidly. Annual
repurchase totals (approximate, from quarterly filings):

| Year | Approximate Buybacks |
|------|---------------------|
| 2018 | $1.3 billion |
| 2019 | $5.0 billion |
| 2020 | $24.7 billion |
| 2021 | $27.1 billion |
| 2022 | $7.9 billion |
| 2023 | $9.2 billion |
| 2024 | $2.9 billion (all in H1) |
| 2025 | $0 |

The peak years of 2020-2021 coincided with pandemic-driven market
dislocation, when Berkshire's stock price fell below the level
Buffett and Munger judged to be intrinsic value. The cessation in
Q3 2024 -- buybacks remained at zero through all of 2025 --
indicated that the stock no longer traded at a discount to
intrinsic value in their assessment. This is the discipline working
in both directions: buying aggressively when the price is right,
and refusing to buy when it is not.

### Apple: The Build and the Trim

Berkshire began buying Apple in 2016 with about $1 billion, growing
the position to approximately $40 billion in cost basis. By the end
of 2023, the position exceeded $150 billion in market value,
comprising roughly half of the equity portfolio. In Q1 2024,
Berkshire sold approximately 115 million shares (about 13 percent
of the position). In Q2 2024, it sold nearly half the remaining
stake -- over 515 million shares total between Q3 2023 and Q2 2024.
The position's market value fell from $174.3 billion (end of 2023)
to $84.2 billion (end of Q2 2024). Buffett hinted at tax
considerations (anticipating potential capital gains tax increases)
and portfolio concentration management. After pausing sales in
early 2025, Berkshire resumed trimming with another 20 million
shares in Q2 2025. The Apple trajectory demonstrates that even
Buffett's highest-conviction holding is subject to position-sizing
discipline and tax-timing considerations.

### Occidental Petroleum: Strategic Financing Then Common Accumulation

In April 2019, Berkshire committed $10 billion in preferred stock
to help Occidental Petroleum finance its $38 billion acquisition of
Anadarko Petroleum. The terms: 100,000 shares of cumulative
perpetual preferred stock with a $100,000 liquidation value per
share, paying an 8 percent annual dividend, plus warrants to
purchase up to 80 million shares of Occidental common stock at
$62.50 per share. This echoed Berkshire's 2008 crisis-era
financings of Goldman Sachs and General Electric: the ability to
deploy $10 billion immediately, without conditions, at a moment of
strategic urgency for the counterparty, enabled terms unavailable
to institutional investors who could not write checks at that speed
and scale. The preferred dividend alone generates $800 million per
year regardless of oil price movements.

Beginning in February 2022, Berkshire started accumulating Occidental
common stock in the open market. By August 2022, Berkshire held over
188 million shares (over 20 percent of the company), and purchases
continued through 2023. Berkshire eventually received regulatory
approval to own up to 50 percent of Occidental. Buffett cited CEO
Vicki Hollub's disciplined capital allocation, the low-cost Permian
Basin assets, and Occidental's Direct Air Capture technology. The
two-phase approach -- preferred financing first, common
accumulation later -- illustrates a capital allocation pattern:
use a crisis-era financing to establish a position with favorable
terms, then build the common stake as conviction deepens.

### Japan Trading Houses: A Bet Beyond the United States

On August 30, 2020 -- Buffett's 90th birthday -- Berkshire
announced it had acquired slightly more than 5 percent stakes in
each of Japan's five largest trading companies (sogo shosha):
Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo. The positions,
held through National Indemnity Company, were acquired over
approximately twelve months through regular purchases on the Tokyo
Stock Exchange. At the time, a 5 percent stake in each was valued
at roughly $6.25 billion. Berkshire pledged to hold long-term and
to cap ownership at 9.9 percent unless approved by the investee
companies' boards. Berkshire had 625.5 billion yen in
yen-denominated bonds outstanding, largely matching the currency
exposure of the investments. The 2024 letter confirmed continued
commitment to the Japan positions, and Abel's 2025 letter
reaffirmed the strategy. This was Berkshire's first significant
non-U.S. equity investment and represented a diversification of
the equity portfolio beyond North America.

### The 2024 Cash Record and the 2025 Transition

At the end of 2024, Berkshire held a record $321.4 billion in cash
and Treasury bills. Operating earnings reached $47.4 billion. The
company paid $26.8 billion in federal income tax -- approximately
5 percent of all corporate income tax collected by the U.S.
government that year, and more than any single company had ever
paid. Buffett used the tax figure to make a broader point about
the compounding miracle: Berkshire shareholders had received only
one cash dividend in 60 years (10 cents per Class A share on
January 3, 1967), and the continuous reinvestment of earnings
enabled the company to build the taxable income that produced the
record payment.

The 2025 letter, Abel's first, reported operating earnings of
$44.5 billion (down from $47.4 billion), insurance float of $176
billion, and cash exceeding $370 billion. Abel stated the capital
allocation principles in Buffett's language: "We will evaluate
value carefully, remain patient, and hold for the long term --
preferably forever." The 2025 Q4 operating profit fell 29 percent
year-over-year, driven by a 54 percent decline in insurance
underwriting profit, producing the largest single-day stock drop
since the succession announcement. The data shows that the
operational challenges Abel inherited -- BNSF's margin gap,
Pilot's thin profitability, insurance cycle pressure -- are real
and will test whether the capital allocation framework alone can
compensate.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocators

The decade's reports reinforce a principle that is simple to state
and difficult to execute: capital allocation is the CEO's most
important job, and it requires a framework that survives both market
euphoria and market panic. Berkshire's record demonstrates three
behaviors that define the discipline. First, the willingness to hold
extraordinary cash balances while markets rise -- passing up
mediocre deals to preserve firepower for rare, high-quality
opportunities. Second, the willingness to buy aggressively when the
price is right, as the $24-27 billion buyback years of 2020-2021
demonstrate. Third, the willingness to admit mistakes publicly and
quantify their cost, as the Precision Castparts write-down shows.
For any capital allocator, the Berkshire decade is a case study in
the asymmetry between the cost of a bad decision (amortized over
years) and the cost of patience (visible every quarter as
opportunity cost). The reports document a management team that
consistently chose the invisible cost over the visible one.

### For Succession Planning in Founder-Led Organizations

Berkshire's succession was not a single event but a multi-year
architecture. Abel was given operating responsibility in 2018, was
identified as heir apparent in 2021, was formally designated in
2025, and took the CEO role in 2026. The board discussed succession
at every meeting. The transition separated the CEO role (operational
and capital deployment authority) from the chairman role (culture and
governance), with Buffett remaining as chairman. For any organization
facing founder succession, the Berkshire model offers three lessons:
start the transition years before it is needed, give the successor
real authority before the formal handover, and preserve the founder's
cultural role after operational authority transfers. The risk is
that the successor inherits a system that depended on the founder's
individual judgment -- a quality that cannot be fully replicated by
process. Abel's 2025 letter suggested an approach of principle-based
delegation: state the capital allocation framework, then hold
subsidiary managers accountable within it. Whether this produces
equivalent results is the open question.

### For Value Investing Philosophy

The decade tested value investing principles against an unusual
environment: a sustained bull market, near-zero interest rates (until
2022), and the dominance of technology companies. Berkshire's
response was to invest in Apple -- a technology company reframed as
a consumer ecosystem with high switching costs and recurring
revenue -- rather than to avoid technology entirely. This was not
abandoning the circle of competence but expanding it, as Buffett
recognized that Apple's user lock-in and pricing power made it
analyzable through the same framework he applied to Coca-Cola and
American Express. The Occidental investment extended the framework
to energy: Buffett's thesis was not a macro oil-price prediction but
an assessment of low-cost assets, disciplined management, and the
preferred-stock structure's asymmetric payoff. The Japan investment
extended the framework geographically. The decade's reports show
value investing not as a static set of prohibitions (no technology,
no energy, no foreign markets) but as a flexible method applied with
discipline: buy outstanding businesses at sensible prices, hold them
for the long term, and manage concentration relative to portfolio
risk.

### For Corporate Governance and Shareholder Communication

Buffett's reporting philosophy -- that a "report" implies a greater
responsibility than mandated data, and that shareholders are owed
honest commentary about what they own -- is a governance standard
that most public companies fail to meet. The 2020 Precision Castparts
admission, the 2024 tax disclosure, and the candor about operational
shortcomings in Abel's 2025 letter all demonstrate that Berkshire
treats its shareholders as owners entitled to the truth, including
unflattering truths about management errors. The implication for
corporate governance is that radical candor does not destroy
shareholder trust; it builds it. Buffett wrote that if you "start
fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney
and be fooling yourself as well." The decade's reports are evidence
that a culture of candor can survive a leadership transition: Abel's
first letter maintained the standard, calling out BNSF's margin gap
and Pilot's weak performance directly rather than burying them in
footnotes. For boards and CEOs, the Berkshire decade demonstrates
that honest reporting is not just an ethical choice but a strategic
one: it attracts long-term shareholders who price the business on
fundamentals rather than narrative, and it creates the trust
reservoir needed to weather difficult periods.

### For Tax-Aware Capital Allocation

The decade's reports illustrate that tax considerations are not a
secondary factor in capital allocation but a primary input that
shapes timing and sizing decisions. Buffett's 2024 explanation of
the Apple trim cited anticipated capital gains tax increases as a
rationale for taking profits while rates were known. The 2024
letter's $26.8 billion federal tax payment -- more than any single
company had ever paid -- was used not as a boast but as a teaching
device: it demonstrated that the 60-year reinvestment policy (one
dividend in 1967) enabled the compounding that built the taxable
income base. For capital allocators, the implication is that tax
efficiency is not about avoidance but about timing: realizing gains
in low-rate environments, deferring them in high-rate environments,
and structuring financings (as with the Occidental preferred stock)
to generate returns in forms that are tax-advantaged. The 2017 Tax
Cuts and Jobs Act produced a one-time $29 billion deferred-tax
benefit for Berkshire, an accounting event that Buffett treated as
non-economic but that increased book value. The decade shows tax
policy as a variable that competent capital allocators monitor and
respond to, not one they ignore.

## Common Pitfalls

### Treating the Cash Pile as a Signal Rather Than a Result

The most common misreading of Berkshire's decade is to interpret the
growing cash pile as a market forecast. Buffett and Abel both
explicitly rejected this framing. The cash is the residual of a
disciplined process: it grows when opportunities do not meet
Berkshire's criteria and shrinks when they do. Treating the cash
level as a sentiment indicator -- "Buffett is bearish" or "Abel is
cautious" -- inverts cause and effect. The cash is high because
prices are high relative to Berkshire's assessment of intrinsic
value, not because management holds a macroeconomic view.

### Confusing the Apple Trim With a Thesis Change

Berkshire's reduction of its Apple position by roughly 56 percent
between 2023 and 2024 prompted speculation that Buffett had lost
confidence in Apple. The evidence suggests otherwise: Buffett
cited tax considerations and concentration management, and Apple
remained Berkshire's largest single position after the trim. The
reduction illustrates position sizing, not thesis abandonment.
Investors who interpreted the sale as a negative signal on Apple's
business quality misread the capital allocation logic.

### Expecting Continuity Without Testing It

The transition from Buffett to Abel is the most analyzed succession
in corporate history, but it remains untested at scale. Abel's first
letter demonstrated command of the reporting philosophy and the
capital allocation framework, but he has not yet faced the defining
test: a major market dislocation requiring large-scale capital
deployment, or a crisis requiring a difficult acquisition decision.
The framework is sound; whether Abel's judgment under pressure
matches Buffett's remains an open question that only time and
adversity will answer.
