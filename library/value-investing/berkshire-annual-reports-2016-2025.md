# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 2016-2025 -- A Decade That Tested Capital Allocation Discipline and Completed a Succession

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 2016 through 2025 document a
decade in which the company closed the $37.2 billion Precision
Castparts acquisition,
recorded an $11 billion write-down tied chiefly to that purchase,
spent approximately $77.9 billion by the author's calculation
repurchasing its own shares, built
and then sharply reduced its largest equity position, and completed
the transfer of the chief executive role from Warren Buffett to Greg
Abel. The author's synthesis is that the ten reports show how
Berkshire applied its capital-allocation framework through a pandemic,
large market moves, and the death of Charlie Munger, while continuing
to report management errors directly to owners. The report period ends in 2025;
post-report events through September 20, 2026 are identified as
updates rather than treated as part of fiscal 2025.

## Background

Berkshire Hathaway's annual report combines audited financial
statements and regulatory disclosure with a shareholder letter about
ownership, capital allocation, and management judgment. Warren Buffett
used that letter throughout his tenure to explain decisions and errors
to owners. In the 2024 letter, he wrote that the word "report" implies
a responsibility beyond mandated data and that shareholders should
receive commentary about what they own and how management thinks. He
also criticized organizations in which "mistake" and "wrong" become
taboo words, arguing that such behavior can encourage managerial
self-deception.

The author's scope treats 2016-2025 as one analytical period. It opens
with the January 29, 2016 closing of Precision Castparts Corp. (PCC)
at approximately $37.2 billion including net debt, and it closes with
Greg Abel's first shareholder letter as CEO, released February 28,
2026, covering fiscal
year 2025. Within this window, Berkshire navigated the Tax Cuts and
Jobs Act of 2017 (which produced a one-time $29.1 billion net benefit
to earnings, driven primarily by a $29.6 billion reduction in net
deferred-tax liabilities), the COVID-19 disruption of 2020, the death
of Charlie Munger on November 28, 2023 at age 99, and Buffett's
announcement on May 3, 2025 that he would recommend Greg Abel as his
successor effective January 1, 2026.

The decade also brackets a change in Berkshire's repurchase framework.
The board authorized a program in September 2011 at no more than 110
percent of book value and raised the limit to 120 percent in December
2012. On July 17, 2018, it removed the book-value ceiling and permitted
repurchases whenever Buffett and Munger believed the price was below
conservatively determined intrinsic value, provided liquidity remained
ample. Berkshire then spent approximately $77.9 billion on repurchases
from 2018 through 2024. The year-end Class A-equivalent share count
declined from about 1.644 million in 2016 to 1.438 million in 2025, a
12.5 percent reduction by the author's calculation. Repurchases stopped
after the second quarter
of 2024 and remained at zero through 2025. They resumed with about
$235 million in the first quarter of 2026 and reached approximately
$4.8 billion for the first half of 2026.

Insurance float grew from approximately $91.6 billion at the end of
2016 to $176 billion by the
end of 2025. Buffett described float as a "revolving fund" rather
than a typical liability: as old claims are paid, new premiums flow
in to replace them. The aggregate can decline, however, and its cost
depends on underwriting results. Over 2016-2025, float increased about
92 percent by the author's calculation. It was not cost-free in every
year, but profitable
underwriting in many years allowed Berkshire to be paid while holding
funds that remained available for investment.

Operating earnings -- Berkshire's non-GAAP measure, which excludes
investment gains and losses and separately identified impairment
charges -- grew from $17.6 billion in 2016 to
$47.4 billion in 2024, then slipped to $44.5 billion in 2025. Per
share, repurchases increased each remaining share's participation in
the businesses. Compounding the annual changes in Berkshire's
per-share market value reported for 2016 through 2025 produces, by the
author's calculation, a 3.82 times cumulative multiple, equivalent to
approximately 14.3 percent a year from year-end 2015 through year-end
2025.

The reports also changed the performance lens during the period. In
2018, Buffett abandoned the long-standing practice of leading with
per-share book value. He gave three reasons: operating businesses had
become more important, those businesses were carried below his estimate
of current value, and repurchases above book value but below intrinsic
value could reduce per-share book value while increasing per-share
intrinsic value. Berkshire then emphasized long-run per-share market
value while warning that annual market prices and GAAP investment gains
can be volatile. This topic therefore uses the change as context for
its later buyback and performance analysis.

Berkshire had discussed management succession publicly for years. In
2018, the board appointed Greg Abel and Ajit Jain as vice chairmen,
with Abel
responsible for all non-insurance operations. In 2021, Charlie Munger
inadvertently revealed that Abel was the designated successor at a
shareholder meeting. Buffett later confirmed the board's choice. When
Buffett announced on May 3, 2025 that he would recommend Abel, the
formal appointment remained a board decision. On May 4 the board voted
unanimously to
appoint Abel president and CEO effective January 1, 2026, with Buffett
remaining chairman at that time.

## Core Concepts

### The Annual Letter as a Capital Allocation Report

The author's reading is that Buffett's letters function as capital
allocation reports written by the person making the decisions. They
explain what Berkshire did with capital and often why, what worked,
and what failed. This disclosure gives shareholders evidence with
which to assess results and the decision process. When Buffett admitted
in the 2020 letter that he "paid too much" for Precision Castparts, he wrote that
he was "simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit
potential" and that the $11 billion write-down was "almost entirely
the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016." The author's
assessment is that this pairing of decision rationale with quantified
error turns the letter into an accountability record, rather than a
summary of favorable outcomes alone.

For this topic, the letter and audited statements are read together.
"Operating earnings" is a management measure that, in the 2025
presentation, removes investment gains and losses and separately
identified impairments from annual business performance, whereas GAAP net
earnings includes realized and unrealized changes that can be large.
The operating measure helps compare subsidiaries, but the excluded
gains and losses still affect shareholder wealth over time. This
method reconciles the letter's explanation with the Form
10-K definitions, balance sheet, cash-flow statement, notes, and share
count rather than treating either presentation as sufficient alone.

### Insurance Float as Long-Duration Capital with a Variable Cost

Float approximates the net policyholder funds that Berkshire's
insurance subsidiaries hold and invest before claims are paid.
Buffett called it a "revolving fund" rather than a typical liability;
it remains an accounting liability. Individual claims are paid while
new premiums and reserves replace some of the outflow. The aggregate
is not legally permanent, and Berkshire's reports explicitly allow
for a gradual decline. The
economic cost of float depends on underwriting results. In profitable
underwriting years, Berkshire receives investment capital while also
earning an underwriting gain; sustained underwriting losses can make
that capital costly. Berkshire measures that cost using underwriting
results relative to average float.
Float grew from $91.6 billion (end of 2016) to $171 billion (end of
2024) to $176 billion (end of 2025), roughly doubling over the decade.
Even the strong 2024 result -- $9.02 billion of after-tax underwriting
earnings across all insurance operations -- came after absorbing
approximately $1.2 billion of after-tax estimated claims from
Hurricanes Helene and Milton.

### The Buyback Decision Framework

Berkshire announced a repurchase program in September 2011 at prices
no higher than 110 percent of book value and raised that ceiling to
120 percent in December 2012. In July 2018, the board eliminated the
book-value threshold and replaced it with a judgment-based test:
Buffett and Munger had to conclude that the price was below
"conservatively determined" intrinsic value, and repurchases could
not impair liquidity. Berkshire's 2025 filing states the current form
of the test: the CEO, after consulting the chairman, must believe the
price is below conservatively determined intrinsic value, and a
repurchase cannot reduce consolidated cash, cash equivalents, and
U.S. Treasury bills below $30 billion. The program has no required
minimum, maximum, or expiration date.

Repurchases totaled approximately $77.9 billion over 2018-2024 by the
author's calculation from reported cash outlays, with
peak spending of $24.7 billion in 2020 and $27.1 billion in 2021.
They stopped after the second quarter of 2024 and remained at zero in
2025. The absence of purchases is consistent with the policy but does
not, by itself, prove a specific valuation judgment; liquidity needs,
other opportunities, and management discretion also remain relevant.
The first half of 2026 provides the next observation: Berkshire
repurchased approximately $4.8 billion, including about $4.5 billion
in the second quarter.

### Elephant Hunting and Acquisition Discipline

Buffett described Berkshire as hoping for an "elephant-sized
acquisition." The decade produced several major capital commitments:
the acquisition of Precision Castparts ($37.2 billion including net
debt, closed 2016), the Pilot
Travel Centers staged acquisition (38.6 percent in 2017, another 41.4
percent for approximately $8.2 billion in 2023, and the remaining 20
percent for $2.6 billion in 2024), and the Occidental Petroleum
financing and investment ($10 billion of preferred stock in 2019,
followed by common stock purchases beginning in 2022). Berkshire
acquired Bell Laboratories on July 31, 2025 and agreed on October 1,
2025 to acquire OxyChem. OxyChem closed on January 2, 2026 at an
announced value of $9.7 billion subject to adjustment; the later annual
report said approximately $9.5 billion was paid, also subject to
adjustment. The author's synthesis is that the reports present
acquisition discipline as both action and refusal:
Berkshire says it responds quickly to opportunities that satisfy its
criteria and rejects those that do not. Its large Treasury position is
therefore available both for insurance resilience and for investments,
not evidence that management has abandoned productive assets.

### Concentration, Position Sizing, and the Apple Trajectory

Berkshire began buying Apple in late 2016. By mid-2018 its general
account owned slightly more than one billion split-adjusted shares at
a cost of $36 billion. After a small 2020 sale, Apple repurchases had
increased Berkshire's ownership percentage to 5.4 percent even though
its share count was lower. At year-end 2023, the SEC-reported holdings
totaled 905.56 million shares worth $174.35 billion. They fell to 400
million shares at June 30, 2024, 300 million at year-end 2024, and
227.92 million at year-end 2025; the 2025 annual report valued the
remaining position at $61.96 billion. Buffett discussed the federal
tax rate when asked about the early-2024 sale, but Berkshire did not
publish a complete causal explanation for the full reduction. The author's
analytical rule is therefore narrow: a large, long-held position can
still be reduced, and share count, ownership percentage, cost basis,
and market value should be evaluated separately.

### The Succession Architecture

Berkshire's succession plan was built over years, not announced in a
day. Abel joined CalEnergy in 1992 and became a Berkshire manager when
Berkshire acquired control of MidAmerican Energy Holdings in 2000.
The business was later renamed Berkshire Hathaway Energy. He was
appointed to Berkshire's board in 2018 and given responsibility for
all non-insurance operations. Munger's 2021 disclosure identified him
as the heir apparent. Buffett's May 2025 announcement formalized the
transition: Abel would become CEO on January 1, 2026, while Buffett
would remain chairman. The board's decision was unanimous. On
September 18, 2026, Berkshire named Buffett Chairman Emeritus while he
remained a director and elected Howard G. Buffett chairman. Greg Abel
therefore holds the CEO role, Howard Buffett holds the chair, and
Warren Buffett remains a director and adviser. The author's assessment
is that the sequence gave Abel years of operating responsibility before
formal authority transferred and ultimately separated executive
management from board leadership.

### Cash as Strategic Optionality

Berkshire's consolidated liquidity grew substantially during the
decade. Its 2016 balance sheet reported $70.9 billion of cash, cash
equivalents, and U.S. Treasury bills in the insurance-and-other group,
while Buffett described a broader $86 billion of "cash and
equivalents." At year-end 2024, insurance and other businesses held
$318.0 billion of cash, equivalents, and Treasury bills net of
payables for unsettled purchases. For year-end 2025, the analogous
net figure was $369.0 billion, while Abel's letter described total
cash and U.S. Treasury holdings as exceeding $370 billion.

The reports attach two functions to this liquidity. Some capital
supports insurance obligations and extreme-loss scenarios; the rest
allows Berkshire to act without financing contingencies when suitable
opportunities appear. Abel wrote that Berkshire would "always aim for
ownership of productive businesses over U.S. Treasuries" and rejected
the interpretation that cash signaled a retreat from investing. The
author's assessment is that liquidity should be evaluated as an input
to resilience and optionality, not as a stand-alone forecast of market
direction.

## The Transition: From Buffett to Abel

The 2025 annual letter, released February 28, 2026, was the first
authored by Greg Abel. At publication, Buffett remained chairman and
was described as being in the office five days a week. Abel retained
the policy of paying no cash dividend while each dollar of retained
earnings is reasonably likely to create more than one dollar of
market value. He also said that Berkshire's concentrated U.S. equity
holdings and its positions in the five Japanese trading houses would
remain important, while allowing that an individual holding could be
adjusted if its long-term economics changed.

Abel's letter included detailed operating discussion of BNSF, Pilot,
insurance, BHE, and major investments. BNSF's operating margin improved
from 32.0 percent in 2024 to
34.5 percent in 2025, but he wrote that its gap to the industry's best
remained too wide. At Pilot, he reported improved operating cash flow
and called Berkshire's inability to manage the business before 2023 a
mistake that would not be repeated. He also tied capital allocation at
Berkshire Hathaway Energy to the regulatory compact: investment would
depend on utilities being able to earn reasonable returns while
serving rising demand.

The governance arrangement continued to evolve after the report. On
September 18, 2026, Warren Buffett became Chairman Emeritus, remained a
director, and Howard G. Buffett became chairman. Greg Abel remained
CEO. The first half of 2026 also supplied early allocation evidence:
Berkshire completed the previously agreed OxyChem purchase,
repurchased approximately $4.8 billion of its shares, and agreed to
acquire Taylor Morrison for approximately $6.8 billion of equity value.
The Taylor Morrison acquisition subsequently closed on July 24, 2026.
The author's
assessment is that these actions demonstrate deployment under Abel,
but they do not yet establish how the new structure will perform
across a full market or insurance cycle.

## Evidence

### Precision Castparts: The $11 Billion Admission

Berkshire closed its acquisition of Precision Castparts Corp. on
January 29, 2016, paying $235 per share in cash -- approximately
$37.2 billion including outstanding net debt. PCC manufactures
complex metal components for aerospace, power, and general industrial
markets. In the author's assessment, its size made the transaction a
consequential test of Berkshire's acquisition discipline.

By the 2020 annual letter, Buffett acknowledged an $11 billion
write-down, almost entirely attributable to the PCC acquisition.
His language was direct: "I paid too much for the company. No one
misled me in any way -- I was simply too optimistic about PCC's
normalized profit potential." He also wrote, "PCC is far from my
first error of that sort. But it's a big one." Berkshire's 2020
report connected the write-down both to Buffett's original
overestimation and to adverse developments across aerospace during
the pandemic; it did not attribute the full error to one aircraft
program or one external shock.

In the author's reading, the PCC case shows one instance in which
Berkshire identified, quantified, and disclosed an allocation error without forcing an
immediate sale. Berkshire retained PCC. The 2025 report said air
travel, aircraft orders, and component demand had recovered, and that
PCC generated $2.4 billion of operating cash flow in 2025, compared
with a $0.9 billion average in 2021-2022 and $1.7 billion in 2015. The
recovery does not reverse the admission of overpayment; it separates
the continuing economics of the business from the price Berkshire
originally paid.

### The Buyback Decade: $78 Billion in Repurchases

Berkshire made a $1.2 billion negotiated repurchase in December 2012,
but did not repurchase shares during 2013-2017. After the July 2018
policy change, annual repurchase totals were as follows (rounded from
Berkshire's annual reports and earnings releases):

| Year | Approximate Buybacks |
|------|---------------------|
| 2018 | $1.3 billion |
| 2019 | $4.9 billion |
| 2020 | $24.7 billion |
| 2021 | $27.1 billion |
| 2022 | $7.9 billion |
| 2023 | $9.2 billion |
| 2024 | $2.9 billion (all in H1) |
| 2025 | $0 |

The 2020-2021 purchases required Buffett and Munger to determine that
the shares were below conservatively estimated intrinsic value and
that liquidity would remain ample. The cessation after the second
quarter of 2024 -- followed by no repurchases in 2025 -- is observable,
but the filings do not assign one exclusive reason to it. Repurchases
resumed under Abel: approximately $235 million in the first quarter of
2026 and $4.8 billion for the first six months. This topic therefore
distinguishes the stated decision rule from any inferred valuation
signal.

### Apple: The Build and the Trim

Berkshire began buying Apple late in 2016 and had invested $36 billion
in slightly more than one billion split-adjusted shares by mid-2018.
At December 31, 2023, its Form 13F holdings totaled 905.56 million
shares with a reported value of $174.35 billion. The corresponding
SEC holdings declined to 400 million shares worth $84.25 billion at
June 30, 2024; 300 million shares worth $75.13 billion at year-end
2024; and 227.92 million shares worth $61.96 billion at year-end 2025.

These figures distinguish two measurements. First, the
market-value decline was not solely a price movement: Berkshire sold
505.56 million shares between year-end 2023 and mid-2024, then another
100 million by year-end. Second, the original $36 billion cost and the
subsequent position-level cost basis are not interchangeable because
Berkshire realized gains as it sold shares. At the 2024 annual meeting,
Buffett discussed the possibility of higher federal tax rates while
explaining the first-quarter sale. That supports tax timing as one
consideration, but the public record does not establish a complete
reason for every later sale. Concentration management is therefore an
interpretation, not a disclosed fact.

### Occidental Petroleum: Strategic Financing Then Common Accumulation

In April 2019, Berkshire committed $10 billion in preferred stock
to help Occidental Petroleum finance its $38 billion acquisition of
Anadarko Petroleum. The terms: 100,000 shares of cumulative
perpetual preferred stock with a $100,000 liquidation value per
share, paying an 8 percent annual dividend, plus warrants to
purchase up to 80 million shares of Occidental common stock at
$62.50 per share. By year-end 2025, redemptions had reduced the
preferred liquidation value to approximately $8.5 billion. Adjustment
provisions had changed the warrants to 83.9 million shares at $59.59,
and the preferred still accrued dividends at 8 percent, implying by
the author's calculation approximately $680 million a year on the
remaining liquidation value.

During 2022, Berkshire accumulated Occidental common stock; its voting
interest exceeded 20 percent on August 4. Purchases continued through
2023. Berkshire eventually received regulatory
approval to own up to 50 percent of Occidental. Buffett cited CEO
Vicki Hollub, Occidental's U.S. oil and gas holdings, and its work on
carbon capture, while explicitly noting that the technology's economic
feasibility had not been proven. Berkshire owned 26.9 percent of the
common stock at year-end 2025, excluding the warrants. The 2025 report
also recorded a pre-tax impairment of approximately $5.7 billion on
that common-stock investment. The author's assessment is that the
sequence is not evidence of a riskless financing: it combined an
income-producing preferred,
warrants, a later common-stock accumulation, and material market-value
risk.

### Japan Trading Houses: A Bet Beyond the United States

On August 31, 2020 in Japan, Berkshire announced that it had acquired
slightly more than 5 percent stakes in
each of five leading Japanese trading companies (sogo shosha):
Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo. The positions,
held through National Indemnity Company, were acquired over
approximately twelve months through regular purchases on the Tokyo
Stock Exchange. Berkshire initially said it intended to hold for the
long term and would not exceed 9.9 percent ownership without board
approval from the investee company. The 2024 letter reported that all
five companies had agreed to moderately relax that ceiling.

At year-end 2025, Berkshire owned 9.7 percent to 10.8 percent of each
company. Aggregate cost was $15.38 billion, market value was $35.37
billion, and 2025 dividends were $862 million. Berkshire reported yen
borrowings roughly equivalent to the investments' cost basis, at an
average cost of 1.2 percent and a weighted-average life of about 5.75
years. The author's assessment is that this structure reduces, but
does not eliminate, currency and business risk; matching investment
cost with yen debt is not the same as hedging the full market value or
future earnings.

### The 2024 Cash Record and the 2025 Transition

At the end of 2024, Berkshire's insurance and other businesses held
$318.0 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and Treasury bills net of
payables for unsettled purchases. Operating earnings reached $47.4
billion. The
company paid $26.8 billion in federal income tax. Buffett wrote that
this was about 5 percent of all U.S. corporate income tax receipts and
more than the government had previously received from any one company.
He used the tax figure to make a broader point about
the compounding miracle: Berkshire shareholders had received only
one cash dividend in 60 years (10 cents per Class A share on
January 3, 1967), and the continuous reinvestment of earnings
enabled the company to build the taxable income that produced the
record payment.

The 2025 letter, Abel's first, reported operating earnings of $44.5
billion, down from $47.4 billion; insurance float of $176 billion;
and cash and U.S. Treasury holdings exceeding $370 billion. It also
reported $46 billion of operating cash flow, no share repurchases,
and a $297.8 billion equity-securities portfolio at year-end. Abel
stated the governing allocation rule as: "We will assess value
carefully, act patiently, and hold for the long term -- preferably
forever." The author's assessment is that the evidence is mixed rather
than uniformly favorable:
insurance and non-insurance businesses continued to generate large
cash flows, while BNSF still had a margin gap and Berkshire recorded
large impairments on Kraft Heinz and Occidental. Those facts define
the baseline against which the post-succession record can be judged.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocators

The author's synthesis is that Berkshire's record supports a
decision framework rather than a rule to remain permanently invested
or permanently liquid. The same organization held more than $369
billion of cash, equivalents, and Treasury bills at year-end 2025,
spent $51.8 billion on repurchases in 2020-2021, bought entire
businesses, and reduced a highly successful Apple position. These
actions are not contradictory when the governing variables are price,
expected return, risk, liquidity, taxes, and available alternatives.

From that synthesis, three practical controls follow. First, define
repurchase authority before acting: Berkshire's policy requires a price below conservatively
determined intrinsic value and preserves a minimum liquidity floor.
Second, separate the quality of an asset from the price paid. PCC
remained an important business even after Buffett concluded that the
purchase price had been too high. Third, retain an error-correction
record. A quantified write-down, an updated assessment of normalized
earnings, and later operating cash-flow data provide more useful
feedback than either defending the original decision or declaring the
asset worthless. Investors and corporate managers can apply the same
controls even when their opportunity sets and liquidity needs differ
from Berkshire's.

Under the same synthesis, the measurement unit is per share, not
aggregate company size alone. A repurchase can reduce cash and equity while increasing a
continuing owner's proportionate interest; an acquisition can increase
earnings while reducing value per share if the price is excessive.
Berkshire's reports provide the inputs for both tests: allocation
amount, Class A-equivalent share count, operating earnings, cash flow,
and remaining liquidity. Comparing those inputs prevents revenue,
assets, or total earnings growth from being mistaken automatically for
successful allocation.

### For Succession Planning in Founder-Led Organizations

Berkshire's succession was a sequence rather than a single event.
Abel received responsibility for non-insurance operations in 2018,
was publicly identified as the intended successor in 2021, was
appointed in 2025, and became CEO on January 1, 2026. Warren Buffett
remained chairman for the initial transition and then became Chairman
Emeritus in September 2026, when Howard Buffett became chairman.

The author's assessment for boards identifies three testable design
choices. Give the successor operational responsibility before the title changes;
state which decisions remain centralized; and specify the roles that
the former leader and board chair will hold after the transfer. The
2025 report makes the allocation of authority unusually explicit:
subsidiary CEOs retain operating autonomy, while Abel stated that
ultimate responsibility for Berkshire's equity investments resides
with him as CEO. The remaining uncertainty should be stated explicitly.
A documented process cannot demonstrate in advance
that judgments under severe market, insurance, or operational stress
will equal those of the predecessor. The first-half 2026 acquisitions
and repurchases are observations, not a full-cycle verdict.

### For Value Investing Philosophy

The author's value-investing interpretation emphasizes process rather
than sector labels. Berkshire bought Apple, accumulated Occidental
common stock after providing preferred financing, and built positions
in five Japanese trading houses. The disclosed criteria remained
recognizable across these different assets: understandable economics,
management and capital deployment, price relative to expected value,
financial resilience, and a long intended holding period.

Under that interpretation, favorable business descriptions are not a
substitute for valuation or risk analysis. Apple generated a large
unrealized gain, yet Berkshire later reduced the position. The
Occidental preferred produced substantial income, but the common
stock incurred a $5.7 billion pre-tax impairment in 2025. Yen borrowing
partially aligned financing with the Japanese investments' cost, but
did not eliminate operating or currency exposure. PCC recovered
operating cash flow, but that did not negate the original
overpayment. For a value investor, the transferable practice is to
track each component separately: operating performance, financing
terms, purchase price, ownership percentage, taxes, realized proceeds,
and remaining exposure.

### For Corporate Governance and Shareholder Communication

Buffett's stated reporting standard is that the annual report should
explain ownership and management thinking beyond the minimum required
data. The PCC admission applies that standard to a chief executive's
own error. Abel's first letter continued the practice in a different
voice: it quantified BNSF's margin gap, described the delayed control
of Pilot as a mistake, reported portfolio impairments, and identified
conditions under which Berkshire Hathaway Energy would deploy more
capital.

The author's assessment is that boards can convert this style into a
repeatable governance control. Require each major allocation decision
to retain its original assumptions; compare later results with those
assumptions; distinguish external shocks from initial estimation
errors; and report material deviations in plain language. Candor alone
does not prove that a decision process is sound, and shareholder trust
cannot be measured from these letters alone. Its practical value is
narrower: an explicit record makes it harder to rewrite the original
thesis after outcomes are known and gives owners evidence with which
to evaluate management.

### For Tax-Aware Capital Allocation

The author's tax-allocation implication is to include tax without
turning it into a complete explanation.
At the 2024 annual meeting, Buffett discussed the possibility of
higher federal tax rates when asked about the first-quarter Apple
sale. That establishes tax as a consideration, but not as the proven
cause of every Apple sale in 2024 and 2025. The disciplined approach
is to compare after-tax outcomes while preserving uncertainty about
motives that management did not disclose.

The annual reports also show taxes at two different analytical levels.
The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act reduced Berkshire's deferred income-tax
liabilities and produced an approximately $29.1 billion one-time net
benefit to
reported net earnings. In 2024, Berkshire paid $26.8 billion in cash
federal income tax, which Buffett described as about 5 percent of all
U.S. corporate income tax receipts and more than the government had
received from any one company. Deferred-tax remeasurement and cash tax
payment are not interchangeable. The practical implication is to identify whether a
tax figure changes current cash, future obligations, reported earnings,
or the timing of realized gains before treating it as economic value.

## Common Pitfalls

### Treating the Cash Pile as a Signal Rather Than a Result

A common misreading of Berkshire's decade is to interpret the
growing cash balance as a complete market forecast. Abel explicitly
rejected the view that it represented a retreat from investing, and
the reports identify insurance resilience as another use of
liquidity. Cash can also change because of security sales,
acquisitions, operating cash flow, taxes, debt, and repurchases.
Inferring one macroeconomic forecast from the balance alone exceeds
the evidence.

### Confusing the Apple Trim With a Thesis Change

Berkshire reduced its Apple share count from 905.56 million at
year-end 2023 to 300 million at year-end 2024, a 66.9 percent decline
by the author's calculation,
and to 227.92 million at year-end 2025. Buffett discussed taxes in
connection with the first-quarter 2024 sale, and Apple remained one
of Berkshire's largest disclosed holdings. Neither fact proves the
reason for all later sales. This topic therefore reports the disclosed
transactions and management's stated comments, then label any thesis,
valuation, or concentration explanation as interpretation.

### Expecting Continuity Without Testing It

Abel's first letter and the first half of 2026 provide evidence of
continuity, operating emphasis, acquisitions, and resumed repurchases.
They do not provide a full market-cycle comparison with Buffett's
record. Conversely, lack of a long record is not evidence of failure.
The author's proposed test is prospective: record the allocation thesis and
price for each material decision, then compare subsequent per-share
outcomes, liquidity, and operating results without moving the original
criteria.
