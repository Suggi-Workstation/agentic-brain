# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 1986-1995 -- The Decade Franchise Investing Took Over

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 1986 through 1995 document
the decade in which Warren Buffett made the shift from statistical
bargains toward durable, high-return businesses explicit in his
published writing. Read as a sequence, the ten reports cover the
purchase of Coca-Cola, the Capital Cities/ABC investment, the Wells
Fargo purchase during a banking panic, and the definition of owner
earnings; they also contain Buffett's rejection of the "cigar butt"
method, his warnings that strong businesses can still be poor
purchases at the wrong price, and his account of the USAir error.
**Synthesis:** Together, the reports show a broader definition of
value that joins price discipline to business quality, management,
reinvestment, and capital intensity.

## Background

The transition did not happen in a single year. Its key operating
example began in 1972, when Blue Chip Stamps, then an affiliate of
Berkshire and later merged into it, bought See's Candies for $25
million. Buffett's 1983 goodwill appendix described See's as having
about $8 million of net tangible assets and earning about $2 million
after tax, a 25 percent after-tax return on tangible capital. The
1991 letter used a slightly different accounting frame: $7 million
of tangible net worth and $4.2 million of pre-tax profit in 1972.
Both presentations support the same point without mixing their
denominators: See's required little tangible capital relative to its
earning power. By 1991, See's had increased annual pre-tax profit to
$42.4 million while requiring only $18 million of additional net
worth; it had distributed $410 million of cumulative pre-tax profit
to Blue Chip and Berkshire. The 1989 letter then stated the general
lesson: it is better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than
a fair company at a wonderful price, because time helps the former
and erodes the latter.

The 1986 letter, dated February 27, 1987, introduced the term
"owner earnings" in its Scott Fetzer accounting appendix. Owner
earnings equal reported earnings plus depreciation, depletion,
amortization, and certain other non-cash charges, less the average
annual capitalized expenditures required to maintain long-term
competitive position and unit volume. Required incremental working
capital also belongs in the deduction. Buffett emphasized that the
maintenance-capital estimate is necessarily imprecise, but more
useful for valuation than a precise GAAP number that does not match
economic reality. The concept separates businesses that can
distribute cash after maintaining their economics from businesses
that must continually reinvest merely to hold their position.

The 1989 letter, dated March 2, 1990, delivered the
philosophical repudiation of the cigar-butt method in the most
quoted passage of the decade. Buffett described the "cigar butt"
approach: a cigar butt found on the street with only one puff left
may not offer much of a smoke, but the bargain purchase makes that
puff all profit. Unless you are a liquidator, Buffett wrote, that
approach to buying businesses is foolish. First, the original
bargain price probably will not turn out to be such a steal -- in a
difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another
surfaces, and there is never just one cockroach in the kitchen.
Second, any initial advantage is quickly eroded by the low return
the mediocre business earns. Time, Buffett concluded, is the friend
of the wonderful business and the enemy of the mediocre. The
passage was not abstract. It was a confession. Buffett admitted he
had learned the lesson the hard way -- several times over --
including the acquisition of a Baltimore department store,
Hochschild Kohn, bought at a substantial discount to book value
with first-class people and unrecorded real estate values, which
still disappointed. Charlie understood this early, Buffett wrote; I
was a slow learner.

The decade opens with Berkshire already a diversified holding
company. During 1986, it closed the Scott Fetzer and Fechheimer Bros.
acquisitions. By year-end, it owned large equity stakes in Capital
Cities/ABC, GEICO, and the Washington Post, and operated See's
Candies, the Buffalo News, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Kirby, World
Book, and the Scott Fetzer group. The three permanent equity
holdings -- Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, and Washington Post -- had a
combined market value of over $1.7 billion at year-end 1986,
close to 75 percent of Berkshire's total net worth, yet delivered
only about $9 million in annual dividend income. The 1986 letter
said the three companies retained a high percentage of earnings that
Berkshire expected eventually to receive as capital gains. By the
end of 1995, Berkshire had added Coca-Cola and Wells Fargo;
Gillette preferred stock purchased in 1989 and converted in 1991;
American Express Percs purchased in 1991 and later converted to
common; and the controlled businesses Borsheim's, H.H. Brown,
Helzberg Diamonds, and R.C. Willey. **Synthesis:** The portfolio had
tilted toward consumer franchises and selected financial businesses.
The associated analytical framework included owner earnings,
look-through earnings, economic goodwill, and the distinction between
price and value.

**Interpretation:** The letters also functioned as teaching
documents. Buffett explained capital allocation, showed calculations,
and recorded mistakes. The 1990 Wells Fargo stress case, the 1991
look-through earnings table,
and the 1989 cigar-butt discussion were written for shareholders as
owners rather than as short-term traders. The performance discussion
was similarly explicit: the 1989 letter warned that the historical
23.8 percent annual book-value growth could not be projected from a
much larger capital base, and the 1991 and 1994 letters repeated the
scale constraint in progressively stronger terms.

## Core Concepts

### Owner Earnings and the Cash-Flow Fallacy

The 1986 appendix defined owner earnings as reported earnings plus
depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash
charges, less the average annual capitalized expenditures required
to maintain competitive position and unit volume. It also requires
a deduction for additional working capital needed for the same
purpose, subject to Buffett's qualification about LIFO inventory.
The estimate is not mechanically equal to reported cash flow:
Buffett criticized presentations that add back depreciation without
deducting the capital spending a business must make over time. He
also cautioned that the required-capital figure must be estimated.

The analytical use is to distinguish accounting earnings from cash
that can be removed after preserving the business's economics. In
the Scott Fetzer example, Buffett estimated maintenance capital near
the old company's $8.3 million depreciation figure rather than the
$19.9 million depreciation created by purchase accounting. At
See's, capital expenditures exceeded depreciation by $0.5-$1
million annually merely to hold competitive position, an explicit
warning that even a strong franchise has maintenance needs.
**Interpretation:** Owner earnings therefore improve on both
unadjusted GAAP profit and the
shortcut of earnings plus non-cash charges.

### Look-Through Earnings

Look-through earnings address the limitation of GAAP accounting for
a company with large minority equity stakes. Berkshire recorded the
dividends it received from investees, but not its proportional share
of their retained earnings. The 1989 letter named this economic
benefit a "look-through" approach, and the 1990 letter supplied a
company-wide calculation. Berkshire's 17 percent share of Capital
Cities/ABC's
1990 earnings exceeded $83 million, yet only about $530,000 -- the
after-tax dividend -- entered Berkshire's GAAP earnings. More than
$82 million remained at Capital Cities/ABC for Berkshire's indirect
benefit.

The calculation adds Berkshire's share of the undistributed
operating earnings of major investees to Berkshire's own reported
operating earnings, then subtracts the incremental tax that would
have applied had those earnings been distributed. On this basis,
the 1990 letter estimated look-through earnings of about $590
million, versus reported operating earnings of $371 million. The
1991 letter reported that look-through earnings had grown since 1965
at almost the same 23 percent rate as book value, but also recorded
a 14 percent decline for 1991. Subsequent letters reported $604
million for 1992, $856 million for 1993, and $1.03 billion for 1994.
The metric is useful only if retained earnings are allocated well;
Buffett repeatedly tied their value to what investee managers did
with them.

### The Cigar-Butt Repudiation and the Quality Doctrine

The 1989 letter's cigar-butt passage is the decade's clearest
statement of the shift. Buffett described buying a struggling
business at a bargain price to capture one remaining puff of profit
and rejected the method as foolish for anyone who is not a
liquidator. First, a difficult business tends to produce additional
problems, so the apparent bargain may not be one. Second, a low
return on capital erodes the initial price advantage as time passes.
He used Hochschild Kohn, a department store bought below book value
with unrecorded real-estate value and a LIFO inventory cushion, as
an example of a statistically cheap purchase that disappointed.

Buffett then stated that it is better to buy a wonderful company at
a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price, adding that
Munger had understood this earlier. The point did not remove price
discipline: the 1983 goodwill appendix had already warned that a good
business is not always a good purchase, and the 1991 letter required
both a sensible price and business performance that validates the
assessment. **Interpretation:** the shift broadened the margin-of-
safety inquiry from asset discount alone to the durability of
earning power, capital needs, management, and price. It did not
authorize paying an unlimited price for quality.

### Economic Goodwill Versus Accounting Goodwill

The 1983 appendix distinguished economic goodwill from accounting
goodwill. Under the purchase-accounting rules then in force,
accounting goodwill was the purchase price left after assigning fair
values to identifiable net assets, and post-1970 goodwill was
amortized through earnings for no more than forty years. Economic
goodwill was the capitalized value of returns on tangible assets in
excess of ordinary market returns.

See's illustrated the difference without requiring a mixed
pre-tax/after-tax ratio. The appendix reported about $8 million of
net tangible assets and about $2 million of after-tax earnings at
purchase, or 25 percent after tax without leverage. It contrasted
See's with a hypothetical business earning the same $2 million on
$18 million of tangible assets. By 1983, See's earned $13 million
after tax on about $20 million of net tangible assets even as its
accounting goodwill was being amortized. Buffett's conclusion was
that accounting goodwill had declined while economic goodwill had
grown substantially. He identified consumer reputation, certain
unregulated franchises, and an enduring low-cost position as sources
of economic goodwill. **Interpretation:** this framework helps
explain Berkshire's preference for businesses whose pricing power
and low capital needs allow earnings and distributable cash to grow,
but it does not by itself establish that any quoted purchase price
is sensible.

### Management Quality and the Decentralized Model

**Synthesis:** The 1986-1995 reports develop a consistent theory of
management quality: Berkshire's operating managers are the source of
its value, and Buffett's contribution is to identify them, leave them
alone, and allocate the cash they generate. The 1986 letter
described the managerial style as the ability to easily expand
Berkshire's activities, because each operating unit runs itself.
The 1990 letter made the point with a story about Buffett's
granddaughter Emily and a magician named Beemer the Clown: Emily
waved a magic wand and took credit for the transformations,
summing up Buffett's view of his own contribution to Berkshire's
business magicians. The managers Buffett praised across the decade
shared identifiable traits. Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital
Cities/ABC understood, trusted, and admired each other; paid able
people well but abhorred excess headcount; attacked costs as
vigorously when profits were at record levels as when they were
under pressure; and stuck with what they understood, letting their
abilities rather than their egos determine what they attempted.
Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen at Wells Fargo exhibited the same
pattern, and Buffett compared them directly to Murphy and Burke.
Roberto Goizueta and Don Keough at Coca-Cola demonstrated a rare
blend of marketing and financial skills, maximizing both the
growth of the product and the rewards that growth brought to
shareholders. The 1987 letter supplied an operating measure: seven
major non-financial units earned $178 million pre-tax on $175
million of historical-cost equity, with only $2 million of net
interest expense; estimated after-tax return on equity was about 57
percent. **Interpretation:** management quality is not merely a
biographical judgment in these reports. It is tested against return
on required capital, cost control, capital allocation, and conduct
across changing conditions.

### Insurance Float and Catastrophe Pricing

Insurance float is money an insurer holds but does not own while
claims remain unpaid. Its economic value depends on both amount and
cost: underwriting profit makes the cost negative, while sustained
underwriting losses can make float more expensive than alternative
funding. The 1990 letter therefore compared underwriting loss with
average float rather than relying only on the combined ratio. It
reported about $1.6 billion of 1990 float at a cost of approximately
1.6 percent, subject to later reserve development and the tax cost
of holding investments through an insurer.

**Synthesis:** The catastrophe business shows how capital strength
and pricing
discipline interact. After Hurricane Hugo and the 1989 California
earthquake, coverage became scarce and Berkshire offered to write up
to $250 million of catastrophe protection. It retained the risks it
wrote rather than depending on other reinsurers. By 1994, Berkshire
had written one short-term California earthquake policy for $400
million and said it could quote up to $500 million of coverage on
the day requested. These policies created lumpy results and a
reported worst-case exposure that Berkshire estimated at about $600
million after tax in 1994. Buffett repeatedly stated the governing
rule: volume should fall when price is inadequate, and risk should
be accepted only when expected return compensates for it.

### The Price-Value Distinction and the Double-Dip Warning

**Synthesis:** The 1989-1991 letters warn that business quality and
investment return are separate questions. The 1989 letter said prior
gains had
combined growth in portfolio-company intrinsic value with a market
"catch-up" from underpricing; full prices reduced the likelihood of
another such benefit. The 1991 letter called this a double dip:
Coca-Cola and Gillette produced earnings growth while their
price-earnings ratios also rose. Buffett judged the reappraisal
warranted but not annually repeatable.

The 1990 letter supplied the shareholder arithmetic. If a buyer pays
a 10 percent premium to intrinsic value, intrinsic value then grows
15 percent annually, and the buyer later sells at the same 10
percent premium, the shareholder also earns 15 percent compounded.
Selling at a smaller premium produces a lower return than the
company's growth. Berkshire therefore preferred its shares to trade
near intrinsic value. **Interpretation:** franchise quality can
support intrinsic-value growth, but the price paid relative to that
value still governs the investor's realized return.

## Evidence

### The Coca-Cola Purchase and Its Decade-Long Validation

Berkshire began buying Coca-Cola in the summer of 1988. At year-end
1988 it reported 14,172,500 shares at a cost of $592.54 million; at
year-end 1989 it reported 23,350,000 shares at a cost of $1.02392
billion. These are the share counts then reported, before later
stock splits. The 1988 letter said Berkshire expected to hold the
investment for a long time and paired it with the broader statement
that the preferred holding period for an outstanding business with
outstanding management is forever. The 1989 letter supplied the
operating thesis: Roberto Goizueta and Don Keough had refocused the
company, accelerated international growth, and combined marketing
with financial discipline.

The subsequent letters distinguish operating validation from market
reappraisal. In 1990, Buffett explained that Coca-Cola could use
retained earnings to repurchase shares, increasing Berkshire's
percentage ownership more efficiently than a dividend followed by a
taxable reinvestment. In 1991, Coca-Cola and Gillette together
accounted for nearly $1.6 billion of Berkshire's $2.1 billion
increase in net worth. Buffett attributed the result to earnings
growth and, even more, to higher valuation multiples; he explicitly
said this double dip could not recur annually. In 1993, he cited
Coca-Cola's physical-volume growth from 207 million equivalent
cases in 1938 to about 10.7 billion in 1993. By year-end 1995,
Berkshire reported 100 million shares at a cost of $1.2989 billion
and a market value of $7.425 billion. **Interpretation:** The record
through 1995 supports the franchise thesis while preserving the
warning that
part of the investment gain came from revaluation, not only from
business compounding.

### The Wells Fargo Purchase During a Banking Panic

The 1990 letter's Wells Fargo analysis applies the quality-and-price
framework to a leveraged financial business. During a broad loss of
confidence in bank reporting, Berkshire increased its ownership to
just under 10 percent. The position cost about $290 million, less
than five times after-tax earnings and less than three times pre-tax
earnings. Buffett emphasized managers Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen,
comparing their cost discipline, mutual trust, and focus to Tom
Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities/ABC.

The risk analysis was explicit. Wells Fargo had $56 billion of
assets and $48 billion of loans and earned well over $1 billion
pre-tax after more than $300 million of loan-loss expense. Buffett
tested a scenario in which 10 percent of all loans became troubled
and losses, including foregone interest, averaged 30 percent of
principal. Under that scenario the bank would roughly break even.
He called it a low-level possibility rather than a forecast. The
1991 letter then reported a break-even look-through result --
dividends received offset by negative retained earnings. By
year-end 1995, Berkshire reported 6,791,218 shares at a cost of
$423.7 million and a market value of $1.4669 billion.

**Interpretation:** the case does not show that management quality
eliminates bank risk. It shows a method: reject a poorly managed bank
even at an apparently cheap price, test losses against earnings and
capital, and require a price that compensates for remaining
uncertainty.

### The Capital Cities/ABC Financing and the Media Franchise

In 1986, Berkshire bought three million Capital Cities/ABC shares at
$172.50 each, a $517.5 million investment associated with Capital
Cities' acquisition of ABC. At year-end 1986, Capital Cities/ABC,
GEICO, and Washington Post together had a market value exceeding
$1.7 billion, close to 75 percent of Berkshire's net worth, but paid
Berkshire only about $9 million of annual income. The 1990 letter
then reported that Berkshire's 17 percent share of Capital
Cities/ABC's earnings exceeded $83 million, while only about
$530,000 entered Berkshire's GAAP earnings. The difference remained
at Capital Cities/ABC.

The case also records deterioration and partial sale, not only
success. The 1991 letter said expanding information and
entertainment choices had weakened the economics of newspapers,
television, and magazines. It concluded that the intrinsic value of
Berkshire's media interests had declined materially, although
Capital Cities/ABC and Washington Post remained unusually well
managed and conservatively financed. In late 1993, Berkshire sold
one-third of its original Capital Cities/ABC position for $630 per
original share. The 1993 letter reported a $297 million after-tax
gain, compared with $152 million of hypothetically taxed retained
earnings attributable to those shares during Berkshire's holding
period. **Interpretation:** The outcome supported look-through
analysis, while the industry reassessment showed that a franchise
judgment must be
revised when competitive conditions change.

### Later-Decade Tests: Scott Fetzer, USAir, and Scale

The 1994 letter revisited Scott Fetzer, purchased in 1986 for $315.2
million when its book value was $172.6 million. From 1986 through
1994, annual earnings rose from $40.3 million to $79.3 million while
ending book value fell to $94 million because the subsidiary sent
substantial cash to Berkshire. The carrying value also declined as
purchase premiums were charged against reported earnings. Buffett
used the divergence to show why rising earning power can increase
intrinsic value even while accounting carrying value falls.

The same letter supplied a negative control. Berkshire wrote down its
USAir preferred stock by $268.5 million after the dividend was
suspended. Buffett called the 1989 purchase an unforced error and
identified the analytical failure: he had not focused adequately on
a high-cost airline's vulnerability after deregulation expanded
low-cost capacity. The 1995 letter reviewed Berkshire's five
convertible preferred purchases and said the group exceeded its
fixed-income benchmark only because of Gillette; it also withdrew the
earlier claim that Berkshire was almost certain to recover principal
plus dividends. **Interpretation:** Business quality analysis
therefore did not prevent
all errors, and preferred-stock seniority did not repair a weak
industry thesis.

At the end of the period, scale itself had become a constraint. The
1994 letter said Berkshire would consider a security only if it could
put at least $100 million into it. The 1995 letter reported that
Helzberg's and R.C. Willey had been acquired during the year and that
the remaining GEICO shares were purchased just after year-end. Those
three acquisitions roughly doubled revenue while increasing shares
outstanding only 1.3 percent and without materially increasing debt.
**Synthesis:** This combination of scale limits, controlled-business
acquisitions, and concentrated public holdings defines the
capital-allocation problem Berkshire carried into the next decade.

## Implications

### For Value Investors: Redefining the Margin of Safety

**Interpretation:** the reports broaden, but do not discard, the
margin-of-safety discipline. Business quality matters because low
capital requirements and durable earning power can preserve and grow
owner earnings. Price matters because a good business can still be
a poor purchase, valuation multiples can contract, and leverage can
turn a small asset error into a large equity loss. The 1983 appendix
made this balance explicit by calling a good business a good place
to look for a purchase, not automatically a good purchase.

The resulting checklist has two independent gates. First, assess the
business: required capital, competitive durability, management,
reinvestment prospects, and downside exposure. Second, assess the
security: price relative to conservatively estimated earning power
and intrinsic value. Coca-Cola illustrates a high-quality business
whose result through 1995 combined operating progress with market
reappraisal. Wells Fargo illustrates quality purchased at a low
earnings multiple during fear. USAir shows that seniority and a high
coupon cannot compensate for an inadequately analyzed industry cost
structure.

For security selection, this means a low multiple is evidence only
after its denominator has been tested. Reported earnings should be
reconciled to owner earnings; maintenance capital and working-capital
needs should be normalized; and the competitive conditions that
support the estimate should be stated. For a franchise, the investor
must also test whether price increases reflect customer value or
temporary scarcity. For a leveraged financial institution, loss
assumptions must be applied to assets before an earnings multiple is
treated as protection. The framework rejects both shortcuts: buying
solely because assets look cheap and buying solely because a
business looks excellent.

### For Capital Allocators: The Reinvestment Question

**Interpretation:** look-through earnings turn retention into an
allocation question. Retained profit is economically valuable when
an investee can use it at attractive rates, including productive
expansion or share repurchases below intrinsic value. It is not
automatically equivalent to cash in the owner's hands. The 1990
Coca-Cola example shows the tax advantage of an investee repurchasing
shares: the repurchase can raise Berkshire's percentage ownership
without first distributing a taxable dividend.

The allocator should therefore compare at least four uses of cash:
reinvestment in the existing operation, acquisition of another
business, repurchase of undervalued shares, and distribution to
owners. Berkshire's model added a fifth choice: send cash from a
subsidiary with limited reinvestment needs to headquarters for use
elsewhere. Scott Fetzer and See's demonstrate this transfer. The
1991 letter's finding that look-through earnings and book value had
grown at nearly the same 23 percent rate since 1965 supports the
framework, but the reported 1991 decline also shows that it is a
measurement discipline, not a promise of smooth growth.

A practical retained-earnings audit should compare the cumulative
amount retained with the increase in per-share owner earnings and
intrinsic value over a multi-year period. Acquisition-driven growth
should be separated from returns on capital already in the business.
Share repurchases should be judged against intrinsic value, because
repurchasing above value transfers wealth from continuing owners to
sellers. When an operating manager cannot deploy incremental capital
above an appropriate hurdle, remitting cash is a successful
allocation decision rather than evidence of weak ambition. The
Berkshire structure worked because subsidiary managers controlled
operations while headquarters retained authority over surplus
capital.

### For Investors Evaluating Financials: Management as the Moat

**Interpretation:** in a business operating at roughly 20-to-1
assets-to-equity, underwriting or lending quality cannot be treated
as a secondary issue. The Wells Fargo scenario translated a severe
loan assumption into an earnings outcome instead of relying on a
low stock-to-book ratio. Its arithmetic implied losses of about
$1.44 billion before considering the ordinary earning stream: 10
percent of $48 billion of loans multiplied by a 30 percent loss
rate. That scale explains why management quality and price both
matter, but neither removes systemic or concentration risk.

Insurance requires the same separation of amount, cost, and tail
risk. Float is advantageous only when its long-run underwriting cost
is acceptable. Berkshire's ability to retain large catastrophe
exposures created a competitive advantage, but the 1994 letter also
quantified a roughly $600 million after-tax worst case and warned
shareholders to accept the resulting volatility before a disaster,
not after it.

The evidence an investor seeks is behavioral and longitudinal:
reserve development, credit growth, pricing when competitors are
aggressive, compensation tied to controllable results, and the
willingness to reduce volume. A favorable recent loss ratio is not
enough, because long-tail claims can change prior-year economics.
The same logic applies to a bank whose current loan losses appear
low late in a credit expansion. Management statements should be
checked against underwriting outcomes across a full cycle, while
liquidity and capital are stress-tested separately from normalized
earnings.

### For Long-Term Shareholders: Entry Price and the Double-Dip

**Interpretation:** a long holding period does not make entry price
irrelevant. The 1990 example shows that the investor's return tracks
intrinsic-value growth only when the valuation relationship at sale
matches the relationship at purchase. Multiple expansion can add a
temporary double dip; multiple contraction can subtract from
otherwise satisfactory business performance.

Retained earnings are a separate source of economic benefit, but
they do not by themselves increase percentage ownership. Ownership
percentage rises when the company retires shares while the investor
holds its position. Coca-Cola's repurchases supplied the report's
example. The practical review sequence is therefore: estimate
intrinsic value from owner earnings; test whether retained earnings
are being used productively; compare price with value; and separate
operating progress from changes in the market multiple.

The decomposition also improves performance review. A satisfactory
share-price result can come from weak operating progress masked by a
higher multiple, while a poor short-term price result can accompany
improving owner earnings. The investor should record the purchase
thesis in terms that can be checked later: expected owner-earnings
growth, reinvestment returns, balance-sheet limits, and the valuation
paid. Review then asks which variable changed. This prevents the
market price from becoming both the original thesis and the later
proof, and it preserves the distinction between an analytical error,
an operating setback, and ordinary quotation volatility.
