# Berkshire Hathaway Annual Reports 1986-1995 -- The Decade Franchise Investing Took Over

Berkshire Hathaway's annual reports from 1986 through 1995 are the
record of the decade in which Warren Buffett's investment philosophy
completed its migration from Benjamin Graham's statistical bargains
to Charlie Munger's durable franchises. Read as a sequence, the ten
reports show the purchase of Coca-Cola, the financing of Capital
Cities/ABC, the Wells Fargo bet during a banking panic, and the
formal definition of owner earnings -- alongside the explicit
repudiation of the "cigar butt" method that had built Berkshire in
its first decade. The reports are the clearest primary source for
understanding how the definition of value changed when a
capital allocator stopped buying cheap assets and started buying
compounding machines.

## Background

The decade 1986-1995 is a natural unit of analysis because it
brackets the period in which the intellectual transition from
Graham-style bargain hunting to Munger-style franchise investing
became, in Buffett's own published writing, complete and
self-conscious. The transition did not happen in a single year.
Its seeds were planted in 1972, when Blue Chip Stamps (an affiliate
controlled by Buffett and Munger) acquired See's Candies for $25
million -- paying roughly three times the $8 million of tangible
net assets that strict Graham doctrine would have demanded as a
ceiling. See's was generating approximately $4.2 million in pre-tax
earnings on that $8 million of tangible capital, a roughly 50
percent pre-tax return on tangible capital. Munger's argument was
that the relationship between a business's net profits and the
tangible assets required to produce them mattered more than the
discount to liquidation value. A business that generates $10
million of profit on $20 million of capital is a different species
from one that generates the same $10 million on $100 million of
brick and machinery. The See's acquisition was the empirical proof.
By the time the 1986-1995 reports begin, See's had been compounding
inside Berkshire for fourteen years, and its lesson -- that a
wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a
wonderful price -- was no longer a hypothesis but a documented
result.

The intellectual context of the decade is the maturation of
Buffett's thinking under Munger's influence, made explicit in the
reports themselves. The 1986 letter, written February 27, 1987,
introduced the concept Buffett called "owner earnings" in an
appendix to the shareholder letter. The definition was precise:
owner earnings equal reported earnings plus depreciation,
depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges, less
the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and
equipment that the business requires to fully maintain its
long-term competitive position and its unit volume. Buffett argued
that GAAP earnings alone were insufficient to measure a business's
true cash-generating power, because accounting rules distort the
distinction between maintenance capital expenditure (a true
operating cost) and growth capital expenditure (an optional
investment). The owner-earnings concept gave Berkshire an
analytical tool for separating businesses that export surplus cash
from businesses that import capital just to stand still. The
concept would inform every major purchase decision of the decade.

The 1989 letter, written February 28, 1990, delivered the
philosophical repudiation of the cigar-butt method in the most
quoted passage of the decade. Buffett described the "cigar butt"
approach: a cigar butt found on the street with only one puff left
may not offer much of a smoke, but the bargain purchase makes that
puff all profit. Unless you are a liquidator, Buffett wrote, that
approach to buying businesses is foolish. First, the original
bargain price probably will not turn out to be such a steal -- in a
difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another
surfaces, and there is never just one cockroach in the kitchen.
Second, any initial advantage is quickly eroded by the low return
the mediocre business earns. Time, Buffett concluded, is the friend
of the wonderful business and the enemy of the mediocre. The
passage was not abstract. It was a confession. Buffett admitted he
had learned the lesson the hard way -- several times over --
including the acquisition of a Baltimore department store,
Hochschild Kohn, bought at a substantial discount to book value
with first-class people and unrecorded real estate values, which
still disappointed. Charlie understood this early, Buffett wrote; I
was a slow learner.

The decade opens with Berkshire already a diversified holding
company. At the start of 1986, the company had closed the Scott
Fetzer acquisition (January 1986) and the Fechheimer Bros.
acquisition (1986), owned large equity stakes in Capital
Cities/ABC, GEICO, and the Washington Post, and operated See's
Candies, the Buffalo News, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Kirby, World
Book, and the Scott Fetzer group. The three permanent equity
holdings -- Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, and Washington Post -- had a
combined market value of over $1.7 billion at year-end 1986,
close to 75 percent of Berkshire's total net worth, yet delivered
only about $9 million in annual dividend income. The economic
engine was the retention and reinvestment of earnings inside those
businesses, not the dividends Berkshire received. By the end of
1995, Berkshire had added Coca-Cola (the decade's defining
purchase), Wells Fargo, Gillette (via preferred stock converted in
1991), American Express (via preferred in 1991), Borsheim's
(1989), H.H. Brown (1991), and Helzberg Diamonds (1995). The
portfolio had tilted decisively toward consumer franchises and
well-managed financials, and the analytical vocabulary --
owner earnings, look-through earnings, economic goodwill, the
distinction between price and value -- had been fully articulated
in the published reports.

The decade is also the period in which Berkshire's shareholder
letter became a teaching document unlike any other in corporate
America. Buffett used the letter not merely to satisfy disclosure
requirements but to explain capital allocation logic, admit
mistakes openly, and build a relationship of trust based on
radical candor. The 1990 letter's Wells Fargo analysis, the 1991
letter's look-through earnings table, and the 1989 letter's
cigar-butt confession were not press releases; they were
instructional texts written by a practitioner for an audience of
owner-investors. The candor extended to performance: Buffett warned
repeatedly that Berkshire could not maintain its 23.8 percent
annual book value growth, and that the universe of opportunities
large enough to move a multi-billion-dollar company was shrinking
with every year of compounding.

## Core Concepts

### Owner Earnings and the Cash-Flow Fallacy

The 1986 letter's appendix defined owner earnings as the cash a
business owner can withdraw without impairing the business's
competitive position and unit volume. The formula is reported
earnings plus depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain
other non-cash charges, less the average annual amount of
capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment that the
business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive
position. Buffett called the common shortcut of adding back
depreciation without subtracting maintenance capital expenditure
the "cash flow fallacy" directly in the 1986 letter. The
distinction matters because a business that reports high earnings
but requires heavy capital expenditure to stay competitive is
worth less than its reported earnings suggest. Owner earnings
separate businesses that generate distributable cash from
businesses that consume cash to maintain themselves. See's
Candies, with its tiny tangible asset base, generated owner
earnings far in excess of its reported earnings net of the
minimal capital it required. A textile mill, by contrast, might
report identical earnings while requiring continuous reinvestment
in looms and inventory just to maintain volume -- its owner
earnings would be far lower. The concept gave Berkshire a
discipline for rejecting businesses that look cheap on accounting
metrics but bleed cash to stay alive.

### Look-Through Earnings

The look-through earnings concept, formalized in the Owner's
Manual distributed to Berkshire shareholders in 1996 but used
analytically throughout the late 1980s and early 1990s letters,
addresses the limitation of GAAP accounting for a company with
large minority equity stakes. Under GAAP, Berkshire recorded only
the dividends it received from investees like Capital Cities/ABC,
Coca-Cola, and GEICO -- not its proportional share of those
companies' retained earnings. Yet the retained earnings, Buffett
argued, were fully as beneficial to Berkshire as if they had been
distributed. The 1990 letter made the case concrete: Berkshire's
17 percent share of Capital Cities/ABC's earnings amounted to more
than $83 million in 1990, yet only about $530,000 was counted in
Berkshire's GAAP earnings. The residual $82 million stayed with
Capital Cities as retained earnings, working for Berkshire's
benefit but unrecorded on its books. The look-through calculation
adds Berkshire's share of the undistributed operating earnings of
its major investees to its own reported operating earnings, then
subtracts an allowance for the tax that would have been paid had
those earnings been distributed as dividends. Buffett argued that
look-through earnings, not GAAP earnings, realistically portrayed
Berkshire's yearly gain from operations. The concept forces
investors to evaluate the reinvestment quality of the businesses
they own stakes in: a dollar of retained earnings is only worth a
dollar if the investee can reinvest it at attractive returns. The
1991 letter reported that since 1965, Berkshire's look-through
earnings had grown at almost the identical 23 percent rate of gain
recorded for book value -- evidence that the retained earnings of
its investees had compounded as productively as the cash Berkshire
received directly.

### The Cigar-Butt Repudiation and the Quality Doctrine

The 1989 letter's cigar-butt passage is the decade's clearest
statement of the philosophical shift. Buffett described the
cigar-butt approach -- buying a struggling business at a bargain
price to capture one puff of profit -- and rejected it as foolish
for anyone who is not a liquidator. The reasoning had two parts.
First, the original bargain price probably will not be such a
steal, because a difficult business generates an endless sequence
of problems; there is never just one cockroach in the kitchen.
Second, any initial advantage is eroded by the low return the
mediocre business earns on its capital over time. Buffett
illustrated with the Hochschild Kohn department store, bought at a
discount to book value with unrecorded real estate and a LIFO
inventory cushion, which still disappointed because the business
itself earned low returns. The conclusion, attributed to Munger's
earlier insight, was that it is far better to buy a wonderful
business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful
price. Time is the friend of the wonderful business and the enemy
of the mediocre. The passage is not a rejection of price
discipline -- Buffett never advocated paying any price for
quality. It is a redefinition of where the margin of safety comes
from: not only from a discount to liquidation value, but from the
durability of the economic moat and the efficiency with which the
business converts capital into cash. The See's Candies experience
was the empirical foundation. A business with a 50 percent pre-tax
return on tangible capital generates surplus cash that can be
reallocated after taxes, becoming a funding mechanism for other
investments rather than a consumer of capital.

### Economic Goodwill Versus Accounting Goodwill

The distinction between economic goodwill and accounting
goodwill, articulated in the appendix to the 1983 letter but
central to the franchise analysis of the 1986-1995 decade, explains
why Berkshire's acquisitions were valued the way they were.
Accounting goodwill is the premium paid over net tangible assets in
an acquisition, amortized as a charge against earnings over a
specified period. Economic goodwill is the capitalized value of the
excess return a business earns on its tangible capital -- the
present value of the cash flows a superior business generates
above what a mundane business with the same assets would produce.
See's Candies illustrated the difference. At acquisition, See's had
$8 million of tangible net assets and earned $4.2 million pre-tax.
A mundane confectioner earning 11 percent on its tangible assets
would have little or no economic goodwill. See's, earning over 50
percent pre-tax on tangible capital, had economic goodwill far
exceeding the accounting goodwill on its balance sheet. The key
insight, developed across the decade's reports, is that economic
goodwill tends to increase over time as a franchise compounds,
while accounting goodwill amortizes to zero. A business with a
strong consumer franchise allows the value of the product to the
purchaser, rather than its production cost, to be the major
determinant of selling price. Consumer franchises are a prime
source of economic goodwill. This framework explains why
Berkshire was willing to pay what looked like a full price for
Coca-Cola and Capital Cities/ABC: the economic goodwill of those
franchises was growing, not shrinking, and the accounting metrics
that made them look expensive understated the compounding value of
their pricing power and distribution scale.

### Management Quality and the Decentralized Model

The 1986-1995 reports develop a consistent theory of management
quality: Berkshire's operating managers are the source of its
value, and Buffett's contribution is to identify them, leave them
alone, and allocate the cash they generate. The 1986 letter
described the managerial style as the ability to easily expand
Berkshire's activities, because each operating unit runs itself.
The 1990 letter made the point with a story about Buffett's
granddaughter Emily and a magician named Beemer the Clown: Emily
waved a magic wand and took credit for the transformations,
summing up Buffett's view of his own contribution to Berkshire's
business magicians. The managers Buffett praised across the decade
shared identifiable traits. Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital
Cities/ABC understood, trusted, and admired each other; paid able
people well but abhorred excess headcount; attacked costs as
vigorously when profits were at record levels as when they were
under pressure; and stuck with what they understood, letting their
abilities rather than their egos determine what they attempted.
Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen at Wells Fargo exhibited the same
pattern, and Buffett compared them directly to Murphy and Burke.
Roberto Goizueta and Don Keough at Coca-Cola demonstrated a rare
blend of marketing and financial skills, maximizing both the
growth of the product and the rewards that growth brought to
shareholders. The concept is that management quality is not a soft
factor but a measurable economic condition: it shows up in
sustained high returns on tangible capital, disciplined cost
control across the business cycle, and the willingness to
reinvest in the business only at attractive returns.

### Insurance Float and Catastrophe Pricing

Insurance float is the second engine of Berkshire's compounding
and the reports of 1986-1995 develop its economics with increasing
specificity. Float is the money an insurer holds between collecting
premiums and paying claims. If underwriting is profitable, float
is negative-cost capital -- Berkshire is paid to hold it. The 1986
letter reported that Berkshire's three permanent equity holdings
(Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, Washington Post) retained a very high
percentage of their earnings, functioning as a form of equity
float. The 1989 letter described the catastrophe coverage
business: after Hurricane Hugo and the Loma Prieta earthquake,
many insurers were bare and CAT writers were licking their wounds,
creating an immediate shortage of catastrophe coverage and a
spike in prices. Berkshire offered to write up to $250 million of
catastrophe coverage, retaining it all rather than laying it off to
reinsurers, because Berkshire's capital base allowed it to assume
risk that smaller, reinsurance-dependent companies could not. The
concept is that float is only valuable if underwriting discipline
is maintained and if the insurer has the capital strength to
retain risk when prices are attractive. The decade's insurance
analysis culminates in the recognition that Berkshire's structure
-- large retained risk, no reliance on lay-off, willingness to
shrink volume when prices are inadequate -- is itself a form of
competitive moat. The 1989 letter stated the principle: when rates
carry an expectation of profit, Berkshire wants to assume as much
risk as is prudent, and in its case that is a lot.

### The Price-Value Distinction and the Double-Dip Warning

The 1989 and 1990 letters articulate a sophisticated warning about
the relationship between intrinsic value and market price that
governs how Berkshire's shareholders should think about returns.
The 1989 letter warned that Berkshire's performance had benefited
from a double-dip: first, the exceptional gains in intrinsic value
that its businesses produced, and second, the market's
reappraisal of those businesses from inappropriately low prices to
full prices. The double-dip, Buffett cautioned, could not recur
annually; shareholders would have to settle for a single dip in
the future. The 1990 letter extended the analysis: ideally, every
Berkshire shareholder's return should closely mirror the company's
intrinsic value growth during their period of ownership. If you
buy at a premium to intrinsic value and sell at a smaller premium,
your return will be inferior to the company's, even if intrinsic
value grows at the target rate. This is why Buffett and Munger
hoped Berkshire would sell consistently at about intrinsic value
rather than at the value-ignoring volatility of the late 1980s.
The concept is that the entry price relative to intrinsic value
determines the shareholder's return, independent of the business's
performance. A wonderful business bought at a premium to intrinsic
value can produce a mediocre shareholder return if the premium
contracts. The price-value distinction is the analytical bedrock
beneath the franchise doctrine: buying a wonderful business is
necessary but not sufficient; the price paid relative to intrinsic
value governs the outcome.

## Evidence

### The Coca-Cola Purchase and Its Decade-Long Validation

The Coca-Cola investment is the decade's richest case study and the
cleanest evidence for the franchise doctrine. Berkshire began
accumulating Coca-Cola shares in late 1988, establishing a position
of approximately 14.2 million shares (split-adjusted) at a total
cost of $592.5 million, and added in 1989, bringing the aggregate
cost to approximately $1.028 billion for roughly 7 percent of the
company. The average purchase price was approximately $5.46 per
share split-adjusted, representing approximately 15 times earnings,
12 times cash flow, and 5 times book value. By the conventional
metrics of Graham-style bargain hunting, this was not a cheap
stock. The 1988 letter stated the thesis: Coca-Cola was a simple,
globally recognizable product with extraordinary pricing power and
a moat that was wide and getting wider. The 1989 letter explained
the management dimension: Roberto Goizueta had become CEO in 1981,
and with Don Keough had rethought and focused the company's
policies, producing a rare blend of marketing and financial
skills that maximized both product growth and shareholder rewards.

The validation came through both operating performance and market
reappraisal. The 1991 letter reported that Coca-Cola and Gillette
together accounted for nearly $1.6 billion of Berkshire's $2.1
billion growth in net worth that year -- a dramatic rise in the
price-earnings ratios of the two stocks. Buffett noted that when
Berkshire loaded up on Coke three years earlier, Berkshire's net
worth was $3.4 billion; by year-end 1991, the Coca-Cola stock alone
was worth more than that. He framed the gain as a double-dip:
partly excellent earnings growth, partly the market's reappraisal
of the stock, which he believed was warranted but could not recur
annually. The method of the evidence is the long-run holding
record: the vast majority of the Coca-Cola shares purchased in
1988-1989 were still held decades later at a cost basis of
approximately $1.3 billion, against market values that reached
over $12 billion by 2000 and over $14 billion by 2012. The
finding is that a franchise purchased at a fair price -- not a
bargain price -- and held through decades of compounding produces
returns that dwarf the returns available from trading in and out
of cheaper but inferior businesses. The investment also validated
the look-through earnings concept: Coca-Cola's retained earnings,
reinvested at high returns or used to repurchase shares, increased
Berkshire's proportional ownership of the world's most valuable
franchise without Berkshire paying a dollar of tax on the
retained earnings.

### The Wells Fargo Purchase During a Banking Panic

The 1990 letter's Wells Fargo analysis is the decade's clearest
evidence that the franchise doctrine applies to financials as
well as consumer brands, and that the discipline of buying a
wonderful business at a fair price can be exercised under market
panic. In 1990, amid a banking crisis in which one huge loan loss
after another was unveiled -- often on the heels of managerial
assurances that all was well -- investors fled bank stocks.
Berkshire purchased a 10 percent interest in Wells Fargo for $290
million, less than five times after-tax earnings and less than
three times pre-tax earnings. The 1990 letter described the
managers: Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen, who Buffett compared to
Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities/ABC. Each pair was
stronger than the sum of its parts because each partner understood,
trusted, and admired the other; both attacked costs as vigorously
when profits were at record levels as when they were under
pressure; both stuck with what they understood.

The evidence of disciplined analysis under uncertainty is the
scenario Buffett constructed. Wells Fargo earned well over $1
billion pre-tax annually after expensing more than $300 million
for loan losses. If 10 percent of all $48 billion of the bank's
loans -- not just real estate loans -- were hit by problems in
1991, and these produced losses averaging 30 percent of
principal, the company would roughly break even. Buffett
described this as a low-level possibility, not a likelihood, and
concluded that a year like that would not distress Berkshire. The
finding is that the franchise doctrine, combined with a margin of
safety derived from the quality of management and the price paid,
allows a capital allocator to act decisively when the market is
panicking about an industry it does not understand. The 1991
letter reported a break-even result at Wells Fargo -- dividends
received offset by negative retained earnings -- which Buffett
had described the prior year as a low-level possibility. The
long-run outcome vindicated the thesis: Wells Fargo became one of
Berkshire's largest and most profitable positions. The lesson is
that the margin of safety in a financial business comes not from
asset coverage but from the quality of management and the price
paid relative to normalized earnings power.

### The Capital Cities/ABC Financing and the Media Franchise

The Capital Cities/ABC investment, which spans the decade's
opening, provides evidence on franchise investing in media and on
the role of a stable, interested major shareholder. In January
1986, Berkshire purchased 3 million shares of Capital Cities at
$172.50 per share, a $517 million investment that helped finance
Capital Cities' acquisition of ABC, a deal roughly four times
larger than Capital Cities itself. The 1986 letter reported that
the three permanent equity holdings -- Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO,
and Washington Post -- had a combined market value of over $1.7
billion, close to 75 percent of Berkshire's net worth, yet
delivered only about $9 million in annual dividend income. The
economic engine was retention and reinvestment inside those
businesses. The 1990 letter reported that Berkshire's 17 percent
share of Capital Cities/ABC's earnings amounted to more than $83
million, of which only about $530,000 was counted in GAAP earnings
-- the residual $82 million stayed with the company as retained
earnings working for Berkshire's benefit but unrecorded on its
books. Buffett described this as the "forgotten-but-not-gone"
earnings and argued that their ownership and subsequent
utilization was all-important, regardless of how they were
accounted for. The method of the evidence is the look-through
earnings record: Berkshire's share of the undistributed earnings
of Capital Cities/ABC was $85 million in 1990 and $61 million in
1991, figures that dwarfed the dividend income but were invisible
under GAAP. The finding is that a media franchise with a capable
management team and a stable major shareholder can compound value
through retained earnings far in excess of what its dividend
stream suggests, and that the analytical tool required to see this
is look-through earnings rather than GAAP earnings. The investment
also demonstrates the franchise doctrine's application to media:
Buffett helped finance the Capital Cities/ABC deal because he
understood broadcasting as a category with durable competitive
economics, not because the stock was statistically cheap.

## Implications

### For Value Investors: Redefining the Margin of Safety

The 1986-1995 reports redefine the margin of safety for the
value investor. Under Graham's doctrine, the margin of safety came
from buying at a discount to liquidation or net-net value -- a
statistical cushion that protected against estimation error and
business deterioration. The franchise doctrine, as documented in
these reports, expands the source of the margin of safety to
include the durability of the economic moat and the efficiency of
capital conversion. A business with a 50 percent pre-tax return on
tangible capital, like See's Candies, has a structural margin of
safety that a textile mill lacks: it can absorb input cost
increases, competitive pressure, and macroeconomic stress without
destroying its cash-generating capacity, because its pricing
power and low reinvestment needs protect its owner earnings. The
practical implication is that the value investor's checklist must
include the quality of the business, not only the cheapness of the
price. The Coca-Cola purchase at 15 times earnings is the
illustration: an investor who applied only Graham's metrics would
have passed on Coca-Cola in 1988, missing the compounding of a
franchise whose economic goodwill was growing faster than its
accounting metrics suggested. The implication is not that price
ceases to matter -- Buffett never advocated paying any price for
quality -- but that the definition of a fair price must
incorporate the present value of the cash flows a superior
business will generate, not only the liquidation value of its
assets. The Wells Fargo purchase at less than five times after-tax
earnings shows that the franchise doctrine and price discipline
can coexist: a wonderful business at a bargain price is the ideal,
and a banking panic can provide it.

### For Capital Allocators: The Reinvestment Question

The reports carry a direct implication for anyone responsible for
capital allocation, whether at a holding company, a family office,
or an operating business with surplus cash. The 1986 letter stated
the principle with Berkshire's characteristic directness: because
Berkshire earns more than average, retains all it earns, and has
operations that require little incremental capital to remain
competitive, the future results of the business are far more
affected by today's capital allocations than would be the case for
a business earning 10 percent and distributing half. If retained
earnings -- and those of major investees -- are employed in an
unproductive manner, the economics deteriorate quickly. The
implication is that the capital allocator's central question is
not whether to deploy capital but where to deploy it at the
highest return. The look-through earnings concept forces the
allocator to evaluate the reinvestment quality of every business
in the portfolio: a dollar retained by an investee is only worth a
dollar if the investee can reinvest it at attractive returns. The
Coca-Cola retained earnings, reinvested in global expansion and
share repurchases, compounded at rates that made them worth far
more than a dollar each. The implication for the allocator is that
holding a wonderful business and letting its management reinvest
retained earnings is often superior to selling the position to
redeploy the cash elsewhere, because the tax cost of selling and
the quality of the alternative both erode the advantage. The
1991 letter's observation that Berkshire's look-through earnings
had grown at almost the identical rate as its book value since
1965 is the evidence that disciplined retention and reinvestment,
not active trading, produced the compounding. The further
implication is that the capital allocator must be willing to do
nothing for long periods -- the Coca-Cola position, built in
1988-1989, was held essentially untouched for decades -- and that
the discipline of inaction is itself a capital allocation skill
that the reports document and the markets rarely reward in the
short run.

### For Investors Evaluating Financials: Management as the Moat

The Wells Fargo investment carries an implication that extends
beyond banking to any highly leveraged industry. In a business
with 20-to-1 leverage, managerial strengths and weaknesses are
magnified twentyfold. The 1990 letter stated that Berkshire had no
interest in purchasing shares of a poorly managed bank at a cheap
price; its only interest was in buying into well-managed banks at
fair prices. The implication for investors evaluating financials
is that the conventional margin of safety -- a discount to book
value or tangible assets -- is insufficient when the business's
stability depends on the quality of underwriting and lending
decisions that asset coverage cannot protect against. A bank can
write down its entire book of loans in a bad year; a 30 percent
loss rate on 10 percent of the loan book, as Buffett's scenario
for Wells Fargo illustrated, can wipe out a year of pre-tax
income. The margin of safety in a financial business comes from
the demonstrated discipline of management across cycles: cost
control when profits are high, conservatism when competitors are
expanding recklessly, and the willingness to shrink volume when
prices are inadequate. Reichardt and Hazen at Wells Fargo
exhibited all three. The practical implication is that the
investor in financials must evaluate management quality as the
primary moat, not a secondary factor. The same logic applies to
insurance: Berkshire's catastrophe coverage business, described in
the 1989 letter, worked because Berkshire had the capital to
retain risk that competitors could not, and the discipline to
write business only when prices carried an expectation of profit.
The investor who applies the franchise doctrine to financials
must identify the managers whose discipline is the moat and
accept that the price of the stock will reflect the market's
assessment of that discipline, which may be wrong during panics.

### For Long-Term Shareholders: Entry Price and the Double-Dip

The 1989 and 1990 letters carry an implication for any investor
who buys and holds: the return you experience depends on the
relationship between your entry price and intrinsic value, not
only on the business's operating performance. The double-dip
framework -- exceptional intrinsic-value growth plus market
reappraisal from low to full prices -- explains why Berkshire's
shareholders in the 1980s earned returns that exceeded the
company's intrinsic-value growth rate. The 1990 letter made the
arithmetic explicit: if you buy at a 10 percent premium to
intrinsic value, intrinsic value grows at 15 percent annually,
and you sell at a 10 percent premium, your return is 15 percent
compounded. But if you buy at a premium and sell at a smaller
premium, your return falls below the company's growth rate. The
implication is that the long-term shareholder cannot ignore the
entry price even when buying a wonderful business. A wonderful
business bought at a premium to intrinsic value can produce a
mediocre shareholder return if the premium contracts over the
holding period, even as the business compounds at an attractive
rate. The practical takeaway is that the investor should aim to
buy at or below intrinsic value, not at a premium, and that the
willingness to wait for such an opportunity -- as Buffett waited
for the banking panic to buy Wells Fargo -- is the discipline
that governs long-term returns. The further implication is that
the investor who buys a wonderful business and holds it through
decades of compounding benefits from a second source of return
that the reports make visible: the retained earnings of the
business, reinvested at high returns by capable management, increase
the investor's proportional ownership of the franchise without
requiring additional capital or triggering taxable events. The
Coca-Cola investment is the proof: the shares bought in 1988-1989
compounded for decades, the retained earnings of the company were
reinvested at high returns, and Berkshire's proportional ownership
of the franchise grew through share repurchases, all without
Berkshire paying a dollar of additional capital or tax.
