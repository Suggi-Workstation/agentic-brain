# Nick Sleep and the Nomad Investment Partnership: Patient Ownership and Scale Economies Shared

Nick Sleep and his partner Qais Zakaria ran the Nomad Investment Partnership from 2001 to 2014, compounding investor capital at approximately 20.8 percent per year net of fees versus 6.5 percent for the MSCI World Index, a 921 percent total return against 117 percent for the index. They did this by concentrating the portfolio in a handful of businesses -- chiefly Costco, Amazon, and Berkshire Hathaway -- that followed a model Sleep called "scale economies shared," in which a company returns its cost advantages to customers rather than pocketing them as profit. At the height of their success they closed the fund, returned all outside capital, and shifted to philanthropic work through the IGY Foundation. Sleep's shareholder letters, written every six months over the partnership's life, have become among the most studied investment documents of their generation.

## Background

Nick Sleep did not train for finance. Born in England in 1968, he studied geography at the University of Edinburgh, drawn to the subject precisely because it refused to be one thing. Geography borrows from geology, economics, psychology, sociology, and statistics simultaneously. Sleep later described it as "the ultimate polymath course" that taught him to ask questions other, more homogeneous disciplines never needed to ask. This multidisciplinary instinct would later define his investing: he reached into biology, physics, philosophy, and retailing history to understand businesses rather than relying on spreadsheet models or sell-side research.

After university, Sleep briefly pursued landscape architecture, taking an apprenticeship at a local firm. The job realities soured him on the profession. He read an obscure book, "Investment Trusts Explained," which intrigued him enough to take a position as a trainee investment analyst at a small Scottish fund company. He later joined Marathon Asset Management in London as an investment analyst around 1995, having previously served at Sun Life of Canada.

At Marathon he met Qais "Zak" Zakaria, an Iraqi-born Cambridge-trained mathematician working as a sell-side analyst at Deutsche Bank specializing in Asian equities. The two encountered each other in the late 1990s while independently seeking investment opportunities in Asian markets, both in their late twenties. Zakaria had fled political turmoil in Iraq; Sleep had faced difficulties navigating the finance industry from an unconventional background. Their pairing worked the way productive partnerships do: Sleep was the narrative and psychology engine, Zakaria the analytical stress test, each with standing permission to tell the other he was wrong. Jeremy Hosking, a Marathon founding shareholder, encouraged them to invest in overlooked and contrarian stocks, a strategy that proved successful during the Asian Financial Crisis when Marathon deployed roughly 500 million dollars into distressed Southeast Asian markets and profited when the region recovered.

A pivotal moment came in May 2000, when Sleep and Zakaria traveled together to Omaha for Berkshire Hathaway's annual shareholder meeting. The experience -- hearing Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger expound on business quality, moats, and patience -- inspired Sleep's plan to start his own fund. Zakaria quickly embraced the idea. Sleep persuaded his Marathon bosses to let him launch the Nomad Investment Partnership with Zakaria as co-manager. The fund began on September 10, 2001 -- one day before the September 11 attacks -- originally as a subsidiary of Marathon Asset Management. It was spun out as an independent fund in 2006.

Sleep's intellectual formation owed much to Robert Pirsig's "Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance," a book he read in his early years and occasionally referenced throughout his career. Pirsig's meditation on quality and the pursuit of excellence despite context resonated with Sleep's temperament. He adopted a principle of doing things right rather than merely for profit, a philosophy he and Zakaria built into Nomad's structure: minimal management fees, performance fees only above a 6 percent hurdle rate modeled on Buffett's partnership, acceptance of only permanent capital from long-term investors, and what they called a "no asshole rule" for partners. They viewed investing as "a very simple discipline" that depended on basic skills and principles, not complex models.

The letters Sleep wrote to partners over thirteen years documented a genuine intellectual journey. They began as the work of cigar-butt investors -- the early portfolio held roughly half its assets in deeply discounted, asset-based businesses and deep value workouts in the Graham tradition, concentrated in markets the mainstream overlooked. Over time, as Sleep and Zakaria recognized that truly exceptional business models are vanishingly scarce, they ruthlessly concentrated their holdings. The 2004 letter articulated the "terminal portfolio" -- the destination they wanted to reach, comprising what Sleep called "honestly run compounding machines." By the end of Nomad's run, virtually the entire fund was invested in three companies. The letters themselves, spanning roughly 218 pages from December 2001 to January 2014, were never intended for public circulation. They circulated quietly among serious investors as bootleg copies until Sleep and Zakaria published an approved, lightly edited version on the IGY Foundation website. Sleep noted in the preamble that the partnership's annual performance was "around twenty percent for twenty years," evidence that investors "don't have to go changing their holdings with their underwear."

## Core Concepts

### Scale Economies Shared

The most famous and lasting contribution of the Nomad letters is the model Sleep called "scale economies shared." Traditional microeconomics and corporate finance teach companies to exploit economies of scale by expanding profit margins -- reaping higher prices and extracting cash from customers once pricing power is achieved. Sleep and Zakaria realized that the greatest compounders in economic history do the opposite: they turn scale efficiencies into a consumer surplus. As the business grows, unit costs fall. Rather than pocketing those savings as higher margins, the company hands them straight back to customers in the form of lower prices, wider selection, and superior service. This creates an invincible flywheel: lower prices attract more customers; higher customer volume drives greater economies of scale; greater scale lowers unit costs further; and lower costs enable still lower prices. Sleep wrote that on a whiteboard in their office they had listed the "very few investment models that work and that we can understand," and that Costco was "the best example we can find of one of them: scale efficiencies shared." He emphasized that most companies pursue scale efficiencies, but few share them -- and the sharing is what makes the model powerful. The center of the model is a paradox: the company grows through giving more back.

### Destination Analysis

Sleep pioneered what he called "destination analysis," a method of evaluating businesses based on their long-term trajectory rather than their near-term financials. The question was never "What will Amazon or Costco earn next quarter?" The only question that mattered was "What will this business look like in 10 or 20 years if its competitive engine remains unbroken?" Sleep formalized three questions: Where is the intended destination for this business in 10 or 20 years? What must management be doing today to raise the probability of arriving at that destination? And what could prevent this company from reaching such a favorable destination? By focusing on the terminal destination rather than the winding path, Nomad could comfortably ignore short-term market panics, temporary multiple compressions, and analyst downgrades, holding businesses as long as their operational engine was widening its moat. Sleep argued that in the equity market, unlike the bond market, returns can be both increased and de-risked by time -- business outcomes are more predictable several years out than in the near term. He called quarterly earnings guidance and Wall Street forecasts "terminal noise" and dismissed those who fixated on it as "junkies who crave information which will be worthless in twelve weeks."

### The Robustness Ratio

In the 2005 interim letter, Sleep introduced the "robustness ratio" as a framework for assessing the magnitude of a company's moat. The ratio compares the consumer benefit -- the value saved by customers -- against the company's own profit, the portion available to shareholders after employee compensation. Sleep cited a consumer benefit of roughly 5 dollars for every 1 dollar of profit retained by Costco: a Costco cardholder saved approximately 175 dollars per year by shopping at Costco, against an annual membership fee of roughly 23 dollars, yielding a net gain of around 150 dollars. He used this ratio not as a magical formula but as an indicator for identifying companies whose competitive advantage was structurally embedded in their relationship with customers. The robustness ratio captured the idea that the best businesses distribute value across stakeholders rather than extracting it for shareholders alone, and that this distribution is what makes the moat difficult to replicate.

### Price-to-Value Ratio

Nomad measured every holding and candidate against a "price-to-value ratio" -- the current market price of a security expressed as a percentage of Sleep and Zakaria's estimated real business value. Their stated goal was to invest "at around half of their real business value," implying a ratio of 0.5. New ideas had to reduce the portfolio's overall price-to-value ratio to justify inclusion. By the end of June 2002, Sleep reported this figure at 51 percent of estimated fair value for the portfolio. The discipline served as a measuring stick: a stock trading at 1 dollar with an estimated value of 3 dollars yielded a ratio of 0.33, which would justify adding. Over time, Sleep and Zakaria came to recognize a limitation -- a company could continue to generate substantial additional intrinsic value even after reaching their perceived 100 percent threshold, because the compounding engine continued to widen the gap between price and destination value.

### Super High-Quality Thinkers

In 2004, Sleep articulated the concept of "super high-quality thinkers" -- companies that consistently outmaneuver their competitors and rigorously allocate capital to reinforce their competitive advantages. The criteria were: intellectually honest and economically rational management; a deliberate choice to out-think competition and allocate capital over many years with discipline; and the ability to resist growing when returns on capital are poor. Sleep estimated that fewer than 5 percent of publicly listed firms do what management thinks is right rather than what plays well with the outside world. The ideal portfolio, which he called the "terminal portfolio," would comprise this list of wonderful, honestly run compounding machines. By the time of the fund's closure, the portfolio mainly comprised three stocks -- Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway -- all run by what Sleep classified as super high-quality thinkers.

### The Equity Yield Curve and the Value of Patience

Sleep developed the concept of the "equity yield curve," arguing that patience has a value and that returns increase with time in the equity market, as they do on a bond market yield curve. In the bond market, higher yields compensate for the risk that principal will not be repaid or will be devalued by inflation. In the equity market, Sleep argued, the mechanism is different: business outcomes can be more predictable several years out than in the near term. He had no idea where the market would end in any given year, but given corporate strategies, capital allocation, and starting valuations, he believed he had some idea of how his companies would evolve over the next several years. This conviction underpinned Nomad's competitive advantage: the aggregate patience of its investor base. Sleep wrote that "good investing is a minority sport" -- to earn returns better than everyone else, one must do things different from the crowd, and the crowd is not patient. He noted that Nomad had no investor turnover since inception and almost no inquiries into performance despite market declines, which he called "very unusual and a huge credit to our investors."

### Concentration and the Cost of Diversification

Sleep was deeply critical of the investment industry's obsession with diversification and benchmark tracking. He argued that thinking via the index led to cascading mistakes: the requirement to have an opinion on everything inside the index regardless of competence, an unwillingness to invest in better opportunities outside the index, and over-diversification. These three mistakes, he wrote, destroy capital. Sleep described index-relative funds as "a ghastly Faustian pact" -- they sell because the client does not trust the manager, but they ensure mediocrity. Nomad's portfolio became progressively more concentrated: from 20 to 30 securities in the early years to virtually three companies by the end. Sleep later identified his most common mistake as thinking of each new investment bottom-up in terms of allocation, starting at 0 percent and moving upward -- an approach susceptible to anchoring bias. He inverted the method: start with the new investment at 100 percent of the fund and move down, because investment success requires betting big when one finds a truly exceptional business.

## Evidence

### Track Record: 921 Percent Over Thirteen Years

Nomad Investment Partnership operated from September 2001 through December 2013, generating a total net return of 921.1 percent, or approximately 20.8 percent annualized net to partners. Over the same period, the MSCI World Index returned 116.9 percent, or approximately 6.5 percent annualized. In dollar terms, an initial 100,000 dollars invested in the MSCI World Index would have grown to roughly 216,900 dollars, whereas the same investment with Nomad would have expanded to approximately 1,021,100 dollars. The fund made roughly 2 billion dollars for its clients over its lifetime. By the time of closure in early 2014, the fund had grown to approximately 3 billion dollars in assets. These figures are corroborated by the Nomad letters themselves, published on the IGY Foundation website, and by William Green's account in "Richer, Wiser, Happier" (2021).

### The Costco Investment

Nomad's investment in Costco demonstrated the scale-economies-shared model in practice. Sleep's analysis, documented in the 2005 interim letter and an earlier interview with the Columbia Business School published in Outstanding Investor's Digest, argued that the market misunderstood Costco's low margins. Critics called Costco a low-margin retailer with an expensive stock and a cost problem. Sleep countered with three heuristics. First, Costco's net profit margin of 1.7 percent (compared to Walmart at 3.6 percent and Target at 4.2 percent) was not a weakness but the point -- the firm was deferring profits to extend the life of the franchise. Second, at 24 times earnings, Costco appeared expensive only because net income was a small residual; the firm could earn Walmart margins by taking pricing up slightly, which would put it on 11 times earnings, but it would be a worse business for it. Third, rising costs as a percentage of revenue reflected investment in warehouse and distribution capacity for the next growth phase, not structural weakness. Sleep estimated Costco could conservatively increase revenue and free cash flow by 13 percent per year -- 4 percent from increasing asset turns of newer stores, 4 percent from same-store growth, and 5 percent from new stores. He called Costco "an early life-cycle company whose competitive moat gets deeper as the company gets bigger." Costco's stock rose from approximately 30 dollars to 380 dollars per share over the holding period, with reinvested dividends, making it a roughly tenfold return.

### The Amazon Investment

Nomad began investing in Amazon around 2006, recognizing it as the digital implementation of scale economies shared. Sleep noted that Jeff Bezos preferred Amazon to be a large company with small margins because sharing efficiency benefits with customers turned size -- "frequently an anchor on business performance" -- into an asset that deepened the moat as the firm grew. Amazon lost roughly half its market value during the 2008 financial crisis; Nomad's portfolio fell 45.3 percent that year. Rather than trade or trim, Sleep wrote to partners explaining that the businesses they owned were widening their lead over distressed competitors. They used the crisis to upgrade the portfolio, concentrating further in Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway. Over the next four years, the fund returned approximately 404 percent. Amazon grew to roughly 30 percent of the fund by 2009. Sleep later admitted his mistake was not making Amazon a 100 percent position, and recommended at the fund's closure that partners hold all their money in Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway for the next ten years. The Amazon holding alone grew 8 to 10 times after the fund closed. According to Green, Zakaria never sold any Amazon shares from his personal portfolio; Sleep eventually sold half his Amazon stake (representing roughly 70 percent of his total holdings at the time) around 2018 and reinvested in ASOS, a decision that proved costly as ASOS subsequently declined over 90 percent from its peak.

### The 2008 Financial Crisis and the Test of Patience

The 2008 crisis tested Nomad's philosophy under maximum stress. When markets halved and Nomad's portfolio plummeted, Sleep refused to panic. He wrote to partners that the businesses they owned were actually widening their competitive lead over distressed rivals. In 2008, the partnership experienced only 2 percent net redemptions -- a figure that demonstrated Nomad attracted the patient partners it deserved. Sleep closed his 2008 letter with the words "Take heart and look to the horizon." The inaction proved decisive: over the following years, the concentrated holdings in Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway compounded dramatically, and the fund's returns far exceeded the index. This episode validated Sleep's argument that volatility is not risk but opportunity, and that the investor's only real advantages are patience and the willingness to sit still when others cannot.

### The Stagecoach Mistake and the Shift from Cigar Butts

An early mistake that shaped Nomad's evolution was the investment in Stagecoach, a UK bus company. The position went poorly and forced Sleep and Zakaria to reconsider their cigar-butt approach. They recognized that deeply undervalued, asset-based businesses could be value traps if the underlying business was deteriorating. This experience, documented in the letters, catalyzed the shift toward "super high-quality thinkers" and the terminal portfolio concept. Sleep wrote in his 2004 letter of "the likely evolution of Partnership Investments," explicitly naming where he wanted the portfolio to go rather than continuing to buy cheap but mediocre businesses. He noted that if he had his time again, he would "not be seduced by some firms' apparent economic cheapness but weigh more heavily their DNA," and that the most profitable insights came from recognizing the deep reality of businesses, not from being more contrarian than everyone else.

## Implications

### For Individual Investors: The Power of Inaction and Concentration

Sleep's record demonstrates that individual investors possess structural advantages the professional industry does not: patience, the absence of benchmark pressure, and the freedom to hold a concentrated portfolio without client redemption risk. Sleep argued that the only things an investor controls are what to buy (preferably earlier than the masses) and how big a position to take. Everything else -- quarterly earnings, market predictions, index performance -- is outside the investor's control and, in Sleep's view, largely irrelevant to long-term outcomes. The implication for individuals is that doing nothing is an active and often correct decision. Sleep held Costco for over 18 years. He argued that the biggest error an investor can make is the sale of a Wal-Mart or a Microsoft in the early stages of the company's growth, a mistake "mathematically far greater than the equivalent sum invested in a firm that goes bankrupt." The industry, he noted, glosses over this because opportunity costs go unrecorded in performance records. For the individual investor, the lesson is to resist the urge to trade, to resist the urge to sell winners to buy losers (as Peter Lynch put it, "don't pick the flowers and water the weeds"), and to concentrate in businesses whose competitive engine is widening.

### For Fund Managers: Aligning Incentives with Long-Term Outcomes

Nomad's fee structure and fund lifecycle offer a template for how an investment partnership can be designed to serve long-term outcomes rather than asset gathering. Sleep and Zakaria charged a minimal management fee to cover overhead and took performance fees only above a 6 percent annual hurdle, modeled on Buffett's partnership. This structure mitigated the incentive to gather assets and meant that underperformance did not burden investors with fees. Sleep communicated expectations relentlessly: he told partners that if the fund could not find opportunities, it might shut down. In 2006, he refused to take on new money because nothing reduced the portfolio's price-to-value ratio. The ultimate alignment came in 2014, when Sleep and Zakaria closed the fund at the height of their success, returned all outside capital, and told partners to hold their money in three stocks for the next decade. No pitch for a successor fund, no reinvention. This decision, nearly unheard of in the asset management industry, illustrated the principle that a fund's structure should serve the investment thesis, not the reverse. Sleep wrote to Warren Buffett upon closure: "It appears to all the world that the performance that Nomad has enjoyed over the years was created by Zak and me. That is not the case. The real work is done by you and the good people at Berkshire."

### For Business Analysis: Identifying the Compounding Engine

Sleep's framework for analyzing businesses has direct application for anyone evaluating companies. The scale-economies-shared model provides a diagnostic: does the company return its cost advantages to customers, or does it extract them as margin? The robustness ratio offers a way to quantify the moat's magnitude: how much value does the customer receive relative to the shareholder? Destination analysis forces the analyst to articulate where the business will be in a decade, what management must do to get there, and what could prevent arrival. The concept of "super high-quality thinkers" provides criteria for evaluating management: intellectual honesty, economic rationality, disciplined capital allocation, and the willingness to resist growth when returns are poor. These tools are not specific to retail or technology -- Sleep applied them across airlines (AirAsia, Ryanair), insurance (GEICO, Berkshire), consumer goods, and e-commerce. The framework's generality makes it transferable across industries and time periods.

### For the Study of Investing: The Letters as Primary Source

The Nomad letters occupy a unique place in the literature of investing. Unlike Buffett's annual letters, which are polished public documents written for a broad audience, Sleep's letters were written for a small group of partners and never intended for wide circulation. They document the thinking process in real time -- including mistakes, second-guessing, and intellectual evolution. They show an investor moving from cigar-butt value investing to concentrated ownership of compounding machines, a journey that mirrors Buffett's own evolution under Munger's influence but is recorded with a candor and self-criticism that the Berkshire letters, written for a public company, cannot match. The letters' circulation as bootleg copies -- passed among serious investors like rare manuscripts before their official publication -- speaks to their value as primary source material. For students of investing, the letters offer what most investment writing cannot: an unvarnished record of how a great investor thought, including when he was wrong.

### The Boundary Between Investor Biography and Investment Philosophy

The Nomad story raises a structural question for the study of investing: where does the person end and the idea begin? Sleep's scale-economies-shared model is a reusable intellectual contribution that belongs in the domain of investment philosophy. But the model cannot be separated from the person who developed it -- Sleep's geography background, his reading of Pirsig, his partnership with Zakaria, and his decision to walk away at the peak all shaped how the model was discovered, applied, and ultimately retired with the fund. The candidate scope for this topic deliberately separates "Sleep's personal development from the reusable ideas his letters produced," recognizing that the ideas (scale economies shared, destination analysis) will be developed as separate topics in the value-investing domain, while this topic focuses on the investor: who he was, how he thought, and what he did.
