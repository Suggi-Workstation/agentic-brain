# Charlie Munger -- Generalist Reasoning and Patient Ownership Reshaped Berkshire

Charlie Munger (1924-2023) influenced investing less by supplying a formula than by changing the decision rules used by Warren Buffett and Berkshire Hathaway: prefer durable businesses to merely cheap assets, test conclusions with models from several disciplines, study incentives, and wait for a small number of unusually favorable opportunities [3][5][6]. His record before and during Berkshire also shows the limits of that approach: concentrated judgment can create exceptional long-run results while still producing severe interim losses, so the method requires both intellectual discipline and a structure able to survive volatility [4].

## Background

Charles Thomas Munger was born in Omaha, Nebraska, on January 1, 1924. He studied mathematics at the University of Michigan before entering the U.S. Army Air Corps during World War II. In the fall of 1943 the military sent him to the California Institute of Technology for its Ceiling and Visibility Unlimited meteorology program, which trained forecasters to support flight operations; he received a meteorology certificate. After his discharge in 1946, he attended Harvard Law School without first completing an undergraduate degree and graduated with honors in 1948 [1]. The author's interpretation is that this route through mathematics, weather forecasting, and law exposed him to quantitative uncertainty, physical systems, and adversarial reasoning before he became a professional investor, although it did not itself prove his later investment skill.

Munger returned to California to practice law. In 1962 he and six other attorneys founded the firm that became Munger, Tolles & Olson [2]. That same year he entered money management, according to Buffett's later account, and ran Wheeler, Munger & Company through 1975 [3][4]. The partnership's published record was strong but uneven. Buffett's 1984 table reported a 19.8 percent average annual compounded return for the overall partnership from 1962 through 1975, 13.7 percent for limited partners, and 5.0 percent for the Dow over the same period [4]. The annual path included partnership losses of 31.9 percent in 1973 and 31.5 percent in 1974, followed by a 73.2 percent gain in 1975 [4]. The record therefore supports two claims at once: Munger generated substantial long-run outperformance, and his concentrated method imposed drawdowns that a reader should not erase by quoting only the compound rate.

Munger and Buffett first met in Omaha in 1959. Buffett wrote that Munger began money management in 1962 and, in 1965, advised him to stop repeating the mistake represented by the Berkshire textile purchase: instead of buying fair businesses at wonderful prices, add wonderful businesses bought at fair prices [3]. The two invested alongside one another before their formal Berkshire relationship. Caltech's institutional account dates Munger's entry onto Berkshire's board to 1978; he served as vice chairman from then until his death [1][3]. Their geographic separation and unequal public visibility did not make Munger a ceremonial deputy. Buffett described him as the architect of the present Berkshire and himself as the general contractor who carried out the design [3]. That description is testimony from Munger's closest collaborator, not an independent causal estimate, but it establishes how Buffett understood the division of intellectual labor.

Munger's career was broader than Berkshire. He acquired the Daily Journal Corporation in 1977 and remained connected to its board until his death; he also chaired and led Wesco Financial from 1984 until Berkshire absorbed the company in 2011 [12]. Costco's 2023 annual report said that he had served on its board for more than 26 years [9]. These roles gave him repeated exposure to operating businesses, boards, capital allocation, and ownership structures rather than only marketable securities. They also supplied public forums in which he explained how he thought, especially Berkshire and Daily Journal shareholder meetings.

Munger's intellectual reputation rested largely on speeches later collected in *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. The official Stripe Press edition identifies the 1994 worldly-wisdom talk and the expanded 2005 version of "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" as central statements of his method [5]. Peter Kaufman's collection did not turn Munger's ideas into an academic theory with controlled tests. It preserved a practitioner's framework: a set of heuristics, examples, and warnings refined through investment, legal, and business experience. That distinction matters because the speeches are primary evidence for what Munger believed, but they are not by themselves proof that every claimed mechanism is universally valid.

Munger died peacefully at a California hospital on November 28, 2023, thirty-three days before his 100th birthday [3][13]. Berkshire's announcement quoted Buffett saying that the company could not have reached its present status without Munger's inspiration, wisdom, and participation [13]. His biographical importance within the investors domain therefore lies in a specific combination: an independently successful partnership manager, a long-serving capital-allocation partner, a board member and company steward, and a teacher who made his own decision process unusually visible.

## Core Concepts

### Quality as a Corrective to Statistical Cheapness

Munger's most consequential intervention was not a rejection of value investing but a change in what counted as value. Graham-style cigar-butt investing sought securities priced below readily measurable asset value, even when the underlying business was mediocre. Buffett wrote that this worked well with small sums but could not provide the foundation for a large and enduring enterprise. He credited Munger with replacing the rule "buy fair businesses at wonderful prices" with "buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" [6]. The revised rule still compares price with value; it expands value beyond balance-sheet assets to include durable competitive advantage, pricing power, capital efficiency, and the length of the reinvestment runway.

This change is easy to oversimplify. "Wonderful" is not a license to ignore price, and "fair" is not a synonym for any market quotation. The 2023 Berkshire letter preserved Munger's full instruction: add wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices, and abandon a method that worked only at small scale [3]. Scale was therefore part of the reasoning. A small partnership can liquidate minor bargains and redeploy the proceeds repeatedly. A large permanent owner needs businesses that can absorb time and capital without being continually replaced. Munger's quality emphasis connected security selection to the economics of the ownership vehicle.

### A Latticework Rather Than a Single Formula

Munger argued that usable knowledge must be organized. In his worldly-wisdom talk, he said isolated facts become useful only when they hang together on a "latticework of theory" and when experience is placed on that lattice. He also warned that a thinker with only one or two models will force reality to fit them, the mental equivalent of treating every problem as a nail because one owns a hammer [5]. The operational claim is not that an investor must master every academic field. Munger estimated that roughly 80 or 90 important models would carry about 90 percent of the freight, with a smaller subset doing most of the work [5].

The disciplines he emphasized included mathematics, engineering, physics, biology, psychology, economics, accounting, and history [5][12]. Their value comes from different error patterns. Accounting can describe reported assets and earnings but may understate an economic franchise. Psychology can expose incentive-caused distortions but cannot establish a company's cash flows. Engineering can contribute redundancy, breakpoints, and margin-of-safety thinking, while probability constrains confidence. The author's synthesis is that the latticework functions as a system of cross-checks: a conclusion is stronger when independent models agree and more suspect when one model supplies all the support.

Munger's model count should not be read as a precise curriculum. The number was a practical estimate, not a measured threshold. Nor does adding models automatically improve a decision. A collector can attach names to familiar biases while continuing to rationalize. The demanding part is choosing the models that govern the situation, identifying where they conflict, and allowing disconfirming evidence to change the conclusion. Munger's warning against the single hammer applies equally to a person whose new hammer is a long checklist.

### Incentives and the Psychology of Misjudgment

Munger treated incentives as both behavioral forces and cognitive forces. The 2005 synthesis in *Poor Charlie's Almanack* begins its list of 25 tendencies with reward-and-punishment superresponse. Munger said that even people who recognize incentives routinely underestimate their power [5]. An incentive can alter conduct directly, but it can also shape what an agent notices, believes, and reports. That is why compensation, status, institutional pressure, and career risk belong in an investment analysis alongside the financial statements.

The same synthesis includes liking and disliking, doubt avoidance, inconsistency avoidance, excessive self-regard, overoptimism, deprival superreaction, social proof, authority influence, and reason respecting [5]. Munger did not claim to have discovered all these tendencies. His contribution was an applied naming and retrieval system designed to make them available during real decisions. This is especially relevant to investors because markets create strong feedback from price, peers, recent outcomes, and incentives. A rising price can look like evidence, social approval can feel like independent confirmation, and a paid advocate can sincerely experience a sales narrative as analysis.

Munger reserved "lollapalooza" for outcomes produced when several tendencies act in the same direction. In the 2005 list, the twenty-fifth tendency is explicitly defined as extreme consequences from a confluence of psychological tendencies favoring one outcome [5]. This matters because many business failures and speculative episodes cannot be explained by one bias. Incentives may reward growth, social proof may validate the strategy, authority may suppress dissent, and inconsistency avoidance may prevent reversal. The combined pressure can be much stronger than any item considered alone. The practical response is not merely to label the episode after it fails, but to examine whether multiple forces are reinforcing the same conclusion before capital is committed.

### Inversion and the Deliberate Search for Failure

Munger repeatedly used inversion: state the result to avoid, identify the paths that produce it, and remove those paths before optimizing for success. The Museum of American Finance's review of his work traces his "invert, always invert" formulation to the mathematician Carl Jacobi and summarizes Munger's view that difficult problems are often easier when addressed backward [12]. Applied to investing, inversion asks what could cause permanent capital loss, forced selling, reputational damage, or an invalidated business thesis. It changes the first question from "How much can this earn?" to "What can make this unable to survive?"

Inversion fits Munger's biography because his role beside Buffett often involved refusal. The same institutional review describes his "abominable no-man" function: a negative feedback system that vetoed deals he considered too risky, expensive, or complex [12]. A veto does not create visible revenue, so organizations can undervalue it. Yet avoiding one ruinous transaction can matter more than approving many adequate ones. The author's interpretation is that Munger's contribution was partly architectural: he made disciplined non-action a legitimate output of the investment process.

### Concentration, Patience, and the Price of Conviction

Munger favored a small number of decisions made with high conviction, but his own partnership record shows that concentration is not emotionally or structurally free. The back-to-back declines in 1973 and 1974 were not minor deviations around a smooth trend [4]. A process can be correct over a full period and still become intolerable to investors, clients, lenders, or managers during the path. Any account of Munger's concentration that omits this sequence turns a risk-bearing method into a slogan.

In his final long-form interview, Munger said that there may be only five or six times in a lifetime when one knows an opportunity is unusually favorable. He warned that people who encounter early successes can mistake rare opportunities for an easy, repeatable pattern, and he described conviction as the product of reading, thinking, and visiting rather than instantaneous intuition [11]. This separates selectivity from bravado. The method calls for heavy work before a concentrated commitment and continued willingness to admit that a purported edge does not exist.

The author's interpretation is that patience is the complementary behavior. A high-quality business that can reinvest or distribute cash for decades does not require constant trading; tax deferral, lower transaction costs, and fewer opportunities for error can become part of the economics. But patience is not passivity toward deteriorating evidence. Munger's broader framework requires revisiting incentives, competitive advantage, and failure modes [5][11]. The distinction is between refusing activity for its own sake and refusing to update.

### Learning as a Compounding Process

Munger made reading part of the method rather than an ornamental habit. The official *Poor Charlie's Almanack* reading page quotes him saying that he had known no wise people over broad subject areas who did not read all the time [5]. Breadth supplied models; biographies and business histories supplied vicarious experience; repeated application connected new material to existing knowledge. The latticework metaphor therefore describes not only decision analysis but also how learning compounds: new ideas become more valuable when they connect with models already understood.

This principle also imposes humility. Reading widely does not confer expertise in every field. Munger's approach pairs breadth with boundaries: use other disciplines to ask better questions, then distinguish an intelligible problem from one that remains too hard. The author's assessment is that this pairing prevents generalism from becoming superficial confidence. The generalist's advantage is not knowing everything; it is recognizing more relevant variables and more reasons not to act.

### Partnership as a Decision System

Buffett and Munger's partnership worked as a decision system with both agreement and corrective tension. Buffett retained operational authority and public responsibility, while Munger supplied a different temperament, repeated his quality argument, and challenged old habits [3][6]. Buffett wrote that they could disagree without arguing and that Berkshire had ultimately been built to Munger's blueprint [6]. These statements should not be generalized into a claim that friendship guarantees good governance. The relevant mechanism was clearer: mutual respect made candid contradiction possible, and a long horizon allowed an idea to be repeated until evidence and results settled it.

Munger's influence also appears in Berkshire's owner orientation. The 1983 letter described shareholders as owner-partners, emphasized per-share intrinsic value rather than corporate size, rejected acquisition wish lists funded at shareholder expense, and committed to candid reporting [8]. Those policies were jointly stated by Buffett and Munger. They connect investment selection with governance: the same discipline used to judge an outside company must govern the allocator's own use of shareholder capital.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

### The Partnership Record: Outperformance With a Severe Path

The clearest quantitative evidence about Munger before Berkshire is Table 5 in Buffett's "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville." For 1962-1975 it reports an average annual compounded rate of 19.8 percent for the overall partnership, 13.7 percent for limited partners, and 5.0 percent for the Dow [4]. The corresponding cumulative results were 1,156.7 percent, 500.1 percent, and 96.8 percent [4]. These figures support the claim that Munger's partnership outperformed its market comparison by a wide margin. They do not identify how much came from security selection, concentration, leverage, fees, or favorable circumstances, and they should not be converted into a promise about any later period.

The annual rows are equally important. The overall partnership fell 31.9 percent in 1973 and 31.5 percent in 1974, then gained 73.2 percent in 1975 [4]. The two-year decline compounds to approximately 53.3 percent, not 63.4 percent, because the second percentage applies to an already reduced base. This recalculation is: `(1 - 0.319) * (1 - 0.315) - 1 = -0.533515`, or about -53.3 percent. The record demonstrates a central tension in Munger's method: concentration can make superior long-run judgment visible, but it can also create a path that many capital providers cannot tolerate. Survival capacity is therefore part of investment skill, not a separate administrative concern.

### See's Candies: The Quality Thesis in Operating Form

See's Candies provides the most documented case of Munger's quality argument changing Berkshire's behavior. Buffett's 2014 review says that Blue Chip Stamps bought See's in 1972 for USD 25 million when the company earned about USD 4 million before tax and used about USD 8 million of net tangible assets. Buffett wrote that Munger considered the seller's USD 30 million request justified, while Buffett resisted paying more than USD 25 million because the price exceeded the tangible-asset standard to which he was accustomed [6].

The method was to identify an asset omitted from conventional book value: a durable customer franchise that supported pricing power and required little incremental capital. By the end of 2014, Buffett reported USD 1.9 billion of cumulative pre-tax earnings from See's and only USD 40 million of added investment [6]. The historical figures are dated measurements, not current totals, but they are sufficient to test the original thesis. See's converted a relatively small tangible-capital base into cash that Berkshire could redeploy, while teaching Buffett how a brand and customer habit could possess economic value absent from the balance sheet [6]. This case corrected the old topic's unsupported and inconsistent purchase and cumulative-profit figures by using Berkshire's own dated account.

### Costco: Business-Model Observation Before Abstraction

Munger's Costco involvement illustrates how he moved from observation to a business model. Costco's 2023 annual report states that he served on its board for more than 26 years and that the company benefited from his business judgment [9]. In the October 2023 *Acquired* interview, Munger described visiting Sol Price's operation, buying Price Club shares in the market, and later joining Costco's board after Buffett declined the invitation [11]. He identified low prices, large efficient stores, high volume, convenient parking, member benefits, rapid inventory turnover, and supplier payment terms as mutually reinforcing elements rather than isolated tactics [11].

This case is evidence of method, not a controlled test of causation. Munger's account explains what he looked for: customer value, cost advantage, capital efficiency, and a culture that preserved the formula. It also shows that the insight was grounded in store visits and conversations before it became a general lesson. The limitation is selection: a successful case described after decades of performance is vulnerable to hindsight. The responsible inference is not that every low-price retailer will become Costco, but that Munger studied how several operational features combined into a durable system.

### BYD: Expertise, Management, and a Deliberate Exception

The BYD investment shows Munger crossing an apparent industry boundary through trusted expertise and management assessment. In a 2013 Columbia Business School interview, Li Lu said he introduced Munger to BYD's case, emphasized founder Wang Chuanfu's engineering ability and the company's learning culture, and stated that Munger's own favorable assessment led to the investment. Li also said that Berkshire was not ideologically opposed to technology; the relevant question was how much Buffett and Munger understood [10].

The case supports a narrower conclusion than later market performance alone would. Munger did not treat an industry label as a substitute for analysis. He evaluated the operator, technical capability, organizational learning, competitive field, and price. His 2023 interview still called BYD a "miracle" and emphasized Wang's unusual ability to understand and manufacture products [11]. The evidence does not establish that the full return was foreseeable, and Munger himself acknowledged uncertainty in other discussions. It shows how a generalist could make a bounded exception: rely on a knowledgeable partner, study the specific manager and system, and refuse the blanket claim that all technology was either investable or uninvestable.

### Berkshire's Aggregate Record and the Attribution Limit

Berkshire's long-run record is consistent with the quality-and-patience architecture, but it is not a clean experiment isolating Munger's contribution. The 2025 annual report records a 19.7 percent compounded annual gain in Berkshire's per-share market value from 1965 through 2025, compared with 10.5 percent for the S&P 500 including dividends. It reports cumulative gains of 6,099,294 percent and 46,061 percent, respectively [7]. Those figures include decisions by Buffett, Munger, operating managers, later investment managers, and many acquired businesses across changing tax, insurance, and market conditions.

Buffett's qualitative testimony supplies the attribution Berkshire itself makes. The 2014 report says Munger broke Buffett's cigar-butt habits and provided the blueprint for the present company [6]. The 2023 report repeats the architecture metaphor and dates the core advice to 1965 [3]. Together, the record and testimony support the conclusion that Munger materially influenced the process associated with Berkshire's results. They do not justify assigning him a numerical share of the return or claiming that mental models alone caused the outcome.

### Primary Writings as Process Evidence

Munger's speeches are evidence about process because they expose the rules he wanted decision-makers to use. The 1994 talk calls for multiple models, cross-disciplinary knowledge, and experience attached to theory; the 2005 psychology synthesis lists 25 tendencies and explains the special danger of interacting biases [5]. These texts have strong authority for the content of Munger's views. Their evidentiary limit is external validity: a vivid case or aphorism is not a substitute for a replicated study.

The practical value of the speeches is therefore diagnostic. They generate questions that can be checked against business evidence: What incentives shape management? Which model is dominating the analysis? What outcome would cause ruin? Are independent mechanisms reinforcing the same conclusion? Did the analyst seek disconfirming facts? Used this way, the framework improves the completeness of an investigation without pretending to provide a mechanical answer.

## Implications

### For Investors: Build a Rejection Process Before a Selection Process

Munger's career implies that investment work should begin with exclusions. Invert the problem by listing threats to survival, identify what lies beyond present competence, and reject situations whose economics or incentives cannot be explained. This is not timidity. It preserves attention and capital for the small set of cases in which several forms of evidence agree. Munger's own statement that truly exceptional opportunities may be rare makes a high rejection rate part of the method rather than evidence that the analyst is inactive [11][12].

A practical review can separate four questions. First, is the business understandable enough to describe its customers, economics, capital needs, and failure modes? Second, does it possess a durable advantage, or is apparent quality only a favorable cycle? Third, are managers and intermediaries rewarded for per-share value creation or for size, activity, and short-term appearance? Fourth, does the price leave room for error? The latticework adds value when each question uses a different model and source base. It adds noise when the analyst collects analogies without resolving contradictions.

The partnership record also requires matching the process to the capital base. A concentrated portfolio may be analytically justified and still be institutionally fragile if investors can redeem after a drawdown, if borrowed money must be repaid, or if the manager cannot endure public underperformance. The approximately 53.3 percent compounded decline across 1973-1974 is the relevant stress case [4]. Before adopting concentration, an investor should ask whether the legal, financial, and psychological structure can survive the drawdown that the strategy has already demonstrated.

### For Value Investors: Quality Does Not Cancel Price

The See's case is often compressed into "buy great businesses," which removes half the analysis. Berkshire examined earnings, tangible capital, pricing power, and the purchase price. Buffett nearly lost the deal because his old framework overweighted tangible assets, but Munger did not argue that any price was acceptable [6]. The implication is to value the economic franchise while preserving a margin against overestimation. Brand, network, switching cost, or culture matters only if it can be connected to customer behavior, cash generation, and reinvestment economics.

A second implication is to distinguish a compounder from a merely good current business. See's could raise prices and distribute cash without proportionate new capital [6]. Costco combined low prices and scale with fast inventory turnover and favorable working-capital dynamics [11]. Those mechanisms explain why time helped the owner. A business that must continually reinvest all its cash merely to defend its position may be excellent for customers and employees while producing a different owner return. Munger's quality framework asks how the advantage converts into per-share value, not whether the company is admirable in the abstract.

### For Boards and Organizations: Treat Incentives as Evidence

Munger's psychology framework implies that stated intentions deserve less weight than reward structures. A manager paid for acquisition volume will see acquisitions differently from an owner paid only by long-run per-share results. A salesperson, consultant, or investment manager can honestly believe an incentive-compatible conclusion; conscious dishonesty is not required [5]. Governance therefore needs explicit examination of compensation, promotion, status, reporting lines, and the penalties for dissent.

The lollapalooza concept adds a systems warning. A bad outcome becomes more likely when several forces align: bonus metrics reward expansion, peers validate the same behavior, authority discourages questions, and prior commitments make reversal humiliating [5]. A board should not check these risks one at a time and declare them small. It should ask whether their interaction creates a one-way pressure. The appropriate control may be structural: independent review, a decision journal, a pre-mortem, a waiting period, or a person authorized to veto a transaction.

Munger's role with Buffett illustrates the value of negative feedback. Buffett's description of Munger as architect and the institutional description of him as an "abominable no-man" point to a partner whose contribution often took the form of correction or refusal [3][12]. Organizations commonly reward visible production more than prevented error because the rejected failure never appears in reported results. The implication is to make dissent an assigned responsibility and to evaluate it by the quality of reasoning, not by the number of approvals it produces.

### For Capital Allocators: Measure Per-Share Progress, Not Organizational Size

The owner principles in Berkshire's 1983 letter reject growth for its own sake. Buffett and Munger framed shareholders as owner-partners, focused on average annual gains in per-share intrinsic value, and said managerial wish lists would not be filled at shareholder expense [8]. This establishes a useful test for acquisitions, internal projects, and repurchases: does the action improve long-run value for each share after considering the opportunity cost of capital?

This test also clarifies patience. Holding cash or declining a deal can be rational when available opportunities do not clear the required return and risk standards. Activity is not a neutral default; it consumes capital, attention, and future flexibility. Conversely, patience cannot become a permanent excuse for avoiding accountable decisions. The allocator should state what evidence would justify action and what conditions would invalidate the current stance.

### For Learners: Connect Breadth to Repeated Application

Munger's reading habit supports a deliberate learning program rather than indiscriminate consumption. The goal is to acquire a limited set of high-utility models, understand their causal logic, and apply them repeatedly to real cases [5]. Mathematics and probability discipline magnitudes and odds. Engineering contributes safety margins and failure analysis. Biology contributes adaptation and selection. Psychology contributes incentives and predictable error. History and biography provide cases in which these mechanisms interact.

The learner should record not only what a model explains but where it fails. A broad reader can become overconfident by finding an analogy for every conclusion. To resist this, each analysis should include the strongest incompatible model and the evidence that would change the decision. This practice makes Munger's latticework an error-correction system rather than a collection of persuasive stories.

### For Partnerships: Design for Candor, Complementarity, and Time

Buffett and Munger did not contribute identical temperaments or public roles. Their system benefited from complementarity: one partner could propose and execute while the other challenged premises and repeated a different standard [3][6]. The general implication is not to imitate their personalities. It is to design a partnership in which disagreement does not threaten status, responsibilities are clear, and a correct objection can survive repeated rejection until the evidence is considered.

Long duration strengthened this arrangement. Trust reduced the cost of candid feedback, and accumulated shared cases created a common vocabulary. Yet duration alone can also produce groupthink. The safeguard is the feature Munger emphasized elsewhere: incentives, inversion, and active search for disconfirming evidence. A durable partnership remains useful only if familiarity makes truth easier to state rather than harder.

### Limits and a Balanced Reading of the Legacy

Munger's aphorisms are memorable because they compress complex ideas, but compression can hide conditions. "Wait," "concentrate," and "buy wonderful businesses" are incomplete without evidence, price, liquidity, and the ability to survive error. His own partnership's volatility is a direct counterweight to simplified imitation [4]. Berkshire's aggregate record also reflects a distinctive insurance-based capital structure, extraordinary partners, and decades of organizational development; an individual investor cannot assume the same conditions.

His psychology catalogue is similarly useful as a checklist, not as a substitute for domain research. Naming incentive-caused bias does not quantify an incentive. Calling an episode a lollapalooza does not establish which mechanisms dominate. The user must return to contracts, financial statements, operating data, interviews, and competing explanations. Munger's framework improves the questions; evidence still determines the answer.

The durable lesson is therefore procedural. Build knowledge across disciplines, organize it so it can be retrieved, study incentives, invert consequential decisions, demand a price that protects against error, act heavily only when understanding and evidence are unusually strong, and preserve a structure that can wait [3][5][6][11][12]. That process explains why Munger mattered as an investor and partner without requiring the claim that he was infallible.
