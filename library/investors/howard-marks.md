# Howard Marks -- Why Mastering Market Cycles and Second-Level Thinking Matters More Than Forecasting

Howard Marks is the co-founder and co-chairman of Oaktree Capital
Management, the world's largest distressed debt investment firm, and one
of the most influential living voices on risk, market cycles, and
investor psychology. His quarterly memos to clients, written since 1990,
are required reading on Wall Street -- Warren Buffett once remarked that
"when I see memos from Howard Marks in my mail, they're the first thing I
open and read." Marks built his career on the conviction that successful
investing depends less on predicting the future and more on understanding
where we stand in the market cycle and adjusting risk exposure
accordingly.

## Background

Howard Stanley Marks was born in 1946 in Queens, New York. He earned a
B.S. in Economics from the Wharton School at the University of
Pennsylvania, graduating cum laude, and an MBA in Accounting and
Marketing from the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, where
he received the George Hay Brown Prize for academic excellence. He later
became a CFA charterholder and Chartered Investment Counselor.

Marks began his career at Citicorp in 1969, where he specialized in
high-yield bonds -- assets then widely dismissed as "junk." This early
exposure to misunderstood, out-of-favor securities shaped his lifelong
conviction that markets frequently misprice risk and that the greatest
opportunities emerge where consensus is most fearful or dismissive. He
later moved to TCW Group, managing portfolios in high-yield and
distressed debt, and developing the disciplined, risk-conscious approach
that would define his career.

In 1995, Marks co-founded Oaktree Capital Management alongside Bruce
Karsh and several colleagues from TCW. The firm's mandate was
distinctive: it would prioritize risk management, transparency, and
consistency over speculative returns. Oaktree specialized in distressed
debt, high-yield bonds, and alternative credit strategies -- areas where
inefficiency and fear created mispricing opportunities. Over three
decades, Oaktree grew to manage over $180 billion in assets, becoming
the dominant institution in distressed debt. The firm's contrarian ethos
was famously captured by its motto: "If we avoid the losers, the winners
will take care of themselves."

The intellectual foundation of Marks' approach draws from multiple
traditions. From Benjamin Graham, he absorbed the margin of safety
concept and the critical distinction between price and intrinsic value.
From Warren Buffett, he adopted the emphasis on temperament and thinking
like an owner. From Charlie Munger, he took the multidisciplinary
approach and the recognition that psychology drives market behavior far
more than quantitative models capture. What Marks adds uniquely is his
systematic analysis of risk asymmetry -- developed through decades in
credit markets, where the downside of permanent capital loss is far more
consequential than missing out on upside.

## Core Concepts

### Second-Level Thinking

Second-level thinking is Marks' most foundational concept. First-level
thinking is simple, superficial, and available to everyone: "This is a
good company, so I should buy the stock." Second-level thinking goes
deeper, asking what is already priced in, what the consensus believes,
and how the consensus might be wrong. It considers not just the facts
about an investment but what others think about those facts -- and what
others think others think.

The critical insight is that first-level thinking cannot produce superior
returns because it is widely shared. If everyone can reach the same
conclusion from the same information, that conclusion is already
reflected in the price. To outperform, an investor must hold a view that
is both non-consensus and correct. This combination is rare and
difficult -- being contrarian for its own sake is not enough; the
contrarian bet must also be right.

Marks provides a concrete example: when economic data shows a recession
approaching, first-level thinking says "sell stocks." Second-level
thinking asks: "Is the recession already priced in? Has everyone else
already sold? Could this actually be a buying opportunity because
pessimism has overshot?" The second-level thinker does not automatically
do the opposite -- they assess what the market has already discounted and
position accordingly.

Second-level thinking requires intellectual rigor, independence, and
emotional discipline. It is uncomfortable because it means being
contrarian at exactly the moments when consensus feels most certain.
Marks notes that "to be a successful investor, you must be willing to
look wrong for a while." The greatest investment opportunities arise
precisely when second-level analysis leads to a conclusion that the crowd
rejects as foolish -- until the crowd eventually catches up.

### Risk as the Probability of Permanent Capital Loss

Marks fundamentally redefines risk compared to academic finance. In
modern portfolio theory, risk is measured by volatility -- the standard
deviation of returns, beta, and other statistical measures. Marks argues
that this definition is dangerously incomplete. The real risk in
investing is not price fluctuation but the probability of permanent
capital loss.

This distinction matters enormously because volatility-based models
treat short-term price declines and genuine business impairment as
equivalent, when they are not. A stock that drops 30% because of
temporary market panic is not the same as a stock that drops 30% because
the underlying business has permanently deteriorated. Marks' credit
market experience reinforced this view: in distressed debt, the key
question is not "how much did the bond price move this month?" but "will
the borrower repay principal and interest?"

Marks further argues that risk is counterintuitive and paradoxical. The
greatest risk arises not when risk is obvious -- after a market crash,
when everyone is fearful -- but when risk is invisible, at the peak of a
bull market when investors believe there is no risk. As he puts it, "the
riskiest thing in the world is the belief that there is no risk." Risk
cannot be measured after the fact; it exists only in the future and
manifests as a range of possible outcomes. A single realized outcome
tells you nothing about how risky the decision was at the time -- good
outcomes can come from reckless bets, and bad outcomes from prudent ones.

### The Pendulum and Market Cycles

Markets do not move in straight lines. They oscillate between extremes
of optimism and pessimism, greed and fear, risk tolerance and risk
aversion. Marks describes this as a pendulum: it spends almost no time
at the midpoint, swinging instead from one extreme to the other. The
investor's job is not to predict when the pendulum will reverse but to
recognize where it currently is and position accordingly.

Marks identifies the three stages of a bull market, as described to him
early in his career: (1) when only a few perceptive investors believe
things will improve, (2) when most investors recognize improvement is
underway, and (3) when everyone assumes things will get better forever.
The third stage is where fortunes are lost, because asset prices have
been bid up far beyond any reasonable assessment of intrinsic value.

Correspondingly, there are three stages of a bear market: (1) when only a
few cautious investors recognize that excess has built up, (2) when most
see that deterioration is happening, and (3) when everyone is convinced
things can only get worse. The third stage represents maximum pessimism
and, paradoxically, minimum risk -- prices have already collapsed, and
the worst-case scenario is largely discounted.

Marks emphasizes that cycles are not simply economic phenomena. They are
driven by human psychology -- the oscillation between euphoria and
despair, between the belief that trees grow to the sky and the belief
that the financial world is ending. Because human nature is constant,
cycles are inevitable. No amount of regulation, technology, or
sophistication will eliminate them. The investor who internalizes this
stops asking "will there be another cycle?" and starts asking "where are
we in this one?"

### The Price-Value Relationship

Marks considers the relationship between price and intrinsic value to be
the single most important concept in investing. His framework is
straightforward: "Good investing isn't about buying good things, it's
about buying things well." A wonderful company can be a terrible
investment if purchased at an inflated price, while a mediocre company
can be an excellent investment if purchased cheaply enough.

This insight seems simple but is routinely violated. Marks draws on his
early career experience with the "Nifty Fifty" -- a group of
high-quality U.S. growth companies in the early 1970s that were
considered so good that any price was justified. When valuations
collapsed in the bear market of 1973-1974, investors who had paid
inflated prices for excellent businesses suffered devastating losses.
The quality of the asset provided no protection against the price paid.

This principle combines with Marks' cycle awareness to produce his core
investment posture: be aggressive when prices are low relative to value
(when fear dominates), and be defensive when prices are high (when greed
dominates). The posture is not about market timing in the predictive
sense but about adjusting risk exposure as the pendulum swings.

## The Memos: A Practitioner's Record in Real Time

Since 1990, Marks has written quarterly investment memos to Oaktree
clients. These memos are not market forecasts -- Marks repeatedly states
that macro prediction is a fool's errand. Instead, they are meditations
on market psychology, risk, valuation, and the recurring patterns that
drive booms and busts. Their cumulative value lies in showing how a
disciplined intellectual framework holds up across different market
environments.

Several memos achieved legendary status through prescient timing. His
"bubble.com" memo in January 2000 warned that internet stocks had
detached from any rational valuation framework, just months before the
dot-com crash. In late 2008, at the depths of the financial crisis,
Marks wrote memos urging investors to buy distressed assets when almost
no one else was buying -- a call that proved spectacularly right as
markets recovered over the following years.

His December 2022 memo "Sea Change" argued that the era of declining
interest rates that had dominated the previous four decades was over.
Marks contended that investors who extrapolated the post-2008 low-rate
environment into the future were making a fundamental error. The
normalization of interest rates, he argued, would have profound
consequences for asset prices, investment strategies, and return
expectations -- a thesis that has been validated as rates have remained
elevated.

The memos reveal a consistent analytical method. Marks always asks three
questions: where are we in the cycle, how much optimism is already
reflected in current asset prices, and is this a time to invest
aggressively or defensively? The answers to these questions form the
compass for portfolio positioning.

## The Most Important Thing: Twenty Principles for Uncommon Sense

In 2011, Marks distilled his memos into a book, "The Most Important
Thing: Uncommon Sense for the Thoughtful Investor," later expanded and
re-released as "The Most Important Thing Illuminated" with annotations
from prominent investors. The book's title reflects Marks' central
insight: there is no single most important thing in investing. Each
principle -- second-level thinking, risk control, cycle awareness,
valuation discipline, contrarianism, patience -- is "the most important
thing" in its turn, and the successful investor must integrate them all.

The book is organized around twenty principles, each the subject of a
chapter. Key among them: understanding market efficiency and its limits
("markets are mostly efficient most of the time, but not perfectly
so"), the primacy of risk control over return maximization, the
importance of being attentive to cycles, the necessity of contrarian
thinking, and the value of patient opportunism -- waiting for the "fat
pitch" rather than swinging at every investment opportunity.

One of the book's most quoted passages captures Marks' philosophy of
epistemic humility: "For a piece of information to be desirable, it has
to satisfy two criteria: it has to be important, and it has to be
knowable." Most macroeconomic predictions pass the first test but fail
the second, yet investors continuously base decisions on them. Marks
argues that accepting the limits of what can be known is not a weakness
but a competitive advantage, because it prevents overconfidence and the
reckless bets that overconfidence produces.

Warren Buffett's endorsement is unusually strong even by his standards:
"This is a rarity, a useful book." He elaborated that he had recommended
it to his own investment staff and kept a copy on his desk. The
endorsement matters not just as marketing but as signal: Buffett, who
has read virtually every investment book, considers Marks' framework
genuinely additive to the Graham-and-Doddsville tradition.

## Evidence

The empirical case for Marks' approach comes from Oaktree's track
record. Over three decades, the firm navigated multiple crises -- the
Asian Financial Crisis (1997), the dot-com bust (2000-2002), the Global
Financial Crisis (2008-2009), the European sovereign debt crisis
(2010-2012), and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020) -- without suffering
catastrophic losses. On the contrary, Oaktree's distressed debt funds
produced their highest returns by deploying capital during periods of
maximum fear when competitors were liquidating or paralyzed.

Marks' specific market calls demonstrate the practical value of
second-level thinking and cycle awareness. His January 2000
"bubble.com" memo identified technology stock valuations as
unsustainable by referencing historical patterns of speculative manias,
from tulip bulbs to the South Sea Bubble. Oaktree avoided the carnage
that followed when the NASDAQ fell 78% from its peak.

Conversely, his late 2008 memos during the financial crisis argued that
prices in credit markets had overshot to the downside. Marks noted that
even if you assumed a severe recession and elevated defaults, many
distressed bonds were trading at prices that implied worse-than-Great
Depression outcomes. This was not a macro forecast -- it was a
second-level analysis of what was already priced in. Oaktree deployed
billions of dollars into distressed credit at these levels, generating
exceptional returns as markets normalized over 2009-2013.

The "Sea Change" memo of December 2022 provides a more recent test. At a
time when many investors were still operating on the assumption that
interest rates would return to the low levels of the 2010s, Marks argued
that the structural conditions had shifted -- inflation was unlikely to
return to the subdued levels of the post-GFC era, and central banks
would maintain higher rates. As of mid-2026, with rates remaining
elevated and many strategies premised on cheap leverage underperforming,
this thesis has held.

Academic research provides indirect support for Marks' framework. The
field of behavioral finance, pioneered by Kahneman and Tversky and
extended by Thaler, Shiller, and others, has documented the systematic
psychological biases -- overconfidence, herding, recency bias, loss
aversion -- that Marks identifies as the drivers of market cycles. His
descriptions of investor psychology during extremes map closely to the
patterns documented in the academic literature on bubbles and crashes.
Marks' contribution is not the discovery of these biases but their
translation into a practical investment framework.

The author's synthesis: Marks' approach represents a middle ground
between the efficient market hypothesis, which implies that active
management cannot consistently add value, and the view that markets are
chronically irrational and easy to beat. Marks acknowledges that markets
are "mostly efficient most of the time," making outperformance genuinely
difficult, but argues that periodic extremes of fear and greed create
windows of inefficiency large enough for disciplined investors to
exploit. This nuanced position is more consistent with the evidence than
either extreme.

## Implications

For the individual investor, Marks' framework offers several practical
applications. The most fundamental is a shift in mental model: stop
asking "what will happen?" and start asking "where are we in the cycle,
and how much of that is already priced in?" This reorientation moves the
investor away from the impossible task of prediction and toward the
achievable task of situational awareness.

Second-level thinking can be practiced as a deliberate discipline. Before
making any investment decision, the investor should explicitly write
down: what does the consensus believe about this asset? What would have
to be true for the consensus to be wrong? What is the margin of safety
at the current price? This simple exercise forces the investor to
confront the uncomfortable possibility that their initial instinct --
which is almost certainly first-level thinking shared by thousands of
other market participants -- may not be an edge at all.

Marks' risk framework is directly applicable to portfolio construction.
Standard portfolio theory optimizes for return given a volatility
constraint. Marks would instead ask: what is the probability of
permanent capital loss in each holding? How correlated are those loss
probabilities across the portfolio? What is the maximum plausible loss
in a severe but not impossible scenario? This approach, sometimes called
risk parity or capital preservation investing, leads to very different
allocations than mean-variance optimization.

The concept of the pendulum has implications for position sizing and
asset allocation. When the pendulum is at an extreme of optimism --
valuations are stretched, credit spreads are tight, and narratives are
uniformly bullish -- the investor should be defensive: smaller position
sizes, higher cash allocations, greater emphasis on capital preservation
over return maximization. When the pendulum is at an extreme of
pessimism -- valuations are compressed, spreads are wide, and narratives
are uniformly bearish -- the investor should gradually become more
aggressive. Marks acknowledges that getting the timing exactly right is
impossible, but getting the direction and magnitude broadly right is
achievable and enormously valuable over a full market cycle.

In the domain of investor psychology, Marks' emphasis on humility and
emotional control challenges the culture of conviction that dominates
much of finance. Investment professionals are rewarded for appearing
confident; clients and employers demand forecasts and certainty. Marks
argues that this creates a structural incentive to be overconfident and
that the best investors resist it. Accepting uncertainty is not passivity
-- it is a disciplined recognition that positions should be sized in
proportion to the strength of one's conviction and that conviction itself
should be calibrated against base rates.

For the broader investment ecosystem, Marks' framework implies that
active management has a legitimate but limited role. In efficient,
consensus-driven markets, passive indexing is the rational default. But
at the extremes, when fear or greed causes systematic mispricing, active
managers who combine second-level thinking with emotional discipline can
generate genuine alpha. This is a more nuanced defense of active
management than the industry typically offers, and it sets a high bar:
only those who truly think differently and better than the consensus
deserve to be paid for it.

## Criticism and Limitations

Marks' philosophy has attracted criticism on several fronts. The most
common is that his framework, while intellectually appealing, is
difficult to operationalize with precision. Identifying where the
pendulum is requires subjective judgment, and even experienced investors
have been wrong about market extremes. Marks acknowledges this
limitation: "we may never know where we're going, but we'd better know
where we are." The question is whether "knowing where we are" is
sufficiently reliable to improve outcomes, or whether it introduces a
new form of overconfidence -- the belief that one can read the cycle
when one is actually inside it.

A related criticism is survivorship bias. Oaktree succeeded where many
other distressed debt and contrarian investors failed. The investors who
bought at what they believed was the point of maximum pessimism -- only
to see prices fall further and their funds redeem -- do not give
lectures or write books. Marks' track record is genuine, but the
counterfactual is invisible, and the line between courage and
foolishness is clear only in retrospect.

Finally, some critics argue that Marks' skepticism about macroeconomic
forecasting is selectively applied. His "Sea Change" memo made a
directional call on interest rates that was, in substance, a macro
forecast, even if framed as a regime-change observation rather than a
point prediction. The distinction between "predicting the future" and
"preparing for a range of outcomes" can be blurry in practice.
