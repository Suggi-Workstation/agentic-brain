# Li Lu -- Patient Ownership Turned Deep Research into a Global Value-Investing Practice

Li Lu built Himalaya Capital around concentrated ownership, intensive business research, and the willingness to wait through severe market volatility rather than trade around it [1][3][4]. His path from a Tiananmen Square student leader to a Columbia graduate and then a trusted investment partner of Charlie Munger made his career unusually visible, but his durable significance lies in a process that joins intellectual honesty, clearly bounded competence, aligned capital, and long holding periods [1][5][6][7]. The public evidence supports a record of influential decisions and long-lived positions, while the private nature of Himalaya means that claims about complete fund performance require explicit caution [3][7][9].

## Background

Li Lu grew up in China and attended Nanjing University before becoming a student leader in the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests [5][6]. After leaving China, he reached the United States and entered Columbia University, where he completed a bachelor's degree in economics, a JD, and an MBA simultaneously in 1996 [5][6]. In his 2013 interview with Columbia Business School's Graham & Doddsville, Li described arriving without money, struggling with English, and carrying large student loans; that testimony gives his later emphasis on survival, capital protection, and self-knowledge a documented personal context rather than a retrospective legend [1].

The decisive intellectual event came in 1993, when Li attended a Warren Buffett lecture at Columbia [1][6]. Li recalled expecting something like a free lunch because he misunderstood Buffett's name, but he found Buffett's explanation of value investing immediately coherent: buy an ownership interest at a bargain price so that an error need not cause a large loss while a correct judgment can produce substantial gain [1]. The episode mattered because it supplied a method that fit his temperament. Reading, mathematics, sustained work, and independent judgment offered a path that did not depend on fluency in an established corporate culture [1].

Li founded Himalaya Capital in 1997, and the firm states that he has managed its principal fund since then [4]. The beginning was not smooth. The Financial Times reported that Himalaya lost 19 percent in its first year amid the Asian financial crisis and that a large investor subsequently withdrew [7]. Li later said that he had initially combined long investing with short selling, but abandoned the short book after concluding that it created unlimited downside, could remain wrong in price even when right about fraud, and diverted attention from the concentrated research required on the long side [1]. He called shorting one of his worst mistakes [1].

This early correction was part of a broader evolution. Li told Graham & Doddsville that he began with very cheap securities because a novice without extensive experience needed a large price cushion [1]. As he studied companies, he became more interested in the operating DNA of businesses: competitive strength, predictability, management, and the organizational capacity to adapt [1]. His description is not a rejection of price discipline. It is a shift from cheap securities as the object of analysis to superior businesses bought at prices that still protect against error [1][2].

Li's relationship with Charlie Munger strengthened that transition. Li described Munger as mentor, teacher, partner, and friend, and said their conversations developed from mutual contacts into a close bond centered on businesses and conduct [1]. The Financial Times later documented Munger's personal confidence in Li and his assessment that Li's early BYD investment was a minor miracle; Markets Insider separately reported Munger's statement that $88 million entrusted to Li became four to five times that amount [7][11]. Li's 2024 Peking University speech described a twenty-year friendship in which Munger's fishing analogy became an investment rule: do not spread effort equally across every market; go where attractive businesses, mispricing, knowledge, and limited competition coincide [3].

Himalaya's present public description reflects the resulting practice. The firm says it primarily studies public companies in Asia and North America and seeks long-term ownership of high-quality businesses with economic moats, growth potential, and trustworthy management; it also says that some holdings date back roughly two decades [4]. That description matches Li's earlier account of moving from low-priced securities toward durable businesses, but it remains a statement of philosophy rather than an independently verified performance report [1][4].

Li also developed a public teaching and writing role. His works include the memoir Moving the Mountain and Civilization, Modernization, Value Investing and China, while his lectures at Peking University's Guanghua School of Management set out his views on fiduciary duty, market economies, modernization, and global value investing [2][3][4]. He helped establish and support Guanghua's value-investing course, explicitly linking it to the Columbia tradition that had introduced him to Buffett [2][3]. This educational work is part of his influence: it transmits a practice shaped by Graham, Buffett, and Munger while adding Li's experience across Chinese and North American markets [2][3].

## Core Concepts

### Intellectual honesty defines the usable circle

Li treats intellectual honesty as the first operating control. In the 2013 interview, he divided knowledge into four categories: what an investor knows, what the investor does not know, what the investor does not need to know, and the possibility that the investor does not know what remains unknown [1]. This taxonomy is practical rather than philosophical. It determines which questions require more research, which opportunities should be rejected, and which macro uncertainties can be acknowledged without pretending to forecast them [1].

His circle of competence follows from that classification. Li said his personal interests in China, Asia, America, and businesses gradually defined where he could build an edge [1]. In his 2015 lecture, he argued that the key feature of a circle is not its size but its boundary: a defensible investment claim must include the premises that would disprove it [2]. A conclusion without stated failure conditions is not a circle of competence; it is confidence without a diagnostic test [2].

This approach makes rejection central to research. Li borrowed Munger's three buckets -- yes, no, and too hard -- and said that most ideas belong in the third [1]. The purpose is to reserve time and attention for situations the investor can understand more fully than competing market participants [1]. The author's synthesis is that this is a concentration rule before it is a portfolio rule: analysis must first be concentrated on a small set of knowable problems before capital can be concentrated in a small set of securities.

### Fundamental research seeks business understanding, not information volume

Li's research process begins broadly. He said ideas arrive through reading, annual reports, major publications, conversations with operators, customers, suppliers, entrepreneurs, and people with deeper expertise in their fields [1]. The source of an idea does not confer authority; the subsequent investigation determines whether the idea survives [1]. This separates idea generation from underwriting and reduces the risk of borrowing another investor's conviction without reproducing the evidence.

The endpoint is an owner-level model of the business. Li advised students to study one enterprise inside and out even if they never purchase it: how it makes money, organizes its finances, allocates excess cash, competes, adapts, and makes managerial decisions [1]. He connected that exercise to Buffett's proposition that a good investor must understand business and that a good businessperson must understand capital allocation [1]. The emphasis is therefore causal. Financial statements matter because they record a business system; they do not replace understanding that system.

Management enters the model, but Li resists easy character judgments. He said management always affects success, yet outsiders often cannot assess quality reliably from presentations or limited meetings [1]. When direct assessment is weak, the investor must rely more heavily on business quality, valuation, incentives, and other observable evidence, while admitting that a missing management judgment remains a limitation [1]. This restraint is important because a charismatic executive can create confidence without increasing knowledge.

Li's preferred forecasting question is also deliberately asymmetric. His Peking lecture asked whether an analyst can estimate what a business would look like if adverse developments accumulated over the next decade [2]. The goal is not a precise ten-year earnings forecast. It is to identify a defensible downside boundary, determine whether the enterprise can survive it, and require a purchase price that limits permanent impairment if the forecast is wrong [2]. This is Graham's margin of safety translated from a balance-sheet discount into a business-survival test.

### Concentration is an output of opportunity cost

Li does not begin with a predetermined regional or position allocation. He said opportunity determines where capital goes and that cash is the initial alternative because it does not decline in nominal terms [1]. A new security must improve the portfolio's overall risk-adjusted opportunity set relative to the existing holdings and cash [1]. This frames concentration as a consequence of scarcity: if only a few ideas exceed a high hurdle, a portfolio will naturally hold few material positions.

That logic differs from concentration based on enthusiasm. Research must establish a probability edge, a favorable downside-upside relationship, and an understanding deep enough to survive contradictory evidence and price volatility [1][2]. Li said investors should swing only when the informational and reasoning edge is overwhelming, while accepting that no forecast reaches certainty [1]. The author's assessment is that his process treats concentration as earned exposure. Position size follows the strength and rarity of the evidence; it does not create conviction by itself.

The public record is consistent with a focused style but does not reveal the whole portfolio. Himalaya's Form 13F for the quarter ended June 30, 2026 reported eight U.S. long-equity entries with an aggregate disclosed value of approximately $3.70 billion [9]. A 13F omits many relevant assets, including most non-U.S. securities, cash, private holdings, and some other instruments, so it cannot establish the concentration or performance of Himalaya's complete funds [9]. It does show that the visible U.S. book contains a small number of reportable entries, which is compatible with, but does not independently prove, Li's stated concentration practice.

### Patience depends on continuing knowledge

Long holding periods are not passive in Li's framework. In 2024 he said that BYD had been held for twenty-two years while its share price fell more than 50 percent at least six times and more than 80 percent once [3]. He presented those declines as tests of whether the investor actually understands the company's value, competitive position, and operating progress [3]. A long horizon without continuing research would be endurance without evidence; continuing research turns volatility into a test of the original model.

Li identified three normal reasons to sell: discovery of an analytical mistake, an extreme valuation that moves far beyond defensible value, or a superior opportunity with a better risk-reward relationship [1][3]. He added a fourth practical reason in 2024: a fund facing redemptions may have to sell because he rejects borrowing to meet withdrawals [3]. The no-borrowing principle protects the ability to remain solvent through a portfolio decline of roughly 50 percent, which Li described as a normal possibility in equity investing [3].

The selling rule also reveals why slight overvaluation does not automatically trigger action. Li argued that an exceptional company's long-term value creation can outrun an analyst's earlier estimate, making a sale difficult to reverse because the stock may continue to look expensive while the business compounds [3]. This is not permission to ignore price. It is recognition that taxes, opportunity cost, business quality, and the probability of finding an equivalent compounder all belong in the sell decision [1][3].

### Alignment converts a fund into patient capital

Li connected investment duration to fund structure. In 2013 he said Himalaya used a Buffett-partnership-style arrangement with no management fee, a 6 percent hurdle, and a 25 percent share of returns above the hurdle; he also said his own investment capital was held in the funds [1]. The arrangement reduced the incentive to gather assets merely to collect fees and made additional capital costly if it could not be deployed above the hurdle [1].

His 2015 lecture placed that structure inside a broader fiduciary standard. Li asked managers to treat every client dollar as the accumulated savings of their own parents and argued that asset management's opacity and high compensation create unusual potential for abuse [2]. In his account, fiduciary duty is not a marketing claim but a constraint on fees, leverage, asset growth, disclosure, and the treatment of downside risk [1][2]. The author's synthesis is that patient investing requires patient liabilities: concentrated positions cannot be held rationally if the fund's leverage, redemptions, or fee incentives force action during temporary declines.

### Global value investing is selective, not macro-dependent

Li's global orientation is rooted in personal familiarity and in his view that market economies create compounding business value across national boundaries [1][2][3]. He distinguishes accepting the macro environment from predicting it. His 2024 formulation was that macro conditions are objective constraints, while the micro level -- the choice and understanding of specific companies -- is where an investor can make a difference [3].

Munger's fishing metaphor sharpens that distinction. An investor does not need to master every economy or company; the task is to identify the limited markets and businesses where the investor has knowledge, favorable economics, and less competition [3]. Li calls his own additional principle the preservation of purchasing power through ownership of dynamic companies in vibrant economies [3]. Whether that contribution proves durable remains an empirical question, but it explains how he connects bottom-up security analysis with a global opportunity set without turning the process into macro trading.

## Evidence

### BYD joined local knowledge, management judgment, and patience

BYD is the clearest public case of Li's method. In 2013 he described founder Wang Chuanfu as an engineer who built a large enterprise from modest initial capital, assembled a strong engineering culture, and repeatedly solved difficult manufacturing problems faster and more cheaply than competitors [1]. Li's thesis emphasized demonstrated organizational learning and the ability to adapt, not a point forecast for one battery chemistry or vehicle model [1].

Li introduced BYD to Charlie Munger, and Berkshire Hathaway bought 225 million shares in September 2008 for an 8.25 percent stake, according to CNBC's account of the transaction and BYD's reporting [8]. The Financial Times later quoted Munger describing the early BYD investment as a minor miracle and credited Li with selling him on the company [7]. These sources support Li's influence on a major Berkshire decision, although they do not show that Li alone made Berkshire's final underwriting or sizing choice [7][8].

The holding also provides evidence for patience under volatility. Li said in 2024 that Himalaya had owned BYD for twenty-two years, had endured at least six drawdowns greater than 50 percent, and had seen company sales rise from roughly RMB1 billion to nearly RMB1 trillion [3]. Those figures are Li's own account and should be read as primary testimony, not an independently audited return series [3]. They nevertheless document the duration of the relationship and the scale of operating change that his research process was designed to follow.

Patience did not mean never selling. South China Morning Post reported that Himalaya sold 10.8 million BYD H shares in July 2021 for approximately HKD2.44 billion, reducing the reported stake from 7.03 percent to 6 percent [10]. The sale shows that a multi-decade owner can trim a position while remaining invested. Public filings reveal the transaction, but not the complete portfolio context or Li's internal opportunity-cost calculation, so the motive should not be inferred beyond the disclosed facts [10].

### Capital IQ and short selling show learning through opposite outcomes

Li's early venture investment in Capital IQ illustrates a successful decision he still regarded as imperfectly held. He said he was the first investor, helped build a product for investment-banking users after recognizing how difficult Bloomberg was to challenge directly, and later sold before the full value became visible [1]. He did not frame the early sale as a grievance because the founders, employees, customers, and later owners all benefited [1]. The case supports his preference for win-win value creation, but no audited transaction return was provided in the inspected source [1].

Short selling supplies negative evidence. Li said the practice was one of his worst mistakes because the payoff was reversed relative to a long position, fraud could persist longer than financing allowed, and the mental burden displaced long-side research [1]. He acknowledged that skilled short sellers exist and framed the conclusion as a mismatch with his own temperament and process rather than a universal prohibition [1]. That distinction is important: the lesson was not that every short is analytically wrong, but that a strategy can be structurally unsuitable even when individual theses are correct.

The 19 percent first-year loss reported by the Financial Times adds an earlier stress test [7]. The loss occurred during the Asian financial crisis and was followed by an investor withdrawal [7]. Li's later emphasis on aligned, patient capital and avoidance of forced selling is consistent with that experience, although the source does not establish a simple one-event causal chain between the loss and every later fund-design choice [1][7].

### Munger's capital supplies external but incomplete validation

Munger's decision to entrust personal capital to Li is unusually strong evidence of confidence from a practitioner known for selectivity [7][11]. Markets Insider reported Munger's statement to the Financial Times that $88 million became four to five times that amount over a long period [7][11]. This supports the claim that Li delivered meaningful results for at least one sophisticated investor, but it is not a substitute for a complete, fee-consistent, independently audited Himalaya return series across all vehicles and dates.

The same limitation applies to public portfolio data. The 2026 Form 13F reports eight entries and approximately $3.70 billion in disclosed U.S. long equities [9]. It is primary regulatory evidence for those positions and that filing date, but not for private holdings, Asian securities, cash, liabilities, realized returns, or assets in separate vehicles [9]. Any performance estimate reconstructed from 13F filings would therefore be partial and potentially misleading.

The author's assessment is that the evidence supports three conclusions and leaves one material gap. First, Li built and has continuously led Himalaya since 1997 [4][5]. Second, documented decisions such as BYD, Capital IQ, and the abandonment of short selling reveal a coherent process that changed in response to experience [1][3][7][8]. Third, Munger's partnership and Berkshire's BYD investment demonstrate influence beyond Li's own firm [7][8]. The unresolved gap is the absence, in the inspected public sources, of a complete independently audited return history that would permit uniform comparison with a benchmark across Himalaya's full life.

### Writings and teaching extend influence beyond positions

Li's 2015 lecture organized value investing around ownership, Mr. Market, margin of safety, and circle of competence, then joined those principles to fiduciary duty and the development of China's capital markets [2]. His 2024 lecture added two claims from later practice: investors should fish where knowledge and opportunity are concentrated, and wealth should be understood as a share of purchasing power preserved through ownership of dynamic companies [3]. These are primary sources for how Li explains his method; they do not by themselves prove that every proposition generates excess returns.

The teaching record matters because it makes the process inspectable. Li states his boundaries, identifies short selling as a mistake, names conditions for selling, and explains why a long holding still requires continuous study [1][3]. That degree of disclosure allows readers to test the internal consistency of his practice even when the complete portfolio and return series remain private. His influence is therefore partly institutional: he helped create a bridge between the Graham-and-Dodd tradition at Columbia and formal value-investing education in China [2][3][6].

## Implications

### For fundamental investors: depth must precede conviction

Li's career challenges the common sequence in which an investor forms a strong view, takes a large position, and then searches for confirming detail. His sequence runs in the opposite direction: classify the problem, reject what is too hard, build an owner-level model, state the disconfirming premises, estimate the downside, and only then let the rarity of the opportunity determine concentration [1][2]. The implication is that conviction is an output of work and demonstrated understanding, not a personality trait.

This produces a practical research discipline. An analyst should be able to explain how the company makes money, why customers and suppliers behave as they do, how management allocates capital, which competitive response would damage the model, and what adverse ten-year conditions the balance sheet and business system could survive [1][2]. If those questions cannot be answered, a low multiple does not create competence. It creates an inexpensive-looking claim whose risk remains unidentified.

Li's method also separates volatility from impairment without dismissing volatility. A 50 percent price decline may be an opportunity, but it can also expose an analytical error, financing weakness, or deteriorating business [2][3]. The decision to hold or buy more depends on updated business evidence, not on the mere fact that the price is lower. BYD's repeated drawdowns were useful tests only because Li continued to study its operating progress and believed his circle remained valid [3].

### For portfolio managers: structure determines whether patience is real

A manager cannot promise a long horizon while using liabilities that demand short-term liquidity. Li's no-borrowing preference, substantial hurdle before incentive compensation, personal capital commitment, and resistance to gathering assets all reduce the probability that market declines force the portfolio to sell [1][3]. The broader implication is that the investment vehicle must be evaluated with the same rigor as the securities. Redemption terms, leverage, fees, partner expectations, and cash policy determine whether the stated philosophy can survive a crisis.

This is especially important for concentrated funds. Concentration increases the consequence of analytical error and can create severe reported volatility even when the underlying businesses remain intact [3][9]. A manager needs enough liquidity to meet legitimate withdrawals, enough alignment that investors understand the strategy, and enough humility to distinguish temporary price pressure from permanent impairment. Li's first-year loss and later rejection of shorting show that portfolio design can change when a structure conflicts with temperament or survival [1][7].

The fee lesson is equally direct. Asset-based management fees can reward growth in assets under management even when opportunities deteriorate [2]. Li's reported partnership formula made new capital a burden unless it could clear the hurdle, which better aligned the manager's economics with investment results [1]. The author's assessment is that no fee structure guarantees good decisions, but a structure that pays the manager for scale regardless of outcomes creates a conflict that long-term rhetoric cannot remove.

### For global investors: local knowledge is an analytical asset, not a macro forecast

Li's background gave him cultural and institutional familiarity with China while his education and career gave him access to North American markets [1][5][6]. He used that combination to study businesses across both regions, but his public process does not require a precise forecast for every political or economic variable [1][3]. Instead, macro conditions define the environment and the analyst searches for companies whose economics, balance sheets, and adaptive capacity can survive a range of outcomes [2][3].

The implication is not that country risk can be ignored. Li's 2024 lecture devoted substantial attention to property rights, market institutions, international relations, and the limits of China's capital-market development [3]. The investable question is whether a specific company's value creation, governance, financing, and competitive position remain robust within those constraints. Global reach expands the opportunity set only when the investor has the knowledge needed to distinguish local advantage from unfamiliar risk.

Munger's fishing principle also warns against equating a large market with a good hunting ground [3]. Opportunity depends on the number and quality of businesses, the intensity of competition among analysts, the investor's own competence, and the availability of a margin of safety. A small, neglected segment can offer better odds than the largest and most fashionable market. This makes global investing selective rather than comprehensive.

### For students of Buffett and Munger: Li Lu is a practitioner, not merely a disciple

Li clearly credits Graham, Buffett, and Munger for the central architecture of his philosophy [1][2][3]. Yet his career adds distinct evidence about applying that architecture across China and North America, converting an early long-short fund into a patient long-owner model, and using personal experience to frame fiduciary duty and intellectual honesty [1][2][4][7]. His significance is therefore not originality in every principle. It is the adaptation and execution of inherited principles under different markets, institutions, and stages of economic development.

His relationship with Munger is reciprocal evidence. Munger influenced Li's focus on great businesses, hard-edged rejection, and fishing where opportunity exists; Li introduced Munger and Berkshire to BYD and managed Munger family capital [3][7][8][11]. The relationship demonstrates how a tradition compounds through partnership: principles flow from teacher to practitioner, while new markets and company knowledge flow back to the teacher.

Li's educational work extends that chain. The Peking University lectures make value investing a discussion of ethics, institutions, business ownership, and purchasing power rather than a low-multiple screen [2][3]. For a student, the central test is not whether those claims sound persuasive, but whether the student can reproduce Li's discipline of stated boundaries, disconfirming evidence, patient capital, and post-mortem learning.

### Limits and what would change the assessment

The strongest criticism is evidentiary opacity. Himalaya is private, its website does not publish a complete audited return history, and regulatory filings cover only selected reportable assets [4][9]. Munger's testimony and individual cases are meaningful, but favorable testimonials and celebrated winners can coexist with omitted losses, different fee bases, or vehicle-specific outcomes. A fuller assessment would require audited fund returns, exposure and cash data, drawdowns, fees, and benchmark definitions across the complete operating period.

A second limitation is concentration. The same structure that lets a correct thesis matter can make a mistaken thesis, governance failure, or country shock disproportionately harmful [3][9]. Li's rejection of leverage and emphasis on margin of safety reduce that danger but cannot eliminate it. The BYD record shows both the reward from a long-lived compounder and the interim price declines a concentrated owner must survive [3].

The author's assessment would strengthen if independently audited data confirmed superior risk-adjusted returns across multiple complete cycles and vehicles after fees, and if future cases showed the same research process succeeding beyond the best-known holdings. It would weaken if the private record materially diverged from public testimonials, if concentration produced permanent losses inconsistent with the stated downside discipline, or if the global framework depended more on favorable country outcomes than on business-level resilience. Those tests preserve the distinction Li himself emphasizes: reputation can identify a question, but evidence must decide it.
