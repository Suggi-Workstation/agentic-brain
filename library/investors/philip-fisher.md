# Philip Fisher -- The Qualitative Growth Investor Who Taught Buffett to Hold

Philip Arthur Fisher (1907-2004) was the pioneer of qualitative growth
investing, a man who built his career on the conviction that the most
important facts about a company cannot be found in its financial
statements. Through his 1958 book Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits,
his "scuttlebutt" research method, and his fifteen-point checklist for
evaluating management quality, Fisher created a framework that
redirected security analysis from the search for statistically cheap
stocks toward the study of business quality, innovation, and
organizational durability. His influence on Warren Buffett -- who
described himself as "15 percent Fisher and 85 percent Graham" -- makes
Fisher the missing link between Benjamin Graham's quantitative value
discipline and the quality-focused, hold-forever approach that defines
modern compounder investing.

## Background

Philip Arthur Fisher was born on September 8, 1907, in San Francisco,
California, the oldest child of Arthur Lawrence Fisher, an orthopedic
surgeon, and Eugenia Samuels Fisher. His paternal ancestors had
immigrated to San Francisco from Prague and Germany in the 1850s. Fisher
grew up in a household where discussions of business and markets were
commonplace; an uncle who visited during grammar school years helped
kindle his lifelong fascination with investment opportunities. The death
of his father in 1959, when Fisher was 52, reinforced his sense of
responsibility and financial independence, values that would shape his
investment philosophy throughout his career.

Fisher's formal education was at Stanford University, where he studied
economics. He enrolled in the newly created Stanford Graduate School of
Business but dropped out in 1928 to begin working as a securities
analyst at the Anglo-London Bank in San Francisco. The decision to leave
graduate school for practical experience was characteristic: Fisher
would later return to Stanford as one of only three people ever to teach
the investment course there, but his fundamental education came from the
market itself, not from academic theory. After a brief stint at a stock
exchange firm, he founded his own money management company, Fisher &
Co., on March 31, 1931, at the age of 23.

The timing was extraordinary. Fisher launched his investment counseling
firm in the depths of the Great Depression, when the Dow Jones
Industrial Average had lost nearly 90 percent of its value from its 1929
peak. Most of the financial industry was in retreat, but Fisher saw
opportunity in the wreckage. He understood that the companies that would
emerge from the depression with durable competitive advantages would
compound wealth for decades, and that the key to identifying them was
not statistical cheapness -- everything was cheap -- but qualitative
excellence in management, products, and organizational capability. This
conviction, formed during the worst market conditions of the century,
became the foundation of his entire investment method.

Fisher & Co. operated from the same San Francisco office building for
over 35 years, and Fisher was famously selective about his clients. He
never served more than a dozen at a time, preferring depth of
understanding over scale of assets. He was a very private person who
gave few interviews and avoided the spotlight. John Train, who profiled
Fisher in his 1980 book The Money Masters, described him as "the most
famous of the older generation of investment counsellors in San
Francisco" and noted that during thirty-five years in his present office
building, "neither he nor his office has changed much." Fisher's
reputation as "an original, profound, and remorselessly thorough
investment thinker" grew steadily among professionals who understood
his work, even as he remained almost unknown to the general public until
he published his first book at the age of 51.

The publication of Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits in 1958 by Harper
& Brothers was the event that transformed Fisher from a respected but
obscure counselor into a recognized pioneer. The book appeared during
the post-World War II bull market, a period of great prosperity and
optimism about American industrial growth. Fisher argued that investors
should look beyond statistical bargains and seek companies with the
organizational capability to sustain growth for many years. The book's
reception was immediate and positive within investment circles; it
established Fisher as the father of growth investing as a disciplined
analytical approach, distinct from mere speculation in fast-rising
stocks.

Fisher followed Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits with three
additional books: Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks (1960),
Conservative Investors Sleep Well (1975), and Developing an Investment
Philosophy (1980). Each developed and refined the qualitative framework
introduced in the first book. Conservative Investors Sleep Well, in
particular, made the case for the quality-and-risk framework explicit:
investors who selected truly superior businesses with durable
competitive advantages could hold them through market cycles and sleep
soundly, because the underlying companies would continue to compound
value regardless of short-term price fluctuations.

Fisher served in the Army Air Corps during World War II, interrupting
his investment career but returning to Fisher & Co. afterward with his
methods intact. His son, Kenneth L. Fisher, founded Fisher Investments
in 1979 and built it into a major institutional asset manager, extending
the family's investment legacy. Philip Fisher retired at the age of 91
and died on March 11, 2004, at his home in San Mateo, California, at
age 96. He had held his Motorola shares -- purchased in 1955 -- for
nearly 49 years until his death.

## Core Concepts

### The Scuttlebutt Method

Fisher's most original and enduring methodological contribution is the
scuttlebutt method, described at length in Chapter 2 of Common Stocks
and Uncommon Profits. The term derives from the "scuttlebutt," the
water barrel on sailing ships where sailors gathered to exchange gossip.
Fisher adapted it into a formal research practice: building an
investment thesis not by reading published financial statements alone,
but by talking systematically to people who know the company from many
different angles.

The scuttlebutt source universe includes customers, suppliers,
competitors, former employees, current employees, research scientists at
the company and its rivals, distributors, trade-association figures, and
industry consultants. Fisher also recommended asking several companies
in an industry about one another -- a comparative element he considered
essential. Each conversation yields a fragment of evidence about product
quality, management depth, competitive position, sales effectiveness, or
corporate culture. Aggregated and cross-checked, the fragments produce a
picture of how a company actually operates that no SEC filing or annual
report can convey.

A critical feature of Fisher's method is the sequencing of management
contact. He did not begin with executives. He investigated customers,
suppliers, competitors, and former employees first, assembling
substantial outside knowledge before meeting management. His practical
rule was to possess at least half the information required for the
investment decision before the management meeting. The reasoning is
direct: an investor who meets management too early enters a conversation
the company largely controls. Executives choose the framing, emphasize
strengths, describe weaknesses as temporary, and direct attention toward
preferred metrics. A chief executive can speak beautifully about culture
and long-term thinking, but that fluency proves only that the executive
can speak beautifully -- not that the research pipeline works, customers
struggle to switch, or middle management is strong. By arriving with
independent evidence, the investor can test management's account against
what customers, suppliers, and competitors have already said, and
recognize inconsistency, evasion, or credible explanation.

The scuttlebutt method is labor-intensive. A single thoroughly
researched company might take months of work, involving dozens of
conversations across multiple parts of the commercial ecosystem. This is
exactly why Fisher believed it produced an edge: most institutional
investors would not do it. The method demanded patience, persistence,
and a willingness to accept that inadequate information should stop the
investigation rather than force a premature decision.

### The Fifteen Points to Look for in a Common Stock

Chapter 3 of Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits presents Fisher's
fifteen-point checklist, the structured framework that compresses his
entire qualitative method into a set of questions an investor must
answer about a candidate stock. The points cover the full spectrum of
business quality, from product-line potential to management integrity:

1. Does the company have products or services with sufficient market
   potential to make possible a sizable increase in sales for at least
   several years?
2. Does management have the determination to continue developing
   products and processes that will further increase total sales
   potential when current growth engines are exhausted?
3. How effective is the company's research and development effort
   relative to its size?
4. Does the company have an above-average sales organization?
5. Does the company have a worthwhile profit margin?
6. What is being done to maintain or improve profit margins?
7. Does the company have outstanding labor and personnel relations?
8. Does the company have outstanding executive relations?
9. Does the company have depth to its management?
10. How good are the company's cost analysis and accounting controls?
11. Are there other aspects of the business, peculiar to the industry,
    which give important clues as to outstanding capability?
12. Does the company have a short-range or long-range outlook in regard
    to profits?
13. In the foreseeable future, will the growth of the company require
    sufficient equity financing so that the larger share count cancels
    existing shareholders' benefit from the growth?
14. Does management talk freely with investors when things are going
    well but "clam up" when troubles or disappointments occur?
15. Does the company have a management of unquestionable integrity?

Fisher held that an honest "yes" to all fifteen points is rare. A
company does not need a perfect score, but it should fail on very few.
The most important point is the fifteenth -- management integrity --
which Fisher considered non-negotiable. Regardless of how high a company
scores on every other point, if there is any serious question about
management integrity, the investor should walk away. Insiders can enrich
themselves at shareholders' expense in nearly infinite ways without
breaking any law: excessive compensation, renting personal properties to
the company above market rates, routing vendors through insider-owned
brokerage firms, issuing excessive stock options. Fisher was absolute on
this: no exceptions.

The fifteen points work best as a connected causal system rather than a
ceremonial checklist. Each qualitative observation should reveal a
business capability, connect to an economic consequence, and remain open
to disconfirming evidence. The points about product potential and R&D
effectiveness (1-3) test whether the company can generate future growth.
The points about sales organization, profit margins, and cost controls
(4-6, 10) test whether the company can convert that growth into
shareholder value. The points about labor, executive relations, and
management depth (7-9) test organizational durability. The points about
industry-specific factors and long-range outlook (11-12) test strategic
orientation. The point about equity financing (13) tests capital
discipline. The points about communication and integrity (14-15) test
character. Together, they form a comprehensive qualitative assessment of
whether a company is built to compound value over decades.

### Concentration and the Case Against Excessive Diversification

Fisher was a concentration investor. He limited his portfolios to
roughly 30 well-researched stocks, and often far fewer -- in some
periods, his book held only 12 to 15 names. His argument was not that
diversification is bad in principle but that diversification past the
point at which the investor can maintain real depth on each name is a
sign of incomplete homework, not prudent risk management. The dilution
penalty of holding mediocre twenty-fifth-best ideas alongside the best
three ideas was greater than the diversification benefit.

This principle is laid out explicitly in Conservative Investors Sleep
Well (1975). Fisher argued that the investor who truly understands a
small number of exceptional businesses faces less risk than the
investor who spreads capital across many companies understood only
superficially. The apparent safety of broad diversification is an
illusion when it means owning companies the investor has not thoroughly
researched. Real safety comes from depth of knowledge, not breadth of
holdings.

Fisher's son Kenneth Fisher continued this philosophical commitment to a
tight book in his own writing and in building Fisher Investments, though
the institutional firm necessarily operated with broader portfolios than
the father's intimate counseling practice. The concentration principle
is one of two main points of contact between Fisher's framework and
Warren Buffett's approach, the other being the focus on management
quality. Buffett's own concentrated portfolio -- Berkshire Hathaway's
largest positions often representing enormous percentages of total
equity -- traces its intellectual lineage directly to Fisher's argument
that depth beats breadth.

### The Selling Discipline: When to Sell Is Almost Never

Fisher's selling discipline is as distinctive as his buying discipline.
In Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits, Chapter 6 addresses when to sell,
and the chapter's brevity -- fewer than ten pages -- is itself part of
the argument: there is not much to say, because the answer is mostly no.
Fisher identified only three valid reasons to sell a correctly purchased
stock. First, the original purchase was a mistake -- the thesis that
justified buying was wrong, and the error should be acknowledged and
corrected. Second, the company no longer qualifies as the same quality
of investment it was at purchase, typically because management quality
degraded or product leadership was lost. Third, a clearly superior
opportunity has emerged that requires capital, and the capital cannot be
raised any other way.

Fisher explicitly rejected the reasons most retail investors actually
use to sell: the price has risen, the price has fallen, the cycle looks
late, the multiple looks high relative to history, the chart looks weak.
None of these, in Fisher's framework, are reasons to part with ownership
in a great business. The discipline of holding through volatility,
drawdowns, and market cycles is the natural consequence of having done
the scuttlebutt work thoroughly before buying. If the investor truly
understands the business -- its products, its management, its
competitive position -- then short-term price movements are noise, not
signal. This philosophy directly shaped Buffett's famous statement in
his 1988 Berkshire Hathaway letter that his "favorite holding period is
forever."

### The Growth Rate Is the Output, Not the Input

A subtle but critical element of Fisher's framework is his treatment of
historical growth rates. A rising historical growth rate is a seductive
result, but Fisher insisted it is not an explanation. The question is
not whether a company has grown but whether the organizational machinery
that produced that growth -- research effectiveness, sales capability,
management depth, cost controls -- remains intact and capable of
generating the next generation of growth. If the internal gears are not
aligned to continuously produce renewal, the upward-pointing line is
just a pleasant memory waiting to end.

This insight is what distinguished Fisher's approach from the naive
growth investing that chases companies with impressive historical
revenue charts. Fisher did not invent the idea of investing in growing
companies; investors already wanted growing companies before his book
appeared in 1958. What they lacked was a convincing way to distinguish a
company capable of renewing its growth from one enjoying a temporary
product cycle, a fashionable market, or a management team skilled at
narrating the future. Fisher's contribution was to systematize a
qualitative process for judging whether a company possessed the
organizational capabilities needed to sustain growth over time. Durable
growth, in Fisher's view, was an organizational production problem, not
a market timing problem.

### Valuation: Not Price-Blind, But Price-Secondary

Fisher is sometimes caricatured as indifferent to valuation, but this
is inaccurate. He discussed price, buying, and selling in his books and
said investors should find something exceptionally good without paying
too much for it. His position was not that valuation does not matter but
that valuation is secondary to business quality. A strong company can
still disappoint when the price assumes a longer or more profitable
growth runway than the business delivers. Fisher's discipline was to
understand the business deeply enough to judge whether the price
reflected a defensible estimate of future compounding, and to avoid
paying prices that embedded heroic assumptions.

The practical consequence is that Fisher would pay fair prices for
exceptional businesses rather than demanding the steep statistical
discounts that Graham's cigar-butt approach required. This is the
intellectual bridge to Buffett's later formulation: "It is far better to
buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a
wonderful price." Fisher's framework justified paying fair -- not
statistically cheap -- prices for businesses whose organizational
excellence would compound value for decades, making the initial price
look cheap in retrospect.

## Evidence

### The Motorola Investment: 49 Years of Compounding

Fisher's most famous investment is Motorola, which he purchased in 1955
and held until his death in 2004 -- nearly 49 years. When Fisher
invested, Motorola's position in the semiconductor industry was
insignificant by Wall Street standards. Through scuttlebutt --
conversations with engineers, customers, and industry contacts -- Fisher
satisfied himself that Motorola's research and development capability
and competitive position were substantially stronger than the market
appreciated. The position became Fisher & Co.'s largest holding.

The returns were extraordinary. According to Fisher's own account, every
$1,000 invested in Motorola in 1957 grew to approximately $1,993,846 --
a return exceeding 20 percent annually over the holding period. The
investment generated handsome returns not through trading in and out but
through patient compounding as Motorola evolved from a small
telecommunications equipment maker into a global technology
conglomerate. Fisher never sold, embodying his own principle that the
time to sell a correctly purchased stock is almost never.

The Motorola case demonstrates the core of Fisher's method in practice.
The decision to buy was based on qualitative research -- understanding
the organizational machinery behind the financial outputs -- not on
statistical cheapness. The decision to hold was based on continued
confidence in that organizational machinery, tested through ongoing
scuttlebutt over decades. The return was a consequence of the business
compounding, not of market timing or price speculation. Fisher's
willingness to hold through every market cycle, recession, and
technology shift for half a century is the practical proof of his
thesis that depth of knowledge enables patience, and patience enables
compounding.

### The Texas Instruments Investment: Early Technology Vision

Fisher's second signature investment was Texas Instruments, which he
acquired in 1956, long before the company went public in 1970. Fisher
bought shares privately at a price around $2.70, based on his
assessment of the company's commitment to research and development, its
focus on innovation, and its forward-thinking management. He saw a
company that was not content with current success but was always looking
to the horizon for the next opportunity in semiconductors -- a field
that, in 1956, most Americans were only beginning to understand through
transistor radios.

The Texas Instruments investment compounded spectacularly. By the time
the stock reached the public market and eventually traded as high as
$200, the gain was approximately 7,400 percent without dividends.
Fisher's own gains were significantly higher because he had purchased
shares privately, before the IPO, at a lower base. By the time Fisher
wrote about this investment in his 1980 book Developing an Investment
Philosophy, his initial stake had grown twentyfold from the already
substantial private-purchase levels.

This investment illustrates Fisher's early identification of the
technology sector as a source of durable growth, decades before the name
"Silicon Valley" existed. Fisher specialized in innovative companies
driven by research and development -- the type of firm for which
California would become known. His earliest holdings were relatively
low-tech by later standards (Dow Chemical, Food Machinery Corporation),
but by the mid-1950s he was investing in the semiconductor pioneers
that would define the second half of the twentieth century. The Texas
Instruments case shows that Fisher's qualitative method was not limited
to any industry: it was a general framework for identifying
organizational capability, applicable wherever research, innovation, and
management quality drove long-term value.

### Buffett's Explicit Attribution: 85 Percent Graham, 15 Percent Fisher

The most consequential evidence of Fisher's influence is Warren
Buffett's repeated and explicit attribution of his investment philosophy
to two mentors: Benjamin Graham and Philip Fisher. Buffett stated on
multiple occasions that he was "15 percent Fisher and 85 percent
Graham." The Fisher contribution, Buffett explained, was the
quality-and-management orientation that justified paying fair prices for
great businesses and holding them for the long term, rather than
demanding statistical bargains and selling when valuations reached
targets.

At a Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, Buffett was asked to explain the
15 percent Fisher component. He recounted reading Fisher's books in the
late 1950s -- Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits and Paths to Wealth
Through Common Stocks -- and then traveling to San Francisco to meet
Fisher in person. Buffett described Fisher's scuttlebutt approach:
going out and talking to competitors, customers, and suppliers to
develop an opinion about what the "castle" is like and what the "moat"
is like. Buffett said this approach had a real effect on him and that
he continued to use it. He specifically cited the technique of asking
any company in an industry, "If you had to invest in one other company
in this industry besides your own, who would it be?" and working around
the industry to build a picture.

In his 2012 Berkshire Hathaway annual report, Buffett ranked Fisher's
Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits behind only The Intelligent Investor
and the 1940 edition of Security Analysis as essential reading for
serious investors. At the 2018 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting,
Buffett called the book "a very, very good book" and described how the
scuttlebutt technique continues to be used by Ted Weschler and Todd
Combs at Berkshire Hathaway. Robert Hagstrom, in The Warren Buffett Way,
notes that while Buffett stated the 85-15 split in 1990, he had probably
moved closer to Fisher over time as his approach evolved from strict
value toward quality-focused growth investing.

Buffett also wrote a 1987 Forbes article titled "What we can learn from
Philip Fisher," in which he described seeking Fisher out after reading
the 1958 book: "When I met him, I was as impressed by the man as by his
ideas." Buffett added that "Much like Ben Graham, Fisher was unassuming,
generous in spirit and an extraordinary teacher," and that even though
Graham and Fisher's investment approaches differed, they were "parallel
in the investment world." John Train, in The Money Masters (1980),
described Buffett as 85 percent influenced by Graham and 15 percent by
Fisher, an assessment Buffett himself has confirmed.

### The Fisher Framework in Modern Quality Investing

Fisher's influence extends well beyond Buffett through the broader
school of quality and compounder investing. The emphasis on durable
competitive advantages, management quality, long-term holding, and
concentration that defines modern quality investing traces its
analytical lineage to Fisher's 1958 framework. Investors who focus on
economic moats, return on invested capital, and the durability of
business quality are operating within a tradition that Fisher
systematized, even when they do not cite him directly.

The persistence of Fisher's methods in professional practice is
documented by their continued use at Berkshire Hathaway, where the
scuttlebutt approach remains part of the research process. More broadly,
the qualitative research practices that institutional investors use --
channel checks, expert network interviews, supplier and customer surveys
-- are formalized descendants of Fisher's scuttlebutt method, adapted
for a regulatory environment that Fisher did not face but pursuing the
same goal: understanding the operating reality behind the financial
statements. Fisher's insight that the most important facts about a
company are qualitative, not quantitative, has been absorbed so deeply
into professional investing practice that it has become part of the
water supply, even as the name of the man who first systematized it is
less widely known than the names of those he influenced.

## Implications

### For Individual Investors: Depth Over Breadth

Fisher's most direct implication for individual investors is the
primacy of depth over breadth. The scuttlebutt method and the
fifteen-point checklist demand that the investor know a company
thoroughly before committing capital -- not just its financial metrics,
but its products, its management, its competitive position, its
organizational culture, and the industry context in which it operates.
This is the opposite of the diversified, screening-based approach that
most retail investors and many institutional investors use. Fisher's
argument is that the screening approach produces superficial knowledge,
and superficial knowledge is not safe regardless of how many companies
it is spread across.

The practical consequence is that Fisher's method favors investors who
have the time, temperament, and patience to do deep research on a small
number of companies. The scuttlebutt method -- talking to customers,
suppliers, competitors, and former employees -- is accessible to
individual investors who are willing to do the work, and in some ways
more accessible than to institutional investors constrained by
compliance, scale, and time pressures. Fisher himself operated with
only a dozen clients and a small portfolio, demonstrating that the
method is designed for concentration, not for managing billions across
hundreds of positions.

For individual investors, Fisher's framework also implies a radically
different approach to selling. The discipline of holding through
volatility, of not selling because the price has risen or fallen, of
revising the thesis only when the underlying facts change, is
counterintuitive in a culture that equates activity with intelligence.
Fisher's implication is that most selling is a mistake born of
impatience or fear, and that the investor who has done the scuttlebutt
work thoroughly has the knowledge base to hold through the noise that
drives others to act. The Motorola holding -- 49 years through every
market cycle -- is the extreme expression of this discipline.

### For Institutional Investors: The Scuttlebutt Descendants

For institutional investors, Fisher's framework has been formalized and
scaled in ways that both preserve and dilute his original method. The
expert network industry -- firms that connect investors with industry
consultants, former employees, and domain experts -- is a direct
commercial descendant of scuttlebutt. Channel checks, customer surveys,
and supplier interviews conducted by sell-side analysts and specialized
research firms are institutionalized versions of what Fisher did
personally, with a phone and a notebook, in the 1950s.

The tension is that institutionalization changes the nature of the
work. Fisher's scuttlebutt was an intimate, judgment-intensive process
conducted by the person making the investment decision. The
institutional version is often conducted by analysts who feed
information into a process rather than making the decision themselves,
and the information is commoditized across many buyers. Fisher's edge
came partly from the fact that most investors would not do the work; the
institutionalization of scuttlebutt means that many investors now do
some version of it, though rarely with Fisher's depth or his
willingness to let inadequate information stop the process.

The implication for institutional investors is that Fisher's method
still works, but the edge has shifted. Where Fisher's advantage was
doing the work at all, the modern advantage is doing it better --
deeper, with better sources, with more rigorous cross-checking, and with
the discipline to integrate qualitative findings with quantitative
analysis rather than treating them as separate exercises. Fisher's
insight that the financial statements need to be interpreted through the
lens of operational reality remains valid, and the institutional
investor who can bridge the qualitative-quantitative divide has an
advantage over those who operate on either side alone.

### For Value Investors: The Bridge to Quality

For value investors specifically, Fisher represents the critical bridge
from Benjamin Graham's statistical cheapness to the quality-focused
approach that characterizes modern value investing. Graham's framework
-- buying stocks below net working capital, seeking a margin of safety
in the numbers -- was powerful in the aftermath of the Great Depression,
when many stocks were statistically cheap. As markets evolved and
capital became more competitive, statistically cheap stocks became
rarer, and the investors who clung to Graham's original metrics found
fewer opportunities. Fisher's framework provided the intellectual path
forward: pay fair prices for exceptional businesses whose organizational
excellence would compound value for decades, rather than demanding steep
discounts on mediocre businesses.

This is the transformation that Buffett underwent, and Fisher was the
catalyst. Buffett started as a Graham disciple, buying cigar butts --
companies trading below liquidation value with one last puff of profit.
His evolution toward buying wonderful companies at fair prices --
Coca-Cola, See's Candies, American Express -- was guided by Fisher's
argument that business quality and management integrity matter more than
statistical cheapness for long-term compounding. Fisher's fifteen
points, his scuttlebutt method, and his holding discipline provided the
framework that made this evolution coherent rather than ad hoc.

The implication for contemporary value investors is that Fisher's
framework is not an alternative to value investing but an expansion of
it. The question of whether a company is undervalued cannot be answered
by price-to-earnings ratios alone; it requires understanding whether the
business has the organizational capability to sustain and grow its
earnings, which is precisely what Fisher's fifteen points and scuttlebutt
method are designed to assess. Value investors who incorporate Fisher's
qualitative rigor into their valuation process are following the path
that Buffett himself took, and that the most successful value investors
of the past half-century have taken.

### For the Study of Investment History: The Missing Link

For the study of investment history and the lineage of investment
ideas, Fisher occupies a specific and essential position: the missing
link between Graham and Buffett. The standard narrative of value
investing runs from Graham through Buffett, with Charlie Munger as the
intermediate figure who pushed Buffett toward quality. This narrative
is incomplete without Fisher. It was Fisher, not Munger, who first
systematized the qualitative assessment of business quality and
management integrity as a disciplined investment approach. Munger
reinforced and refined the quality orientation, but the intellectual
foundation -- the fifteen points, the scuttlebutt method, the hold-
forever discipline -- was Fisher's, published in 1958 when Munger was
still practicing law and Buffett was still buying cigar butts.

Understanding Fisher is therefore essential to understanding the
evolution of value investing from a statistical discipline to a
qualitative one. The trajectory from Graham's Security Analysis (1934)
through Fisher's Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (1958) to Buffett's
Berkshire Hathaway letters (1965-present) is not a straight line but a
synthesis, and Fisher is the synthesizer whose framework made the
evolution possible. Investors who study only Graham and Buffett miss
the mechanism of transformation; studying Fisher reveals how the
qualitative turn in value investing was theorized and practiced before
it became the dominant approach.
