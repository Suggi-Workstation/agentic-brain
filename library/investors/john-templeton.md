# John Templeton -- The Global Contrarian Who Turned Maximum Pessimism into Maximum Returns

Sir John Templeton (1912-2008) was a pioneer of global mutual-fund investing who built his record by buying nations, industries, and companies at what he called "points of maximum pessimism" [1]. His Templeton Growth Fund returned about 14.5% a year from 1954 to 1992 [3], and an independent analysis found that it beat a world stock index by about 3.7 percentage points a year, with almost all of the margin earned in bear markets [2]. He sold his fund company to Franklin Resources in 1992 and turned to the philanthropy that had long engaged him, including the Templeton Prize and the John Templeton Foundation [1][4].

## Background

### Early life and education

John Marks Templeton was born on November 29, 1912, in Winchester, Tennessee [1]. He followed his brother to Yale University, supported himself through the Depression, and graduated in 1934 near the top of his class as president of Phi Beta Kappa. A Rhodes Scholarship took him to Balliol College, Oxford, where he took a degree in law in 1936 [1]. Thrift was a family value: The Economist wrote that he "revered thrift and had a horror of debt," habits his parents instilled in small-town Tennessee [1]. In a 1999 Money interview he said that, with no money of his own after his education, he and his wife deliberately saved fifty cents of every dollar [5].

Whether the Oxford years shaped his later investing is an interpretation found in secondary sources. Wikipedia states that his time at Oxford started an interest in global investing, which was uncommon in the United States but more common in the United Kingdom because of the British Empire [17]. Davis and Nairn, whose 2012 study is the most detailed analysis of his fund record, add that he visited scores of countries on his regular travels abroad, which in their view made him well qualified to introduce American investors to the benefits of international stocks [2].

### Early career and the Graham connection

The Foundation dates the start of his Wall Street career to 1938 [1]. The Tennessee Encyclopedia describes a brief period as an investment counselor at Fenner and Beane in New York, followed by service from 1937 to 1940 as vice-president of the National Geophysical Company in Dallas [11]. The two chronologies do not align on dates, and this topic does not reconcile them. In 1940 he bought a small investment firm that became Templeton, Dobbrow and Vance, the early foundation of his business [4].

Secondary accounts describe Templeton as a student of Benjamin Graham [17]. The sources read for this review do not document a classroom connection. They do document Graham's influence on Templeton's stated rules: in his 1993 article "16 Rules for Investment Success," Templeton quotes Graham's advice to "buy when most people...including experts...are pessimistic, and sell when they are actively optimistic" in support of buying low [6].

### The mutual funds

In 1954 Templeton launched the Templeton Growth Fund. He domiciled it in Canada, which then had no capital gains tax, and presented it as one of the first mutual funds to invest globally [4]. The global orientation developed gradually. For its first few years the portfolio consisted mainly of Canadian stocks and bonds. By the 1967 letter to shareholders the twelve largest common stock holdings included two Dutch companies (Philips and Royal Dutch Petroleum) and one each from Japan, Germany, Sweden, and Great Britain, and the fund held fewer than 30 investments [2]. During the 1950s he also started funds specializing in nuclear energy, chemicals, electronics, and technology [4].

Davis and Nairn, working from continuous daily performance data for the fund from 1954, report that the first decade was unremarkable: the fund would have struggled to keep pace with the MSCI World Index, had that index existed then [2]. Consistent outperformance came only in the 1970s [2]. Davis and Nairn attribute the turn to two developments. First, his performance improved dramatically after he moved to the Bahamas; they conclude that working alone, far from the daily dramas of Wall Street, improved his stock selection [2]. Second, as a commercial matter, in 1974 Jack Galbraith began marketing the fund full-time, which turned the improved record of a small fund into the base of a global fund-management business [2]. Templeton moved to Nassau in the late 1960s and became a naturalized British citizen [1]. Forbes dates his renunciation of United States citizenship to 1968 and notes that residence in the Bahamas meant he paid no United States income tax [12]. A tax motive therefore accompanied any psychological one.

### The 1992 sale and the later years

In July 1992 Franklin Resources announced that it would buy Templeton, Galbraith & Hansberger Ltd., the manager of 78 funds with $21.3 billion in assets, for $913 million [9]. The acquisition closed on October 30, 1992 [8]. Franklin's 1994 annual report gives a lower figure: it paid $731.6 million plus assumed liabilities, which with other adjustments raised the purchase price for financial reporting to about $786 million [8]. The Guardian reports that $440 million of the $913 million went to Templeton personally [10], and Money estimated his proceeds at about $400 million [5]. Scientific American gives $440 million as the sale price [18]; the author's reading of the Guardian's breakdown is that this figure is Templeton's own share rather than the total.

After the sale he gave his attention to philanthropy. He established the Templeton Prize in 1972 and the John Templeton Foundation in 1987, and in 1987 Queen Elizabeth II created him a Knight Bachelor for his philanthropic accomplishments [1]. He also endowed Templeton College at Oxford [10]. In 1999 Money reported that the money he managed belonged solely to him and his charitable foundations [5]. The Guardian reports that he liquidated his own and his clients' technology stocks before the dot-com bubble burst [10]. He died in Nassau, the Bahamas, on July 8, 2008, aged 95 [3].

## Core Concepts

### Maximum Pessimism as a Buying Rule

Templeton's best-known maxim reads: "Bull markets are born on pessimism, grow on scepticism, mature on optimism and die on euphoria. The time of maximum pessimism is the best time to buy, and the time of maximum optimism is the best time to sell" [7]. The Guardian called the second sentence his most famous aphorism [10]. The Foundation describes the application: he took "buy low, sell high" to an extreme, picking nations, industries, and companies that were hitting rock bottom [1].

In "16 Rules for Investment Success," Templeton explained why the rule is hard to follow. Prices are high when many investors are buying, and low when demand is low, investors have pulled back, and people are discouraged and pessimistic. He wrote that it is extremely difficult to go against the crowd, "to buy when everyone else is selling or has sold, to buy when things look darkest," and that an investor who buys what everyone else buys will get the same results as everyone else: "you can't outperform the market if you buy the market." He closed the rule with "So simple in concept. So difficult in execution" [6]. Davis and Nairn record a related remark: "People are always asking me where the outlook is good, but that is the wrong question. The right question is: Where is the outlook most miserable?" [2].

The rule moves the analyst's question from forecasting to pricing. The investor is asked not to predict what happens next but to find where sentiment is most negative and then to test whether the price already reflects that sentiment. This is the author's interpretation of the maxims; neither the 1993 article nor Davis and Nairn states it in these terms.

### Value Discipline: Bargains Among Quality Stocks

Pessimism alone was not the purchase criterion. Rule 5 of the 1993 article tells the investor to search for bargains among quality stocks. It defines quality as a company strongly entrenched as the sales leader in a growing market, a technological leader in a field that depends on innovation, a strong management team with a proven record, a well-capitalized early entrant into a new market, or a trusted brand with high margins; Templeton compared judging quality to reviewing a restaurant, where one expects a superior result rather than a perfect one [6]. Rule 6 says to buy value, not market trends or the economic outlook, because individual stocks determine the market and can rise in a bear market and fall in a bull market [6]. Rule 8 says that in most instances the investor is buying either earnings or assets, and notes that the earnings of market indexes fluctuate around the replacement book value of their shares [6]. Davis and Nairn summarize his philosophy in one sentence: "Buy stocks for less than they are worth and hold them as long as it takes for the market to appreciate how undervalued they are" [2].

His valuation anchors were set against earnings or assets. Money reports that in the 1950s many Japanese stocks traded at a price-to-earnings ratio of about three, which led him to regard Japan as the cheapest market in the world [5]. The author's interpretation is that the maxims pair two conditions: the asset is unpopular, and it is cheap relative to the earnings or assets of a business of acceptable quality. Unpopularity without a valuation test is a different strategy, one that treats every price decline as an opportunity, and it is not the one he described.

### Searching Worldwide

Templeton treated the world as a single search area. Rule 7 says to diversify "by industry, by risk, and by country," and that "if you search worldwide, you will find more bargains -- and possibly better bargains -- than in any single nation" [6]. Maxim 9 repeats the point and adds the safety of diversification [7]. Davis and Nairn present the argument in two parts: widening the range of opportunities could only increase the supply of potential bargains, and the risk of investing abroad was exaggerated and could be offset by diversification. They quote his claim that a stock portfolio with investments around the world is likely to yield, in the long run, a higher return at a lower level of volatility than a simple, diversified single-nation portfolio [2]. At the time, the authors write, many investment professionals held that anything more than a small foreign allocation was too risky, and legislation prevented many pension funds and charitable foundations from investing in securities outside the United States [2].

The fund's holdings show the argument in practice. In 1967 Shiseido, a Japanese cosmetics company and the fund's only Japanese stock, made up 13% of the fund's value, and the portfolio held fewer than 30 investments [2]. The author's interpretation is that this combination of a wide search area and a concentrated portfolio is the distinctive feature of the approach: global diversification enlarged the pool from which a small number of bargains was drawn, rather than spreading capital thinly across many markets.

### When to Sell, Hold Cash, and Stay Calm

The second half of the maxim is the sell rule. After the Japanese market became popular, Templeton looked for cheaper alternatives; Money reports that by 1980 he had almost entirely cashed out of Japan [5]. Davis and Nairn judge that his decision to abandon the Japanese market was too early [2]. The case shows the cost of the rule: selling at the first signs of popularity gives up part of the later gain.

The 1993 article sets out the surrounding disciplines. Rule 3 says that no one kind of investment is always best and that there are times to sit on cash, because cash enables the investor to take advantage of opportunities [6]. Rule 2 says to invest rather than trade or speculate, quoting Lucien Hooper on the better results of relaxed, long-term owners compared with traders [6]. Rule 9 says to monitor investments aggressively because no bull or bear market is permanent and no stock can be bought and forgotten; Templeton noted that one of every three Dow Jones Industrial Average issues changed between 1978 and 1990 [6]. Rule 10 addresses crashes: the time to sell is before the crash; afterward the investor should ask whether he would buy the stocks today, and if no more attractive stocks can be found, hold what he has [6]. Money reports that in the 1987 crash Templeton bought stocks that had been slaughtered, reasoning that if a stock such as Ford was good value before the fall, it was better value after it [5].

The rules were not a ban on market-level judgment. Rule 6 tells investors to buy stocks rather than the economic outlook, yet in Rule 16 Templeton wrote that there was "at least an even chance" that the Dow Jones Industrials would have reached 6,000 by the start of the 21st century [6]. The author's reading is that he distrusted forecasts as a basis for choosing stocks while holding a general view that equities rise over long periods.

### Real Returns and the Difficulty of Beating the Market

Rule 1 defines the objective as maximum total real return, meaning the return after taxes and after inflation [6]. He illustrated the point with 4% inflation, which would reduce the buying power of a $100,000 portfolio to $68,000 in ten years, so that the portfolio would have to grow by 47% merely to stay even [6]. He treated beating the market as difficult: unmanaged indexes pay no commissions or salaries, and investment companies are never fully invested because they must hold cash to redeem shares, so a fund that consistently and significantly outperforms is doing a superb job [6]. Rule 15 adds that there is no free lunch; the commission on an initial public offering, for example, is built into the price of the stock, which is a reason why most new stocks decline after the offering [6].

### Humility, Faith, and Optimism

Templeton tied his investing to humility. The motto he created for the Foundation, "How little we know, how eager to learn," is described by the Foundation as exemplifying his philosophy in the financial markets as well as in philanthropy [1]. Rule 14 of the 1993 article states that an investor who has all the answers does not even understand all the questions [6], and the same idea appears among the maxims [7]. Rule 11 warns that "this time is different" are among the four most costly words in the annals of investing [6].

He also tied it to faith. Templeton was a Presbyterian elder who held an unorthodox "humble approach" to theology [1][4], and Rule 12 reads: "If you begin with a prayer, you can think more clearly and make fewer mistakes" [6]. Money identified his calm, bolstered by religious faith, as his "secret weapon," and quoted him saying that even when he took a beating, "I never was depressed or despairing" [5]. Rule 16 gives the optimist's case: "For 100 years optimists have carried the day in US stocks," and investors should not be fearful or negative too often [6]. The Wall Street Journal's obituary, quoted by the Foundation, was titled "Maximum Optimist" [1]. The author's interpretation is that optimism and contrarianism are linked in his method: a buyer at maximum pessimism is betting that the pessimism is temporary, and a belief in long-run progress is what makes that bet psychologically sustainable.

## Evidence

### The 1939 basket of low-priced stocks

The Foundation records that when war began in Europe in 1939, Templeton borrowed money to buy 100 shares each in 104 companies selling at one dollar per share or less, including 34 companies in bankruptcy; only four turned out to be worthless, and he made large profits on the others [1]. Forbes puts the outlay at about $10,400 and reports proceeds of more than $40,000 about four years later [12]. In the Money interview Templeton recalled borrowing $10,000 and counted 37 bankrupt companies; he said that when he liquidated the holdings five years later he had a profit on 100 of the 104 and "roughly five times my money" [5]. Money reported that Templeton thought a world war would electrify America's shattered economy and save even the feeblest companies [5].

The accounts disagree on the number of bankrupt firms (34 or 37) and on the result: by the Forbes figures the proceeds were at least 3.8 times the outlay (the author's calculation, $40,000 divided by $10,400), while his own recollection was about five times. The author's interpretation is that the case tests a portfolio rule rather than stock selection: holding 104 positions meant that individual bankruptcies could not determine the outcome, while the low prices paid left room for the survivors to recover.

### The record of the Templeton Growth Fund, 1954-1992

The New York Times reported a 14.5% average annual return for the fund from 1954 to 1992, with a $10,000 investment and reinvested dividends growing to $2 million [3]. The Foundation gives the same $2 million figure [1], and Money rounded the return to 15% a year [5]. Davis and Nairn give a different dollar figure: $10,000 invested at launch grew to $1,736,100 after 38 years [2]. The author's calculation shows that the published figures are not exactly consistent with each other. A $10,000 investment growing to $2 million over 38 years is about 15.0% a year, whereas $1,736,100 corresponds to about 14.5%. The sources read do not explain the difference.

Davis and Nairn tested the record against a world index using continuous daily data. The average 12-month total return was 16.0% a year, 3.7 percentage points above the world stock market. The fund produced a positive return, in nominal and in real terms, over every 60-month period in the 38 years. Its worst five-year real return was 8.6% (1.7% a year), for the 60 months to July 1982, while the MSCI World Index failed to produce a real five-year return on several occasions. Almost all of the outperformance came in bear markets, when the fund's losses were smaller than the market's [2]. They also report that the first decade lagged, and that the record improved over time rather than deteriorating as the fund grew [2]. The author's reading is that the result is a record defined by low downside and a late start rather than by uniform outperformance.

### Japan

Davis and Nairn date the Japanese position from fund data and shareholder letters. Templeton bought Japanese shares for his own account through a broker in the mid-1950s, and in May 1959 he wrote a long memo to clients on the export success of Japanese companies. By 1967 Shiseido, the fund's only Japanese stock, was 13% of the fund. The heavy commitment came in the 1970s, when as much as 60% of the fund's assets were in Japanese stocks [2]. Money places the 60% commitment in the 1950s; it adds that he bought Hitachi and Fuji Film, that by 1980 he had almost entirely cashed out, and that he had quintupled his money [5]. The two accounts conflict on timing, and this topic follows the dating by Davis and Nairn, who worked from the fund's own data. Their verdict is that the decision to abandon the Japanese market was a success overall but came too early [2].

### Bear markets and crashes

Money reports that in 1968, with the United States market surging, Templeton was heavily underweight in United States stocks. Between 1969 and 1974 many stocks fell 70%, while his fund, largely invested in Japan and Canada, returned a positive 50% [5]. In the 1987 crash he bought stocks that had been slaughtered [5]. Before the dot-com bubble burst, he liquidated his own and his clients' technology stocks [10]. These episodes are consistent with the finding of Davis and Nairn that the fund's outperformance was concentrated in bear markets [2]. The 1969-1974 and 1987 episodes rest on one magazine profile [5], and the dot-com episode on an obituary [10]; this review did not verify them against fund data.

### Limits of the evidence

The record is that of one manager and one fund. After the Franklin sale the fund continued under other managers; its annual report for the year ended August 31, 2019 shows a 10-year average annual total return of 5.94% for Class A shares, excluding sales charges, against 9.19% for the MSCI All Country World Index [13]. That figure describes later results of the fund, not Templeton's. The author's interpretation is that it separates the person and the era from the fund's name: the name is not evidence of how the method performs now.

Davis and Nairn also report a study of market behavior in 35 countries that, in their account, shows why his analytical methods were conducive to superior long-term returns [2]; this review did not examine that data. The author's assessment, synthesizing the available evidence, is that the record reflects a consistent framework rather than luck or a single bet: it spans 38 years, many countries, and several market cycles, and the independent analysis finds that smaller losses in bad markets, rather than larger gains in good ones, drove the margin [2]. The record also coincided with particular conditions: a decade of lagging results at the start, and an improvement in the 1970s that Davis and Nairn associate with Templeton's move to the Bahamas [2]. Whether the same margin is achievable in a different environment is an open question.

## Implications

### For individual investors

The 1993 article makes the objective concrete: the target is real return after taxes and inflation, and it is reached by owning stocks for long periods rather than trading them [6]. Rules 2, 9 and 10 combine long holding with active monitoring and a prohibition on panic selling; the same article warns that the market should not be treated as a casino and that frequent trading can consume profits in commissions [6]. The Forbes account of the 1939 trade has the positions sold about four years after purchase [12]. The author's reading is that the practical discipline is patience measured in years, not months.

The author's assessment is that the central difficulty is behavioral rather than analytical. Maximum pessimism is not a technical signal on a chart; it is a state in which buying means overriding the instinct for self-preservation, and Templeton said as much in calling the rule "so difficult in execution" [6]. Money quoted him on the cause: most investors get led astray by emotions, becoming careless and optimistic when they have large profits and cautious and pessimistic when they have large losses, and he said he had always worked on having more self-control [5]. The individual investor can borrow the structure of his rules -- price first, quality second, a diversified basket, no forecasting of the next quarter -- more easily than the temperament. Rule 13 adds a caution: outperforming the market is hard, because the standard is the professionals who run large institutions, not the average investor [6].

### For portfolio construction

Templeton's global argument bears on home-country bias. As of September 30, 2026 the United States was 64.21% of the MSCI All Country World Index, which covers about 85% of the global investable equity opportunity set [14]. The same factsheets report a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 16.28 for MSCI ACWI and 12.65 for MSCI ACWI ex USA [14][15]. Because the United States is the largest weight in the first index, the author infers that United States equities were priced at a higher multiple than the rest of the world on that date; the factsheets do not state this directly.

The same factsheets contain a caution. From December 31, 1987 to September 30, 2026 the MSCI ACWI ex USA Index returned 6.79% a year in gross United States dollar terms, against 8.89% for MSCI ACWI [14][15]; MSCI states that its performance figures may be actual or back-tested, and the two indexes launched in 1995 and 1990 respectively [14][15]. Diversification outside the United States has not guaranteed outperformance over that horizon. Templeton's claim was narrower: that searching worldwide enlarges the supply of bargains and adds the safety of diversification [6][7]. The author's assessment is that the lesson for portfolio construction is a method (compare prices and quality across markets, and buy where the price is lowest relative to value) rather than a standing allocation to non-United States equities.

### For the investment industry

Davis and Nairn attribute a dramatic improvement in Templeton's results to his move to the Bahamas, where he worked alone and far from the daily noise of Wall Street, using only one or two hours a day for research [2]. He was also an owner-manager: he chaired Templeton, Galbraith & Hansberger [9] and received $440 million of the $913 million sale price [10], from which the author infers a large ownership stake. The author's assessment is that independence of this kind is uncommon in professional management. A manager who buys what others are selling and is early or wrong may be judged by clients or employers before the valuation gap closes, whereas an owner-manager may be able to wait. The 1993 article acknowledges a related constraint on the industry: funds hold cash for redemptions and bear costs that unmanaged indexes do not, so a fund that consistently outperforms is doing a better job than the raw numbers suggest [6].

### For philanthropy and institution-building

The author's reading is that Templeton created and endowed institutions rather than only donating to existing ones. The Templeton Prize, established in 1972, is valued at GBP 1,100,000 and is set above the Nobel Prizes by design, which Templeton intended to signal that advances in the spiritual domain are no less important than those in other fields [1]. The New York Times reported that he described it as an effort to redress the absence of a Nobel Prize for religion; its first winner was Mother Teresa [4][10]. The John Templeton Foundation, established in 1987, reports an endowment of about $4 billion as of December 31, 2025 and total giving of $150 million in 2025 [16]. At the time of his death the charities he established gave away about $70 million a year [4]. Its funding areas include character virtue development, individual freedom and free markets, life sciences, mathematical and physical sciences, public engagement, and religion, science, and society [1]. He also endowed Templeton College at Oxford, which had been set up as the Oxford Centre for Management Studies [10].

The philanthropy has drawn published objections. Scientific American reported at his death that critics charged that, in trying to reconcile science and religion, Templeton was twisting scientific concepts in religion's name, and quoted the biologist P. Z. Myers calling the funding a "fruitless and pointless endeavor" [18]. The author's assessment is that Templeton applied the same rule to philanthropy as to investing: fund what the consensus undervalues, and expect criticism while the area is out of favor. Money reported that he called tithing the single best investment a person can make and gave away $10 for each dollar he spent on himself [5]; those figures are his own claims and were not independently verified.
