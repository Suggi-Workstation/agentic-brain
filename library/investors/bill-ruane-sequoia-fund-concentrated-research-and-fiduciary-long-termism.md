# Bill Ruane -- Research-Led Concentration and a Willingness to Turn Away Capital Built Sequoia's Long Record

Bill Ruane, a student of Benjamin Graham whom Warren Buffett later called the only person he recommended to his partners when he closed his partnership in 1969, built Sequoia Fund around one organizing idea: the people who manage the portfolio should be the people who do the research, and the research should be deep enough to justify owning a few businesses in size for many years [1][2][3]. In the author's assessment, his record shows that concentration, low turnover, and the 1982 decision to close the fund to new investors were parts of one fiduciary design; the fund's later Valeant episode shows how that design depends on the brakes that surround it [3][9][12][15].

## Background

William John Ruane was born in Chicago on October 24, 1925, and grew up in Oak Park, Illinois [7]. Harvard Business School's leadership database records Navy service and a Harvard graduate degree, and the Washington Post obituary dates his master's degree to 1949 [8][9]. After business school he went to Wall Street; the Washington Post reports that he joined Kidder Peabody after receiving his degree and stayed for 20 years [2][8]. Buffett wrote that, after starting on Wall Street, Ruane concluded he needed "a real business education" and took Graham's course at Columbia, where the two men met in early 1951 [2]. Buffett's 1969 partnership letter gives the same year and says he had since observed Ruane's "character, temperament and intellect" at close range [1]. Ruane's documented connection to Graham was as a student in Graham's Columbia course; Buffett's first-hand accounts do not describe him as a Graham-Newman employee [1][2].

Ruane's account management before 1969 produced the record that made Buffett's later recommendation possible. In October 1969, Buffett reported that a composite of Ruane's individual accounts had averaged more than 40% a year in 1956-1961 and again in 1964-1968, managed on average with $5 million to $10 million [1]. The same letter disclosed the exception: in 1962 Ruane was down about 50%, "undoubtedly somewhat as a product of the euphoric experience of the earlier years," and 1963 was about breakeven while he "re-oriented his thinking" [1]. Buffett described the overall record as averaging fairly close to the Buffett Partnership's but with considerably greater variation, noting that Ruane had not used the control positions and workouts that moderated the partnership's swings and that their portfolios would typically show only a mild overlap [1].

In 1969 Ruane and Richard Cunniff formed a New York Stock Exchange firm, Ruane, Cunniff & Stires, Inc. [1][8]. The Washington Post reported that they raised $20 million from investors, most of whom came on Buffett's recommendation; Buffett's October 1969 letter estimated the firm was then managing $20 million to $30 million [1][8]. Ruane later wrote that he and Cunniff had already been researching and investing in the same manner for more than fifteen years [3]. When Buffett closed his partnership, he asked Ruane to set up a fund that could take all of the former partners, and Ruane did so [2]. Sequoia Fund, Inc. -- whose SEC filing header records the former name Cimarron Fund until June 25, 1970 -- first offered shares to the public on July 15, 1970 [3]. The fund's current history states that Ruane and Cunniff launched it in the belief that a carefully selected, intensively researched group of businesses bought at attractive prices and held for the long term would outperform the market [17]. Robert Goldfarb joined in 1971 and, in Ruane's words, deserved "equal billing" [3].

Buffett's endorsement carried personal as well as professional weight. He told his partners that Ruane was one of three trustees with "carte blanche on investment matters" if Buffett and his wife died while their children were minors [1]. He also told them that he considered Ruane "an exceptionally high probability decision on character and a high probability one on investment performance" [1]. The fund's start nevertheless came, as Buffett later put it, "at a terrible time," in the two-tier market that penalized value-oriented managers; his former partners, "to an amazing degree," stayed and added money [2].

Sequoia closed to new investors in 1982. Harvard Business School's profile attributes the decision to Ruane's belief that taking on more investors would dilute his effectiveness for existing clients; the fund remained closed at his death and, by a later press account, until 2008 [6][8][9][16]. Ruane also served on the boards of GEICO and The Washington Post Company; Washington Post chairman Donald Graham said Ruane "helped with every acquisition we made" during and after his board service [8][10]. His philanthropy concentrated on education and mental health, including the adoption of a Harlem block on 118th Street, a reading program for schools, and the TeenScreen youth screening program [7][8][10]. He died of complications from lung cancer on October 4, 2005, while still co-managing Sequoia [8]. Because Ruane was a control person of the adviser, his death terminated Sequoia's advisory contract by assignment and required interim and new contracts [6].

The author's assessment is that Ruane belongs in the investors domain because his influence ran through specific decisions rather than through a written doctrine: how he organized research, when he accepted or refused capital, how much he concentrated, whom he trained, and how he arranged succession [1][3][6][9].

## Core Concepts

### Research Was the Job, Not a Preliminary Step

Ruane's clearest statement of method came in his February 1997 letter as Sequoia's chairman. He quoted a 1969 memo stating that the new firm wanted to do "only one thing": have the principals concentrate on security analysis, use their own research rather than outside research, and manage portfolios themselves rather than split the work between a "research department" and "portfolio managers" [3]. He contrasted this with the Wall Street career path in which an analyst hopes to be promoted to portfolio manager. In his view, for a long-term investor "the analyst function is paramount and portfolio management follows naturally," and his business card, if the firm used titles, would read "Bill Ruane, Research Analyst" [3].

The same letter described the organization that applied the principle. By 1997 the research group numbered ten people, including Ruane, Rick Cunniff, Goldfarb, Carley Cunniff, Greg Alexander, and Jon Brandt; portfolio companies, Ruane wrote, would report "searching questions from just about every one of the ten of us" each year [3]. When the firm had "the seed of a good idea," everyone worked on it to cover the facts "like 'the dew covers Dixie'" [3]. The author's assessment is that this structure removed a common handoff failure: no portfolio manager had to act on a summary written by someone who did not bear the consequences of the decision.

### Concentration Was Justified by Conviction Built in Research

Ruane connected research depth directly to portfolio construction. "We believe that truth is in the details," he wrote, and intensive research was "of immense importance in avoiding big mistakes and developing the positive convictions required to own and hold concentrated investment positions" [3]. The causal order matters: research produced conviction, and conviction was the precondition for size. Concentration was not presented as a source of return by itself.

The portfolio disclosures show what this meant in practice. Sequoia was registered as a non-diversified fund [3]. At the end of 1996, Berkshire Hathaway alone represented 27.93% of total investments, Freddie Mac 15.22%, and Progressive 11.54%; the five largest positions together were 66.8% of total investments by the author's calculation from the schedule [3]. Berkshire remained the largest holding at 29.19% at the end of 1998, 29.11% at the end of 1999, and 33.8% of assets at the end of 2005 [4][5][6]. The 2005 report summarized the philosophy as making "concentrated commitments of capital in a limited number of companies that have superior long-term economic prospects" at attractive prices, and warned that the fund would therefore often differ significantly from the indexes in any given year [6].

### Quality at a Price, With Valuation as the Brake

Sequoia's letters during Ruane's chairmanship describe a sequence of tests. In the 1999 annual report the managers listed them: own high-quality companies with good earnings prospects and sustainable superior returns on capital; buy them at prices that underestimate their value; own a lot of the stock when the first two conditions coincide; and hold for many years while fundamentals remain sound [5]. They added that the second criterion "dramatically narrows the field," because great companies are usually already recognized by the market [5].

Valuation operated as a brake as well as an entry test. In 1997 Ruane described the portfolio as among the finest groups of companies the firm had owned, with an aggregate return on equity above 20%, but said his enthusiasm was "tempered only by its current level of valuation" and quoted Herbert Stein: "if something cannot go on forever it will not" [3]. The 1998 report recorded sales of long-held Walt Disney and Johnson & Johnson positions "primarily on the basis of valuation" and a year-end cash position of just over 20%, explaining that the managers "would rather hold cash than invest in equities with valuations we believe are over-enthusiastic" [4]. The author's assessment is that Ruane's version of quality investing kept Graham's price discipline but applied it to durable compounders rather than to statistical bargains.

### Long Holding Periods Were the Default

Low turnover followed from the research model. Annual portfolio turnover was between 8% and 32% from 1992 through 1999 and between 3% and 8% from 2001 through 2005 [3][4][5][6]. Berkshire Hathaway illustrates the time horizon: the position cost $165.8 million and was worth $717.3 million at the end of 1996, and cost $161.0 million against a value of $1,131.8 million at the end of 1999 [3][5]. The managers stated that they would generally hold through short-term problems, sector disfavor, or a stock getting "a bit ahead of itself," and would sell when valuation became excessive relative to likely earnings [5].

### Size Was Treated as the Anchor of Performance

Capacity appears in the earliest documents about Ruane. Buffett's 1969 letter named "the probability of managing significantly larger sums" as the first of three likely negatives for Ruane's future results, and observed that a successful manager faces that problem "rather quickly" [1]. In 1984 Buffett noted that Ruane had beaten the target despite "progressively larger sums of money" and added: "Size is the anchor of performance. There is no question about it" [2]. Closing Sequoia to new investors in 1982 was Ruane's operational answer: protect the effectiveness of the process for existing clients rather than maximize assets and fee revenue [9].

The closing did not freeze the capital base. The 1996 report still recorded $136.4 million of share sales and $197.0 million of repurchases during the year [3]. The author's interpretation is that closure limited one source of growth -- new clients attracted by the record -- while leaving existing shareholders able to add or withdraw. Buffett's 2016 letter later generalized the hazard Ruane addressed: a good record attracts "a torrent of money," and huge sums act as an anchor on performance [11]. The same letter quoted Ruane's own summary of how the industry degrades: "In investment management, the progression is from the innovators to the imitators to the swarming incompetents" [11].

### Circle of Competence Applied Under Pressure

Ruane's firm applied the circle-of-competence idea when it was commercially costly. The 1998 report said Sequoia avoided technology companies because the business was "outside our circle of competence," while acknowledging technology's growing effect on the companies it did own [4]. In early 2000, after shareholders accused the managers of "smugness," the letter replied that businesses with rapid technological change and shorter periods of predictable advantage were "extremely daunting to analyze," and that their valuations required a certainty the managers "simply cannot muster" [5]. The letter quoted Graham and Dodd's 1934 warning about the "new-era theory" [5].

### Alignment and Character as Fiduciary Assets

Ruane's arrangements tied his family's welfare to the fund. He wrote in 1997 that the common-stock portion of his family's investment accounts was "almost entirely invested in Sequoia," that his will named Goldfarb as trustee and money manager for his family's finances, and that Carley Cunniff was named successor trustee [3]. This mirrored Buffett's 1969 decision to name Ruane as a trustee for his own children [1]. The fund paid a flat advisory fee of 1% of average net assets that did not rise or fall with assets, and the adviser reimbursed expenses above a stated cap [3]. The adviser also received brokerage commissions on fund trades -- $309,715 in 1996 against $24.0 million of advisory fees -- an arrangement disclosed in the annual report [3]. At Sequoia's 2005 shareholders' meeting, Ruane repeated Buffett's two rules: "Rule No. 1: Don't lose money. Rule No. 2: Don't forget Rule No. 1" [8].

### Succession Was Built Into the Research Team

Ruane designed succession through the research organization rather than through a single heir. He stated in 1997 that Goldfarb would provide "the leadership in research excellence" for many years and was already a major shareholder of the management company [3]. After Ruane's death, the board approved a new advisory contract on the understanding that Goldfarb and David Poppe would co-manage the fund and that the adviser had no plans to change how it managed the fund [6]. The author's assessment is that this preserved continuity of process and ownership; whether it preserved the internal checks that had governed concentration is a separate question addressed in the Evidence section.

## Evidence

### Buffett's 1969 Recommendation Was an Ex Ante Forecast With Stated Risks

The October 9, 1969 Buffett Partnership letter is the most important primary document on Ruane because it was written before the Sequoia record existed. Buffett described Ruane as "the money manager within my knowledge who ranks the highest when combining the factors of integrity, ability and continued availability to all partners" [1]. He disclosed both the 40%-plus composite averages and the roughly 50% loss of 1962, and he listed three risks: larger sums, absorption in the operating detail of running a stock-exchange firm, and the probability that even excellent management would gain only limited advantage over passive investment in the coming decade [1]. Buffett estimated that even advisers in the top 1% to 2% were unlikely to beat the group expectancy by much more than 4 percentage points a year, and wrote that "the odds are good that Bill Ruane is in this select category" [1].

Method and finding: the letter is a dated prediction with explicit criteria, which allows a later test. In 1984 Buffett returned to it, stating "There's no hindsight involved here," and that he had said a four-point annual advantage over the S&P would be solid performance; Ruane had "achieved well over that" [2]. Buffett's 2016 letter stated that he had identified Ruane "60 years ago as almost certain to deliver superior investment returns over the long haul" [11]. The author's assessment is that the forecast passed its stated test, while the disclosure of the 1962 drawdown shows that the selection was made with the method's volatility in view.

### The First Sequoia Years Tested Whether Clients Would Stay

Buffett's 1984 essay printed Sequoia's annual record from July 15, 1970 through the first quarter of 1984 [2]. Sequoia trailed the S&P 500 in each of its first four reporting periods: 12.1% against 20.6% in the partial 1970 period, 13.5% against 14.3% in 1971, 8.7% against 18.9% in 1972, and -24.0% against -14.8% in 1973 [2]. It then declined less than the index in 1974 (-15.7% against -26.4%) and gained 60.5% and 72.3% in 1975 and 1976, against 37.2% and 23.6% [2]. Over the whole period the fund reported a 775.3% gain against 270.0% for the S&P 500 and compound annual returns of 17.2% against 10.0%, or 18.2% before the 1% management fee [2].

The fund's own illustration of a $10,000 investment shows how long the early test lasted. An investment made on July 15, 1970 was worth $11,242 on May 31, 1973 and $10,013 on May 31, 1974; by December 31, 1982 it was worth $69,920 [3]. The author's calculation from those figures is that the early investor was roughly flat after almost four years, yet the full period from inception through 1982 compounded at about 16.9% a year. Buffett's observation that his former partners "not only stayed with him but added money" is therefore evidence about client temperament as well as manager skill [2].

### Portfolio Disclosures Document Concentration and Patience

The annual reports filed with the SEC show the portfolio during Ruane's chairmanship. At the end of 1996, common stocks were 91.85% of investments and U.S. government obligations 8.15%; Berkshire, Freddie Mac, Progressive, Fifth Third, and Johnson & Johnson accounted for about two-thirds of investments [3]. Sequoia owned 5% or more of Progressive's voting securities, making Progressive an affiliated company under the Investment Company Act [3]. Net assets were $2,581.0 million, and turnover was 23% [3]. These figures establish that the fund's concentration was not a rhetorical claim; it was visible in audited schedules.

### The 1997-2002 Cycle Shows Both the Cost and the Payoff of Discipline

The late-1990s letters provide a case in which the managers' own stated method came under strain. In 1998 Sequoia returned 35.3% against 28.6% for the S&P 500, and the managers warned that their roughly 35% compound annual return since 1994 was "not sustainable" [4]. In 1999 the fund fell 16.5% while the S&P 500 rose 21.0% [5]. The managers attributed about 80% of the decline to Progressive and Berkshire Hathaway, whose auto-insurance and reinsurance businesses faced a cyclical downturn, and wrote that they had "underestimated the severity of the cycle" [5].

The 1999 letter also contained an explicit admission about process drift. Because the firm had "a tendency to leave a fair amount of money on the table," it had "adopted a tolerance for holding stocks at higher valuation levels than in the past"; that evolution contributed to the outsized 1997 and 1998 results and was "a factor in our unusually poor results in 1999" [5]. By the managers' own account, relaxing the valuation limit on holdings raised returns in the boom and deepened the decline afterward [5].

The following years show the other side. The 2005 report's comparison of $10,000 invested at the end of 1995 implies, by the author's calculation, returns of 20.1%, 10.5%, and -2.6% for Sequoia in 2000, 2001, and 2002, against -9.1%, -11.9%, and -22.1% for the S&P 500 [6]. From the end of 1999 to the end of 2002, Sequoia's comparison value rose 29.2% while the S&P 500's fell 37.6% [6]. The 1999 letter stated that technology stocks accounted for almost all of the index's gains that year [5]. The author's interpretation is that the refusal to buy technology at prevailing valuations contributed to the 1999 relative shortfall and helped protect the fund in the decline that followed [5][6].

### The Long-Run Record Through Ruane's Death

The fund's illustration shows that $10,000 invested at the public offering on July 15, 1970 was worth $1,095,125 at the end of 1999 and $1,869,038 at the end of 2005, with distributions reinvested and no deduction for taxes [5][6]. The author's calculation is an annualized return of about 15.9% over the 35.5 years. The end-1999 figure is consistent with the Forbes estimate, reported in the Washington Post obituary, that $10,000 invested at inception had become $1.1 million by 1999 [5][8]. For the ten years ended December 31, 2005, the fund reported 12.88% a year against 9.07% for the S&P 500 [6]. The New York Times noted that the fund outperformed overall since 1970 "although in some individual years it trailed" the index [7].

The obituaries differ on scale. The Washington Post wrote that Sequoia managed more than $14 billion; the New York Times attributed more than $14 billion to Ruane, Cunniff & Goldfarb as a firm and $3.5 billion to Sequoia [7][8]. The fund's own report shows net assets of $3,573.3 million at the end of 2005, consistent with the New York Times figures [6].

### The Post-Ruane Valeant Episode Tested the Institution He Built

The fund's largest later failure occurred after Ruane's death and is not evidence about his own decisions. It is evidence about the durability of the culture he left. Beginning around 2010, the firm built a position in Valeant Pharmaceuticals; Kiplinger reported that because the fund did not sell a single share as the stock rose, the holding grew to more than 30% of Sequoia's assets [15][16]. In October 2015 two independent directors, Vinod Ahooja and Sharon Osberg, resigned after raising concerns about the size of the stake; the New York Times reported that the directors had repeatedly expressed concern over the size of the stake, and that the managers had disclosed the purchase of 1.5 million more shares, making Sequoia the holder of more than 10% of Valeant [12][13]. Osberg later recalled resigning the day after learning of another large purchase [15].

When Valeant's shares collapsed, Goldfarb, then 71, retired as chief executive in March 2016, and Poppe became lead manager of what Bloomberg described as a $5.6 billion fund [14]. The Washington Post later reported that Sequoia's assets under management had roughly halved, from $8 billion before the scandal to $4.2 billion [15]. Buffett said in 2016 that Ruane "ran Sequoia until roughly 2005" and that even including the recent troubles he did not know a U.S. mutual fund with a better record from inception [15]. The author's assessment is that the episode separated two parts of Ruane's design: the conviction-building research culture survived his death, but the valuation brake and willingness to heed dissent that had checked concentration did not prove equally durable.

## Implications

### For Active Investors: Research Depth Is the Precondition for Concentration

Ruane's practice reverses the common shortcut of adopting a concentrated portfolio and then searching for confidence. His firm built conviction through research done by the people who bore the result, and only then held positions in size [3]. An investor who copies the position sizes without the research organization reproduces the visible risk without the safeguard. The author's assessment is that the transferable rule is to make concentration conditional on documented evidence and to keep the people who make the decisions close to the primary research.

The 1999 letter adds a second rule. A manager who loosens a valuation discipline because it "left money on the table" should expect the cost to appear later, often in concentrated holdings that have already risen [5]. Writing down the sell criteria before a boom, and recording any deliberate change to them, allows the investor to see process drift as it happens rather than only in hindsight.

Circle-of-competence decisions also have a measurable cost. Sequoia trailed the S&P 500 by 37.5 percentage points in 1999, a year in which its letter said technology produced almost all of the index's gains, and then led it substantially over 2000-2002 [5][6]. The author's interpretation is that a competence boundary is useful only if the investor can endure the periods in which it is expensive; otherwise it will be abandoned at the worst moment.

### For Investment-Firm Builders: Capacity Is a Fiduciary Choice

Closing Sequoia in 1982 traded fee growth for the effectiveness of the process for existing clients [9]. Buffett had listed asset growth first among Ruane's likely negatives in 1969, and his 2016 letter described the general pattern by which success attracts money and size drags on returns [1][11]. A firm builder can therefore treat capacity as a decision to be made in advance, not a constraint discovered after performance fades.

The design also had limits. Closing to new investors did not stop existing shareholders from adding money; the 1996 report shows $136.4 million of share sales in a single year [3]. The author's assessment is that a capacity policy should state which flows it limits and which it does not, so that clients and directors can judge whether it matches the strategy's real constraints.

### For Allocators Selecting Managers: Judge Character, Process, and Stated Risks Before the Outcome

Buffett's 1969 letter is a model of manager selection under uncertainty. It identified integrity, ability, and availability as criteria; disclosed the candidate's worst year and its likely cause; named the specific risks that could reduce future results; gave a modest quantitative expectation for excellent management; and stated that the main risk was average performance rather than disaster [1]. Each element can be tested later, which is why the 1984 essay could claim the recommendation involved no hindsight [2].

Sequoia's first four years show the second half of selection: the client must be prepared to stay. A recommendation that is correct over fourteen years can still look wrong after four, as the fund's $10,013 value in May 1974 illustrates [3]. The author's interpretation is that allocators should agree on the evaluation horizon and the evidence that would end the relationship before investing, rather than reacting to early relative results.

### For Fund Shareholders: Behavior Determines the Return Actually Earned

A fund's reported compound return is earned only by holders who stay through its worst intervals. Buffett warned his partners in 1969 that a two-year setback "may sound like a short time when included in a table of performance" but "may feel like a long time when your net worth is down 50%," and advised them to size their equity commitment to their need for regular income and stable principal [1]. Sequoia's record repeatedly tested that advice: an investor at the 1970 offering was roughly flat in May 1974, and an investor at the end of 1998 lost 16.5% in 1999 while the S&P 500 gained 21.0% [3][5]. Both periods were followed by strong relative results [2][6].

The author's interpretation is that a concentrated fund transfers part of the manager's discipline to the shareholder. The manager can hold an out-of-favor position only if the capital base does not leave; Buffett's account that his former partners stayed and added money during Sequoia's weak start describes a client base that supplied that patience [2]. Shareholders who cannot tolerate long divergence from an index are poorly matched to this kind of fund, however good its process.

### For Track-Record Analysts: Separate the Founder's Era From the Vehicle's

Sequoia's single inception date covers several management regimes. Buffett's own boundary was that Ruane "ran Sequoia until roughly 2005" [15]. From the 1970 offering through 2005, the fund's illustration implies about 15.9% a year by the author's calculation [6]; later regimes include the Valeant period and, since 2016, an investment committee [14][17]. The author's assessment is that crediting Ruane with the whole since-inception figure, or blaming him for the Valeant losses, would misassign evidence; the record should be divided at documented changes in decision-makers.

### For Boards and Successors: Concentration Needs a Working Brake

The Valeant episode shows the governance problem that a successful concentrated fund creates. Independent directors who repeatedly questioned the position size resigned when the managers continued buying [12][13][15]. Sequoia's registration as a non-diversified fund permitted large positions [3]; the author's assessment is that the protection must therefore come from internal valuation discipline, a culture that tests the largest holdings hardest, and directors whose objections change decisions [12][15]. Ruane's own era had shown a similar strain in 1999, when two insurance-related holdings drove about 80% of a decline [5].

For successors, the lesson is that inheriting a process includes inheriting its brakes. Ruane designed continuity in ownership, research leadership, and family trusteeship [3][6]. The author's assessment is that a succession plan for a concentrated strategy should also name the limits on position size, the person or body able to stop additions, and the evidence that would require a reduction, because the founder's personal restraint does not transfer automatically.

### For Students of Graham-and-Doddsville: Shared Principles, Different Implementations

Ruane, Schloss, and Buffett learned from Graham but built very different portfolios. Buffett noted in 1969 that Ruane had not used control positions and workouts and that his results varied more, and in 1984 he presented Sequoia's record alongside very different Graham-derived managers [1][2]. Ruane's version combined intensive business research, quality criteria, and valuation limits with concentration and long holding periods [3][5]. The author's assessment is that his career supports the view that Graham's legacy was a discipline of price and evidence rather than a single screen or portfolio size.

## Criticisms

Several limits qualify the record. First, it is a team record. Ruane wrote that Goldfarb deserved equal billing from 1971, and Sequoia's letters were written in the collective "we," so specific decisions cannot be assigned to Ruane alone [3][5]. Second, much of the performance evidence is self-reported in fund materials or presented by Buffett, a friend and advocate; the annual reports are audited financial statements, but the narrative interpretations are the managers' own [2][3][6]. Third, Berkshire Hathaway represented roughly 28% to 34% of the fund at the year-ends reported here; the author's assessment is that Sequoia's results in those years were partly a bet on Buffett's capital allocation [3][4][5][6]. Fourth, the margin over the index has not persisted under later management: through September 30, 2025 the fund reported 13.53% a year since inception against 11.48% for the S&P 500, but 9.94% against 15.29% for the latest ten years [17]. Finally, the adviser earned brokerage commissions on fund trades in addition to its advisory fee; the author's assessment is that this disclosed arrangement nonetheless created a potential conflict [3].
