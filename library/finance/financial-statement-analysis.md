# Financial Statement Analysis -- How to Read a Business Through Its Numbers

Financial statement analysis is the systematic examination of a
company's balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement to
assess its financial health, operating performance, and intrinsic
value. It is the fundamental tool that converts raw accounting data
into investable insight -- revealing not just what a company earned,
but how it earned it, whether those earnings are sustainable, and what
risks are embedded in its capital structure. Without financial
statement analysis, an investor is buying a ticker symbol, not a
business.

## Background

Modern financial statement analysis traces its roots to the early 20th
century, when the rise of public stock markets created demand for
standardized ways to compare companies. Benjamin Graham, often called
the father of security analysis, codified the discipline in "Security
Analysis" (1934, with David Dodd), establishing the principle that
financial statements -- not market sentiment -- should drive investment
decisions. Graham's approach treated stocks as fractional ownership of
real businesses, and financial statements as the primary window into
those businesses' economics.

The regulatory framework that makes analysis possible emerged from the
Great Depression. The Securities Act of 1933 and Securities Exchange
Act of 1934 created the SEC and mandated standardized, audited
financial reporting for public companies. This produced the three-statement
framework that analysts use today: the balance sheet (a snapshot of
assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time), the income
statement (revenues and expenses over a period), and the cash flow
statement (actual cash movements, reconciling accrual accounting to
cash reality).

The accounting standards that govern these statements -- Generally
Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) in the United States and
International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) globally -- provide
consistency but also introduce artifacts that analysts must understand.
Depreciation schedules, revenue recognition policies, inventory
valuation methods, and goodwill treatment all create gaps between
accounting numbers and economic reality. Financial statement analysis
is as much about understanding what the numbers do NOT say as what they
do.

The DuPont Corporation pioneered ratio decomposition in the 1920s,
breaking Return on Equity into its component drivers: profit margin,
asset turnover, and financial leverage. This framework remains the
analyst's most powerful diagnostic tool a century later, revealing
whether a company's returns come from pricing power, operational
efficiency, or aggressive borrowing.

## Core Concepts

### The Three Statements and How They Connect

The **balance sheet** shows what a company owns (assets), what it owes
(liabilities), and the residual claim of shareholders (equity) at a
specific date. The fundamental equation is Assets = Liabilities +
Shareholders' Equity. Current assets (cash, receivables, inventory) are
those expected to convert to cash within a year; non-current assets
(property, plant, equipment, goodwill, intangibles) represent
long-term investments. Current liabilities (payables, short-term debt)
are obligations due within a year; long-term liabilities (bonds, leases,
deferred taxes) represent longer-duration claims.

The **income statement** shows revenues, expenses, and profit over a
period -- typically a quarter or year. The key line items cascade: Revenue
minus Cost of Goods Sold equals Gross Profit; minus Operating Expenses
(SG&A, R&D, depreciation) equals Operating Income (EBIT); minus
Interest Expense and Taxes equals Net Income. Each level reveals
different aspects of the business: gross margin shows pricing power and
production efficiency; operating margin shows overhead discipline; net
margin shows the bottom line after all claims.

The **cash flow statement** reconciles accrual-basis net income to
actual cash generated or consumed, divided into three sections.
Operating cash flow shows cash from the core business -- this is the
number that matters most, as it cannot be manufactured through
accounting choices. Investing cash flow shows capital expenditures,
acquisitions, and asset sales -- revealing whether the company is
investing for growth or harvesting. Financing cash flow shows debt
issuance/repayment, equity issuance/buybacks, and dividends -- revealing
how the company funds itself and returns capital to shareholders.

The three statements are linked: net income from the income statement
flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet and is the starting
point for the cash flow statement. Capital expenditures on the cash
flow statement increase PP&E on the balance sheet. Depreciation on the
income statement reduces PP&E on the balance sheet and is added back on
the cash flow statement (a non-cash expense). Understanding these
links allows an analyst to trace any transaction through all three
statements and verify consistency.

### The Ratio Framework

Financial ratios organize hundreds of data points into a manageable set
of diagnostic signals. They fall into five categories:

**Profitability ratios** measure the ability to generate earnings
relative to revenue, assets, or equity. Gross margin (Gross Profit /
Revenue) reveals pricing power. Operating margin (Operating Income /
Revenue) shows operational efficiency. Net margin (Net Income /
Revenue) shows what reaches the bottom line. Return on Assets (Net
Income / Total Assets) measures how efficiently the company uses its
asset base. Return on Equity (Net Income / Shareholders' Equity)
measures the return to shareholders specifically.

**Efficiency ratios** measure how well the company manages its
resources. Asset turnover (Revenue / Total Assets) shows how much
revenue each dollar of assets generates -- a capital-light business
should have high turnover. Inventory turnover (COGS / Average
Inventory) shows how quickly inventory sells; too low suggests
obsolescence, too high suggests stockouts. Receivables turnover
(Revenue / Average Accounts Receivable) shows how quickly customers
pay; deterioration signals collection problems or channel stuffing.

**Liquidity ratios** measure the ability to meet short-term obligations.
The current ratio (Current Assets / Current Liabilities) is the
broadest measure; below 1.0 means current liabilities exceed current
assets, a potential distress signal. The quick ratio excludes inventory
(which may be hard to liquidate quickly). Operating cash flow to
current liabilities is a more reliable measure since it uses actual
cash rather than accrual-based current assets.

**Leverage ratios** measure the extent of debt financing and the
associated risk. Debt-to-Equity (Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity) is
the most common; higher means more financial risk but also higher
potential returns through leverage. The interest coverage ratio (EBIT /
Interest Expense) measures how comfortably operating profits cover
interest payments; below 2.0 is a warning, below 1.0 means the company
cannot cover interest from operations. The equity multiplier (Total
Assets / Shareholders' Equity) is the leverage component in DuPont
analysis.

**Market value ratios** connect accounting numbers to market prices.
Price-to-Earnings (Market Price / Earnings Per Share) is the most
widely quoted but also the most abused -- it compresses an entire
business into a single number and says nothing about capital structure
or growth. Price-to-Book (Market Price / Book Value Per Share) is
Graham's classic value metric, though increasingly unreliable as
intangible assets dominate modern balance sheets. Enterprise Value to
EBITDA incorporates debt and provides a capital-structure-neutral
valuation multiple.

### ROE, ROIC, and the Value Creation Test

Return on Equity (Net Income / Shareholders' Equity) is the most quoted
return metric but also the most gameable. A company can boost ROE by
taking on debt (increasing assets and earnings without increasing
equity), buying back shares (shrinking the equity denominator), or
writing down assets (reducing book equity). High ROE driven by leverage
is a risk signal, not a quality signal.

Return on Invested Capital (NOPAT / Invested Capital) solves the
leverage problem by measuring the return on ALL capital -- debt and
equity combined -- using an unlevered profit measure (NOPAT, or Net
Operating Profit After Tax). NOPAT is EBIT taxed at the statutory rate,
stripping out the financing decision entirely. Invested capital is debt
plus equity, typically net of excess cash. ROIC measures pure operating
efficiency: how well does management deploy the capital entrusted to
it, regardless of source?

The value creation test compares ROIC to the Weighted Average Cost of
Capital (WACC). If ROIC > WACC, the company creates value with each
dollar invested -- it earns more than its capital costs. If ROIC < WACC,
the company destroys value -- growth makes the problem worse, not
better. This single comparison separates great businesses from mediocre
ones more reliably than any other metric. Aswath Damodaran, whose NYU
Stern datasets track returns across thousands of global firms, treats
the ROIC-WACC spread as the core signal of competitive advantage.

### DuPont Analysis: Decomposing Returns

DuPont analysis breaks ROE into three multiplicative drivers, revealing
WHERE returns come from:

ROE = Net Profit Margin x Asset Turnover x Equity Multiplier

Or equivalently: (Net Income / Revenue) x (Revenue / Total Assets) x
(Total Assets / Shareholders' Equity).

A company with 20% ROE might achieve it through high margins (software
company: 20% margin x 1.0 turnover x 1.0 leverage), high turnover
(retailer: 5% margin x 4.0 turnover x 1.0 leverage), or high leverage
(bank: 1% margin x 0.05 turnover x 400x leverage -- though the standard
formulation is less useful for financial institutions). The same ROE
tells entirely different stories.

The five-step DuPont extends this by decomposing net profit margin into
tax burden, interest burden, and operating margin, separating the
effects of tax strategy, financing choices, and core operations. This
reveals, for example, whether margin improvement comes from genuine
operational gains or one-time tax benefits.

### Cash Flow: Where the Truth Lives

Earnings are an opinion; cash flow is a fact. Accrual accounting gives
management discretion over revenue recognition, depreciation schedules,
reserve estimates, and expense capitalization -- all of which affect
reported earnings without affecting cash. The cash flow statement
strips away these choices and shows what actually happened.

Free Cash Flow (Operating Cash Flow minus Capital Expenditures) is the
cash available to shareholders after the company has maintained or
expanded its asset base. It is the number that ultimately drives
intrinsic value in a discounted cash flow model. A company that
consistently reports growing earnings but stagnant or declining free
cash flow is almost certainly using aggressive accounting. The
earnings-cash flow divergence is one of the most reliable red flags in
financial analysis.

## Evidence

Academic research strongly supports the predictive power of financial
statement analysis. Ou and Penman (1989) demonstrated that a composite
of financial ratios predicts future earnings changes and stock returns
beyond what is captured in current prices, suggesting the market does
not fully impound financial statement information. Piotroski (2000)
developed the F-Score, a nine-point screen based entirely on financial
statement metrics (profitability, leverage, liquidity, operating
efficiency), and showed that high-F-Score value stocks dramatically
outperform low-F-Score value stocks. The F-Score's continued predictive
power two decades later confirms that financial statement analysis
identifies mispricing that the market does not fully correct.

Sloan (1996) demonstrated the accrual anomaly: stocks with high
accruals (large gaps between earnings and cash flow) subsequently
underperform stocks with low accruals, because investors naively fixate
on reported earnings without distinguishing the cash and accrual
components. The magnitude is economically significant: a hedge strategy
long the lowest-accrual decile and short the highest-accrual decile
produced abnormal returns of approximately 10% annually over the sample
period. This finding, replicated across international markets and time
periods, is the empirical foundation for the earnings-quality literature
and directly validates the cash flow analysis described above.

Beneish (1999) developed the M-Score, an eight-variable model using
financial statement ratios to detect earnings manipulation. The model
correctly identified Enron as a likely manipulator years before its
collapse, and subsequent research has validated its predictive accuracy
across multiple markets and time periods. The M-Score's variables --
including days sales in receivables, gross margin index, asset quality
index, and sales growth index -- are all derived from common financial
statement line items, demonstrating that publicly available accounting
data contains sufficient signal to detect fraud before it becomes
public.

Basu (1997) established the accounting conservatism principle:
financial statements reflect bad news more quickly than good news
because of asymmetric verification requirements for gains versus losses.
This means earnings response coefficients are higher for negative
returns than positive returns, and the timeliness of loss recognition
is a measure of reporting quality. Analysts who understand this
asymmetry can read between the lines of financial statements, giving
more weight to what is conservatively reported and applying skepticism
to what appears optimistically presented.

Damodaran's extensive datasets, tracking ROIC, WACC, and their spread
across thousands of global firms over decades, demonstrate that
companies with sustained ROIC-WACC spreads above 5% trade at
significant valuation premiums -- often 3-5x book value or higher --
while companies with negative spreads trade at discounts to book value.
The relationship between returns on capital and valuation multiples is
one of the most robust empirical regularities in finance, persisting
across industries, geographies, and market cycles. A company that
consistently earns 15% on capital when its cost of capital is 8% is not
just more profitable -- it is structurally more valuable, and the market
recognizes this over time.

The practical implication is that financial statement analysis is not a
theoretical exercise -- it produces actionable signals that the market
systematically underweights. The analyst who does the work of reading
the statements, computing the ratios, and tracing the connections has
an information advantage over the investor who reads only the headline
numbers. Importantly, this advantage does not require inside information
or complex models -- it requires only the discipline to do work that the
marginal investor does not.

## Implications

For **value investors**, financial statement analysis is the
non-negotiable foundation. Before estimating intrinsic value, before
assessing competitive position, before forming any opinion about a
business, the analyst must understand its financial statements. The
balance sheet reveals asset quality and financial risk -- a company with
high goodwill relative to tangible equity grew through acquisition and
may have overpaid; a company with rising receivables relative to sales
may be extending credit to marginal customers to maintain growth. The
income statement reveals profitability trends and margin structure --
stable or expanding gross margins signal pricing power; declining
gross margins signal competitive pressure or input cost inflation that
cannot be passed to customers. The cash flow statement reveals whether
those profits are real -- a company reporting record earnings but
negative free cash flow is consuming capital, not generating it. The
ratios connect these statements and surface patterns that any single
number would miss: a rising debt-to-equity ratio combined with a
falling interest coverage ratio signals deteriorating credit quality
well before any default. Graham's insistence on "thorough analysis"
began here, and every Buffett partnership letter contains detailed
discussion of the financial statements of the businesses he owned.

The investor who does this work avoids the most common and costly
mistake in equity investing: buying a superficially cheap stock that is
cheap for a reason the financial statements would have revealed --
deteriorating asset quality, hidden liabilities, or earnings that exist
only on the accrual basis.

For **credit analysts**, the focus shifts from profitability to
solvency. The key questions are not "how much can this company earn?"
but "can this company pay its debts?" Interest coverage, debt-to-EBITDA,
current ratio, and operating cash flow to total debt are the critical
metrics. A company can be highly profitable and still go bankrupt if
its debt matures before its cash arrives -- the maturity schedule of
debt, disclosed in the financial statement notes, is as important as
the total debt balance. Financial statement analysis for credit is
about timing and certainty, not just magnitude. The credit analyst also
focuses on asset coverage: in a liquidation scenario, what assets back
each class of debt, and at what recovery rate? The balance sheet
provides this information, but only if the analyst reads the notes on
collateral, subsidiary guarantees, and intercompany obligations.

For **corporate managers**, financial statement analysis provides the
diagnostic toolkit for running the business. Margin trends reveal
pricing power or competitive pressure. Asset turnover trends reveal
operational efficiency or bloat -- a company whose revenue grows but
asset turnover declines is becoming less efficient, its growth consuming
more capital per dollar of revenue than before. The ROIC-WACC spread
tells management whether their growth strategy creates or destroys
value. DuPont decomposition tells them which lever to pull: if margins
are high but turnover is low, the company may be overcapitalized and
should return capital to shareholders; if margins are low but turnover
is high, the company should focus on cost discipline and pricing power.

For **all analysts**, the discipline of financial statement analysis
forces engagement with the business rather than the narrative. A
company's annual report may describe "industry-leading innovation" and
"operational excellence," but the financial statements will show
declining gross margins, rising receivables, and negative free cash
flow -- or they will not. The statements are the test. Everything else is
storytelling. This distinction -- between what management claims and
what the numbers show -- is the single most valuable skill that
financial statement analysis develops. It is a built-in bullshit
detector for business narratives, and it works regardless of industry,
geography, or market conditions.

## Common Pitfalls

**Ratio without context.** A 25% ROE means something different for a
software company (asset-light, high margin) than for a bank (asset-heavy,
leveraged). Industry averages, historical trends, and peer comparisons
are essential. No ratio is meaningful in isolation.

**Ignoring the notes.** The financial statement notes contain the
assumptions behind the numbers: revenue recognition policies, lease
accounting, pension obligations, contingent liabilities, related-party
transactions. Many of the most significant risks and opportunities in a
business are disclosed only in the notes, not in the headline
statements.

**Equating earnings with cash.** The accrual anomaly persists because
investors continue to anchor on earnings per share. A company with $1
billion in net income and negative $500 million in free cash flow is in
a very different position than one with the opposite. Always trace
earnings to cash.

**Over-reliance on accounting book values.** Book value per share was a
useful anchor when companies owned factories and inventory. Today, the
most valuable assets of many businesses -- brands, patents, network
effects, data, proprietary algorithms -- do not appear on the balance
sheet at all. Book value is increasingly a measure of accounting
history, not economic reality.
