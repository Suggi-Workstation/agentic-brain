# Chip War -- Chris Miller Argues That Control of Scarce Computing Power, Not Oil, Now Decides Economic and Military Rivalry

Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology (Scribner, 2022) is economic historian Chris Miller's account of how the semiconductor industry grew out of Cold War military demand into a globally specialized production system whose few irreplaceable chokepoints now shape economic strength and military power [1][6][8]. Its central claim is that the rivalry between the United States and China is a contest over computing power that runs through design software, lithography tools, and Taiwanese fabrication plants rather than through oil fields [1][5][8]. The book won the 2022 Financial Times Business Book of the Year award and appeared days before Washington imposed sweeping export controls on China, so its thesis can now be read against four years of the policy it describes [3][5][9][11].

## Background

Chris Miller is an associate professor of international history at Tufts University's Fletcher School who had previously written books about the Soviet Union and Russia [1][2]. He has explained that the project began with a question about Russian technology. Russia had brilliant scientists, an impressive education system, and real successes, such as launching the first satellite and exploding the second atomic bomb, yet it "utterly failed at producing computing power"; Miller concluded that a key reason was that it could never manufacture cutting-edge chips [2]. That origin shapes the book. Its organizing question is comparative and political -- why some countries can produce certain advanced technologies and others cannot -- rather than a question about how individual devices work [2][6].

The book was published in October 2022, a few days before the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) announced, on 7 October 2022, rules restricting China's ability to buy advanced computing chips, to build supercomputers, and to manufacture advanced semiconductors [3][9]. It became a New York Times bestseller and won the Financial Times Business Book of the Year award for 2022, and The Economist and The New Yorker both listed it as a book of the year [2][5][8]. Barry Eichengreen, reviewing it in Foreign Affairs, called it "at once edifying and entertaining" but noted that Miller "finalized the book before recent shifts in U.S. policy" toward denying China advanced chip-making technology [4]. Noah Smith observed that Miller was in high demand after the controls were announced, as the expert best positioned to explain them to the public [3].

Historian of technology W. Patrick McCray placed the book in a specific tradition. Canonical histories of computing run from Alan Turing's 1937 paper and Claude Shannon's switching logic through ENIAC, the 1947 transistor, the integrated circuit, and Moore's 1965 observation; Miller instead organizes his history around geopolitical shifts in the production of chips [1]. McCray compares the approach to Daniel Yergin's history of the oil industry, The Prize, and to Sven Beckert's history of cotton: like those books, Chip War centers on the production of a strategic commodity rather than on its consumption, and it treats business and statecraft as the main forces [1]. McCray's overall judgment is that Miller bet on the claim that the strategic importance of chips is "overlooked at our peril," and that the bet "has largely paid off" [1].

The book is 464 pages long; McCray describes it as roughly 350 pages excluding notes, divided into 54 short chapters arranged roughly chronologically [1][6]. Reviewers summarize an arc that begins with the transistor and the first Silicon Valley chip firms, passes through Soviet attempts to copy American chips, the Japanese challenge of the 1980s, and the rise of South Korean and Taiwanese producers, and ends with the US-China rivalry over artificial intelligence and future wireless networks [6][7]. Gallagher notes that the technical exposition runs from William Shockley's 1945 theorizing about a solid-state valve to the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography used in modern fabrication [6]. Squatrito describes the narrative as a relay that moves from laboratory to factory to cabinet room, driven throughout by the pressure to increase transistor counts [8].

The policy context at publication was unusually charged. President Biden had signed the CHIPS and Science Act on 9 August 2022; McCray reports that it allocated $39 billion over five years for semiconductor production incentives and $13 billion for research programs, after Senate passage by 64-33 and House passage by 243-187 [1]. Speaker Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan in August 2022 had prompted days of Chinese military exercises, which made Western dependence on Taiwanese chips politically visible [1]. The 2020-2021 chip shortage had already taught manufacturers that a car missing a single specific chip could not be completed, leaving thousands of nearly finished vehicles waiting in factories [2]. The book thus arrived as both a history and a briefing for a policy debate that was already under way.

This entry reconstructs the book's argument from Miller's own published statements and from full-text reviews that report the book's content. Quotations from the book are those that reviewers reproduce, and characterizations of particular chapters are attributed to the reviewer who made them.

## Core Concepts

### Computing power as the foundation of national power

The book's governing thesis, in Gallagher's summary, is that "the semiconductor industry shapes international politics, the world economy, and the global balance of power" [6]. Squatrito quotes Miller's sharpest formulation: "[t]he rivalry between the United States and China may well be determined by computing power" [8]. The claim rests on scale and ubiquity. Krammer opens his review with a passage from the book stating that in the previous year the chip industry "produced more transistors than the combined quantity of all goods produced by all other companies, in all other industries, in all human history" [7]. Chips sit inside phones, cars, household appliances, the electricity grid, and weapons, and in Squatrito's summary nations that control their production also control access to military advantage and innovation across many sectors of the economy [2][5][8].

Miller has clarified how this claim applies to military power, where it is easiest to misread. A typical new car contains about a thousand chips, most of them simple, but its high-value features -- infotainment, radar or lidar sensing, and semi-autonomous driving -- need sophisticated chips, some of which sit in cell towers and data centers rather than in the car [3]. Military systems follow the same pattern: most chips in tanks and missiles are low-end, but the chips that provide differentiated capability are not, and many of the most important ones are in the networks and data centers that guide and train weapons [3]. His example is the HIMARS rocket system used in Ukraine, whose munitions do not need ultra-advanced chips but rely on targeting information produced by large arrays of sensors and processors [3]. The argument is therefore not that any single chip is decisive. Miller states that there is no chip whose absence would halt China's military modernization, because engineers can always design around a component; the cost of doing so is a growing set of trade-offs in performance, power consumption, and reliability [3].

### Chokepoints, not reserves: where the oil analogy helps and where it fails

Commentators have long called chips "the new oil," and the Financial Times award citation describes semiconductors as "more indispensable than oil" [1][5]. The publisher's synopsis adds that China spends more money importing chips than buying oil, a comparison Krammer also reports from the book [5][7]. McCray grants that the comparison makes sense up to a point: both are strategically important global commodities essential to the functioning of contemporary society [1].

McCray marks where the analogy fails. Oil is a natural resource found in particular places; chips are manufactured by people and firms in places they choose, and silicon is the second most abundant element on the planet [1]. He quotes Intel's then chief executive Patrick Gelsinger: "God decided where the oil reserves are, we get to decide where the fabs are" [1]. The anxiety about where chips are made is therefore a problem created by business and state decisions, not by geology. McCray also notes that no country dominates oil the way Taiwan dominates chip-making: by his figures the United States, the largest oil producer, supplies about 15 percent of global oil, while Taiwan builds around 65 percent of the world's chips and over 90 percent of the most advanced ones [1].

The book's analytical unit, as its reviewers describe it, is the chokepoint: a stage of production supplied by one firm or a handful of firms [1][8]. McCray singles out the Dutch firm ASML, founded in 1984, whose lithography machines cost $200 million or more and without which, in his summary, global chip-making would stop [1]. Squatrito adds electronic design automation (EDA) software, the toolchain used to draw and verify transistor layouts, which three firms -- Synopsys, Cadence, and Siemens -- dominate, giving their home countries outsized control over who can design advanced processors [8]. The author's synthesis is that in this model power comes not from owning a large share of a commodity but from owning a step that every competitor must pass through.

The author's assessment is that the concentration statistics attached to this argument need care. Eichengreen's review says "over a fifth of all chip fabrication happens in Taiwan" [4]; the publisher's synopsis says 37 percent of the global supply of chips is made there [5]; Krammer gives 37 percent in his text and 41 percent in a footnote attributed to the book [7]; McCray gives around 65 percent [1]; and Miller has said that Taiwanese firms produce "over a third of the new computing power the world adds each year" and that TSMC makes 90 percent of the world's most advanced processor chips [2]. These figures measure different bases -- fabrication capacity, unit output, computing power added, and leading-edge share -- so a reader should identify which base a claim uses before comparing it with another.

### The state created the market and now depends on it

Eichengreen condenses the book's political economy into a reversal. Early chip firms depended on demand from the US defense and military establishment; today that establishment depends on the chip industry for critical strategic and battlefield capabilities [4]. McCray reports Miller's observation that the US government bought almost all of the integrated circuits that Texas Instruments and Fairchild produced in the early 1960s [1]. In the 1980s, chips became the foundation of the Pentagon's "offset" strategy, associated with William Perry, which used superior and more plentiful American chips to underpin precision weapons, satellite navigation, and command-and-control systems against Soviet advantages in manpower and materiel [1]. The publisher's synopsis states the most ambitious version of the claim: faster chips helped defeat the Soviet Union [5].

State support recurs in every national story. McCray notes that the federal government answered Silicon Valley's appeals against Japanese competition by funding the SEMATECH consortium, founded in 1987, and DARPA computing programs, while chipmakers argued that semiconductors were a strategic resource like oil or steel [1]. He also observes that this pattern of state support appears in Hong Kong, South Korea, the Soviet Union, and China, where Huawei received some $75 billion in state subsidies [1]. Krammer summarizes Miller's account of Japanese chip giants growing through significant government support and of TSMC's success depending on "massive governmental support" [7]. McCray draws the theme's conclusion: the industry's free-market self-image, voiced by Steve Jobs's 1996 remark that Silicon Valley "doesn't traditionally look for handouts," is historically inaccurate [1]. The industry was built with state demand and support, and states now treat it as a security asset [1][4].

### Technology cannot simply be stolen: the Soviet copying strategy

The Soviet Union is the book's negative case. Reviewers summarize Miller's account of a Soviet strategy of reverse-engineering Texas Instruments circuits, recruiting spy networks that included the American engineers Alfred Sarant and Joel Barr, and building Zelenograd as a Soviet counterpart to Silicon Valley [6][7]. Gallagher's summary of the result is direct: Soviet spies could acquire the most advanced American chips, but they could not replicate the precise manufacturing processes needed to produce their own, and the copying strategy left Soviet technology years behind [6]. McCray recounts the response of engineers at Digital Equipment Corporation, who etched a microscopic message in Russian on their chips: "VAX -- when you care enough to steal the very best" [1].

The author's synthesis is that the scarce asset in this account is process knowledge, not designs or samples. Miller applies the same reasoning to China's effort to build its own lithography tools. ASML needed three decades and close collaboration with TSMC and Intel to develop EUV machines, and replication requires more than copying components: hundreds of thousands of parts must work reliably enough for high-volume production, and if each one failed only once a year the tool would almost never run [3]. Copying a machine, like copying a chip, does not transfer the tacit knowledge needed to operate it at yield.

### Specialization and offshoring: how the United States gave away fabrication

The book's account of globalization begins in the 1960s. McCray highlights Texas Instruments' 1968 decision to open a fabrication plant in Taiwan, followed by chip work in Singapore, Hong Kong, the Philippines, and Malaysia [1]. In the 1980s, the emergence of foundries -- firms that manufacture chips designed by others -- separated design from fabrication, and American firms initially welcomed Asian factories as counterweights to the Japanese threat [1]. Krammer summarizes Miller's account of Washington applying "the enemy of my enemy is my friend" by limiting Japanese access to the US market and transferring technology to South Korean firms, after which Samsung became the leading memory-chip maker by the early 1990s [7]. When Japan's economy faltered and the Soviet Union collapsed, Miller writes, "The Cold War was over; Silicon Valley had won" [1].

Morris Chang is the pivotal figure. Born in mainland China in 1931, educated at MIT, and employed at Texas Instruments from 1958, he moved to Taiwan after being passed over for the top job at Texas Instruments and founded TSMC in 1987 with extensive state support [1][6][7]. Miller attributes TSMC's rise to two choices: it designed no chips of its own and manufactured only for others, and its exclusive focus on manufacturing let it hone more advanced processes than any other firm [2]. Gallagher reads the book's broader causal claim as a paradox: strengths of US post-Cold War strategy, including multilateral supply chains in Asia, caused today's weaknesses, because Silicon Valley concentrated on advancing technology rather than manufacturing it and, in Miller's argument, US firms replaced Ph.D.-holding innovators with MBA managers focused on efficiency and profit margins [6]. The publisher's synopsis states the moral bluntly: America's edge was undermined by "the naive assumption" that letting Taiwan, Korea, and Europe take over manufacturing served American interests [5]. McCray recalls the warning of AMD founder Jerry Sanders that "real men have fabs" [1].

### Taiwan as linchpin, and the limits of the silicon shield

Taiwan is where the book's arguments converge. Miller has said that losing access to Taiwan-made chips would cause the worst disruption to manufacturing output since the Great Depression: world smartphone production would fall by far more than half, many PC processors can currently be made only in Taiwan, almost all of the graphics processors that run AI applications in data centers are made there, and even less advanced chips would be scarce, since 31 percent of capacity for the processor chips widely used in autos and household goods is in Taiwan and 23 percent in China [2]. Rebuilding that capacity elsewhere would take years because the tools are scarce and themselves contain chips, imposing several trillion dollars of losses on global manufacturing [2]. Gallagher quotes Miller's observation that Taiwan's leadership recognizes the chip industry as "its greatest source of leverage on the international stage" [6].

The book's chokepoint logic implies that the island's fabs would be of little use to an invader. McCray reports Miller's assessment that Chinese aggression, whether a blockade or an invasion, would likely fail, and links it to the industry's dependence on global supply chains and skilled workers: without ASML's lithography machines, Taiwan's fabs, even if occupied, would slowly stop working [1]. Yet Miller does not rely on this interdependence to keep the peace. Asked whether Taiwan's "silicon shield" makes war less likely, he answered, "I hope that President Tsai is right, but I'm not sure that she is," citing Germany's failed bet that energy interdependence would restrain Russia [2]. He has also noted that the United States committed itself to Taiwan's defense "since before the first semiconductor was invented" [2]. The author's synthesis is that the book separates two claims that are often merged: interdependence makes conquest unrewarding, but it does not reliably deter.

### China's catch-up and the logic of denial

The book treats China as a challenger unlike Japan or South Korea. McCray identifies two differences in Miller's account: China's proximity to Taiwan, and the fact that Japan and South Korea depended on American military protection [1]. Krammer summarizes Miller's account of Xi Jinping's strategy for reducing dependence on foreign technology through heavy state investment, mandatory joint ventures, infringement of intellectual property, and attempts to acquire Western firms [7]. Miller's own verdict on the results was that China's government had been "a mediocre venture capitalist," producing real successes in chip design alongside "abject failures and some outright frauds" [2].

Squatrito describes the US response that the book lays out as two tracks: subsidies for domestic capacity and research through the CHIPS Act, with guardrails that bar subsidized firms from expanding advanced lines in China, and denial of key tools, including coordination with the Dutch government to keep EUV machines out of competitors' reach [8]. In Squatrito's reading, export controls bind the United States to partners in the Netherlands and Japan [8], and Miller has said that neither China risk -- disrupted access to Taiwan's chips or Chinese technological catch-up -- can be solved by the United States acting alone [3].

## Evidence

The book is a narrative history, so its evidence base can be assessed in two ways: by how specialists judged its historical method, and by how its central claims fared once the export controls it describes were applied. The cases below cover both.

### Case 1: Specialist reviews of the historical method

McCray reviewed an advance copy as a historian of computing, comparing the book with the canonical literature and with O'Mara's history of Silicon Valley [1]. His finding is that the book offers "a top-level rather than intimate or insider view" of the industry's migration from the United States to East Asia [1]. He wanted a finer-grained analysis of corporate decision-making -- for example, of how Samsung moved from dried fish to semiconductors -- and attributes its absence partly to source limits: corporate archives are hard to access, and a deep global history would require Chinese, Korean, and Japanese sources or a team of co-authors [1]. He also judges that the 54 short chapters produce a sense of "temporal, topical, and spatial dislocation," with little continuity of actors from chapter to chapter, which he suggests may reflect publisher pressure for a fast-paced narrative [1].

Gallagher, an Air Force officer reviewing for Joint Force Quarterly, read the book as strategy rather than history. His finding is that it supplies "a coherent realist rationale for the defense of Taiwan" that omits the usual arguments from democracy and liberal ideology, and that it is valuable to joint-force decision-makers for that reason [6]. His criticism is that Miller writes primarily from an American perspective, "picks a side," and could have explored Chinese identity and alternative reasons why Xi Jinping seeks reunification [6]. Krammer, an international-business scholar writing in the Journal of International Business Policy, read the book as historical evidence against economic nationalism: in his reading it "persuasively condemns nationalistic reactions," suggesting that self-sufficiency policies never delivered [7]. Squatrito's 2025 review in St Antony's International Review found the book "a clear, concrete map of the chokepoints, players, and high stakes that define the 21st century" but judged the European Union, India, and the energy demands of chip production underexplored [8].

The three readings differ in what they take from the same book. A historian found it thin on archives, a military reviewer found it strategically persuasive, and an international-business scholar found it a warning against autarky. The author's assessment is that this divergence reflects the book's design: it assembles evidence for a thesis about power and leaves the reader to draw policy conclusions.

### Case 2: The October 2022 controls as a test of the chokepoint map

The BIS rules of 7 October 2022 provide a direct comparison with the book's map of chokepoints. They added advanced computing chips and certain semiconductor manufacturing equipment to the Commerce Control List; required licenses for items destined for supercomputer or semiconductor production end uses in China; extended US jurisdiction over certain foreign-made items; and required licenses for exports to Chinese fabs producing logic chips at 16 or 14 nanometers or below with non-planar transistors, DRAM at 18-nanometer half-pitch or less, or NAND flash with 128 layers or more, with a presumption of denial for Chinese-owned facilities [9]. The rules also restricted US persons from supporting chip production at such facilities without a license [9]. The Congressional Research Service reports that the United States then negotiated with Japan and the Netherlands to align equipment controls [11].

The method of comparison is simple: if the book's map is right, policy aimed at China's progress should target tools, know-how, and advanced chips rather than broad trade. The author's finding is that the rules did exactly that, covering equipment, the expertise of US persons, and leading-edge chips, and that they were followed by negotiations to bring Japanese and Dutch equipment controls into line [9][11]. Miller's own forecast in 2023 was that the controls were "likely to work at delaying China's progress toward the most cutting edge capabilities," that they would need regular updates because export control is "a game of whac-a-mole," and that expanding them to lower technology levels, where China already had domestic capability, was less likely to work [3].

### Case 3: Workarounds and adaptation, 2022-2025

CRS's September 2025 review of the controls documents how firms and governments adapted [11]. After BIS controlled Nvidia's A100 and H100 graphics processors in October 2022, Nvidia sold modified A800 and H800 chips in China; the Chinese firm DeepSeek used the H800 for its AI language model; after BIS controlled those chips in 2023, Nvidia offered the H20 [11]. The memory maker CXMC reportedly recalculated its chip features to fall below the October 2022 thresholds, and Huawei used third-party design firms to have its Ascend AI chips fabricated at TSMC [11]. Huawei had earlier, in November 2020, sold its Honor business to a Chinese government consortium that BIS did not place on the Entity List [11]. Stockpiling ahead of anticipated rules and advanced packaging that combines mature-node chips offered further routes around controls [11]. In July 2025, the US firm Cadence pleaded guilty and paid about $213 million to resolve charges over EDA sales to China's National University of Defense Technology [11].

BIS revised the rules repeatedly: it added Macau in January 2023, closed the gap for chips designed just below the 2022 thresholds in October 2023, extended controls to high-bandwidth memory, DRAM, and advanced-packaging equipment and added 140 Chinese entities in December 2024, and proposed a global licensing system for AI chips in January 2025 [11]. The finding is consistent with Miller's whac-a-mole description [3][11]. Whether the controls achieved their purpose is less settled. Krammer concluded in 2023 that they "appear to have curbed Chinese advancements" but that their long-term effectiveness remained unclear [7]. CRS noted in 2025 that Congress might direct BIS to report on Chinese semiconductor advances, including the ability of Chinese firms to produce chips at consistent quality and scale [11]; the author's reading is that the public evidence on effectiveness remains incomplete.

### Case 4: Reciprocal chokepoints and policy reversal, 2025-2026

A further test of the book's framework came from China's response and from changes in US policy. CRS reports that China used export controls on germanium, gallium, and rare-earth magnets, along with antitrust and antidumping actions, to pressure and retaliate against US controls [11]. In July 2025, BIS rescinded license requirements for EDA firms after China agreed to resume licensing rare-earth magnets for US firms, and BIS reportedly withheld export-control actions during tariff talks [11]. In May 2025, the Commerce Department rescinded the January 2025 AI Diffusion Rule before its compliance date, arguing that it would have "stifled American innovation" [10]. In August 2025, BIS approved Nvidia's H20 and AMD's MI308 for sale in China on terms under which the US government would receive 15 percent of proceeds [11].

On 8 December 2025, President Trump announced that Nvidia's H200, described by CRS as the company's second-best AI chip, could be shipped to approved customers in China [12][13]. In January 2026, BIS issued a rule moving the H200 and similar chips from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review, provided applicants show that the exports will not reduce production capacity available to US customers, that the Chinese buyer has adopted export-compliance procedures including customer screening, and that the chips have passed independent third-party testing in the United States [12][13]. A 25 percent payment to the US government was implemented as a Section 232 import tariff; CRS legal analysts judged it debatable whether this is consistent with Section 232 and with the statutory bar on fees charged in connection with export licenses, and uncertain whether a court would find it consistent with the Constitution's Export Clause [13].

The author's interpretation is that these events confirm the book's mechanism while exposing the question on which, in Eichengreen's reading, it "delivers no verdict": whether US export controls would contain China's industry or only spur further Chinese support for it [4]. Chokepoints did confer leverage, but China held chokepoints too, in minerals and magnets, and US leverage was traded in negotiations and partly converted into government revenue [11][13]. After 2024 the decisive variable was bargaining and domestic politics as much as technology.

## Implications

### For readers: what to take from the book

The book's most durable contribution is a way of seeing the semiconductor industry as a map of dependencies. It teaches readers to ask which steps in a production chain have only one or two suppliers, who owns them, and which governments can control their export [1][8]. The author's assessment is that the habit transfers to other strategic goods, from critical minerals to AI models. Its second contribution is the reversal described by Eichengreen: the military created the chip industry, and the military now depends on it [4]. Readers interested in defense, industrial policy, or technology investment gain a historical account of how that dependence formed.

Readers should also read with discipline. The concentration figures attached to Taiwan vary between sources from over a fifth to around 65 percent, depending on whether they count fabrication capacity, chip supply, or computing power [1][2][4][5][7]. The causal claim that chips helped defeat the Soviet Union is reported by McCray and stated in the publisher's synopsis [1][5], but the reviews summarized above do not test how much weight it can bear against other causes of Soviet decline. The author's assessment is that the book is strongest as a history of how chokepoints formed and weakest as a forecast of how states would use them.

### For policymakers: chokepoints are perishable assets

The book's policy lesson is that leverage over technology lies in specific, narrow stages of production, and that this leverage works only if allies act together [3][8]. The October 2022 controls followed this logic, and the United States then negotiated with Japan and the Netherlands to align their equipment controls [9][11]. The subsequent record adds three cautions. First, controls require constant maintenance: firms redesign products to sit just below thresholds, and intermediaries move work to unlisted entities [11]. Miller anticipated this, recommending regular updates while warning against expanding controls to technology levels where China can already substitute domestic products [3]. Second, leverage is reciprocal: China's minerals and magnets proved to be bargaining chips that bought relief from US controls [11]. Third, controls that are negotiated or monetized raise legal and credibility questions, which CRS identifies in the 2025 revenue-sharing terms and the 2026 tariff arrangement [11][13].

On the domestic side, Miller's later statements extend the book's diagnosis to the cost of rebuilding fabrication. About 70 percent of a cutting-edge fab's cost is in tools that cost the same everywhere, so the decisive differences lie in tax treatment of capital spending, construction costs, utilities, permitting, and the know-how of experienced builders [3]. He has argued that US permitting under environmental review makes fab construction slower than in Europe, that the CHIPS investment tax credit, scheduled to expire at the end of 2026, should be made permanent, and that research funds should help startups reach the prototype stage [3]. He has also warned that governments cannot collectively manage the chip industry, and said he gets nervous when political leaders use phrases like "supply chain," which they do not understand in sufficient detail, or "resilience," which in his words "doesn't mean anything" [3]. The author's assessment is that industrial policy aimed at chips works best, on Miller's account, when it lowers the specific costs of building and operating fabs rather than announcing capacity targets.

### For investors and corporate strategists: policy is now a business variable

For investors, the book and its aftermath show that geopolitical exposure can matter as much as technology. Nvidia's experience illustrates the point. Between 2022 and 2025 the company designed successive China-specific products to fit control thresholds -- the A800 and H800, then the H20, then the planned B30 and B40 -- saw the H20 placed under a license requirement in 2025 and then licensed on revenue-sharing terms, and faced a Chinese antitrust finding and the effective blacklisting of one of its China-specific chips [11]. In January 2026 the H200 became eligible for case-by-case licensing [12][13]. CRS cites a May 2025 report in which Nvidia's chief executive said that US restrictions had cut the company's China market share nearly in half [11]. The author's synthesis is that revenue from a large market can depend on export-licensing decisions made for reasons unrelated to a firm's products.

Miller's account of Japan's decline offers a second strategic lesson. He attributes it to two errors: Japanese firms pursued market share rather than profitability, and they neglected software [3]. The most durable chip franchises, in his account, rest on software ecosystems -- Intel's x86 architecture tied to Windows, Apple's chips designed around iOS, and Nvidia's CUDA programming interface -- while Japanese firms ended up in commodity memory, where price dominates [3]. He argues that China is replicating the market-share error today [3]. The author's assessment is that investors should distinguish chip firms protected by an ecosystem from firms competing on price in commodity segments, a distinction also central to the brain's topic on semiconductor industry economics.

### For historians and analysts: method lessons

The book also shows the limits of a narrative history written for a general audience. McCray's critique -- limited corporate archives, few Asian-language sources, many short chapters -- identifies what a scholarly history of the same subject would need [1]. Gallagher's critique identifies a second gap: the absence of a Chinese perspective on Taiwan and on China's goals [6]. The author's recommendation is that analysts who use the book for forecasting supplement it with sources that address these gaps, and with current records of export-control rules, which BIS revised at least six times between October 2022 and September 2025 [11].

Finally, readers have disagreed about what the book concludes on export controls. Krammer read the book as an argument against nationalism and as pessimistic about export controls, stating that Miller suggests the bans "are likely to fail their mandate" and "at best" delay China's progress [7]. Miller, by contrast, told Smith in 2023 that he thought the controls were likely to work at delaying China's progress toward the cutting edge [3]. Eichengreen found no verdict at all [4]. The author's assessment is that a book about chokepoints became a Rorschach test for views on economic security, and that readers should check claims about what the book concludes against the author's own statements.

## Criticisms

Reviewers raise several recurring objections. On perspective, Gallagher notes that Miller writes primarily from an American perspective, although he acknowledges the views of Taiwan and other producing countries, and that on the present conflict he assumes the reader will accept the predominance of Western literature; McCray notes that a global history would need sources in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese [1][6]. On omissions, McCray writes that the book does not mention the environmental effects of making electronics, such as Silicon Valley's Superfund sites and the water demands of fabs, and that women appear mainly in a chapter on the largely female assembly workforce of the 1960s [1]. Squatrito, by contrast, says the book mentions environmental costs briefly but does not address the energy intensity of chip production, and he finds the European Union and India underexplored [8].

On politics, McCray observes that Miller is more critical of the Obama administration's attitude toward China -- "almost everyone had drunk their own Kool-Aid about globalization" -- than of the subsequent China hawks, and notes that some experts dispute whether Trump-era measures achieved their goals [1]. He also asks how Miller, who credits Trump for pushing back against Chinese companies such as Huawei, would explain the large number of Trump-aligned members of Congress who voted against the CHIPS Act [1]. On the oil analogy, McCray notes that chips were promoted as a strategic resource like oil in the 1980s even as oil prices were falling, and that how a commodity comes to be considered "strategic" is a question the book could have explored more fully [1].
