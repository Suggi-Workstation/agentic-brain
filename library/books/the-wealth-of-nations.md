# The Wealth of Nations -- Smith Made Prosperity a System of Specialization, Exchange, and Public Institutions

Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations argues that national prosperity comes from productive labor organized through specialization, exchange, capital accumulation, competition, and institutions that secure justice, rather than from hoarded money or protected merchants [1][4][5]. The book is therefore not a slogan for selfishness or a complete case for laissez-faire: it is a five-part inquiry into how commercial society creates wealth, distributes income, invites capture, and requires public authority [1][3][9]. Its lasting value lies in treating markets, laws, public finance, moral norms, and political power as parts of one system rather than as separate subjects [2][8][9].

## Background

An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations first appeared in 1776 and was revised during Smith's lifetime; the Econlib edition used here follows Edwin Cannan's 1904 compilation of Smith's fifth edition of 1789 [1]. Smith wrote as a Scottish moral philosopher observing an expanding commercial society, not as a modern economist working inside an established discipline. The University of Glasgow describes the work as both analytical study and historical narrative, directed at the question of what makes nations wealthy and at the effects of commerce on society for better and worse [4]. That dual method matters: Smith moves repeatedly between abstract mechanisms, historical comparisons, institutional criticism, and policy design rather than presenting a single mathematical model [1][4][9].

The book followed The Theory of Moral Sentiments, first published in 1759, in which Smith explained moral judgment through sympathy, social interaction, rules, virtues, and the imagined standpoint of an impartial spectator [2][3]. Smith later said that The Wealth of Nations partly fulfilled his earlier promise to examine law, government, police, revenue, and arms [2]. Modern scholarship therefore rejects a clean division between an altruistic early Smith and a selfish economic Smith. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy treats his political views as recoverable from both books and from his lectures on jurisprudence, while Amartya Sen emphasizes that Smith distinguished the sufficiency of self-interest in a particular exchange from the broader motivations and institutions needed to sustain economic life [3][8]. Jerry Evensky likewise reads the economic argument within Smith's larger ethics and jurisprudence [9].

The immediate intellectual target was the mercantile system. Mercantilist policy treated bullion, trade surpluses, import restraints, bounties, colonial monopolies, and privileged companies as routes to national power. Smith instead defined a country's real wealth through the annual produce available for consumption and asked what raises the productive powers of labor [1][4]. He also rejected the physiocratic claim that land alone creates a net product, although he learned from the physiocrats to analyze the economy as an interdependent order [1][9]. His argument was not that government should disappear, but that policy should be judged by whether it enlarges the real income and security of the population rather than the revenue or influence of favored groups [1][4][5].

The architecture of the book develops that argument across five books. Book I explains specialization, exchange, money, prices, wages, profit, and rent. Book II analyzes stock, money, capital accumulation, and productive labor. Book III uses economic history to explain different paths of development. Book IV attacks mercantilism and physiocracy, including import restraints, bounties, colonies, and monopoly. Book V turns to defense, justice, public works, education, taxation, and public debt [1][9]. Evensky's reader's guide stresses that this sequence moves from economic processes, through system dynamics and historical evidence, to rival doctrines and policy implications [9]. Reading only the pin factory or the invisible-hand passage therefore removes each from the institutional and political argument that gives it meaning [1][7][9].

The book also belongs to a specific eighteenth-century setting. Smith wrote before modern national accounts, marginal analysis, industrial organization, modern labor law, mass representative democracy, and the mature factory system. Yet he observed commercial expansion, colonial monopoly, corporate government in India, unequal bargaining between workers and masters, public debt, and the fiscal needs of the state [1][3][10]. The University of Glasgow consequently presents the work as both foundational and radical: it reframed wealth around general living standards, attacked protectionist interests, and criticized forces behind colonial and slave trades [4]. At the same time, David Williams cautions that Smith's opposition to colonialism often focused on costs to the metropolitan country and remained entangled with an eighteenth-century idea of historical progress [10]. The book should thus be read neither as timeless scripture nor as a museum piece, but as a historically bounded inquiry whose method often survives better than its particulars.

Later readers compressed this wide argument into a few emblems: the pin factory, the butcher-brewer-baker exchange, and the invisible hand. Joseph Persky shows that the invisible hand moved from a historically conditional illustration into a general mythology of self-regulating markets [7]. The University of Glasgow similarly warns that Smith's metaphor is often misappropriated as proof that free markets automatically reach equilibrium without public authority [4]. Those reductions obscure the book's repeated attention to justice, public goods, education, bargaining power, monopoly, and political capture [1][3][5][6]. Recovering the full book means reconstructing the chain Smith actually proposed: specialization raises output; exchange widens specialization; capital finances it; competition disciplines private power; justice secures expectations; and public institutions address tasks or harms that private exchange alone will not reliably solve [1][5][9].

## Core Concepts

### Wealth Is Annual Production for Human Use

Smith begins by locating wealth in the annual labor of a nation and the goods and services it supplies, rather than in a stock of gold and silver [1]. This shift changes the unit of judgment. A commercial policy is not successful merely because it brings bullion into the treasury or raises a merchant's profit; it must increase the real command over necessities and conveniences available to society [1][4]. The University of Glasgow interprets this as a move from merchant wealth toward the living standards of the population [4]. Smith did not possess modern gross domestic product, but his opening framework anticipates a flow measure of national income and makes ordinary consumption, not royal treasure, the test of opulence [1][4].

This definition also connects production to distribution. The annual product becomes wages to labor, profit to owners of stock, and rent to landowners, with other revenues derived from those broad sources [1]. Smith analyzes these shares as both prices and social relations. Wages are formed by contract, but workers and masters do not bargain from equal positions: employers are fewer, can coordinate more easily, and can usually subsist longer during a dispute, while workers need immediate income [1]. Smith nevertheless associates rising real wages with a growing economy and treats the condition of the laboring majority as a central indicator of improvement [1][3]. Prosperity in the book is therefore not simply aggregate output; its social meaning depends on whether growth reaches the people whose labor produces it.

### Division of Labor Creates Productivity

The pin factory is Smith's most famous demonstration. Dividing pin manufacture into specialized operations raises output because repetition improves dexterity, workers save time otherwise lost moving between tasks, and specialization encourages labor-saving machinery [1]. The point extends beyond a workshop. Smith's woolen-coat example traces a common product through shepherds, sorters, dyers, carriers, toolmakers, merchants, and many other contributors, showing that ordinary consumption depends on cooperation among people who never meet [1]. The author's synthesis is that the pin factory supplies the microscopic mechanism while the coat supplies the network view: productivity emerges from coordinated specialization inside firms and across markets.

Specialization is not unlimited. Smith's third chapter states that the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market: a worker can specialize narrowly only when enough buyers exist to absorb the output [1]. Roads, canals, ports, towns, foreign trade, and institutional security therefore affect productivity by changing the size and reliability of exchange [1][5]. This is a compounding mechanism. Wider markets support finer specialization; finer specialization lowers cost and expands output; greater output supports exchange, saving, and further investment [1][9]. The argument joins geography, infrastructure, trade, and organization rather than treating productivity as a purely technical property of machines.

Smith also records a cost. Repetitive work can narrow a worker's faculties and civic capacity, which is why Book V discusses instruction for people whose occupations provide little intellectual variety [1][6]. Scholars disagree on the exact funding arrangement Smith ultimately endorsed. James Otteson argues that Smith explored public provision but leaned toward private provision under limited public support, while documenting a broader scholarly consensus that reads Smith as favoring a public role [6]. The disagreement itself establishes the narrower point: Smith did not treat maximum task fragmentation as an unqualified social good. The productive gain created an educational and civic problem that institutions had to consider [1][6].

### Exchange Coordinates Specialized Strangers

Smith attributes specialization to a human propensity to truck, barter, and exchange, not to a prior plan that assigns occupations [1]. Once people depend on the products of others, they ordinarily secure cooperation by offering terms advantageous to the other party. The butcher-brewer-baker passage makes a limited claim about obtaining dinner in a commercial transaction: a buyer appeals to sellers' interests rather than relying on their benevolence [1]. It does not claim that self-interest is the only human motive, that every self-interested action benefits society, or that moral obligations disappear at the market's edge [2][3][8].

The Theory of Moral Sentiments begins from fellow-feeling and develops standards of propriety through social judgment and the impartial spectator [2][3]. Sen therefore distinguishes the role of self-interest at the moment of exchange from trust, justice, duty, generosity, and public reasoning across social life [8]. The author's synthesis is that Smith uses different motivational assumptions for different settings. Anonymous exchange cannot depend on intimate benevolence, but commercial society still depends on people who recognize rules, restrain injury, keep promises, and judge conduct from perspectives wider than immediate advantage [2][3][8].

### Prices Coordinate, but Power Shapes Distribution

Smith distinguishes a commodity's natural price, sufficient to cover the ordinary wages, profit, and rent required for production, from its market price, which fluctuates with effective demand and the quantity brought to market [1]. Entry, exit, scarcity, and competition tend to move market prices toward ordinary levels, but monopoly, regulation, secrecy, custom, and unequal bargaining can obstruct that movement [1]. This is not the later marginalist theory of value, and some of Smith's labor-value and tax-incidence reasoning did not survive subsequent economics [1][9]. Its durable contribution is the attempt to explain prices as signals embedded in production costs, class incomes, institutions, and competitive conditions.

Smith is especially suspicious when producers organize politically. Merchants and manufacturers possess concentrated information and incentives, and their proposals may disguise private advantage as national interest [1][4]. Import restraints grant domestic producers a monopoly against their own countrymen; chartered companies combine commercial privilege with public power; masters can tacitly coordinate against workers; and occupational rules can restrict entry [1][3]. Competition is therefore not an assumption that power has vanished. It is an institutional achievement that requires rules against privilege, secure access, and skepticism toward claims made by groups positioned to capture policy [1][4][9].

### Capital Accumulation Connects Saving to Growth

Book II explains how stock supports workers during production, supplies tools and materials, and can be accumulated and redirected toward future output [1]. Smith distinguishes consumption that maintains productive labor from expenditures that do not reproduce a marketable value, a classification rooted in his period and not identical to modern national accounting [1][9]. The central dynamic is clearer than the terminology: saving matters when it finances productive activity, while the division of labor becomes more powerful when capital equips specialists and introduces machinery [1][4].

This creates a circular process rather than a one-time gain. Productive organization generates surplus; some surplus becomes stock; stock advances wages and inputs; investment supports more specialized and capital-intensive production; and the enlarged annual product permits further accumulation [1][4][9]. Smith's growth theory thus links behavior at the firm level to national development. It also explains why security matters: people will not accumulate and employ stock when property, contracts, or personal safety are persistently exposed to arbitrary seizure [5].

### Natural Liberty Requires Justice and Competition

Smith's system of natural liberty removes many policies that direct capital toward favored uses, but it does not remove the state [1][3]. Douglas Irwin identifies the administration of justice as a neglected mechanism in Smith's account: secure property and reliable enforcement allow people to keep the fruits of labor, invest, exchange, and deepen specialization [5]. The Stanford Encyclopedia likewise describes Smith's preferred policy as rule of law plus selective restrictions, such as some banking rules, rather than a wholly hands-off order [3]. Freedom in this framework is structured by legal security and bounded by justice.

Book V assigns the sovereign responsibilities for defense, justice, and public works or institutions whose social benefits cannot reliably repay a private investor [1]. Smith discusses roads, bridges, canals, harbors, and education, often asking whether fees can connect payment to use and whether local or competitive administration can restrain negligence [1][6]. His four maxims of taxation concern proportional contribution, certainty, convenience, and economy in collection [1]. These proposals are not a modern welfare-state blueprint, but they refute the idea that Smith's state merely guards property and otherwise withdraws.

### Mercantilism Is Both an Error and a Capture System

Book IV attacks mercantilism at two levels. Analytically, bullion is a means of exchange rather than the substance of national wealth, and a trade balance does not by itself measure whether people are better supplied [1]. Politically, tariffs, bounties, navigation rules, and exclusive trading rights redirect labor and capital toward protected interests while raising costs for consumers [1][4]. Smith's case for freer trade is thus joined to a theory of rent-seeking: bad doctrine persists because concentrated beneficiaries can organize more effectively than dispersed losers.

The invisible-hand passage occurs within this conditional argument about the direction of investment, not as a general theorem that every private choice maximizes social welfare [1][7]. Persky's textual analysis emphasizes historically specific assumptions behind the passage and the later mutation of the metaphor into mythology [7]. The University of Glasgow also stresses that competitive benefits arise only under conditions that private interests may undermine [4]. The author's assessment is that Smith's more general rule is not "trust self-interest" but "test whether institutions align private advantage with public output while preserving justice and competition."

### Empire Exposes the Union of Monopoly and Sovereignty

Smith's treatment of colonies and the East India Company extends the capture argument beyond domestic tariffs. He attacks colonial monopolies for imposing military and administrative costs, distorting trade, and allowing merchants to exercise political power [1][3][4]. His criticism of a trading company acting as sovereign shows why corporate governance is not merely an internal business matter: when commercial agents wield public authority, the incentives of trader and ruler become dangerously confused [1][3].

The anti-imperial conclusion needs qualification. Williams argues that Smith often opposed colonial rule because of its costs and corruption for the metropolitan country, while retaining a theory of historical improvement that could describe colonial contact as progressive [10]. Smith's proposals also included institutional alternatives rather than a single modern doctrine of self-determination [1][10]. The book therefore supplies strong tools against monopoly empire without fully escaping the assumptions of eighteenth-century European political economy.

## Evidence

### The Five-Book Structure Tests a System, Not a Slogan

The first body of evidence is the text's architecture. Evensky's scholarly guide uses a sequential reading method: Book I defines terms and economic processes; Book II develops system dynamics; Book III presents a historical case; Book IV evaluates rival systems; and Book V derives policy implications [9]. That method finds that ethics and jurisprudence remain connected to the economic analysis, rather than appearing as later exceptions to an otherwise pure market doctrine [9]. The primary text supports the finding. Specialization and exchange in Book I lead to capital in Book II, historical institutions in Book III, mercantile capture in Book IV, and justice, public works, taxation, and debt in Book V [1]. A reading that cites only the butcher, the pin factory, or the invisible hand fails this whole-work test.

The University of Glasgow reaches a compatible result through intellectual history. Its account places the book in Smith's work as a moral philosopher and in the commercial society of eighteenth-century Glasgow, then compares familiar interpretations with the book's treatment of institutions and vested interests [4]. It finds that Smith redefined wealth around general wellbeing, saw trade and specialization as productive, and nevertheless accepted public action under specified conditions [4]. The convergence between structural reading and historical contextualization supports a qualified interpretation: markets are central, but their performance depends on legal, moral, and political arrangements [4][9].

### Institutional Security Explains Why Specialization Does Not Spread Automatically

Irwin examines a specific causal question left open by a superficial reading of the division of labor: if specialization raises productivity, why do some countries fail to realize it [5]? His method combines close reading of Smith's discussions of justice, property, and development with modern empirical research on institutions and comparative income [5]. The finding is that Smith treated a tolerable administration of justice as a condition for accumulation and exchange. Where people cannot secure the fruits of labor, they have weaker reasons to invest, expose capital, or enter long chains of specialization [5].

This evidence changes the interpretation of the market-size thesis. A large population or territory is not enough; the usable market depends on security, transport, enforcement, and confidence that agreements will be honored [1][5]. The modern institutional literature cited by Irwin supports the direction of Smith's argument, although it does not validate every eighteenth-century historical example or establish that property rights alone cause development [5]. The careful conclusion is conditional: Smith anticipated a durable research program in which institutions mediate the path from exchange opportunities to productivity.

### The Invisible Hand Is a Contextual Passage, Not the Book's Master Concept

Persky studies the reception and textual context of the invisible-hand metaphor rather than treating later textbook usage as evidence of Smith's intent [7]. He finds that the image came to carry the essential message of Anglo-American political economy, but that the generalized story rests on assumptions that are narrower and more historically specific than its mythology suggests [7]. The primary text confirms that the passage appears in Book IV's analysis of domestic investment and import restraints, surrounded by discussion of risk, profit, monopoly, and political opposition [1]. It does not replace the rest of the book's analysis of market failure, public duties, or capture.

This does not make unintended order irrelevant. Smith repeatedly explains how exchange, price adjustment, capital allocation, and competition can coordinate action without central direction [1]. The evidence instead limits the claim: beneficial unintended outcomes occur under conditions, and the same self-interest can produce collusion, monopoly, lobbying, or imperial abuse when institutions reward those strategies [1][4][7]. The metaphor is most accurate as one mechanism inside Smith's political economy, not as a universal law.

### Moral Sentiments and Commercial Conduct Form One Research Program

Sen's method is comparative philosophical interpretation. He reads Smith's economics alongside The Theory of Moral Sentiments and asks which motivations are sufficient in which contexts [8]. His finding is that self-interest can facilitate mutually advantageous exchange without being a complete theory of human conduct, justice, or institutional stability [8]. The Stanford Encyclopedia reaches a parallel conclusion by reconstructing Smith's political philosophy from moral philosophy, jurisprudence lectures, and The Wealth of Nations [3].

The primary texts support this integration. The Theory of Moral Sentiments develops sympathy, propriety, duty, virtue, and impartial judgment, while The Wealth of Nations examines exchange among people who cannot rely on personal benevolence [1][2]. These claims are complementary if they answer different questions: how moral judgment develops, and how large societies coordinate specialized production. The evidence contradicts the claim that Smith replaced morality with greed in 1776. It also cautions against the reverse claim that moral sympathy alone can organize an impersonal economy [1][2][3][8].

### Education Shows That Productive Institutions Can Damage Human Capabilities

Smith's discussion of education is evidence against a one-dimensional productivity doctrine. He praises the output gains from specialization but later warns that monotonous work can diminish intellectual and civic capacities [1][6]. Otteson reviews the text and the scholarly consensus on public education, then argues that Smith considered public provision while retaining stronger reservations than many interpreters acknowledge [6]. The finding is contested at the policy level but stable at the diagnostic level: commercial organization can create harms that do not appear in output statistics and that require an institutional response [1][6].

This case also demonstrates Smith's method. He does not infer policy from a single principle. He compares benefits, incentives, administrative failure, payment mechanisms, and effects on character [1][6]. That procedure is more durable than any exact eighteenth-century school-finance recommendation. It asks what a market arrangement produces, what it erodes, who can act, and how the remedy may itself fail.

### Colonial Criticism Is Powerful but Historically Limited

The colonial chapters provide a final test because they place commerce, war, monopoly, and government in one case [1]. Smith criticizes exclusive trade and corporate sovereignty, and the University of Glasgow emphasizes his attack on interests driving colonial and slave trades [4]. Williams tests the stronger proposition that Smith offers a fully anti-colonial liberal theory by analyzing the reasons Smith gives and the historical framework he employs [10]. His finding is more limited: much of Smith's opposition turns on metropolitan costs and bad government, while his developmental assumptions leave an ambiguous legacy [10].

The evidence therefore resists both celebration and dismissal. Smith identified how monopoly privilege can turn commerce into coercive rule and how distant costs are hidden from domestic beneficiaries [1][3]. He did not provide a complete account of colonized peoples' political agency or a framework adequate to modern postcolonial judgment [10]. The book's institutional criticism remains useful, but its moral and historical limits must be made explicit rather than repaired by selective quotation.

## Implications

For economists and policymakers, the book's first implication is to define the objective before choosing the instrument. Smith asks whether policy increases real production and the wellbeing of the population, not whether it raises bullion reserves, protects a named industry, or enlarges state revenue [1][4]. Modern equivalents of the mercantile mistake arise whenever a visible proxy - exports, investment announcements, domestic content, market capitalization, or tax receipts - is treated as the final social objective. The Smithian question is what the policy does to productivity, consumer access, wages, competition, security, and the allocation of labor and capital. This is an interpretation of his method, not a claim that he supplied modern cost-benefit analysis.

A second implication is that market size and state capacity are complements. Exchange supports specialization, but usable exchange depends on roads, ports, courts, predictable taxes, personal security, and rules that keep privileged actors from closing entry [1][5]. Policy debates framed as "market versus government" therefore miss Smith's architecture. A capable state can enlarge decentralized choice by enforcing justice and supplying infrastructure, while an intrusive or captured state can narrow choice by granting monopoly and directing capital toward political clients [1][3][5]. The relevant distinction is not public versus private in the abstract, but whether an institution widens mutually beneficial cooperation while controlling coercion and capture.

For managers, the division of labor remains a powerful but incomplete operating model. Specialization can raise dexterity, reduce switching costs, support tools, and make output scalable [1]. Yet Smith's warning about mental narrowing means that a firm should also examine learning, task variety, worker voice, and the portability of skill [1][6]. A process can be efficient in units per hour while degrading the capabilities on which adaptation and citizenship depend. The author's assessment is that modern job design should combine specialization where repetition genuinely improves performance with rotation, training, feedback, and autonomy where narrowness creates fragility or human cost.

For investors, Smith offers an institutional version of competitive analysis. Durable prosperity requires capital accumulation, but returns created by productivity differ from returns created by privilege [1]. A protected tariff, exclusive license, occupational barrier, or political concession may produce high profits while reducing national output and inviting reversal. Smith's suspicion of merchants' policy advice suggests separating evidence about a firm's operating advantage from evidence about its access to state protection [1][4]. The first may indicate a capability; the second may indicate rent extraction, political dependency, or both. This is a practical bridge to corporate governance: agents who control capital or delegated authority must be judged by the incentives attached to that control, not by the labels "private" or "public."

For labor analysis, the book rejects the fiction that every contract is made between equally situated parties. Smith's account of masters' ability to coordinate and wait, compared with workers' immediate need for income, is an early bargaining-power analysis [1]. Competition can raise wages when employers bid for scarce labor, but legal restrictions, concentrated ownership, and unequal reserves shape the starting position [1]. The implication is not a complete modern labor policy. It is a diagnostic rule: before treating a wage as the neutral result of supply and demand, examine exit options, concentration, association rights, information, and the time each side can afford to wait.

For trade policy, Smith's enduring contribution is less the command "remove every tariff" than the demand to identify whose interest a restriction serves [1][4]. Wider markets can deepen specialization and lower cost, while abrupt adjustment can impose concentrated losses that Smith's framework did not fully model. His own exceptions, historical examples, and attention to institutions argue against mechanical application [1][9]. A Smithian review should compare consumer costs, producer gains, security claims, retaliation risks, transition paths, and the political durability of protection. It should also presume that organized beneficiaries will describe private advantage as public necessity, because that is the recurrent mechanism Book IV exposes [1][4].

For public finance, the four tax maxims remain a disciplined starting checklist: contribution should relate to capacity or protected interest, liabilities should be certain, payment should be convenient, and collection should not impose needless cost [1]. Modern taxation requires concepts Smith lacked, including formal incidence analysis, optimal-tax theory, broad administrative data, and explicit treatment of externalities. His framework is still useful because it treats tax design as an institutional problem of fairness, predictability, compliance, and administrative burden rather than merely a revenue target. The same reasoning applies to user charges for infrastructure: a fee can connect use to maintenance, but distribution, monopoly access, and spillovers determine whether that arrangement is just or efficient [1].

For readers of political argument, the invisible-hand episode teaches source discipline. A memorable phrase can become more influential than the surrounding work and then be cited as if later doctrine were the author's own [4][7]. The corrective is simple: locate the phrase, identify the problem under discussion, list the conditions the argument assumes, and compare it with the author's treatment of failure cases. Applied to Smith, that procedure preserves the insight that decentralized action can generate order while preventing the unsupported conclusion that all private incentives automatically serve the public [1][7].

The moral-philosophical context also changes how commercial success should be evaluated. Exchange among strangers often works through reciprocal advantage rather than benevolence, but justice, trust, restraint, and public judgment remain prerequisites for a stable commercial society [2][3][8]. A society can become richer while allowing work to deform capacities, privilege to capture law, or distant coercion to subsidize consumption [1][6][10]. Smith's own analysis does not resolve every conflict, but it makes such conflicts visible. The author's synthesis is that material abundance is a means of human improvement, not a substitute for moral and political judgment.

The colonial chapters add a final warning about scale and distance. Commercial institutions can connect strangers productively, but they can also conceal coercion when beneficiaries are separated from governed populations and when a company combines profit-seeking with sovereign power [1][3][10]. Modern applications extend beyond formal empire to contractors, platforms, utilities, and financial institutions exercising quasi-public authority. The analogy is interpretive and should not erase historical differences. Its value is the governance question Smith makes unavoidable: who bears the cost, who can contest the decision, and what happens when an agent paid for commercial success also controls the rules?

The book's durable lesson is therefore conditional rather than ideological. Prosperity compounds when specialization, exchange, capital, competition, justice, and public infrastructure reinforce one another; it decays when monopoly, capture, insecurity, and unaccountable power break those links [1][4][5][9]. What should change a reader's mind is evidence that one of those mechanisms does not operate in the case at hand. Smith's framework invites inquiry into conditions and incentives, not ritual invocation of markets or the state.
