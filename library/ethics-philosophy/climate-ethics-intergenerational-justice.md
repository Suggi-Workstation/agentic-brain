# Climate Justice Requires Duties Proportional to Harm, Capacity, Benefit, and Vulnerability

Climate ethics asks how the burdens and benefits of climate action should be shared when contributions, exposure, wealth, power, and decision-making authority are distributed unequally across countries, communities, species, and generations. Its central claim is that no single rule -- not equal shares, polluter pays, ability to pay, rights, aggregate welfare, or precaution -- can by itself assign every duty of mitigation, adaptation, remedy, and transition fairly. A defensible allocation combines backward-looking responsibility with forward-looking capacity, protects basic rights and the vulnerable, and gives affected people and future interests meaningful representation [1][5][6][7].

## Background

Climate change became an explicit problem of international justice when scientific findings about a shared atmosphere met political disagreement about development, responsibility, and risk. The 1992 United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change states that parties should protect the climate system for present and future generations on the basis of equity and common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities. Its principles also direct special attention to vulnerable developing countries, precaution where serious or irreversible damage is threatened, and sustainable development [6]. Those clauses do not supply a complete theory of justice, but they identify the enduring variables: contribution, capability, vulnerability, uncertainty, development, and time.

The ethical problem is structurally harder than an ordinary dispute between identifiable contemporaries. Greenhouse gases mix globally, emissions accumulate across long periods, and some consequences persist beyond the political horizon of the people who authorize them. Stephen Gardiner describes this convergence of global, intergenerational, and theoretical problems as a "perfect moral storm." The structure makes delay attractive because present actors can retain benefits while dispersing costs among distant people, future people, and nonhuman nature; it also creates incentives to describe self-serving delay as reasonable disagreement [3]. This diagnosis is ethical rather than climatic: climate science estimates causes, pathways, and risks, while ethics asks whose claims should govern action under those conditions.

The Paris Agreement preserved the Convention's equity principle while changing its institutional form. Its preamble refers to human rights, vulnerable groups, the right to development, gender equality, intergenerational equity, ecosystem integrity, climate justice, and a just transition of the workforce. Article 2 states that implementation should reflect equity and common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in light of different national circumstances [7]. These references matter because they reject a purely aggregate objective, but most are broad commitments rather than formulas that determine a country's exact carbon budget, financial contribution, or remedial liability.

The physical and social evidence makes distribution unavoidable. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change concludes that emissions are distributed unevenly both in the present and cumulatively since 1850, while communities that have historically contributed least are often disproportionately vulnerable. It also finds that vulnerability is shaped by income, marginalization, governance, and historical patterns of inequality, not only by exposure to a climatic hazard [8]. Climate justice therefore concerns both the distribution of environmental change and the social institutions that convert a similar hazard into sharply different losses.

Climate justice joins several questions that must remain analytically separate. Mitigation concerns reducing or removing emissions to limit future warming. Adaptation concerns reducing exposure and vulnerability to impacts that occur despite mitigation. Loss and damage concerns economic and non-economic harms that are not averted or adequately adapted to. A just transition concerns the distribution and governance of the social and economic changes caused by decarbonization. These tasks overlap, but a fair mitigation rule does not automatically determine who should receive adaptation finance, how irreversible cultural loss should be treated, or what workers and communities are owed during industrial change [1][7][9].

Intergenerational justice adds a vertical distribution problem across time to the horizontal problem among people alive now. Present decisions influence the climate, infrastructure, institutions, and resource base inherited by people who cannot vote in current elections or negotiate current contracts. Theories of intergenerational justice disagree over whether the duty is to leave everyone above a threshold, preserve equality, give priority to the worse off, maintain capabilities, protect rights, or improve welfare over time [1][2]. They also disagree about whether duties must be person-affecting -- owed to a determinate individual who is made worse off -- or can rest on impersonal reasons about the quality and justice of future lives [2].

The non-identity problem explains why a narrow comparison can fail. A long-term policy can alter migration, work, relationships, and reproduction, so different future people may exist under different emissions pathways. A future person whose life is worth living may not be worse off than that same person would have been under a cleaner policy, because that person might not otherwise have existed. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's intergenerational-justice analysis explains that rights-based and threshold accounts can respond with non-comparative standards: present action can be wrong because it leaves future people below conditions owed to any person, not because it makes a particular future person worse off than an impossible personal baseline [2].

Discounting introduces another layer of moral judgment. Economic analysis commonly discounts future consumption when future people are expected to be wealthier, but the justification depends on empirical expectations about growth and on the chosen principle of intergenerational justice. A prioritarian view, a sufficientarian threshold, and an aggregate-welfare view need not treat the same future gain or loss alike; uncertainty about growth and catastrophic or irreversible harm further weakens any claim that one discount rate is ethically neutral [1]. Discounting can organize comparisons, but it cannot by itself decide how much less a future life, right, ecosystem, or irreversible loss should count.

Nonhuman nature also tests the boundaries of justice. Ecosystems and species can be valued instrumentally for human welfare, relationally as constituents of cultures and ways of life, or intrinsically as entities not reducible to human use. UNESCO's ethical declaration links human solidarity to interdependence with other organisms, ecosystems, and the environment, while the Paris Agreement notes ecosystem integrity and biodiversity [5][7]. These texts do not settle the moral status of species, but they make clear that a complete climate ethic cannot treat every ecological loss as interchangeable with monetary compensation.

The field consequently moved from asking whether climate action is justified to asking which action, by whom, for whom, under what constraints, and through what process. Simon Caney's analysis of the duties of the advantaged shows why familiar principles must be revised and combined: polluter pays captures an important responsibility but leaves gaps, while ability to pay can assign duties where causal responsibility is unavailable or insufficient [4]. The mature question is not which slogan wins. It is which combination of principles assigns each kind of burden without sacrificing subsistence, concealing inherited advantage, rewarding avoidable pollution, or excluding those who bear the consequences.

## Core Concepts

### Four Distinct Objects of Justice

A climate decision distributes at least four different objects. First are permissible emissions and the benefits produced through their use. Second are the costs of mitigation, including investment, foregone activities, and institutional change. Third are the costs and benefits of adaptation, remedy, and compensation. Fourth is political authority: who defines the problem, selects evidence, chooses trade-offs, and reviews outcomes. Treating these as one burden creates category errors. A country can receive a limited development-oriented emissions allowance while still owing finance because of wealth, and a community can deserve adaptation priority because of vulnerability without being granted authority to impose all mitigation costs on others [1][6][7].

This distinction also separates allocation from evaluation. Climate science can estimate the consequences of cumulative emissions and compare pathways; economics can estimate opportunity costs and behavioral responses; law can specify obligations adopted by states. None of those disciplines alone determines whether an allocation is fair. Ethical judgment enters when decision-makers choose the relevant currency -- welfare, rights, capabilities, resources, risks, or opportunities -- and decide whether equality, priority, sufficiency, responsibility, or another relation should govern it [1][8].

### Polluter Pays and Historical Responsibility

The polluter-pays principle assigns burdens in proportion to an actor's contribution to harmful emissions. Its strongest moral basis is corrective: agents that cause an avoidable harm should bear the costs of prevention and remedy rather than externalize them to victims. Applied prospectively, it also changes incentives by making a current emitter account for social costs. Applied historically, it supports larger duties for states, firms, or populations whose cumulative emissions contributed more to present warming [1][4][10].

A rigorous version must answer at least four questions. It must identify the relevant polluter, choose a time period, distinguish permissible from excessive emissions, and specify whether liability requires knowledge, control, negligence, or causal contribution alone. National totals, territorial emissions, consumption-based emissions, corporate production, and individual consumption allocate responsibility differently. The historical record also includes emissions made before climate risks were widely understood and emissions tied to basic subsistence rather than luxury. Caney therefore argues that polluter pays is important but incomplete, especially when causal contribution cannot cover every cost or when applying it without a floor would push poor people below an acceptable standard of living [4].

Collective continuity complicates but does not erase historical responsibility. Present citizens are not personally identical to past emitters, yet states, firms, and institutions can persist, hold assets, enforce claims, and transmit advantages and disadvantages. A backward-looking principle can therefore concern inherited institutional benefits and continuing relationships rather than inherited personal guilt. The author's synthesis is that historical emissions should affect present duties where a continuing institution or asset base carries forward benefits, capacity, or authority, but the burden should not be described as personal blame for acts outside an individual's control [1][2][4].

### Beneficiary Pays

The beneficiary-pays principle assigns some burden to those who benefit from emission-intensive development or from unjust arrangements, even when they did not cause the original emissions culpably. Its appeal is strongest where infrastructure, wealth, technology, or institutional power created through past emissions remains concentrated among present beneficiaries. It can address the gap left when original polluters are dead or dissolved and can explain why inherited capacity is morally relevant rather than merely fortunate [1][4].

The principle has limits. A benefit may be involuntary, diffuse, difficult to measure, or accompanied by harms. Descendants can inherit both carbon-intensive assets and polluted environments. Benefits from a generally unjust process are not automatically equivalent to possession of stolen property, and forcing a person to surrender every unchosen benefit can become excessively demanding. Beneficiary pays is therefore better used as one input to a hybrid allocation than as a complete liability rule. It strengthens duties where present advantage is traceable to a harmful system, but it needs a protected minimum and an account of which benefits are morally relevant [1][4].

### Ability to Pay and Capability

Ability to pay is forward-looking. It assigns greater burdens to actors that can contribute resources without sacrificing basic needs or comparable morally urgent claims. This principle can finance action where polluter pays leaves a remainder: natural causes, orphaned historical emissions, insolvent polluters, uncertain attribution, or urgent needs that cannot wait for final liability. Caney's modified approach gives ability to pay a supplementary role and treats inherited high-emission wealth as a reason for even greater responsibility [4]. The UNFCCC's reference to respective capabilities and the Paris Agreement's reference to national circumstances institutionalize part of this logic [6][7].

Capacity includes more than current income. Fiscal space, technology, administrative competence, access to capital, institutional stability, and the ability to absorb transition costs affect what an actor can do. At the same time, a capacity rule must distinguish resources needed to secure a decent minimum from discretionary affluence. If every available resource is treated as taxable capacity, the rule can obstruct poverty reduction; if wealth is measured too narrowly, highly capable actors can hide behind formal equality. The author's synthesis is to measure capacity after protecting basic rights and development thresholds, then increase duties progressively as discretionary resources and institutional leverage rise [4][6][12].

### Rights, Sufficiency, and Priority

A rights-based approach begins with protected interests rather than aggregate net benefit. The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights identifies threats to life, health, food, water, housing, and self-determination, and states that climate justice requires equality, participation, equitable burden sharing, accountability, and protection of vulnerable people [12]. Rights can set side constraints: a policy that maximizes total benefit may still be unacceptable if it foreseeably destroys subsistence, coerces displacement without safeguards, or excludes affected people from remedy.

Sufficientarianism asks whether everyone has enough of the relevant goods or capabilities. In climate ethics, a sufficientarian floor can protect subsistence emissions, essential energy, food security, health, housing, and adaptive capacity before less urgent consumption. Intergenerational sufficientarianism asks present institutions to avoid leaving future people below an adequate threshold [1][2]. The attraction is moral clarity at the bottom: luxury gains should not take priority over basic survival. The difficulty is specifying the threshold and deciding whether justice has anything more to say once everyone is above it.

Prioritarianism gives extra moral weight to benefits for people who are worse off without requiring strict equality. It can justify directing adaptation resources toward highly vulnerable groups even when the same expenditure would produce a larger measured asset benefit in a wealthy area. It can also guide mitigation when climate risks threaten to push already disadvantaged people farther below secure conditions. Unlike a simple vulnerability rule, priority must still ask how much benefit an intervention creates and whether another group faces a more severe or irreversible loss [1][2][8].

Rights, sufficiency, and priority are related but not interchangeable. Rights identify claims that should not be traded away casually; sufficiency identifies an absolute floor; priority supplies a weighting across improvements. A policy can keep aggregate welfare high while violating a right, raise everyone above a minimum while preserving severe inequality, or favor the worse off while failing to remedy wrongful causation. A plural climate ethic uses these principles to constrain and rank allocations rather than pretending that one scalar objective captures them all [1][2][12].

### Equal Shares, Carbon Budgets, and Development

Because cumulative carbon dioxide emissions largely determine warming, a finite temperature objective implies a limited carbon budget. Equality can be applied to that budget through equal per-capita shares, equal long-run shares, contraction and convergence, or another rule. Equal per-capita allocation has an intuitive basis in equal moral worth, but it does not by itself account for historical use, different subsistence needs, vulnerability, prior deprivation, or the capacity to decarbonize [1][8]. Grandfathering current emissions has the opposite defect: it can convert a history of high use into a continuing entitlement.

The UNFCCC's common-but-differentiated formula expresses a hybrid rather than equality alone. Present and future generations share an interest in climate protection, all parties have responsibilities, and developed parties should lead because contribution and capability differ [6]. The Paris Agreement retains that principle while allowing nationally determined contributions [7]. Normatively, differentiation is justified only when the variables and trade-offs are public. A country cannot establish fairness merely by calling its own contribution ambitious; its claim should be assessed against cumulative use, current capacity, development needs, vulnerability, and the remaining budget [1][6][7].

### Precaution Under Risk and Uncertainty

The precautionary approach addresses threats that are serious or irreversible when knowledge is incomplete. Both the UNFCCC and UNESCO state that lack of full scientific certainty should not justify postponing cost-effective preventive measures under such threats [5][6]. Precaution is not a command to eliminate every risk regardless of cost. A defensible version specifies the protected values, the severity and reversibility of harm, the evidence threshold, the feasible remedies, and the risks created by intervention itself [1][5].

Uncertainty can strengthen or weaken different actions. Uncertain regional impacts may support flexible adaptation pathways, while uncertainty around irreversible thresholds can support earlier mitigation. It does not follow that the most pessimistic scenario must control every decision. The author's synthesis is a proportional rule: increase verification, safety margin, reversibility, and protective action as possible harm becomes more severe and less reversible, while comparing the harms of action, delay, and foregone alternatives [1][5][8].

### Intergenerational Duties, Non-Identity, and Discounting

Future people lack present political power but not morally relevant interests. A threshold approach can require current actors to preserve basic capabilities and avoid severe climate danger; an egalitarian approach can object to transferring a depleted environmental inheritance; a prioritarian approach can give extra weight to future people expected to face worse conditions; and a rights account can prohibit exposing them to avoidable threats to basic interests [1][2]. These principles do not require certainty about the identities of future individuals.

The non-identity problem cautions against grounding every duty in making a particular person worse off than that same person would otherwise have been. Intergenerational accounts can instead use non-comparative harm, rights, capabilities, or impersonal reasons. The morally relevant comparison becomes the condition created for whoever exists, not an impossible comparison between one individual's life under mutually exclusive population histories [2]. This response preserves responsibility while requiring explicit defense of the threshold or value used.

Discounting should separate empirical growth assumptions from pure temporal preference and from risk. If future people are richer, a marginal unit of consumption may benefit them less; if climate damage, unequal growth, or institutional fragility makes them poorer, the direction can reverse. Rights violations, irreversible species loss, and severe threshold risks may also resist conversion into discounted consumption equivalents [1]. The author's synthesis is to disclose discount assumptions, test several defensible rates and growth paths, and report physical and rights-based thresholds separately from present-value totals.

### Mitigation, Adaptation, Loss and Damage, and Just Transition

Mitigation duties prevent additional harm and preserve the remaining budget. Adaptation duties reduce vulnerability to harm that mitigation does not avoid. Loss-and-damage duties address residual economic and non-economic losses. These functions imply different allocation principles. Historical responsibility and current emissions are especially relevant to mitigation and remedy; vulnerability and effectiveness are especially relevant to adaptation recipients; capacity and inherited benefit can help allocate finance across all three [1][7][8][12].

Just transition concerns the means as well as the endpoint of decarbonization. The International Labour Organization's guidelines were developed through a tripartite process involving governments, employers, and workers, and emphasize social dialogue, labor rights, employment planning, skills, and social protection [9]. This framework prevents a false choice between climate protection and worker protection. It also prevents the costs of changing an emission-intensive system from being assigned automatically to workers and places with the least control over investment decisions.

Procedural and recognition justice cut across every category. Fair treatment requires meaningful participation, access to information, reasons that affected people can evaluate, avenues for challenge, and attention to Indigenous peoples, local communities, migrants, people with disabilities, women, children, and others in vulnerable situations [5][7][12]. Procedure cannot replace a fair distribution, but it can expose missing interests, contested values, and hidden incidence. Recognition also matters because losses of culture, place, identity, and ecological relations may not be adequately represented as market-value damages [1][5].

### A Hybrid Standard

The author's synthesis is a constrained hybrid standard. First, protect basic rights and a sufficiency floor for present and future people. Second, assign prevention and remedy in proportion to avoidable contribution where attribution and agency are adequate. Third, add duties for inherited benefit and ability to pay where historical responsibility is incomplete or urgent needs remain. Fourth, prioritize protection for the most vulnerable and the worst off. Fifth, require precaution in proportion to severity and irreversibility. Sixth, make the allocation and its assumptions contestable through inclusive procedure [1][2][4][5][6][12].

This standard does not eliminate moral disagreement. It makes disagreement auditable by showing which principle assigns which burden and where principles conflict. A high-emitting poor community may receive a subsistence protection under sufficiency while a wealthy institution financing its infrastructure bears a larger transition cost under capacity and benefit. A wealthy low-current-emitting state may still owe finance because of inherited advantage, while a rapidly growing emitter may owe stronger prospective mitigation because current choices determine future harm. Hybrid reasoning is strongest when it names such distinctions rather than averaging them into an unexplained equity score.

## Evidence

### The IPCC Synthesis Connects Unequal Contribution to Unequal Vulnerability

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report is an assessment rather than a single experiment. It integrates peer-reviewed physical, ecological, economic, and social research, including formal detection-and-attribution studies that compare observations with climate-model simulations. The report concludes that human activities caused the observed global warming, that present and cumulative emissions are unevenly distributed, and that communities contributing least have often been disproportionately affected [8]. Its method matters ethically because it separates empirical attribution and confidence from the later normative question of how those facts should alter duties.

The report also documents a distributional pattern. Between 2010 and 2020, mortality from floods, droughts, and storms was 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions than in regions with very low vulnerability. It attributes vulnerability differences partly to poverty, marginalization, governance, and historical and ongoing inequality. This finding does not prove one burden-sharing principle, because exposure and adaptive capacity have multiple causes. It does show that allocating resources only by the market value of exposed assets would ignore large differences in human vulnerability [8].

The carbon-budget evidence creates an intergenerational constraint. The IPCC estimates historical cumulative net carbon dioxide emissions from 1850 through 2019 at about 2,400 +/- 240 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide, about 83 percent of the central total budget associated with a 50 percent probability of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees C. It also states that every additional tonne of carbon dioxide adds to warming [8]. The ethical implication is not a scientifically determined allocation, but a scarcity fact: greater use now leaves less room for later development and raises future risk.

### National Warming Contributions Can Be Estimated, but Metric Choices Matter

Jones and co-authors constructed an open national dataset of contributions to global mean surface temperature from historical carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide emissions during 1851-2021. Their method applies the IPCC best estimate of the transient climate response to cumulative carbon dioxide emissions, uses 100-year global-warming-potential values for nitrous oxide, and uses a method better suited to methane's shorter lifetime. They validated the combined dataset against the IPCC estimate: their modeled warming between 1851-1900 and 2010-2019 was 1.39 degrees C, compared with the IPCC estimate of 1.4 degrees C [10].

The study shows why responsibility is measurable but not mechanically settled. Country contributions change when land-use carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide are added to fossil carbon dioxide. Including methane and nitrous oxide raised estimated warming contributions for India, China, and Brazil substantially more, in percentage terms, than for several other large emitters; land-use shares also differed sharply among countries [10]. These are scientific measurement results, not moral verdicts. They demonstrate that a historical-responsibility rule must disclose gases, sectors, dates, uncertainty, and accounting boundaries before claiming precision.

The authors also quantify uncertainty in the emissions inputs, including larger uncertainties for land-use carbon dioxide and for methane and nitrous oxide than for fossil carbon dioxide in well-reported inventories [10]. This limits any claim that a liability share can be read directly from one emissions table. It does not support ignoring history. It supports using ranges, multiple defensible metrics, and a supplementary principle such as capacity when uncertain attribution leaves urgent costs unassigned [4][10].

### Counterfactual Growth Analysis Finds a Contribution-Impact Asymmetry

Diffenbaugh and Burke estimated the economic effect of historical anthropogenic warming from 1961 through 2010. They compared Historical and Natural simulations from 21 CMIP5 climate models, created country-level temperature paths without anthropogenic forcing, applied an empirically estimated nonlinear relationship between temperature and economic growth, and measured inequality in observed and counterfactual income distributions [11]. This is a model-based counterfactual, not a direct observation of an alternate world, so its results depend on the estimated growth response and climate models.

Their findings nevertheless identify a marked asymmetry. All 18 countries with cumulative emissions below 10 tonnes of carbon dioxide per capita in the study period had negative median estimated economic impacts, with a median effect of -27 percent relative to the counterfactual. Among 36 countries with 10-100 tonnes per capita, 34 had negative estimated impacts, with a median of -24 percent. Among 19 countries above 300 tonnes per capita, 14 had positive estimated impacts, with a median benefit of 13 percent among those 14 [11]. The study does not prove that every high emitter benefited or owes the same duty. It supplies evidence against assuming that contributors and victims are distributed symmetrically.

This case supports integration rather than a single principle. Polluter pays responds to the contribution side, prioritarian and rights-based principles respond to the welfare and basic-interest side, and ability to pay responds to the resource side. Because model uncertainty remains material, the findings are better used to test whether an allocation recognizes the observed direction of inequality than to calculate exact compensation from one growth estimate [1][4][11].

### International Agreements Encode a Hybrid but Leave It Underdetermined

The UNFCCC and Paris Agreement are institutional cases showing how ethical principles become negotiated rules. The Convention combines equity, differentiated responsibility, capability, vulnerability, precaution, and sustainable development. The Paris Agreement combines universal participation and nationally determined action with repeated references to those principles, human rights, intergenerational equity, and just transition [6][7]. The result is not one philosophical theory but a political architecture that preserves several considerations states were unwilling to collapse into a single formula.

This hybrid enabled broad participation, but ethical underdetermination remains. "Highest possible ambition," "respective capabilities," and "different national circumstances" require interpretation, and nationally determined contributions do not validate themselves merely through submission [7]. The case therefore distinguishes positive law from normative justification. A treaty can create real procedural and substantive duties while leaving open whether the total effort, country shares, finance, or treatment of residual loss is just [1][7].

Loss and damage makes the gap visible. Paris recognizes the importance of averting, minimizing, and addressing losses associated with extreme events and slow-onset change, while its adoption decision states that Article 8 does not provide a basis for liability or compensation [7]. That legal compromise does not settle the philosophical questions of remedy, wrongful harm, solidarity, or historical benefit. It shows that legal categories can limit claims for negotiated reasons even when the underlying ethical arguments continue.

### Just Transition Guidelines Make Procedure Operational

The ILO's just-transition guidelines provide a final institutional case. The Office reviewed country policies and sector strategies, and a tripartite meeting of experts representing governments, employers, and workers amended and adopted non-binding guidance in 2015. The resulting framework centers social dialogue, rights at work, employment policy, skills, social protection, and coherent industrial and macroeconomic policy [9]. Its method is deliberative and comparative rather than experimental.

The finding is practical: transition burdens are not limited to aggregate investment costs. They include job displacement, lost local revenue, obsolete skills, bargaining power, workplace safety, and unequal access to new opportunities. By requiring affected workers and social partners to participate, the framework converts procedural justice into institutions rather than treating consultation as a symbolic supplement [9]. It does not determine the global carbon budget, but it supplies a tested governance structure for distributing one class of transition burdens.

Together, these sources support a bounded conclusion. Scientific assessment establishes human causation, cumulative scarcity, unequal contribution, and unequal vulnerability; empirical studies estimate historical attribution and distributive effects with disclosed uncertainty; treaties and guidelines show how plural principles are operationalized and where political compromise leaves moral questions unresolved [6][7][8][9][10][11]. The evidence does not identify one universally correct allocation. It rejects allocations that ignore contribution, vulnerability, capacity, time, or participation without justification.

## Implications

### Allocate Duties by Function, Not by One Universal Percentage

A defensible process begins by identifying the duty being allocated. For mitigation, the relevant variables include current and cumulative emissions, feasible alternatives, capacity, and a protected development floor. For adaptation, they include vulnerability, expected effectiveness, local authority, and the capacity to implement and maintain measures. For loss and damage, they include causation, historical responsibility, beneficiary relations, severity, irreversibility, and unmet needs. For just transition, they include control over the decision, dependence on the affected activity, exposure to job and community losses, and access to replacement opportunities [1][4][8][9].

Using one percentage across all functions hides value judgments. A country's fair mitigation share need not equal its fair share of adaptation finance, and a community's priority as an adaptation recipient does not imply greater causal responsibility. The author's synthesis is to publish separate allocation tables for emissions, finance, residual loss, and transition support, then show how polluter pays, benefit, capacity, rights, and vulnerability affect each table. This makes double counting and omitted duties visible.

### Protect a Floor Before Distributing Discretionary Sacrifice

No allocation should push people below basic conditions needed for life, health, food, water, housing, energy access, or political agency. This is the operational role of rights and sufficiency [1][2][12]. A subsistence floor does not grant an unlimited right to carbon-intensive development; it requires that decarbonization pathways provide affordable alternatives and that wealthier actors help finance them. The relevant comparison is not high emissions or poverty, but which distribution of technology, finance, and remaining emissions secures development while respecting the climate constraint.

Above that floor, duties should rise with discretionary consumption, capacity, and avoidable contribution. This avoids treating one unit of luxury consumption and one unit of subsistence as morally equivalent. It also avoids the inverse error of exempting every activity in a lower-income country regardless of who benefits. Distribution within states matters: a national average can conceal wealthy high emitters and vulnerable low emitters in the same jurisdiction [1][4][8].

### Treat Carbon Budgets as Ethical Constraints on Development Choices

A remaining carbon budget is a physical constraint associated with a chosen probability and temperature goal, not a scientifically pre-assigned set of national entitlements [8]. Allocation should therefore state the temperature objective, probability, gases, negative-emissions assumptions, period, and treatment of historical use. It should then test equal per-capita shares, responsibility-and-capacity approaches, development thresholds, and other defensible rules rather than presenting one method as self-evident [1][10].

Grandfathering deserves special scrutiny because it can reward incumbency: a high current emitter receives a larger future share because it already emits more. Political feasibility may justify temporary transition arrangements, but feasibility should be labeled as such rather than redescribed as justice. Conversely, immediate equal per-capita allocation can impose abrupt costs on systems built around inherited infrastructure. The author's synthesis is a declining transition path constrained by a global budget, with faster change and more finance from actors combining high historical use, high current discretionary emissions, and high capability [1][4][6].

### Separate Urgent Finance From Final Liability

People facing immediate adaptation needs or irreversible loss should not have to wait until every causal and legal dispute is resolved. Ability to pay, solidarity, and vulnerability can justify interim finance, while later processes refine responsibility and contribution [4][5][12]. This is not an acquittal of polluters. It prevents uncertainty over liability from becoming a mechanism for transferring all delay risk to victims.

Financial design should also distinguish grants, concessional loans, insurance, compensation, and investment. Requiring already indebted vulnerable communities to borrow for harms they contributed little to can reproduce the underlying injustice. At the same time, no single financial label guarantees fairness; access rules, conditionality, local participation, non-economic loss, and accountability determine who actually benefits. These conclusions are the author's synthesis from the rights, solidarity, vulnerability, and burden-sharing principles in the sources [1][5][8][12].

### Make Just Transition Broader Than Worker Compensation

A just transition should anticipate effects before closures and investments occur. The ILO framework supports social dialogue, rights at work, skills policy, employment planning, and social protection [9]. A complete plan also maps municipal revenue, small suppliers, housing, pensions, health coverage, land restoration, and who owns new low-carbon assets. A one-time severance payment cannot substitute for a viable regional transition if an entire community depends on an industry.

Participation must carry decision rights, information, and review opportunities. Workers and communities should be able to test job projections, training plans, environmental remediation, and the distribution of public subsidies. The author's synthesis is that support should be tied to measurable transition obligations: wage and benefit protection where appropriate, funded retraining linked to plausible jobs, remediation, transparent subsidy conditions, and scheduled review. The ethical purpose is not to preserve every incumbent activity; it is to prevent decarbonization costs from falling by default on people with the least control [7][9].

### Use Precaution Without Hiding Trade-Offs

Precaution requires action before complete certainty when potential harm is serious or irreversible, but it also requires comparison of response risks [5][6]. A mitigation policy can protect future people while harming present subsistence if badly designed; an adaptation project can reduce one hazard while displacing another community; a large removal program can create land and governance risks. The correct response is not paralysis. It is to identify the feared harm, evidence level, affected groups, alternatives, reversibility, monitoring, and exit rule.

For uncertain but potentially irreversible outcomes, decision-makers should favor measures that preserve options and reduce lock-in. Near-term emissions reduction lowers cumulative risk, while modular and adaptive investments can be revised as evidence changes [8]. This principle does not make reversibility an absolute value: delaying protective infrastructure can itself lock in harm. The author's synthesis is to choose the reversible option among similarly protective alternatives and to require a stronger justification for actions that transfer irreversible risk to nonconsenting or unrepresented people.

### Audit Discounting Rather Than Burying It in a Model

Every long-run appraisal should report how much of its result comes from the discount rate, assumed growth, damage function, inequality weighting, and terminal horizon. Growth discounting can be reasonable if future beneficiaries are reliably expected to be richer, but that premise should be tested across regions and within future generations [1]. Diffenbaugh and Burke's findings warn against assuming uniform enrichment: historical warming was associated with negative modeled economic effects in many low-emitting countries [11].

Physical thresholds and rights claims should be reported alongside monetized present values. A discounted monetary total can be useful for comparing resource paths, but it should not silently convert deaths, displacement, cultural destruction, species loss, or irreversible ecosystem change into ordinary consumption losses. When results reverse under plausible discount assumptions, the disagreement is substantive evidence about the fragility of the recommendation, not a technical detail to hide [1][2].

### Represent Future People Through Institutions, Not Imagined Consent

Future people cannot authorize present policies, so claims about what they would consent to are inherently hypothetical. Institutions can nevertheless represent future interests through carbon budgets, long-term risk assessments, independent guardians, legislative impact statements, constitutional or statutory duties, judicial review, and mandatory periodic reassessment. These mechanisms do not discover one future preference; they create friction against short-term decisions that impose severe, unreviewed, or irreversible burdens [2][3].

Representation should also preserve plurality within future generations. Future people will not form one homogeneous constituency, and policies can reduce average risk while worsening inequality among them. The author's synthesis is to require both a vertical test -- what conditions are transferred through time -- and a horizontal test -- how those conditions are likely to be distributed among future people. A policy that leaves adequate average wealth but predictable groups below basic thresholds fails the second test [1][2].

### Keep Science, Law, and Ethics in Their Proper Roles

Climate science determines neither guilt nor a fair national share. It constrains ethical reasoning by estimating causal contribution, risk, uncertainty, and feasible pathways [8][10]. Positive law determines neither the full moral duty nor the justice of the negotiated compromise. It creates authoritative obligations and institutions, but provisions can reflect bargaining limits, including deliberate limits on liability [6][7]. Ethics cannot replace either discipline: it must use the scientific record accurately and distinguish aspirational judgment from enforceable law.

A sound public claim therefore states its type. "Cumulative emissions caused warming" is an empirical attribution claim. "Those contributors should pay" is a moral claim requiring a responsibility principle. "Parties must submit nationally determined contributions" is a legal claim. "Those contributions are fair" is an evaluative claim requiring an allocation standard. Keeping these statements separate prevents scientific authority from being borrowed to conceal a moral premise and prevents treaty language from being treated as proof that justice has been achieved [1][6][7][8].

### Apply a Transparent Climate-Justice Test

The author's synthesis is an eight-question test for policy. What climate harm or transition is addressed? Which people, communities, ecosystems, and generations bear the benefits and risks? Who contributed causally, who benefited, and who has capacity? Which rights and sufficiency floors constrain the options? How are vulnerability and priority measured? What uncertainty and irreversibility justify precaution? Who participates, challenges evidence, and receives remedy? What future evidence or threshold will trigger revision [1][2][4][5][8][9][12]?

A policy passes only if its answers are mutually coherent. High aggregate benefit does not cure an avoidable rights violation; participation does not cure an indefensible allocation; historical responsibility does not justify pushing poor current people below subsistence; capacity does not erase causal responsibility; and uncertainty does not excuse delay where serious irreversible harm is credibly threatened. Climate justice is therefore not a decorative distributional adjustment to climate policy. It is the framework that determines whose interests count, which burdens are legitimate, and whether action across time and borders can be publicly justified.
