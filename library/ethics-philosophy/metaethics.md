# Metaethics Determines What Moral Claims Mean Before Normative Theories Can Guide Action

Metaethics investigates what moral judgments mean, whether any are true, what could make them true, how they could be known, and why they appear to guide action. It does not decide whether deontology, consequentialism, or virtue ethics gives the best first-order rules; it examines the semantic, metaphysical, epistemological, and psychological commitments that make any such rules intelligible and authoritative [1]. Its central lesson is that familiar oppositions such as realism versus anti-realism do not name two complete doctrines: a defensible position must answer several connected but separable questions [2][3][6].

## Background

Metaethical questions arise whenever moral practice is examined from one level above its ordinary use. A first-order judgment says that an act is wrong, a person is virtuous, or a distribution is unjust. A metaethical inquiry asks what the word "wrong" contributes, whether the judgment expresses a belief that can be true or false, whether its truth depends on any person's attitudes, how a speaker could know it, and what connection it bears to motivation or reasons for action. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy defines the field through this combination of metaphysical, epistemological, semantic, and psychological questions [1]. This second-order orientation separates metaethics from normative ethics without making it practically irrelevant: conclusions about the status of moral claims affect how disagreement, justification, reform, and uncertainty should be understood.

The field's enduring problems predate its modern name. The Euthyphro problem asks whether an act is right because an authority approves it or whether the authority approves it because it is right. The first answer threatens to make morality dependent on will; the second leaves the moral standard independent and still in need of explanation [1]. Hume's moral philosophy raised two further challenges. He argued that moral distinctions are not derived from reason alone and emphasized that moral judgment has a connection to passion and action that an inert description does not obviously possess [11]. The famous is-ought problem, as later metaethics developed it, asks what licenses a normative conclusion when the premises state only how the world is. These questions established a recurring constraint: an account of morality must explain both its apparent objectivity and its practical force [1][11].

G. E. Moore's 1903 Principia Ethica helped establish metaethics as a distinct branch by attacking reductions of moral concepts to natural concepts [4]. Moore's open-question argument begins from the observation that a competent speaker can understand a proposed natural description - for example, that something is pleasant, desired, or conducive to flourishing - while still intelligibly asking whether it is good. Moore took this openness to show that "good" is not analytically identical in meaning to any such natural description and defended a form of non-naturalist realism [4][5]. Later criticism separated the semantic result from the metaphysical conclusion: even if two expressions are not synonymous, the properties they identify might still be identical, just as scientific identities need not be knowable from meaning alone [4][10]. The argument nevertheless forced later naturalists to explain moral meaning rather than merely assert a reduction.

Logical positivism and related empiricist programs encouraged a different response. Early emotivists and prescriptivists treated moral utterances primarily as expressions of attitudes or prescriptions rather than reports of special moral facts [6]. Modern non-cognitivism is more sophisticated than the slogan that moral speech merely vents emotion. Expressivist theories attempt to explain planning, endorsement, disagreement, inference, and the apparently truth-like surface of moral language in terms of practical attitudes [6]. Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivist work are prominent attempts to earn realist-seeming features without beginning from stance-independent moral properties [6]. Their central burden is to explain why moral sentences participate in negation, conditionals, and valid inference if their primary function differs from ordinary description.

J. L. Mackie's error theory accepted a cognitivist reading of moral discourse while denying its success. On this view, ordinary moral judgments purport to represent objectively prescriptive properties, so they are truth-apt, but no such properties exist; ordinary positive moral claims are therefore systematically false [3][8]. Mackie's argument from queerness pressed both an ontological and an epistemological concern: intrinsically action-guiding properties would be unlike the familiar properties recognized in a naturalistic worldview, and knowledge of them would appear to require an equally unusual faculty [3][10]. Error theory differs sharply from non-cognitivism. The error theorist agrees that moral sentences try to state facts and says that they fail, whereas the non-cognitivist gives moral language a different primary function [6][8].

The late twentieth-century debate broadened rather than converged. Moral naturalists defended realist views on which moral facts are stance-independent natural facts, sometimes through reduction and sometimes through non-reductive identity or constitution [4]. Non-naturalists retained realism while treating moral properties as irreducibly normative rather than natural or supernatural [5]. Constructivists argued that the authority or truth of at least some norms is constituted through an appropriate procedure of practical reasoning, agency, agreement, or reflective endorsement rather than discovered as a wholly independent object [7]. Each family contains important internal variations, and their classifications can overlap or become disputed when theorists use different standards for realism, mind-independence, truth, or objectivity [3][7][10].

Contemporary metaethics therefore cannot be represented by one line running from realism to anti-realism. The 2020 PhilPapers Survey illustrates both structured disagreement and the persistence of multiple live options. Across 1,719 respondents familiar enough to answer the main metaethics question, 62.07 percent accepted or leaned toward moral realism and 26.12 percent toward moral anti-realism, with the remainder selecting other responses. On a more detailed question answered by 1,024 sufficiently familiar respondents, 31.64 percent accepted or leaned toward naturalist realism, 26.56 percent toward non-naturalism, 20.80 percent toward constructivism, 10.64 percent toward expressivism, and 5.27 percent toward error theory; overlapping response rules mean these figures should be read as reported survey selections rather than an exclusive taxonomy [14]. The result is evidence of disciplinary distribution, not evidence that the majority view is true.

## Core Concepts

### Four questions organize the field

A clear map begins by separating four questions. The semantic question asks what moral sentences and judgments do: do they express beliefs, attitudes, prescriptions, plans, or some combination? The metaphysical question asks what, if anything, makes moral claims true and whether moral properties exist independently of evaluative stances. The epistemological question asks how moral belief could be justified or known, especially amid disagreement and causal influences on judgment. The psychological and practical question asks how moral judgment connects to motivation and reasons for action [1][8][12]. A complete theory need not answer each question with the same mechanism, but its answers must cohere.

These questions are logically distinct. Cognitivism says that moral judgments express belief-like states and are apt for truth or falsity; it does not by itself say that any positive moral claim is true [6]. An error theorist is therefore a cognitivist anti-realist: moral judgments make representational claims, but those claims systematically fail [3][6]. A naturalist and a non-naturalist can both be realists while disagreeing about the nature of moral facts [4][5]. A constructivist can treat moral judgments as truth-apt while denying that their correctness is fixed by facts wholly independent of every eligible practical standpoint [7]. The author's synthesis is that debates become confused when an answer on one axis is treated as if it settled the others.

### Cognitivism, non-cognitivism, and the logic of moral language

Cognitivism holds that moral statements express beliefs and can be true or false [6]. This fits ordinary grammatical form: "deception is wrong" appears to predicate wrongness, can be denied, can figure in an explanation, and can serve as a premise. Cognitivism also makes moral disagreement look like disagreement about how things are. Its burden is to explain the distinctive practical character of the beliefs. If moral belief is psychologically like any other belief, why does a sincere moral judgment often appear connected to motivation in a way that belief about distance or color is not [8]?

Non-cognitivism treats the primary function of moral judgment as practical rather than representational. Emotivist accounts emphasize approval and disapproval; prescriptivist accounts emphasize universalizable prescriptions; expressivist accounts understand moral speech through attitudes, norms, or plans [6]. This makes the action-guiding role of moral language less mysterious because accepting a moral judgment already involves a practical orientation. It also avoids commitment to unusual moral properties. The difficulty is continuity with ordinary logic. A speaker can accept "if lying is wrong, getting another person to lie is wrong" without yet condemning any particular lie. When that conditional combines with "lying is wrong," the conclusion must preserve the same meaning of "wrong." This is the embedding or Frege-Geach problem, and sophisticated expressivist theories construct semantic accounts intended to preserve those inferential relations [6].

Quasi-realism is an expressivist project rather than a halfway admission that robust realism is true. It seeks to explain why agents may legitimately use moral truth, fact, knowledge, and objectivity vocabulary even when the underlying account begins with evaluative attitudes [6]. Its attraction is economical: it aims to retain the surface commitments of moral practice without adding stance-independent moral entities. Its risk is explanatory convergence. If the theory earns every realist-seeming feature, critics ask what substantive difference remains; if it cannot earn them, ordinary moral inference or disagreement may be lost. The label therefore marks a program whose success depends on detailed semantic and practical explanation, not a simple compromise.

### Realism, objectivity, and error theory

A minimal characterization of moral realism combines two claims: moral assertions purport to report facts in light of which they are true or false, and at least some such assertions are true [2]. Many robust realists add that the relevant facts are stance-independent, meaning that their obtaining does not depend on anyone's approval, acceptance, or idealized evaluative attitudes [2][13]. This addition captures the thought that a society can be morally mistaken even when its norms are stable and sincerely endorsed. Realism alone does not identify which moral claims are true or select deontology, consequentialism, or virtue ethics [2].

Anti-realism is not one contradictory proposition. In a useful broad classification, non-cognitivists deny that moral claims have the relevant fact-reporting function, error theorists accept that function but deny that any positive moral claims succeed, and non-objectivists or some constructivists accept moral truth while making it depend in a specified way on attitudes, practices, or procedures [3]. Moral relativism is also not automatically identical with anti-realism: a relativist can hold that moral claims state truths relative to a culture or standard, while an anti-realist may reject relativism for other reasons [3]. Moral skepticism concerns knowledge or justification and can be combined with more than one metaphysical view.

Error theory has a two-stage structure. The conceptual stage argues that ordinary moral discourse commits speakers to objective or categorically authoritative values. The ontological stage argues that the world contains no properties satisfying that commitment [3][8]. An opponent can resist either stage. Expressivists deny that ordinary discourse carries the representational commitment the error theorist attributes to it. Naturalist realists argue that moral facts need not be ontologically queer because they are natural facts. Non-naturalists accept irreducible normativity but deny that its distinctiveness is a reason for elimination [4][5][10]. The theory's strength is that it takes the apparent objectivity of ordinary moral language seriously; its practical challenge is to explain what agents should do with moral discourse after concluding that its literal presupposition fails.

### Naturalist and non-naturalist realism

Moral naturalism is the realist view that stance-independent moral facts or properties are natural facts or properties [4]. Analytic naturalists seek an analysis of moral terms in natural terms. Synthetic or non-analytic naturalists deny that the identity must be available through conceptual analysis; they compare a moral-natural identity to scientific identities discovered through substantive inquiry [4]. Cornell realism is a prominent non-reductive approach that uses causal and explanatory roles to identify moral properties while allowing moral vocabulary to resist a simple definition [4]. Naturalism promises continuity with scientific knowledge and an accessible account of moral epistemology.

Naturalism faces two related objections. Moore's open-question argument challenges analytic reduction because a speaker can understand the proposed natural description while still asking whether it is morally good [4][5]. The normativity objection asks how a natural property, described without evaluative vocabulary, could by itself supply a reason, requirement, or standard rather than merely another fact [4][10]. A naturalist can answer that identities need not be analytic, that functional or causal roles fix reference, or that reason-giving relations themselves belong to the natural world. Each answer incurs further semantic and metaphysical commitments that must be defended.

Moral non-naturalism is a family of realist views according to which moral properties are not reducible to natural properties; many versions treat them as irreducibly normative [5]. It preserves the apparent autonomy of moral reasons and avoids defining away their action-guiding character. Some versions pair this metaphysics with a priori justification, intuition, or rational insight, although non-naturalism as a metaphysical thesis does not by itself dictate one epistemology [5]. Its burdens are integration and access: how do non-natural moral facts relate systematically to natural facts, and how can natural agents reliably know them [1][5]?

Supervenience sharpens this issue. Moral thought ordinarily assumes that there cannot be a moral difference without some relevant non-moral difference: two cases identical in every natural respect cannot differ morally without explanation [4][5][10]. Naturalists can explain this by identity or constitution. Non-naturalists must explain why irreducible moral properties necessarily track natural configurations. Expressivists try to explain the constraint through consistency in practical attitudes or plans [6]. Supervenience does not decide the debate, but it supplies a common test: every theory must account for stable dependence without simply restating it.

### Constructivism and the source of authority

Constructivism explains moral or normative correctness through an appropriate procedure or standpoint of practical reasoning rather than through recognition of values wholly prior to reasoning [7]. Kantian versions connect standards to the constitutive requirements of rational agency or the autonomy of reason. Contractualist and society-based versions appeal to principles that suitably situated agents could agree to or could not reasonably reject. Humean versions begin from an agent's evaluative attitudes and ask what survives informed, coherent reflection [7]. These differences matter because a procedure based on rational agency aims at a different kind of universality from a procedure based on contingent desires.

Constructivism attempts to explain practical authority from within agency. A norm binds not because an external moral object exerts force, but because rejecting the norm would conflict with the conditions of reasoning, agency, mutual justification, or coherent endorsement specified by the theory [7]. The Euthyphro question reappears: is a norm correct because the procedure selects it, or does the procedure select it because independent reasons already make it correct? If the first, the procedure may seem arbitrary unless its authority is independently justified. If the second, constructivism may presuppose rather than construct normativity [7].

Constructivism's position in the realism debate is correspondingly unsettled. Some constructivists deny stance-independent moral facts and count as anti-realists. Others defend objectivity, truth, and reasons robust enough to accept a modest realist label while rejecting metaphysical guarantees beyond sound normative reasoning [7]. The classification should follow the theory's dependencies rather than its preferred label: which facts fix correctness, what idealizations are used, why those idealizations have authority, and whether an entirely different evaluative standpoint could be mistaken by a standard independent of it.

### Moral knowledge, disagreement, and genealogical influence

Moral epistemology asks how moral beliefs are justified, whether they amount to knowledge, and what persistent disagreement implies [12]. Disagreement by itself does not prove that there is no fact of the matter. People also disagree about empirical questions. Moral disagreement becomes a special challenge when informed and reflective agents appear to agree on the non-moral facts yet continue to diverge, or when judgments vary with culture, upbringing, interest, and emotion [2][12]. Realists may explain divergence through factual error, bias, unequal concepts, different evidence, or the difficulty of moral inquiry. Anti-realists may explain it as conflict among practical attitudes or standards [2][3]. The evidential question is comparative: which account explains the pattern without assuming its conclusion?

Moral knowledge also depends on the metaphysical theory. Naturalists can appeal to empirical knowledge, causal explanation, inference, and reflective moral theory, although they must show why the relevant natural facts are moral [4]. Non-naturalists may appeal to a priori reasoning or intuition, but must explain reliability and disagreement [5][12]. Constructivists can treat knowledge as competent application of the appropriate reasoning procedure, but must justify that procedure [7]. Error theorists conclude that positive moral knowledge fails because its purported objects do not exist [3]. Expressivists reconstruct knowledge claims within a practical discourse rather than treating them as detection of independent properties [6].

Genealogical challenges ask whether the causes of moral belief support or undermine its justification. Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma targets realist theories of value by arguing that evolutionary forces substantially influenced basic evaluative tendencies [13]. If those forces bear no relation to independent evaluative truths, many judgments appear accidentally aligned and therefore unreliable. If natural selection tracked independent evaluative truth, the realist owes a scientifically competitive explanation of why truth-tracking, rather than adaptively useful response, produced the relevant tendencies [13]. Street argues that an adaptive-link explanation is more parsimonious: evaluative tendencies connected circumstances to reproductively advantageous responses without needing independent values to enter the causal story [13]. The argument is contested and conditional on its empirical and philosophical premises, but it establishes a general requirement that a metaethical theory explain why the causal history of belief is or is not epistemically defeating.

### Motivation, reasons, and two kinds of internalism

The relation between judgment and motivation must be distinguished from the relation between reasons and desire. Motivational judgment internalism claims, in one form, that sincere moral judgment is necessarily or conceptually connected to some motivation to act accordingly; externalism denies that such a connection is necessary, even if it is common in psychologically normal agents [8]. Humean theories of motivation generally distinguish belief from desire and hold that belief alone does not motivate [8][11]. This supports non-cognitivist accounts if moral judgments motivate by their nature, but the inference is not compulsory: hybrid states, rational requirements, and conditional forms of internalism provide alternatives [8].

Reasons internalism addresses a different issue. In an influential formulation, an agent has a reason only if some sound deliberative route connects the action to the agent's motivational set; reasons externalism allows reasons that obtain independently of such a route [9]. Internalism explains how reasons can motivate but risks making moral reasons hostage to vicious, impoverished, or indifferent desires. Externalism protects the thought that an agent can have reason not to be cruel even if nothing in the agent's present motivations supports restraint, but it must explain how that reason can engage the agent [9]. A metaethical theory should therefore state separately whether moral judgments motivate, whether moral facts supply reasons, and whether reasons depend on actual or idealized motivation.

## Evidence

Metaethics is primarily a philosophical field, so much of its evidence consists of conceptual distinctions, formal or semantic tests, explanatory comparisons, counterexamples, and genealogical arguments rather than experiments that directly detect moral facts [1][10]. That does not make every position equally supported. It means that the method and conclusion of each case must be stated accurately: a semantic argument tests meaning, an explanatory argument compares accounts, a survey reports professional judgment, and none by itself proves a complete metaphysics.

### Moore's open-question case tests semantic reduction

Moore's method was a conceptual test. For a proposed naturalistic analysis of a moral term, ask whether a competent speaker who understands both sides can still intelligibly wonder whether the natural property is morally good [4][5]. If "good" meant exactly "pleasant," then "pleasure is pleasant, but is it good?" should be closed in the way that "a bachelor is unmarried, but is he unmarried?" is closed. The apparent openness supported Moore's finding that moral concepts cannot be analytically reduced to the proposed natural concepts [4][5].

The finding is narrower than Moore's metaphysical conclusion. Critics note that co-reference or property identity does not require synonymy: a discovered identity can be informative even when competent speakers understand each term separately [4][10]. The case therefore supplies continuing evidence against simple definitional naturalism, but it does not by itself establish irreducible non-natural properties. Its importance is methodological: it forces a theorist to distinguish claims about concepts, meanings, properties, and methods of discovery.

### Mackie's queerness case tests ontological and epistemic cost

Mackie's error-theoretic case begins with a conceptual interpretation of ordinary moral discourse as committed to objective prescriptions, then asks whether the world contains anything matching that commitment [3][8][10]. The method is an inference from alleged explanatory and ontological cost: intrinsically prescriptive properties would be different from familiar natural properties, and reliable access to them would require explanation. Mackie's finding was not that moral language lacks sense, but that it systematically presupposes values that do not exist [3][8].

Each step remains contestable. Expressivists challenge the conceptual premise by denying that moral discourse primarily reports such properties [6]. Naturalists deny the ontological premise by identifying moral facts with natural facts [4]. Non-naturalists deny that irreducible normativity is objectionably queer or that every legitimate fact must fit a narrowly causal model [5][10]. The case nevertheless identifies a real burden: a realist must explain both what moral facts are and why belief-forming practices are appropriately connected to them.

### The embedding problem tests expressivist logic

The Frege-Geach or embedding problem uses valid moral arguments as a semantic stress test [6]. Consider: if lying is wrong, inducing another person to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; therefore inducing another person to lie is wrong. The occurrence of "wrong" in the conditional cannot simply express the speaker's disapproval of lying, because a speaker may accept the conditional without accepting its antecedent. Yet its meaning must remain stable enough for modus ponens to be valid [6].

The method asks an expressivist theory to construct compositional meanings and attitude relations that preserve negation, conditionals, consistency, and inference. Modern expressivists have developed sophisticated answers rather than ignoring the problem [6]. The evidential result is therefore not a settled refutation. It is a performance criterion: an account that explains isolated moral exclamations but cannot recover embedded reasoning is incomplete, while an account that recovers every inferential feature must still show that its practical-state explanation remains substantively different from cognitivism.

### Supervenience tests cross-level dependence

Supervenience supplies a common case across theories. The method compares two possible situations that are identical in all relevant non-moral respects and asks whether they could differ morally without any underlying difference [4][5][6][10]. Ordinary moral reasoning strongly resists an unexplained moral difference. Naturalism explains the constraint through identity, constitution, or grounding in natural facts [4]. Non-naturalism must explain necessary covariance between distinct domains [5]. Expressivism attempts to derive the constraint from rational consistency in plans or evaluative attitudes [6].

The finding is comparative rather than decisive. Every major family can propose an explanation, but those explanations expose their costs. Identity offers direct dependence but faces the open-question and normativity challenges. Irreducible properties preserve distinctiveness but require a principle connecting levels. Attitude-based explanations avoid a second ontology but must recover the necessity and objectivity attributed to the pattern. Supervenience therefore narrows the acceptable theory space even though it does not select one winner.

### Street's Darwinian dilemma tests genealogical reliability

Street's 2006 paper presents a structured explanatory dilemma for stance-independent value realism [13]. Its first premise is that natural selection exerted extensive indirect influence on basic evaluative tendencies, which then shaped reflective judgments. Street explicitly treats the evolutionary picture as conditional and distinguishes population-level selection from the causes of a particular person's judgment [13]. Her method compares two realist explanations of the relation between evolutionary influence and independent evaluative truth.

On the no-relation horn, selection is a distorting influence, so widespread true judgment would require an unexplained coincidence. On the relation horn, the realist proposes truth-tracking, but Street compares this with an adaptive-link account: tendencies such as protecting offspring and reciprocating help promoted reproduction by linking circumstances to advantageous responses, whether or not they tracked stance-independent value [13]. She judges the adaptive account more parsimonious, clearer, and more explanatory. She then argues that constructivist or other attitude-dependent anti-realism can reverse the direction of explanation: causal processes shape starting attitudes, and standards emerge through their disciplined reflection rather than causing their evolutionary selection [13].

The finding is not that evolutionary influence logically entails anti-realism. The argument depends on the extent of that influence, the adequacy of tracking responses, and whether realist epistemology has resources not captured by the dilemma. Its evidential value lies in forcing any theory to specify when a causal genealogy defeats a belief. An explanation of why a belief arose is not automatically a refutation, but it becomes epistemically threatening when the causal process appears insensitive to the property the belief purports to track [12][13].

### The PhilPapers survey measures the debate, not moral truth

Bourget and Chalmers's 2020 PhilPapers Survey asked professional philosophers and related respondents standardized questions about one hundred philosophical issues; the published results report sample sizes, response categories, correlations, and subgroup analyses [14]. For the main metaethical question, 62.07 percent of 1,719 sufficiently familiar respondents accepted or leaned toward realism, 26.12 percent toward anti-realism, and 12.68 percent selected other responses. For moral judgment, 69.25 percent of 1,636 accepted or leaned toward cognitivism and 20.72 percent toward non-cognitivism. For moral motivation, 41.01 percent of 1,429 accepted or leaned toward internalism and 39.33 percent toward externalism, with a large other category [14].

The method provides a snapshot of expert commitments, not an adjudication by vote. The more detailed morality question dispersed support across non-naturalism, naturalist realism, constructivism, expressivism, error theory, and other answers [14]. This pattern supports two bounded findings. First, realism and cognitivism are majority positions in the surveyed population. Second, no detailed metaethical package commands a consensus comparable to a settled scientific result. The survey therefore supports treating the field as a live explanatory competition and warns against presenting one taxonomy or doctrine as uncontested.

## Implications

### Moral disagreement should be diagnosed before it is debated

Metaethics changes the interpretation of disagreement. Two speakers who say "this act is wrong" may disagree about non-moral facts, first-order principles, the meaning of moral predicates, the dependence of truth on a standpoint, or the authority of reasons. Treating every conflict as a dispute over one stance-independent fact assumes realism before defending it; treating every conflict as a clash of attitudes assumes an anti-realist semantics [2][3][6]. A disciplined analysis should first identify the level of disagreement and then ask what evidence could matter at that level.

This does not make ordinary disagreement futile. Realists can seek better factual evidence, expose inconsistency, refine principles, and explain why bias or limited concepts produced error [2][12]. Constructivists can test whether judgments survive the relevant idealized procedure or can be justified to the affected agents [7]. Expressivists can examine coherence among commitments and the practical consequences of plans [6]. Error theorists can distinguish literal falsity from the possible instrumental or reforming use of moral language [3]. The author's synthesis is that productive disagreement requires a declared metaethical burden of proof rather than an unspoken one.

### Moral uncertainty requires uncertainty about frameworks as well as theories

The existing library topic on moral uncertainty examines what to do when first-order ethical theories conflict. Metaethics adds a deeper layer: an agent may also be uncertain whether moral verdicts report stance-independent facts, express plans, result from a constructivist procedure, or systematically fail. That uncertainty affects what counts as evidence, how credence should be interpreted, and whether numerical comparison across theories is legitimate [1][7]. A calculation that assigns choiceworthiness scores assumes more semantic and metaphysical structure than an expressivist, error theorist, or some constructivists may accept.

The practical response is not paralysis. The author's synthesis is to seek recommendations robust across metaethical interpretations. Avoiding coercion, exposing factual assumptions, preserving appeal, testing reversibility, and documenting reasons can be supported by realists as responses to moral facts, by constructivists as conditions of justifiable procedure, and by expressivists as coherent commitments. Agreement reached through different foundations is not proof of one foundation, but it can support action while higher-order inquiry continues. When recommendations diverge, the decision record should identify which metaethical assumption is pivotal rather than hiding it inside a moral score.

### Empirical moral psychology cannot settle normativity by itself

Evolutionary biology, neuroscience, anthropology, and psychology can explain patterns in moral judgment, motivation, emotion, and cultural variation. Humean theories, naturalism, and genealogical arguments make those findings especially relevant [8][11][13]. Yet a causal explanation of why people judge an act wrong does not by itself show that the judgment is false, just as explaining vision does not show that visible objects are unreal. A debunking inference needs an additional claim that the causal process is insensitive to the truth or reliability condition relevant to the judgment [12][13].

The reverse inference also fails. Cross-cultural convergence or a neural signature does not by itself prove stance-independent moral truth. Convergence may reflect shared biology, common problems, social learning, or truth-tracking; a metaethical argument is needed to select among those explanations [12][13]. The author's synthesis is a three-ledger rule. Record the empirical finding, the causal explanation, and the normative or metaethical inference separately. This prevents scientific description from silently becoming moral authority and prevents philosophy from ignoring evidence about how judgment is formed.

### Law and public policy must expose their normative bridge premises

Policy arguments usually combine empirical predictions with normative premises. Evidence may estimate how a rule changes welfare, risk, liberty, or distribution, but it cannot by itself establish which changes justify coercion. The is-ought problem requires a bridge: a principle about rights, welfare, fairness, legitimacy, or reasons [1][11]. Public reasoning improves when that bridge is explicit. Disputants can then distinguish disagreement about predicted consequences from disagreement about moral authority.

Metaethical pluralism also supports procedural safeguards. A public institution cannot assume that every citizen accepts one metaphysics of value. Constructivist approaches emphasize justifiability through reasoning or agreement, while realist approaches can regard fair procedure as required by independent moral facts [2][7]. Expressivists can defend institutions that make commitments consistent and contestable [6]. The convergence does not eliminate substantive conflict, but it explains why transparency, participation, reason-giving, review, and correction are more than administrative preferences: they allow decisions to remain answerable across divergent accounts of moral authority.

### Artificial intelligence must not present encoded values as discovered facts

An AI system that ranks outcomes, filters content, allocates resources, or recommends actions embeds evaluative choices in objectives, labels, thresholds, and override rules. Calling the output "ethical" does not answer whether the system represents moral facts, aggregates attitudes, follows a constructivist procedure, or imitates patterns in training data [1][6][7]. The worst failure is to convert a contested evaluative assumption into an opaque technical parameter and then report the result as if computation discovered its authority.

A defensible system should separate descriptive prediction from normative selection. It should state whose values supplied the objective, what idealization or aggregation procedure was used, which constraints are treated as non-negotiable, and how disagreement or appeal changes the result. A naturalist realist may believe that some objectives track natural moral facts; a constructivist may require an appropriate procedure of justification; an expressivist may emphasize plans and commitments; an error theorist may prefer explicitly non-moral decision language [3][4][6][7]. System documentation should make these alternatives visible rather than pretending that model accuracy resolves them.

Moral motivation creates an additional design warning. Producing a sentence classified as a moral judgment does not establish that a system possesses a human-like motive, reason, commitment, or capacity for responsibility [8][9]. The semantic output and the practical state must be evaluated separately. For institutions deploying AI, accountability therefore remains with agents who choose objectives, authorize use, and can respond to reasons. This conclusion is an institutional implication under present uncertainty, not a claim that metaethics has proved artificial moral agency impossible.

### Individuals and organizations need a metaethical audit trail

A practical audit can make hidden commitments testable. First, state the first-order claim and the decision it supports. Second, identify whether the claim is being treated as truth-apt. Third, specify what would make it true: a natural fact, a non-natural fact, a reasoning procedure, an idealized attitude, a social standard, or nothing because the sentence serves another function. Fourth, state how the claim is known or justified. Fifth, identify its connection to reasons and motivation. Sixth, test whether an opposing agent is alleged to be mistaken, differently situated, differently motivated, or using a different standard [1][3][6][7][8][9].

This audit does not require resolving the field before acting. It identifies where confidence should fall. A claim is weaker when its conclusion depends on a controversial bridge that has not been named, when its evidence is genealogically disconnected from what it purports to track, or when its account of authority changes across audiences without explanation. It is stronger when the semantics, ontology, epistemology, and motivational account are mutually consistent and when the recommendation survives plausible alternatives. The author's synthesis is that metaethics functions as quality control for moral reasoning: it cannot supply every first-order answer, but it can prevent an answer from borrowing truth, objectivity, knowledge, or authority without paying the explanatory cost.

### Intellectual humility is a result, not a surrender

The persistence of realism, naturalism, non-naturalism, constructivism, expressivism, and error theory among specialists shows that the central questions remain contested [14]. That fact does not entail relativism or equal credibility. It establishes a reason to distinguish a position's internal coherence, explanatory reach, empirical compatibility, and unresolved burdens. A realist must explain moral facts and access to them; a naturalist must explain normativity without reduction by stipulation; a non-naturalist must explain integration and knowledge; an expressivist must explain logic and objectivity; an error theorist must explain moral practice after error; a constructivist must explain the authority of its procedure [3][4][5][6][7][10].

The practical virtue is calibrated commitment. Agents can act on their best-supported moral judgments while recording what would change them and which metaethical assumptions remain uncertain. Institutions can seek decisions that are factually informed, procedurally defensible, revisable, and explicit about value conflict. The final implication is not that moral truth is unavailable. It is that any claim to moral truth, error, construction, or expression carries a specific explanatory burden, and responsible reasoning makes that burden visible.
