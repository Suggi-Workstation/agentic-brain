# Personal Identity and the Self -- Why What Matters in Survival Can Come Apart from Whether We Persist

Personal identity asks what makes a person at one time the same individual as a person at another time, and what follows when that sameness is uncertain, partial, or divided. The modern debate shows that several questions travel under this name: what it takes for a person to continue existing, what a person has reason to care about in survival, and which past actions, promises, and wishes bind a later person. Because competing accounts (psychological continuity, animal continuity, narrative, and reductionist views) answer these questions differently, they give different verdicts on dementia, brain stimulation, duplication, death, and digital replicas [1][2][5].

## Background

The phrase "personal identity" covers several separate questions, and much of the confusion in the field comes from running them together. Olson's survey distinguishes at least seven: the characterization question (which properties a person takes to define him or her), the personhood question (what a person is), the persistence question (what it takes to continue existing), the evidence question (how we find out who is who), the population question (how many people there are at a given time), the personal ontology question (what kind of thing we are), and the question of what matters in survival [1]. Olson also avoids the word "self", because it is sometimes a synonym for "person" but often names an unchanging immaterial subject of consciousness, and it is often used without any clear meaning [1]. This topic follows the same discipline. "Identity" here means numerical identity, a thing's sameness with itself over time, as opposed to qualitative similarity and as opposed to social or political identity such as national or ethnic affiliation [2].

The modern debate begins with John Locke, who added the chapter "Of Identity and Diversity" to the second edition of his Essay Concerning Human Understanding in 1694 [3]. Locke separated three ideas that ordinary speech runs together: the same substance, the same man (a human animal, whose identity consists "in nothing but a participation of the same continued life"), and the same person [3]. A person, for Locke, is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places", and personal identity reaches "as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought" [3]. His prince-and-cobbler case, in which the soul of a prince carrying the consciousness of the prince's life enters a cobbler's body, was meant to show that the resulting being would be the same person as the prince but the same man as the cobbler [3]. Locke called "person" "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit", which tied the metaphysics to reward, punishment, and accountability from the outset [3]. Shoemaker's remark that the history of the topic is "a series of footnotes to Locke", quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on animalism, expresses how much of the later debate responds to this one chapter [10].

Two eighteenth-century challenges set the agenda that followed. Hume looked inward for the self and reported that "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception"; persons are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions", and the identity we attribute to the mind is "only a fictitious one", produced by the imagination's easy transition along relations of resemblance and causation [4]. On memory, Hume added that it "does not so much produce as discover personal identity", because we extend the causal chain of our perceptions beyond what we remember [4]. Reid and Butler, in the line of objection described by Olson, attacked the memory criterion with two arguments. The transitivity objection (Reid's brave officer) observes that a general may remember the officer's deeds and the officer may remember the boy's, while the general does not remember the boy; identity is transitive but direct memory connection is not. The circularity objection (Butler) observes that genuine remembering already presupposes that the remembered experience was one's own, so memory cannot be used to define identity [1][2].

Twentieth-century work repaired the Lockean view rather than abandoning it. Shoemaker and Penelhum introduced quasi-memory, which keeps the causal link to a past experience but drops the requirement that the experience was one's own, and Shoemaker replaced memory with the broader notion of causal dependence among psychological states [1]. Parfit's 1971 paper pressed the case of a person whose brain is divided and argued that the question of identity can lack an answer without any loss of what is important [5]. Williams and others defended physical continuity as a rival, and in the 1990s animalists such as van Inwagen, Olson, and Snowdon argued that we are biological organisms whose persistence is a matter of animal life, with no psychological condition at all [1][10]. Olson reports that most philosophers writing since the early twentieth century have endorsed some version of a psychological-continuity view, while on the question of what kind of thing we are there is "no consensus or even a dominant view" [1]. Narrative accounts, which locate identity in the self-told story of a life, entered through Schechtman's The Constitution of Selves (1996), which presents the account as an answer to the characterization question [2][8].

The sequence matters for what follows. Each position was framed to solve a problem its predecessor exposed, and the practical stakes (survival, responsibility, and what the dead or the demented are owed) were part of the formulation from the start [3][5]. The topic below therefore treats the metaphysics and the ethics together, but keeps the questions apart wherever the sources do.

## Core Concepts

### Three questions that must be kept apart

The persistence question asks what is necessary and sufficient for a past or future being to be someone existing now. Olson lists three common ways of misreading it. It is not a question about the meaning of the word "person", answerable by reflecting on language, because what it takes for us to persist may depend on whether we are biological organisms, which cannot be known a priori. It is not the question whether someone remains "the same person", as when a woman who loses her memory or changes character is said to be a different person; that way of speaking presupposes that she still exists and concerns the characterization question. And it is not narrowly a question about persons, because whether you were ever an embryo or could exist in an irreversible vegetative state depends on how persistence works for beings that are not currently persons [1]. Olson separates the evidence question as well: fingerprints are good evidence of who left a mark, but having your fingerprints is neither necessary nor sufficient for being you [1]. Dufner adds the distinction between numerical and qualitative identity: identical twins may be qualitatively alike but are numerically two [2].

### Psychological continuity and connectedness

The psychological view holds that person X at one time is the same person as Y at another time if and only if X is uniquely psychologically continuous with Y. Continuity is a chain of strong psychological connections, which can include recollections, intentions, beliefs, desires, and perhaps character traits, where later states causally depend on earlier ones [2]. Parfit's definitions fix the vocabulary. Psychological connectedness requires direct relations, such as a quasi-memory of an experience, a later action carrying out an earlier intention, or a lasting characteristic expressed at both times; connectedness is not transitive and is a matter of degree. Psychological continuity requires only overlapping chains of such direct relations, so it is transitive [5]. Reid's brave officer shows why the distinction is needed: the general is connected to the officer and the officer to the boy, so the general is continuous with the boy even though no direct connection runs between them [2].

Causal dependence also answers an objection that defeats the simple memory criterion. There are long periods, such as dreamless sleep, when nothing can be remembered, yet most of the beliefs held on waking are the same ones held before sleep, and they have caused themselves to continue; so psychological continuity can span times when one is unconscious [1]. An open question is which kind of causal link counts. If all the mental contents of one brain were copied into another, the resulting being would have inherited the original's mental properties "in a funny way", and psychological-continuity theorists disagree on whether that is the right way (Shoemaker says yes, Unger says no) [1].

### Fission and the non-branching clause

The central test case is fission. Suppose the two hemispheres of your brain are transplanted, each into a different empty head. Each recipient is psychologically continuous with you, so a view on which any continuous being is you implies that you are both, which is impossible because the two are plainly two people [1]. Parfit, following Wiggins, finds only three possible descriptions: you do not survive, you survive as one of the two, or you survive as both. The first is hard to believe because one transplanted hemisphere would suffice for survival, so "how could a double success be a failure?"; the second cannot say what makes you one of two exactly similar people [5].

Two solutions are on offer. The multiple-occupancy view says that there were already two people occupying your place before the operation, who share their earlier stages and diverge afterward; it is usually combined with the metaphysics of temporal parts [1]. The non-branching view adds a clause: a being is you only if it is psychologically continuous with you and no other being is, so fission is death, even though preserving only one hemisphere would be survival [1][5]. Parfit's own treatment in 1971 was more radical. He proposed giving up the language of identity for such cases and saying that the original person survives as two without being either, because the relation that has all that matters in ordinary survival holds to both resulting people [5].

### Reductionism and the claim that identity is not what matters

Parfit identified two beliefs as targets: that in every describable case the question of identity must have an answer, and that important questions (survival, memory, responsibility) turn on that answer [5]. He argued that in cases such as fission the first belief is implausible and the second false: what matters is a relation of degree that holds between a person and later persons, and numerical identity, which is all-or-nothing and one-one, is not what matters [5]. Karpus and Strasser summarize Parfit's later position as reductionism, the view that there is no fact separate from the facts about brain and body that constitutes our continued existence, together with the claim that what matters is psychological connectedness and/or continuity with the right kind of cause, which Parfit called relation R [9].

Dufner lists four positions on what matters: numerical identity as a psychological being, psychological continuity, psychological connectedness, or both of the last two. If connectedness is given most weight, a single life becomes a succession of overlapping selves, special self-concern should be discounted with distance, and a future dementia patient bearing your name may warrant little more special concern than any other future person. Defenders of continuity (Dufner cites Merricks) reply that continuity better accounts for the value of personal development, because intentional character change reduces connections to the past without reducing the ground for self-concern [2]. Hume reached a structurally similar conclusion by another route: because the relations that produce identity "diminish by insensible degrees", there is no standard for deciding when they acquire or lose a title to the name of identity, and the disputes are "grammatical" rather than philosophical [4].

### Animal and bodily continuity

Animalism holds that we human people are animals. It does not say that each person has a body that is an animal, or that each person is constituted by an animal, and it leaves open both non-animal people (robots, angels) and human animals that are not people (fetuses, patients in a persistent vegetative state) [10]. Organic animalists say that an animal persists as long as its life processes continue, so death ends our existence; somatic animalists say that persistence requires only that the life-apt organization of parts remain sufficiently intact [10].

The standard argument is the thinking animal argument: a human animal sits in your chair, it is thinking, you are the thinking being in your chair, so the animal is you. Denying the third premise implies two thinkers where one was supposed [10]. This is the other side of the too-many-thinkers objection to psychological views: if a person goes with a transplanted brain but no organism does, then the organism left behind is a second thinker with the same thoughts, and one cannot know which of the two one is [1]. The costs of animalism are the transplant and transfer intuitions, on which you would stay behind with an empty head, and the remnant-person problem, which asks where a person comes from when a cerebrum is removed and sustained but not yet implanted [1][10]. Animalists answer that the transfer verdict conflicts with other pre-philosophical commitments, such as the belief that there is one thinker where you are [10].

Dufner stresses that animalism need not settle ethics: the relation that determines persistence and the relations that matter for self-concern, responsibility, or killing can come apart, so an animalist can hold that psychological relations are what matter even though biological continuity is what persists [2]. Karpus and Strasser report that in his later writing Parfit suggested that persons are best thought of as (usually) embodied parts of human beings: personhood is usually constituted in the brain, but it may be intricately related to the wider body, so that severe changes to the body could affect it to a great extent [9].

### Narrative and relational accounts

Schechtman's narrative self-constitution view holds that a person creates his identity by forming an autobiographical narrative. To be identity-constituting a narrative must meet an articulation constraint (the narrator can explain why he does, believes, and feels what he does) and a reality constraint (the narrative coheres with basic facts about the world) [8]. Schechtman's question is the characterization question, which characteristics belong to a person, not what it takes for the person to continue to exist [2][8]. Korsgaard makes a parallel point from practical reason: an agent must view herself as unified through time because deliberation, action, and life plans extend through time, irrespective of how strong the psychological connections between her temporal stages happen to be [2].

Baylis develops a relational version. Identity is "a dynamic, socially, culturally, politically, and historically situated communicative activity", and an identity-constituting narrative must satisfy an equilibrium constraint: it balances how a person understands herself with the minimal uptake others give it. Her formula is that identity lies "not in my body or in my brain, but in the negotiated spaces between my body and brain, and the bodies and brains of others" [8]. Embodiment enters here in a different sense than in animalism: bodies are read by others (race, gender, ability, age) and shape the narratives available to a person [8].

Dufner notes that the direction of explanation between ethics and identity is itself contested. The usual order derives the ethics from the metaphysics, but on the normative reading different moral theories generate different ideals of unity across time; utilitarianism fits a picture of persons as containers for valuable experiences, whereas Kantian views need stronger diachronic unity [2].

### Anticriterialism

A third answer to the persistence question denies that there are informative necessary and sufficient conditions at all. Merricks and others hold that psychological and physical continuity are evidence of persistence but neither guarantee nor are required for it [1]. The author's assessment is that this position, if correct, would undercut the fission methodology, which presupposes that thought experiments reveal the conditions of identity.

### Summary of the main accounts

| Account | What persistence consists in | Main support | Main difficulty |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Psychological continuity | Unique, non-branching chain of causally connected psychological states | Brain-transplant intuitions; tracks what matters | Fission; too many thinkers [1] |
| Animalism | Continuation of animal life or life-apt organization | Thinking animal argument; fetuses and vegetative states | Transplant and remnant-person cases [10] |
| Narrative | Not a persistence account; answers which characteristics are a person's | Moral agency and self-interpretation | Trivially true if every life event threatens it [8] |
| Reductionism | Relation R, a matter of degree | Fission; Parfit's argument | Revisionary about self-concern [5] |
| Anticriterialism | No informative conditions | Failure of all proposed criteria | Leaves persistence unexplained [1] |

## Evidence

Evidence in this field comes in two kinds that should not be confused. Thought experiments test the coherence and implications of accounts and yield philosophical findings. Psychological studies measure how people attribute identity and yield descriptive findings. Neither establishes what persistence consists in on its own [1][6].

### Thought experiments and their findings

Parfit's 1971 divided-mind case uses a hypothetical voluntary control of the bridge between the hemispheres. A student near the end of an examination disconnects his hemispheres, assigns each hand to one of two calculations, and reunites his mind to write a fair copy. Each stream seems continuous with the one stream before the division; when the streams are reunited he seems to remember having thought out two calculations without awareness of the other in each. Parfit infers that a person's mental history "need not be like a canal, with only one channel" but could be "like a river, with islands, and with separate streams", which makes the fission description (one person with two bodies and a divided mind) intelligible [5]. He notes that actual split-brain operations to treat epilepsy appeared, in the surgeon's words, to create two spheres of consciousness [5].

Locke's cases test the claim that consciousness, not substance, makes the person. In the prince-and-cobbler case the verdict is that the cobbler's body carries the prince's person. In the case of Socrates waking and sleeping, Locke argues that if the two do not share consciousness they are different persons, and that punishing one for what the other thought would be no more right than punishing one twin for the deeds of the other [3]. Olson's duplicating machine adds an epistemic finding: if a perfect duplicate is created, each of two beings will have the same apparent memories and believe itself to be the original, and nothing available from the inside distinguishes the two [1].

### Folk judgments about identity: the essential moral self

Strohminger and Nichols (2014) ran five online experiments with American participants to ask which parts of the mind people treat as essential to numerical identity [6]. In Study 1 (n = 148, between subjects) participants read a story of a man whose brain transplant left him with no change, with visual object agnosia, with loss of autobiographical memories, with apathy, or with loss of moral conscience, and rated agreement that the recipient was still the same man on a seven-point scale (higher meaning less the same). Means were 2.3 for control and 2.7 for agnosia (not reliably different), 3.7 for amnesia and 3.7 for apathy, and 4.8 for the moral deficit [6]. In Study 2 (n = 209) participants judged how much a person would change if a pill removed one of 62 traits. Moral traits scored a mean of 60.7 percent change, personality 51.8, desires 40.4, memory 39.2, and perception 37.7; losing the ability to recognize loved ones scored 69.0 percent against 18.9 for losing the ability to recognize celebrities [6].

Study 3 (n = 318) asked which traits move when a soul enters a new body, and Study 4 (n = 118) which traits survive reincarnation; in both, moral traits ranked first, and in Study 4 every moral trait was chosen over every personality trait (honest was chosen on 89.0 percent of trials, creative on 46.6). Study 5 (n = 79) asked about an old friend not seen for 40 years; moral change (mean 62.3) outweighed personality (45.3), basic cognition (38.1), memory (36.1), preferences (31.1), and perception (29.7) [6]. The authors report that belief in souls or dualism did not alter the ranking [6]. They interpret the pattern as showing that folk identity tracks the faculties that matter for social relationships, and they raise the possibility that philosophers' emphasis on memory reflects a conflation of the first-person experience of the self with the assessment of another person's identity [6].

### Neurodegeneration and perceived identity

Strohminger and Nichols (2015) moved from vignettes to real caregivers. They recruited 248 family members and friends of patients with frontotemporal dementia (n = 76), Alzheimer's disease (n = 114), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (n = 58, a control with mainly motor effects) through online support groups; 85 percent of respondents were female and 74 percent were describing a spouse or partner. Respondents rated symptoms, rated the patient's change on 15 moral and 15 non-moral personality traits, and answered four questions on perceived identity, such as whether the patient seemed like a stranger [7]. Perceived identity change was greatest in frontotemporal dementia, intermediate in Alzheimer's disease, and least in ALS (frontotemporal dementia versus ALS d = 1.54; Alzheimer's versus ALS d = 1.05; frontotemporal dementia versus Alzheimer's d = 0.45), although the groups did not differ in day-to-day functional impairment [7].

In the structural equation model, moral change was by far the strongest predictor of perceived identity change (beta = 0.73), aphasia a weaker one (beta = 0.15), and amnesia, personality change, depression, apraxia, and disease duration were not reliable predictors. In the Alzheimer's-only model, moral change was the only significant predictor [7]. Perceived identity change fully mediated the effect of moral change on deterioration of the caregiver relationship (the direct effect fell to a nonsignificant beta = 0.06) [7]. The authors conclude that their results depart from theories that ground identity in memory, distinctiveness, dispositional emotion, or global mental function [7].

### A case from neurosurgery

Baylis (2013) examines identity claims about deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease through patient narratives and two philosophical cases. Patients' reports quoted in the literature include "I don't feel like myself anymore" and "I feel like an electric doll". Schechtman's hypothetical Mr. Garrison, a shy Republican with a strong work ethic who becomes outgoing, a Democrat, and a campaigner after stimulation, shows how a narrative theorist can find a threat: his new passions were caused by brain manipulation, so he cannot explain them as flowing from his plans and beliefs [8]. Baylis replies by analyzing the case of the philosopher Helmut Dubiel, whose autobiography reports that stimulation removed his tremor and dyskinesias but left him with slurred speech and a sense of alienation, and who rejected a more discreet placement of the stimulator with the words "then I would feel like a machine". Her analysis locates the harms in Dubiel's account in alienation, stigma, and the forced trade-off between speaking clearly and walking; the clearest way in which stimulation can threaten identity, she argues, is by undermining agency, as when pathological gambling appears as a side effect [8].

### A case from digital replication

Karpus and Strasser (2025) report several cases as background. A fine-tuned language model, DigiDan, was built on the writings of the philosopher Daniel Dennett with his consent, under an agreement that no third party would have access and that it would not outlive him; it was deleted after his death. In a study reported by Schwitzgebel and colleagues, experts found it difficult to tell its answers from Dennett's, and non-philosophers did no better than chance [9]. In an art project, a group re-enacted five years of one person's search history in a film; the data donor ended the encounter when her own memories began to blur with the re-enactment [9]. These are reported by the authors, not verified here; they illustrate why the theoretical question of whether a replica extends a person is no longer idle.

## Implications

### For clinical ethics and dementia care

Dementia is the real-life case in which the theories diverge. If an advance directive written by a competent person is to bind her later, demented self, the question is whether the later patient is the same person and, if not, whether that matters. Dufner's example is Margo, who wrote a directive refusing life-saving treatment in severe dementia but appears happy in daily life. Most people believe the dementia patient is identical to the earlier adult; this can be preserved by adopting the biological view or by lowering the psychological threshold for persistence (McMahan), so the directive is the demented Margo's own. Dworkin argues that the patient cannot revise her critical interests, which concern the value of her life as a whole, so the directive should be respected; Jaworska argues that Margo retains a capacity to value, so her present values should not be dismissed [2]. McMahan, as reported by Dufner, holds that a late-stage patient is the same person and merits significant but somewhat reduced special concern given the loss of connectedness [2].

The caregiver evidence adds a practical consideration that the metaphysics cannot supply. Strohminger and Nichols found that families experience identity loss mainly when moral faculties decline, and that this perception mediates the deterioration of the relationship; they suggest that therapies should aim at preserving moral function [7]. The author's assessment is that this does not show that moral change is what constitutes identity (the study is third-person and descriptive), but it is a reason for clinicians to attend to caregivers' perceptions as a harm in their own right.

### For neurotechnology and neuroethics

For deep brain stimulation, psychiatric drugs, and future neural interfaces, the sources agree that "personality change" and "identity change" should not be conflated. Baylis argues that promises, wages, and punishment attach to the person regardless of changes in personality, and that a person who has undergone religious conversion is not thereby absolved of prior crimes [8]. The ethical test she proposes is agency: stimulation is a threat to identity only to the extent that the person can no longer contribute meaningfully to the authorship of her own life [8]. Schechtman's articulation constraint pushes in the same direction by asking whether the patient can give an intelligible account of why she now acts as she does [8]. The author's synthesis is that clinical practice should monitor consent, agency, and the person's ability to give an account of change, not only measured trait shifts. Baylis also warns that if disruption of narrative flow counted as a threat to identity, nearly every dramatic life event would be one, which is a reason for caution about the vocabulary of threat [8].

### For law and moral responsibility

Locke held that personal identity is the ground of reward and punishment, and that courts punish the drunk and the sleepwalker because human laws cannot distinguish real from counterfeit ignorance, whereas at the final judgment no one would answer for what he knows nothing of [3]. Contemporary views differ on what responsibility requires. Some hold that it presupposes identity (Butler, Reid), others that continuity or connectedness is enough, with or without identity. Parfit suggested that responsibility may decline in degrees as connections weaken, and his Russian nobleman illustrates the choice: a man who intends to give his future estates to the peasants, anticipating that his socialist ideals will fade, signs a document revocable only with his wife's consent and makes her promise to stay committed to his younger socialist self; when his ideals do fade, she must decide whether the promise still binds. If continuity is what matters she remains bound, if connectedness is what matters she may not be, and if both matter her duty is preserved but reduced [2].

Dufner distinguishes accountability from attributability: severe dementia may remove the capacity to be held accountable for past acts while leaving it fitting to attribute attitudes to the patient. For punishment of crimes long past, the answer depends on what punishment is for: if it is a symbolic condemnation addressed to society at large, punishing a demented offender may still make sense; if it is a communication addressed to the offender, it is pointless [2]. The author's assessment is that the metaphysics does not decide such cases alone, but it clarifies which fact (identity, continuity, connectedness, or capacity) a rule of responsibility is tracking.

### For death and the boundaries of life

The theories imply different answers about when we begin and end. Marquis's argument against abortion, that killing deprives a being of a valuable future like ours, presupposes that each of us was once a fetus, and so favors a biological view of identity while also relying on a psychological view of what makes the future valuable. Dufner concludes that biological views provide stronger support for prohibiting abortion than psychological ones, while stressing that animalists are not forced to endorse a prohibition [2]. Blatti notes that animalists must say that each of us was once or may become a non-person, and asks how that reconciles with ethical commitments on abortion and euthanasia [10]. At the end of life, whole-brain death requires loss of both consciousness and organismic integration, which can be read as a compromise between psychological and biological views, while the higher-brain standard presupposes a psychological view and implies that a patient in a persistent vegetative state is dead [2].

### For ethical theory

Parfit's conclusions also bear on the structure of morality. Rawls charged utilitarianism with not taking seriously the distinction between persons; Parfit replied that if the unity within a life is weaker than we assume, the separateness of persons is less deep, which weakens the objection against utilitarian aggregation and, on Parfit's view, makes prioritarianism a more moderate way to give distribution weight [2]. Dufner observes that the appeal to separateness has been used to support contractualism, libertarian side-constraints, rational egoism, and limits on aggregation, which suggests that it does limited work except as a negative argument against unrestricted utilitarianism [2]. Parfit's 1971 conclusion on self-interest is a precursor: he argued that the principle of self-interest has no force of its own, and that egoism and fear of distant death are strengthened by beliefs about identity he considered false [5].

### For digital replicas and artificial persons

Karpus and Strasser apply Parfit to replicas. If personhood is graded and relation R can hold in degrees, a sufficiently faithful replica could extend a person's personhood, a weak one at most a "sliver". Whether a replica without conscious states can be psychologically connected to its origin depends on whether consciousness is necessary for connectedness, which they leave open: if it is necessary, a "zombie" replica extends nothing but may mediate connections to other persons, as a book can; if it is not, it may carry slivers of personhood [9]. Their practical recommendations are that replicas should disclose their digital nature and the strength of connection to the original, that the replicated person's consent and a say over psychological data matter more than data privacy alone suggests, and that responsibility for a replica's output may need to be graded [9]. The author's assessment is that these arguments depend on the contested premise that Parfit's reductionism is true. An animalist would likely deny that a replica could be the original person, since animal persistence is a matter of organic life [10], and would then treat replicas as raising questions of consent, deception, and posthumous interests without a question of survival.

## Criticisms

Every account has a published weak point. The non-branching view implies that preserving both hemispheres can end one's existence while preserving one saves it, which Olson describes as the opposite of what one should expect, and Parfit himself conceded in 1971 that he could not see how to disprove the belief that identity questions must have answers, only to make it seem implausible [1][5]. Psychological views face the too-many-thinkers objection, and animalism faces the transplant intuition and the remnant-person problem [1][10]. Narrative views face Baylis's charge that if disruption of narrative flow is a threat to identity, then marriage, bereavement, and illness are threats too, so the claim becomes trivially true [8]. Dufner points out that the thought experiments aim at the normative question of which relation merits special concern, not at the empirical question of which relation people feel concern about, and cites critical discussions of the method [2]. Anticriterialists deny that there are informative conditions of persistence for such thought experiments to reveal [1].

The empirical evidence has its own limits. The 2014 experiments used paid online American participants and hypothetical stories, and the authors' stated scope is descriptive, about lay theories, not metaphysical claims [6]. The 2015 study is a self-selected volunteer sample of caregivers, mostly female and mostly describing spouses, measuring third-person perception, and the authors note that first-person identity may follow different rules [7].

## Common Pitfalls

The most frequent error is moving between the questions. Saying that someone is "no longer the same person" after a personality change is a claim about characterization, not about whether she exists [1], and Baylis adds that personality and personal identity are not synonymous [8]. A second error is treating evidence of identity (fingerprints, memory reports) as what identity consists in [1]. A third is reading folk judgments, such as the primacy of moral traits in Strohminger and Nichols, as findings about metaphysics; they describe attribution [6][7]. A fourth is assuming that one preferred theory settles a practical case: an animalist can accept that psychological relations matter most for self-concern, and a psychological theorist can accept that dementia patients are the same person, so the metaphysics under-determines the ethics [2]. A fifth is assuming that "the self" names a single thing, when the sources use it for a person, an immaterial subject, a narrative, or a bundle [1][4][8].
