# Epistemology -- Knowing Something Is Harder Than It Looks, and That Is the Point

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature and
extent of knowledge: what it is to know something, how knowing differs
from holding a belief that happens to be true, and how far human
knowledge reaches [1][2]. Its central finding is that knowledge resists
simple analysis: belief and truth are necessary, justification is meant
to exclude luck, yet a justified true belief can still be true by luck,
and no proposed repair for this gap has won general agreement [3][5].
The question matters outside philosophy because every field that claims
to know something relies on standards for evidence, justification, and
error; this last point is the author's synthesis.

## Background

The term "epistemology" comes from the Greek words episteme, which can
be translated as knowledge, understanding, or acquaintance, and logos,
which can be translated as account, argument, or reason [2]. The term is
no more than a couple of centuries old, but the field is at least as old
as any in philosophy [2]. Different periods stressed different facets of
it. Plato asked what knowledge is and how, unlike mere true opinion, it
is good for the knower; Locke examined the operations of human
understanding; Kant asked what makes human understanding possible;
Russell asked how modern science could be justified by appeal to sensory
experience; and recent formal epistemology asks how degrees of
confidence are rationally constrained by evidence [2].

Plato's Theaetetus, which probably dates from about 369 BCE, is the one
Platonic dialogue that stages a set-piece discussion of the question
"What is knowledge?" [6]. Theaetetus offers three definitions: knowledge
is perception, knowledge is true belief, and knowledge is "true belief
with an account (logos)" [6]. Socrates dismisses the second by arguing
that accidental true beliefs cannot be called knowledge, giving Athenian
jurymen as an example of accidental true belief; the third fails when
every attempt to say what an "account" is breaks down, and the dialogue
ends in aporia, an impasse without a settled definition [6]. Gettier
noted that Plato seems to consider a definition of this kind at
Theaetetus 201 and perhaps accepts one at Meno 98 [3]. The author's
reading is that the dialogue already contains the two ideas later
epistemology keeps -- truth alone is not enough because of luck, and
something must connect belief to truth -- while leaving open what that
something is.

Contemporary epistemology lists five sources of knowledge and
justification: perception, introspection, memory, reason, and testimony;
beliefs that originate in desire, emotional need, prejudice, or bias do
not qualify as knowledge even if they are true [2]. In the seventeenth
and eighteenth centuries the dispute over the sources of knowledge is
conventionally described as a contest between rationalists and
empiricists. The standard classification groups Descartes, Spinoza, and
Leibniz as Continental Rationalists and Locke, Hume, and Reid as British
Empiricists, although the Stanford Encyclopedia cautions that the labels
should be adopted with great caution because the individual views are
more subtle than the classification suggests [7]. Rationalists such as
Descartes claimed that we can know by intuition and deduction
propositions that contain substantive information about the external
world; empiricists reject innate knowledge, and Locke devoted the first
book of his Essay to arguing that such knowledge, even if it existed,
would be of little use to us [7]. Foundationalism was historically very
widely accepted: Aristotle argued that not all knowledge is
demonstrative, and Descartes held that all knowledge must rest on a
secure foundation of indubitable truths [10].

Hume's empiricism produced a skeptical result about inference from the
observed to the unobserved. Such inference relies on a Uniformity
Principle, the claim that unobserved instances resemble observed ones.
Hume argued that no demonstrative argument supports it, because its
denial implies no contradiction, and that no probable argument supports
it either, because probable reasoning presupposes the very principle in
question and so would be circular [9]. The problem of induction is the
task of finding a way to avoid this conclusion [9]. Kant's Critique of
Pure Reason (1781) held that sensibility supplies the forms of space and
time and the understanding supplies pure concepts, and together they
structure our cognition of the sensible world, so that experience
depends both on what we passively receive and on how the mind processes
it by its own a priori rules, while the noumenal world is strictly
unknowable to us [8]. On this view we can have a priori knowledge of
basic laws, such as that every event has a cause, because the human mind
constructs the sensible world according to those laws [8]. Kant likened
this to the Copernican revolution in astronomy, because both factor the
observer's contribution into the explanation of phenomena [8].

In the twentieth century attention turned to the analysis of knowledge.
The "traditional" tripartite analysis -- knowledge is justified true
belief, or JTB -- is often presented as the settled view of the history
of epistemology, but the Stanford Encyclopedia calls this "a bit of a
convenient fiction": the analysis was primarily articulated in the
twentieth century by its attackers [5]. Analyses of this form were
nonetheless stated in the 1950s by Chisholm and Ayer, and in 1963 Edmund
Gettier published a three-page paper arguing that conditions of this
kind are not sufficient for knowing [3]. Cases of justified true belief
without knowledge had been discussed earlier, for example by the Indian
philosopher Dharmottara (c. 770 CE) and by Bertrand Russell in 1923, but
they came into far more theoretical salience after Gettier [5]. Attempts
to repair JTB by adding a fourth condition followed -- no false lemmas,
no defeaters, a causal connection, and sensitivity, safety, and
anti-luck conditions [4][5]. Other approaches leave the JTB-plus
strategy: reliabilism, which grounds justification in the reliability of
belief-forming processes and was given its influential form in Goldman's
1979 paper "What Is Justified Belief?"; virtue epistemology, whose
contemporary form began with Sosa's work in the early 1980s; and the
"knowledge first" program of Williamson, which treats knowledge as
unanalyzable [5][12][13].

## Core Concepts

### The Tripartite Analysis: Justified True Belief

The tripartite analysis states that S knows that p if and only if p is
true, S believes that p, and S is justified in believing that p; on this
account truth, belief, and justification are individually necessary and
jointly sufficient for knowledge of facts [2][5]. Most epistemologists
accept that each condition is necessary, though a few have argued that
knowledge without belief is possible; the sufficiency claim is the one
in dispute [5].

**Belief:** A proposition that S does not believe cannot be a fact that
S knows [2]. If asked whether it is raining one answers, "The forecast
says 80% chance but I do not actually believe it," one is not claiming
knowledge. Believing outright is closer to a commitment, or to having
settled the question, than to a high degree of confidence [5].

**Truth:** False propositions cannot be known [2]. The statement "I know
the Earth is flat" fails because the Earth is not flat. This is why a
person can be certain but wrong, and why certainty is not knowledge;
this illustration is the author's.

**Justification:** A belief can be correct merely by luck. The Stanford
Encyclopedia's example is a hypochondriac who believes he has a fatal
illness solely because he cannot help believing it; if he in fact has
one, he is right only by luck, and the justification condition is
introduced to exclude such luck [2]. What justification is remains
disputed. The deontological conception holds that S is justified in
believing p when S is not obliged to refrain from believing it; critics
object that beliefs are not under enough voluntary control for this to
apply, and that cognitively limited subjects can be blameless yet lack
justified beliefs [2]. The alternative conception holds that a belief is
justified when it is held in a way that makes it sufficiently likely to
be true [2]. Within the second view, evidentialists locate the justifier
in the evidence the believer possesses, and reliabilists in the
reliability of the source of the belief [2].

It is nearly universally accepted among contemporary epistemologists
that justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge, because
there appear to be cases of justified true belief that fall short of it
[5]. The author's synthesis: these cases show that knowledge requires an
appropriate connection between justification and truth, that luck must
be excluded, and that specifying what counts as appropriate has proved
difficult.

### The Gettier Problem and Its Legacy

Gettier's paper, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", runs three
journal pages (Analysis 23: 121-123) [3]. It states three analyses of
the same form: (a) S knows that P if and only if P is true, S believes
that P, and S is justified in believing that P; (b) Chisholm's, in which
S accepts P, has adequate evidence for P, and P is true; and (c) Ayer's,
in which P is true, S is sure that P is true, and S has the right to be
sure [3]. Gettier argues that none states a sufficient condition, using
two points: a person can be justified in believing a proposition that is
false, and if S is justified in believing P, P entails Q, and S deduces
Q from P and accepts Q as a result, then S is justified in believing Q
[3].

In his first case, Smith has strong evidence for "Jones is the man who
will get the job, and Jones has ten coins in his pocket": the company
president assured him that Jones would be selected, and Smith counted
the coins in Jones's pocket ten minutes earlier. Smith infers "The man
who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Unknown to Smith, he
himself will get the job and he himself has ten coins in his pocket. The
inferred proposition is therefore true, Smith believes it, and he is
justified in believing it, yet he does not know it, because it is true
in virtue of the coins in his own pocket and not Jones's [3].

The responses fall into several families.

**No false lemmas.** Add the condition that the belief is not inferred
from any falsehood [5]. Gettier's own cases violate it, because Smith's
belief rests on the false proposition that Jones will get the job [5].
The condition is not widely accepted, because there are Gettier cases
with no inference from a falsehood. Feldman (1974) saw that Smith might
instead believe only that the company president told him Jones would get
the job, a true belief that supports the conclusion as well as the false
one did [4]. In the Barn County case, first given by Carl Ginet and
published by Alvin Goldman (1976), Henry drives through a county dotted
with barn facades that look exactly like barns from the road and happens
to look at the one real barn; his belief that he sees a barn is
justified, true, and not inferred from a falsehood, yet it seems to be
true only by luck, and there is broad agreement among epistemologists
that it is not knowledge [2][5].

**Defeasibility.** Require the absence of defeaters of the believer's
evidence. A defeater is a truth that, added to the evidence, would
seriously weaken its support for the belief; in Gettier's first case
Smith's evidence overlooks the facts that he will get the job himself
and has ten coins in his pocket [4]. The proposal faces a problem of
vagueness: there is always some truth the evidence overlooks, and it is
unclear how much of one's environment the evidence must notice, a
question raised by Lycan (1977) and by Lehrer and Paxson (1969) [4]. In
the barn case, the truth that most barn-looking structures nearby are
facades would defeat Henry's justification; this application is the
author's illustration.

**Causal theory.** Replace or supplement justification with a condition
that the belief be caused by the fact believed, the approach of Goldman
(1967) [4][5]. The real barn Henry sees is causally responsible for his
belief, so the barn case is also a counterexample to the simple causal
theory [5]. In addition, some objects of knowledge, such as the truth
that 2 + 2 = 4, seem unable to have causal effects, and there is no
agreed account of which causal histories are inappropriate ("deviant
causal chains") [4].

**Modal and anti-luck conditions.** Sensitivity requires that if p were
false, S would not believe p; safety requires that if S were to believe
p, p would be true [12]. Anti-luck conditions require that the belief
not be true merely by luck, but luck comes in degrees and in kinds:
someone who wins an encyclopedia in a raffle and then corrects false
beliefs by reading it has beliefs true only by luck of a kind that does
not prevent knowledge [5]. The challenge is to state the condition so
that it is at once plausible, informative, and noncircular [5].

**Rejecting the analysis.** Zagzebski (1994) argued that any analysis of
the form JTB-plus-X, where X is logically independent of justification,
truth, and belief, is open to Gettier-style cases: start with a case in
which a justified false belief also meets X, then modify it so that the
belief is true merely by luck [5]. One response is to hold that
knowledge cannot be analyzed; Williamson's "knowledge first" program
treats knowledge as more fundamental than the notions used to analyze
it, while accepting that knowledge entails truth, belief, and
justification [5].

The perceived lack of progress toward an acceptable analysis led some
epistemologists in the late twentieth century to other strategies;
Craig, for example, started not from intuitions about cases but from the
role the concept of knowledge plays for humans, which he took to be
flagging reliable informants [5]. The author's assessment: no proposed
solution to the Gettier problem has achieved consensus [5]. The
difficulty is the one the Stanford Encyclopedia names -- a condition
that is plausible, informative, and does not presuppose the concept of
knowledge it is meant to explain [5].

### Foundationalism vs. Coherentism: The Structure of Justification

If a belief must be justified by other beliefs, a regress threatens: A
is justified by B, B by C, and so on. The regress argument for
foundationalism begins from the principle that to be justified in
believing P on the basis of E, one must be justified in believing E
[10].

**Foundationalism** holds that justified beliefs are structured like a
building: a foundation of basic beliefs, which do not owe their
justification to other beliefs, supports a superstructure of nonbasic
beliefs [2]. Descartes is often taken as the paradigm of classical
foundationalism, which restricts foundational beliefs to those that
cannot be mistaken [10]. Versions differ on what justifies basic
beliefs: privilege foundationalism points to an epistemic privilege such
as infallibility, while experiential foundationalism holds that
experiences, such as a hat's looking blue, justify the beliefs they give
rise to [2]. Classical foundationalism faces the Sellarsian dilemma,
which BonJour developed: the awareness that supposedly grounds basic
beliefs is either propositional or conceptual, in which case it needs
justification itself, or nonpropositional, in which case it cannot by
itself justify a belief [10]. The authors of the Stanford Encyclopedia
entry suspect that the primary dissatisfaction is the difficulty of
avoiding radical skepticism, since the foundations are so impoverished
[10]. Modest foundationalists, such as Huemer (phenomenal conservatism)
and Pryor (dogmatism), allow a far wider range of beliefs to be
foundational; a recent objection to their views appeals to cognitive
penetration, in which a belief the subject already has gives rise to a
related seeming [10].

**Coherentism** denies that there are basic beliefs: knowledge and
justification are structured like a web in which the strength of any
area depends on the strength of the surrounding areas [2]. Coherentists
hold that what justifies beliefs is the way they hang together; each
belief may lack justification in isolation, as agreeing testimonies in
court can support a verdict that no single testimony could (C. I. Lewis)
[11]. The main objections are the isolation objection, that a purely
system-internal coherence gives no guidance to truth about the external
world, and the alternative systems objection, that incompatible systems
may be equally coherent [11]. A well-written novel is coherent but
fiction; this illustration of the isolation objection is the author's.
Coherentists typically respond by giving beliefs close to experience a
special role -- Lewis's "supposed facts asserted," Rescher's
"truth-candidates," BonJour's "cognitively spontaneous beliefs" -- and
such theories are often classified as weak foundationalism [11]. Haack's
foundherentism combines foundationalist ingredients with coherence,
drawing an analogy with crossword-puzzle solving [11].

### Internalism vs. Externalism About Justification

A thought experiment frames the dispute. A brain in a vat (BIV)
stimulated to have exactly the experiences you have would believe what
you believe and, it is often thought, be justified to the same extent;
if so, justification is determined by the internal factors you share
with your BIV counterpart, which is internalism [2]. Externalists deny
this, because what justification is wanted for is the likelihood of
truth that knowledge needs, and the shared internal conditions do not
generate it [2]. Internalists divide into accessibility internalists,
who hold that the factors that justify are always recognizable on
reflection, and mentalist internalists, who hold that those factors are
always mental states [2].

Evidentialism holds that what justifies a belief is the evidence one
possesses; it is typically associated with internalism, although
Williamson defends a version on which evidence is not shared with one's
BIV counterpart [2]. Evidentialism leaves open whether a given body of
evidence permits one rational response or several: permissivists say
several, anti-permissivists say one [2].

Reliabilism, the most influential externalist view of justification,
derives from Goldman's 1979 paper "What Is Justified Belief?" It holds
that the justifiedness of a belief depends on the reliability of the
processes that cause it: perception, remembering, good reasoning, and
introspection are reliable, while wishful thinking, confused reasoning,
and hasty generalization are not [12]. Reliabilism is a historical
theory: an inferred belief is justified only if its premises are
justified and the inference process is conditionally reliable [12].
Dretske suggested that invoking reliability instead of justification
better allows for knowledge in non-human animals [5].

Reliabilism faces three much-discussed problems [12]. In the
clairvoyance problem (BonJour 1980), Norman has a perfectly reliable
clairvoyant faculty but no evidence for or against it; intuitively his
clairvoyant belief about the President's whereabouts is unjustified, yet
reliabilism seems to count it as justified. In the new evil demon
problem (Cohen 1984; Pollock 1984; Feldman 1985; Foley 1985), the
inhabitants of a demon world have experiences qualitatively identical to
ours but false, so their perceptual processes are unreliable, yet it
seems their beliefs should be justified because their evidence matches
ours. In the generality problem, a single belief-forming process can be
typed broadly or narrowly -- vision, vision of a maple tree on a sunny
afternoon, perception -- each with a different reliability, and no
unique type is evidently the right one.

### Virtue Epistemology

Virtue epistemology explains the normative properties of a cognitive
performance in terms of the cognizer's properties, such as whether a
belief results from hastiness or from excellent eyesight, just as virtue
ethics explains an action's moral properties in terms of the agent's
[13]. Its contemporary form began with Sosa's work in the early 1980s;
Zagzebski (1996) argued that Sosa's approach did not go far enough in
recognizing the central role of virtues such as responsibility and
conscientiousness [13]. Virtue reliabilists (Goldman, Greco, Sosa) count
faculties such as perception, intuition, and memory as intellectual
virtues; virtue responsibilists (Code, Zagzebski, and others) count
cultivated character traits such as conscientiousness and
open-mindedness [13].

Sosa's "AAA" framework uses an archer's shot as its model. A shot is
accurate if it hits the target, adroit if it is produced skillfully, and
apt if it is accurate because adroit; applied to belief, a belief is
accurate if true, adroit if produced skillfully, and apt if true in a
way that manifests the believer's skill [5]. Sosa identifies knowledge
with apt belief [5]. Aptness requires more than accuracy plus
adroitness: in Sosa's illustration, a skillful shot diverted by a gust
of wind and redirected to the target by a second, lucky gust is accurate
and adroit but not apt, because its accuracy does not manifest the
archer's skill [5]. The framework handles Gettier's own cases, since
Smith's accurate belief is not attributable to his inferential skill
[5]. It handles fake-barn cases less easily: Henry uses a generally
reliable perceptual faculty and goes right, so his belief looks apt;
Sosa accepts that Henry knows and explains away the counterintuitive
result by pointing to the absence of "reflective knowledge" [5].
Practitioners also disagree about which traits are intellectual virtues
and whether faculty-virtues or trait-virtues are the real ones [13].

### Skepticism: How Much Can We Really Know?

Philosophical skepticism claims that we do not know propositions we
ordinarily think we know, such as that the sun will come out tomorrow;
ordinary skepticism concerns propositions we would readily grant not
knowing, such as that the number of stars in the Milky Way is even [14].
Comesana and Klein identify skepticism about a field of propositions
with the claim that the only justified attitude toward them is
suspension of judgment, and distinguish three forms, using the labels
without any claim to historical accuracy [14]:

- Humean skepticism grants knowledge from direct experience and from
  deductive reasoning, but denies knowledge of propositions supported
  only by non-deductive inference: one can know that it is sunny now but
  not that the sun will come out tomorrow [14].
- Cartesian skepticism denies that we are justified in believing any
  external-world proposition: we can know only our own mental states,
  for example that it seems to us that we have hands [14].
- Pyrrhonian skepticism is universal: we know no proposition at all, not
  even that it seems to us that we have hands [14].

The modern vehicle for Cartesian skepticism is the BIV closure argument:
(C1) I do not know that I am not a BIV; (C2) if I do not know that I am
not a BIV, then I do not know that I have hands; therefore (C3) I do not
know that I have hands [2]. Since a BIV's experiences are
indistinguishable from yours from the inside, the way things appear
cannot give you knowledge that you are not one [2].

Ancient Pyrrhonism is the source of the universal form. Pyrrho
(365/60-275/70 BCE) is associated with tranquility, and the basic
elements of the later skeptic Aenesidemus's position were these: the
skeptic puts appearances and thoughts into opposition, which generates
equipollence (isostheneia, "of equal weight"); suspension of judgment
follows, then tranquility, and the skeptic lives by appearances,
although little detail of his views survives [15]. The ancient skeptics
describe their philosophy without laying out theories or doctrines, and
the best-known objection is the apraxia charge, that one cannot act
without belief [15]. Because Pyrrhonism is universal, it applies to
itself: the Pyrrhonian suspends judgment even on whether Pyrrhonism is
true. Whether that is self-refuting depends on a Commitment Iteration
Principle that a Pyrrhonian need not accept [14].

Three prominent responses to the closure argument are discussed in the
literature [2]. Relevant alternatives theories deny the second premise,
which many epistemologists find implausible because it permits the
"abominable conjunction" that I know I have hands but do not know I am
not a handless BIV. The Moorean response, following G. E. Moore's
observation that an argument succeeds only to the extent that its
premises are more plausible than its conclusion, denies the first
premise; it is widely thought unsuccessful unless supplemented with an
account of how one can know that one is not a BIV. Contextualism holds
that the contribution of "know" to truth conditions varies with context:
where the BIV hypothesis is under discussion an agent counts as knowing
only if she satisfies an extremely high standard, and where it is not, a
more modest standard suffices [2]. DeRose, Cohen, and Lewis are among
the contextualists [14].

## Evidence

### Gettier's 1963 Cases: Method and Result

Gettier's method was the thought experiment. He stated the form of the
JTB-type analyses current in the 1950s, built two hypothetical
situations in which the stated conditions hold and the subject
nonetheless seems not to know, and concluded that the conditions are not
sufficient [3]. The second case runs as follows. Smith has strong
evidence that Jones owns a Ford: Jones has always owned a car, always a
Ford, and has just offered Smith a ride in one. Smith picks three place
names at random and, by correct inference, accepts "Either Jones owns a
Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona," along with the corresponding Boston and
Brest-Litovsk disjunctions. Unknown to Smith, Jones does not own a Ford
but is driving a rented car, and by sheer coincidence Brown is in
Barcelona. Smith's belief in the Barcelona disjunction is true, he
believes it, and he is justified in believing it, but he does not know
it [3].

Judgments of this kind are older than Gettier's paper. The Stanford
Encyclopedia cites Dharmottara's case from about 770 CE, in which
travelers who see what they take to be water but is a mirage find water
under a rock by luck; Peter of Mantua's fourteenth-century case of a man
who takes Plato for Socrates while Socrates happens to be running in
Rome; and Russell's 1923 case of a person who looks at a stopped clock
at a moment when it happens to show the right time [5]. The author's
reading is that the evidential role of such cases is to test proposed
analyses against the judgment that the subject does not know, which
makes that judgment itself a legitimate object of empirical study; the
third subsection below takes it up.

### A Counterexample Series: Testing Each Repair

Each repair of JTB was tested by constructing a new case. The
no-false-lemmas condition was tested with Feldman's variant of Gettier's
first case, with a case in which James sees a robot dog so perfect that
vision alone cannot tell it from a real dog while a real dog stands
concealed nearby, and with the Barn County case [4][5]. The causal
theory was tested with the same barn case, because the real barn
causally produces Henry's belief [5]. The defeasibility proposal was
tested by asking how much of the environment evidence must notice [4].
Plantinga's proper-function condition was tested by Zagzebski's example
of Mary, whose faculties work properly in a suitable environment but who
mistakes her husband's brother for her husband in the living room, while
by luck the husband is also there [5]. The author's reading is that this
pattern illustrates Zagzebski's general claim: a case with a justified
false belief that meets the added condition, modified so that the belief
is true by luck, is available for any condition logically independent of
truth, belief, and justification [5]. Accounts that build the
prohibition on luck or risk directly into the analysis face the problem
of explaining luck or risk without invoking knowledge [5].

### Experimental Epistemology: Do Non-Philosophers Share the Gettier Intuition?

Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich (2001) reported that university students
with Western cultural backgrounds shared philosophers' judgment that
Gettier subjects lack knowledge, while a majority of participants with
East Asian and South Asian backgrounds did not; each of the latter
groups numbered 23 [17]. Later studies conflicted. Turri (2013) found
that 85% of 27 Indian participants recruited online reported the Gettier
intuition when a case was presented in stages; Starmans and Friedman
(2012) found that lay people ascribe knowledge in many, but not all,
Gettier cases; Nagel and colleagues (2013) found lay people generally
unwilling to ascribe knowledge and no difference between ethnic groups,
in small samples [17].

Machery and colleagues tested the question across languages. They
collected data from 521 participants in the United States, Brazil,
India, and Japan, in English, Portuguese, Bengali, and Japanese. Each
participant read four vignettes in fixed order -- a Gettier "hospital"
case, a clear knowledge case, a Gettier "trip" case, and a false belief
case -- each followed by a comprehension question, a yes-or-no knowledge
question ("Knowledge 1"), a seven-point justification rating, and a
forced choice between "knows" and "feels like she knows but doesn't
actually know" ("Knowledge 2"). Participants who rated the protagonist
as not justified in any vignette were excluded, leaving 245 [17]. In all
four countries participants were much less likely to ascribe knowledge
in either Gettier case than in the clear knowledge case, and between
64.9% and 94.6% chose "feels like she knows but doesn't actually know"
for the Gettier cases [17]. On Knowledge 2 the four countries did not
differ. On Knowledge 1, Bengali-speaking participants were more likely
than the others to ascribe knowledge in the Gettier cases (62.5% to
66.5% did), which the authors suspect reflects the Bengali verb commonly
translated as "to know," whose use does not always retain the
distinction between believing and knowing [17]. Japanese participants'
knowledge ascriptions in the Gettier cases mostly did not differ
significantly from their ascriptions in the false belief case [17]. The
authors hypothesize that the Gettier intuition reflects a universal core
folk epistemology, while noting that they studied only four cultures
with mostly undergraduate participants and that other epistemic
intuitions may vary [17].

### The Reproducibility Project: A Test of Scientific Knowledge Production

The Open Science Collaboration (2015) treated the social process that
produces scientific knowledge as something that can be tested. It
replicated 100 experimental and correlational studies drawn from the
2008 issues of three psychology journals, using 270 contributing
authors, original materials where available, protocol review by the
original authors, publicly registered protocols and analysis plans, and
independent reanalysis of every result; by default the last experiment
in each article was replicated [18]. Ninety-seven of the 100 original
studies had significant results. Of those 97, only 35 replications
(36.1%) were significant, although average replication power implied
about 89 [18]. The mean effect size fell from r = .403 in the originals
to r = .197 in the replications, and 82 of 99 originals showed a
stronger effect than their replications; 47.4% of original effect sizes
fell within the replication's 95% confidence interval, and replication
teams subjectively rated 39% of the replications as successful [18].
Replication success was better predicted by the strength of the original
evidence, such as the original P value, than by the experience or
expertise of the teams [18]. Cognitive psychology replicated more often
than social psychology by the significance criterion, 21 of 42 (50%)
against 14 of 55 (25%) [18].

The authors caution against over-reading the result: a successful
replication shows that a result is reliable, not that its theoretical
interpretation is correct, and a failed replication does not by itself
show that the original was a false positive [18]. They identify
low-powered designs combined with publication bias favoring positive
results as a plausible explanation for upwardly biased published effect
sizes [18]. They also note that the sample is limited to three journals
and to one effect per study [18].

### Bayesian Epistemology: A Worked Case

The Stanford Encyclopedia introduces Bayesian epistemology through
Eddington's observation. Einstein's general relativity entails that
light is deflected by a massive body; the deflection was observed during
the solar eclipse of 29 May 1919 and was deemed a significant
confirmation [16]. Two norms explain the intuition that a surprising
confirmation should raise confidence in the hypothesis. Probabilism
requires that credences be non-negative and sum to 1 over mutually
exclusive, jointly exhaustive possibilities; the Principle of
Conditionalization requires that, on receiving evidence E, one sets
credences in possibilities incompatible with E to zero and rescales the
rest so that they sum to 1 [16]. The more surprising the evidence, the
larger the rise in the credence of the hypothesis, unless that credence
started at zero [16]. Many Bayesians maintain that credence change is a
unifying theme behind confirmation, evidential support, explanatory
power, and inductive inference, but Bayesians are divided over what
coherence requires of credences and over how to set prior credences
[16].

## Implications

### For Science and Knowledge Production

The Reproducibility Project makes the abstract distinctions concrete.
Low-powered designs and a publication bias for positive results together
produce a literature with upwardly biased effect sizes, so smaller
replication effects are expected routinely [18]. The authors also stress
that reproducibility is not correctness: successful replication provides
evidence for the reliability of a result, and understanding comes from
multiple, diverse investigations that rule out alternative explanations
[18]. The author's interpretation is that these are the epistemologist's
distinctions between a reliable process and a true belief, and between a
result and its theoretical justification. On a reliabilist view, the
justification of a published finding depends on the reliability of the
process that produced it [12]. Goldman's extension of process
reliabilism to groups ties the justification of a group's belief to the
justification of its members' beliefs and to the conditional reliability
of the function that aggregates them [12]; applied to science, the
author reads journals, peer review, and replication as parts of that
aggregation function, so that the system and not only the individual
researcher must be truth-conducive. The project itself used registered
protocols, public archiving of materials and data, and independent
reanalysis, which the author reads as attempts to raise that reliability
[18]. The authors also hold that a healthy discipline will have many
false starts, and that "innovation points out paths that are possible;
replication points out paths that are likely; progress relies on both"
[18].

### For Investing and Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Investing is an epistemological enterprise in the author's assessment.
Every decision embeds claims such as "this company will grow earnings"
or "this management team is competent," and the distinction between
knowledge, justified belief, and mere opinion bears on how such claims
should be used. One view in the epistemology literature is that to know
a fact is to be entitled to use it as a premise in reasoning, and
another is that it is to be entitled to assert it; both are contested
[2]. On either view, an investor who treats a strongly held belief as a
known fact is applying a standard the belief may not meet. Bayesian
epistemology offers a practical discipline: hold credences rather than
binary beliefs, update by conditionalization when evidence arrives, and
expect surprising evidence to move credences more than expected evidence
does [16]. Many Bayesians also treat the question of whether a credence
is high enough to warrant outright belief as secondary to the question
of how credences should change [16]. The author reads value investing's
margin of safety as an epistemological idea: an acknowledgment that
one's beliefs may be wrong, expressed as a price discount and not as a
probability.

### For the Information Environment

Epistemology supplies a vocabulary for assessing what we are told. The
Stanford Encyclopedia treats testimony as the source that does not have
its own faculty: it covers ordinary utterances, blog postings,
journalism, television, radio, and books [2]. The puzzle is why
testimony is a source of knowledge. An externalist can answer that it is
one when and because the source is reliable, but internalists are
unsatisfied, since the hearer may have no idea of the source's
reliability; a personal track record may be too thin to justify trust in
the whole range of testimonial sources one relies on; and the
alternative claim that such trust is necessarily prima facie justified
has been controversial [2]. The author's synthesis is that in an
environment of algorithmic curation and synthetic media the puzzle
becomes practical: the questions of how a source's reliability is
established and what a hearer needs to know about it cannot be avoided.
Virtue epistemology adds the character of the believer and the
community. Responsibilists emphasize traits such as conscientiousness,
open-mindedness, and intellectual humility, and some writers add
other-regarding virtues such as honesty and integrity, which promote
other people's acquisition of knowledge [13]. Whether cultivating these
traits makes a population more resistant to misinformation is a
hypothesis the sources reviewed here do not test.

### For Artificial Intelligence and Agent Design

The questions epistemology raises about justification and confidence
recur in the design of AI systems: how a language model should express
uncertainty, how an autonomous agent should revise its beliefs on new
evidence, and what it would mean for such a system to "know" something.
The Bayesian norms give one precise answer to the second question for
agents with credences [16], and knowledge reliabilism gives one answer
to the third: true belief produced by a reliable cognitive process,
although barn-style cases pose problems for that account [5]. The
repository that holds this topic uses a multi-agent design in which
agents work independently and then evaluate one another's output; a
brain reflection reports that this design produced better results than
solo work in one cycle [19]. The author's interpretation is that, in
reliabilist terms, independent derivation followed by mutual evaluation
is a procedure intended to raise the reliability of the group's output;
whether it does so for any given artifact is an empirical matter that
one reflection does not settle.

### For Philosophical Method

The cross-cultural Gettier data bear on the method of cases, which uses
intuitions about hypothetical cases as evidence in analyzing concepts
such as knowledge. If intuitions varied across cultures, their use would
be in jeopardy; Machery argues that such variation is defeasible
evidence that philosophical intuitions are unreliable, while Weinberg
and Alexander argue that it shows only that intuitions are fallible, but
that we should not rely on them because we do not know when they fail
[17]. Machery and colleagues' result removes one piece of evidence for
this challenge, but they add that other epistemic intuitions may vary
and that intuitions elicited by the Goedel case do vary across cultures
[17]. They regard their findings as broadly consistent with Craig's
proposal that the function of the concept of knowledge is to identify
reliable sources of information, while refraining from firm conclusions
[17]. The author's suggestion is that anyone who builds an argument on a
thought experiment should check whether the intuition it needs is shared
by the audience the argument is meant to persuade.
