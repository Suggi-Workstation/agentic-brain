# Business Ethics -- Corporate Power Creates Duties That Profit and Compliance Cannot Exhaust

Business ethics asks how corporations and the people who govern them should use organized economic power when their choices affect workers, customers, suppliers, communities, political institutions, and the natural environment. Its central claim is that economic viability matters to a durable business, but neither legality nor profitability alone determines whether conduct respects rights, distributes risk fairly, avoids complicity, and provides remedy when harm occurs [1][5][6]. The practical task is therefore to connect moral justification to decision rights, incentives, information, voice, oversight, and enforceable correction rather than treat ethics as philanthropy or public relations.

## Background

Moral questions about commerce long predate the modern corporation, but contemporary business ethics developed as a distinct field when philosophers and management scholars began examining the purposes, permissions, and obligations of large firms. The field is normative: it asks what business actors ought to do, not merely what firms commonly do or what conduct produces favorable reputation [1]. This distinction matters because a descriptive pattern of profit seeking cannot establish that profit is the only justified end, while an association between responsible conduct and financial performance cannot by itself establish a moral duty.

The modern debate often begins with shareholder primacy. Milton Friedman argued that a corporate executive is an agent of the owners and normally should conduct the business in accordance with their desire for profit, while obeying law and the ethical customs of society [2]. His argument was not the claim that fraud, coercion, or any profitable illegality is permissible. It was an agency and political-legitimacy objection: an executive who spends owners' resources on self-selected social purposes may impose what resembles a private tax without democratic authorization [2]. This position gives managers a relatively determinate objective and warns that vague social mandates can conceal personal preference or managerial self-protection.

Stakeholder theory challenged the idea that shareholders alone supply the corporation's morally relevant purpose. Donaldson and Preston distinguished descriptive, instrumental, and normative forms of stakeholder theory: one can describe how firms actually manage relationships, investigate whether stakeholder management supports conventional performance, or argue that stakeholders have legitimate interests that deserve consideration in their own right [3]. The normative version cannot be reduced to the claim that treating workers or customers well eventually raises shareholder value. If another person's rights matter only when respecting them is profitable, the person remains an instrument rather than a stakeholder with independent moral standing.

Michael Jensen proposed an influential attempted reconciliation. He argued that managers need a single-valued objective for making trade-offs and that conventional stakeholder theory can leave them answerable to no determinate performance test. His "enlightened value maximization" retains long-run firm value as the decision criterion while insisting that a firm cannot maximize durable value by ignoring customers, employees, suppliers, regulators, or communities [4]. The framework improves on short-term profit seeking, but it remains instrumental: stakeholder claims ultimately enter because of their effect on firm value. Rights-based critics can therefore still ask whether some actions remain wrong even when violating a stakeholder would increase total firm value.

The dispute also raises a prior question: who bears moral responsibility when a corporation acts? The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes a live debate over whether a firm can be a moral agent in its own right or whether corporate praise and blame are shorthand for the responsibility of natural persons [1]. Organizational rules can combine many actions into a stable policy that no single employee designed or controlled. Yet attributing responsibility to the firm must not erase the role duties of directors, executives, managers, professionals, employees, investors, and business partners. The author's synthesis is layered responsibility: institutional responsibility explains the organized policy and the entity's duty to repair it; individual responsibility follows authority, knowledge, contribution, capacity to object, and capacity to prevent or remedy harm.

International standards converted parts of this philosophical debate into operational expectations. The United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights organize responsibility through the State duty to protect, the corporate responsibility to respect, and access to remedy. They state that the corporate responsibility applies independently of a State's willingness or ability to protect rights and calls for a policy commitment, ongoing human-rights due diligence, and processes to remediate adverse impacts the enterprise causes or contributes to [5]. The OECD Guidelines extend risk-based due diligence across human rights, labour, environment, bribery, consumer interests, disclosure, science and technology, competition, taxation, products, services, and business relationships [6]. The ILO MNE Declaration supplies tripartite guidance on employment, training, working conditions, industrial relations, and the principles of international labour standards [7].

These instruments distinguish ethics from philanthropy. Carroll's four-part framework separates economic, legal, ethical, and philanthropic responsibilities; it presents them as concurrent components rather than permission to substitute charitable giving for lawful and ethical operations [8]. A donation does not repair forced labour in a supplier, deceptive product design, corruption in procurement, discriminatory employment, unsafe production, or pollution shifted onto a community. The author's synthesis is that responsibility begins with how the firm earns, not with how it gives away a fraction of what it earned.

The history of the debate has therefore moved from a binary slogan toward an accountability problem. Shareholder primacy identifies the danger of unbounded managerial discretion; stakeholder theory identifies people whose interests may otherwise be externalized; rights frameworks identify minimum constraints; consequential reasoning identifies the distribution and scale of benefits and harms; virtue ethics identifies character and organizational culture; and legitimacy theory asks who authorized private power over shared conditions [1][3][5][6]. No framework alone determines every trade-off. Together they establish that corporate responsibility must be justified, allocated to decision-makers, translated into systems, and tested by outcomes and remedy.

## Core Concepts

### Corporate Responsibility Is Layered, Not Displaced

A corporation can coordinate actions through budgets, policies, reporting lines, incentives, software, contracts, and board resolutions even when its members change. Whether those processes amount to a corporate intention and independent moral agency remains disputed [1]. This organizational continuity nevertheless makes it reasonable to assign duties to the firm: stop harmful conduct, preserve evidence, compensate affected people, reform systems, and disclose material failures [1][5]. Entity-level responsibility is especially important when harm results from an interaction among departments rather than one rogue actor.

Institutional responsibility does not absorb individual responsibility. Directors authorize purpose, risk appetite, executive selection, incentives, and oversight. Executives allocate resources and establish operational priorities. Managers translate those priorities into targets and tolerated shortcuts. Professionals possess domain-specific duties when technical knowledge reveals danger. Employees retain ordinary duties not to deceive or knowingly harm, although coercion, information limits, and retaliation risk can reduce culpability. Owners and investors influence governance through capital, votes, engagement, and exit. Suppliers and contractors bear duties for their own conduct while buyers bear responsibility for the pressure, specifications, monitoring, and purchasing terms they create [5][6].

The author's synthesis is a control-contribution-remedy test. Ask what each actor controlled, what it knew or should reasonably have known, how its conduct caused, contributed to, or was linked to the impact, what objection or prevention was feasible, and what remedy it can now supply. This test prevents two opposite errors: blaming an abstract corporation so that no person answers, and blaming one person so that the system that selected and rewarded the conduct remains intact.

### Shareholder Primacy Defines an Objective but Not a Moral Exemption

The strongest shareholder argument is not that shareholders are the only people who matter. It is that executives exercise delegated power over assets they do not personally own, and a clear objective permits owners and boards to evaluate that power [2][4]. In a viable for-profit enterprise, profit can signal that customers value output above priced input cost, help finance investment, absorb risk, pay obligations, and permit the enterprise to continue [2][4]. A business that cannot create economic value cannot discharge responsibilities indefinitely.

The moral limit is that ownership does not normally authorize every value-increasing use of other people or shared resources. Contracts can be incomplete, bargaining power unequal, information hidden, and harms imposed on parties who never consented. Law can lag technology, vary by jurisdiction, be weakly enforced, or omit harms that remain foreseeable. Friedman's own formulation includes law and ethical custom, while contemporary business ethics treats shareholder and stakeholder ends as constrained by morality [1][2]. "Legal" and "profitable" are therefore inputs to moral analysis, not complete verdicts.

A further governance problem arises when profit is defined too narrowly. Quarterly earnings, stock price, accounting return, and long-run firm value are different objectives. Incentives tied to a short horizon can reward transferring cost to employees, suppliers, customers, taxpayers, or future owners. Jensen's enlightened approach recognizes that durable value depends on stakeholder relationships, but a value criterion still needs constraints on rights, deception, coercion, corruption, and catastrophic risk [4]. The author's synthesis is bounded value creation: pursue sustainable economic value aggressively within explicit moral side constraints and subject unresolved trade-offs to accountable governance.

### Stakeholders Have Different Claims, Not One Undifferentiated Interest

A stakeholder is not merely anyone who expresses an opinion about a company. Normative relevance can arise from rights, exposure to risk, contribution, dependency, a promise or contract, lack of exit, or the firm's exercise of power. Employees may have claims grounded in safe work, non-discrimination, voice, and promised compensation. Customers may have claims grounded in safety, truthful information, privacy, and fair dealing. Suppliers may have claims against opportunistic changes, impossible lead times, or purchasing terms that make compliant production infeasible. Communities may have claims concerning pollution, land, public services, cultural survival, and emergency risk. Shareholders have claims grounded in governance rights, disclosure, and entrusted capital [3][5][6].

These claims are not automatically commensurable. A small consumer convenience cannot simply cancel a severe labour-rights violation because both appear in one stakeholder score. Rights can operate as constraints, while welfare and efficiency govern choices within those constraints. Distribution also matters: an aggregate benefit may be produced by concentrating irreversible harm on a group with little voice. Stakeholder management therefore requires classification before balancing. The decision record should state which claims are rights, obligations, risks, preferences, or benefits; who bears each; and which claims are treated as non-negotiable.

Stakeholder theory also creates a representation question. Managers cannot infer every affected interest from internal data, and the most vulnerable stakeholders often possess the least bargaining power. Legitimate process may require worker representation, community consultation, independent expertise, customer research, grievance channels, or participation by groups exposed through supply chains [5][6][7]. Consultation is not consent, and hearing a stakeholder is not proof that the final decision is justified. Its function is to correct information asymmetry, expose distribution, and require reasons that affected people can contest.

### Duties, Consequences, Virtues, and Legitimacy Ask Different Questions

Deontological analysis asks whether conduct respects persons as ends and complies with duties such as honesty, promise keeping, non-discrimination, and non-complicity. It is strongest where aggregate calculation would otherwise permit deception or sacrifice of an unconsenting minority. In business, this framework supports truthful marketing, informed consent, safe work, respect for privacy, fair process, and refusal to treat human rights as optional costs [5][7].

Consequential analysis asks who benefits, who is harmed, how likely and severe each outcome is, whether effects are reversible, and what alternatives exist. It is necessary for product safety, environmental risk, public health, technology deployment, restructuring, and supply-chain decisions because rules alone cannot rank every uncertain outcome. Its danger is false aggregation: uncertain benefits to many can be used rhetorically to erase concentrated rights violations. A defensible analysis separates expected welfare, distribution, tail risk, and rights constraints rather than hiding them in one net score.

Virtue ethics asks what kind of organization and decision-maker the practice cultivates. Integrity, courage, justice, practical wisdom, honesty, and temperance concern stable dispositions, not isolated compliance. This lens explains why a code of conduct can coexist with misconduct: employees learn the operative values from promotion, budget, target, and retaliation decisions. Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales found that proclaimed values were unrelated to performance in their sample, while employees' perceptions that top managers were trustworthy and ethical were associated with stronger performance [10]. The result is observational, but it supports distinguishing advertised character from experienced character.

Legitimacy asks why private actors may exercise power over conditions that others cannot readily avoid. A large employer, platform, lender, pharmaceutical company, utility, or dominant buyer may shape opportunity, speech, health, privacy, or regional livelihoods. Formal ownership does not answer whether that power is justified. Transparency, participation, consistent rules, appeal, independent review, and remedy can make power more answerable, while secrecy and unilateral discretion weaken its claim to legitimacy. The author's synthesis is that corporate ethics becomes more demanding as power, dependency, information asymmetry, and irreversibility increase.

### Complicity Extends Responsibility Through Business Relationships

A firm may not directly employ a harmed worker or physically release a pollutant, yet its purchasing practices, specifications, financing, product design, data access, or commercial leverage can contribute to another entity's conduct. The UN Guiding Principles distinguish impacts a business causes, contributes to, or is directly linked to through business relationships; the appropriate response differs with involvement and leverage [5]. The OECD Guidelines likewise apply risk-based due diligence to operations, products, services, supply chains, and other business relationships while warning against shifting responsibility away from the entity that caused the impact [6].

Complicity is therefore not resolved by inserting a supplier code into a contract. A buyer that demands unrealistically low prices and short lead times while requiring costly safety standards creates contradictory incentives. A technology provider that can foresee high-risk misuse cannot rely only on a general acceptable-use clause. A financier can be linked to harm without operating the project. The morally relevant questions are whether the firm enabled, facilitated, incentivized, benefited from, ignored, or retained leverage over the conduct, and whether disengagement would prevent harm or merely abandon affected people [5][6].

The author's synthesis is that leverage creates a duty of use, not a guarantee of control. A firm should identify where its decisions alter another actor's feasible choices, set expectations before contracting, preserve audit and information rights, align price and timing with compliance, escalate proportionately, cooperate in remediation, and disengage responsibly when continued connection cannot be justified. The duty becomes stronger when harm is severe, the relationship is central, warning signals are repeated, and the firm helped create the conditions.

### Due Diligence Is a Decision Process, Not a Warranty

Risk-based due diligence asks the enterprise to embed responsible conduct in policy and management systems; identify and assess actual and potential adverse impacts; cease, prevent, or mitigate them; track implementation and results; communicate how impacts are addressed; and provide or cooperate in remediation where appropriate [5][6]. The object is risk to people and the environment, not only financial risk to the company. This reverses a common enterprise-risk lens: a labour abuse can be morally material even if no investor is likely to notice it.

Due diligence is ongoing because products, suppliers, conflict conditions, technology, and affected populations change [5][6]. It should be proportionate to severity and likelihood, but severity can justify action even when probability is uncertain. The process also requires engagement with potentially affected groups and credible information beyond management's preferred channels. A checklist completed by the same unit rewarded for launch or sourcing speed does not provide independent challenge.

Due diligence is not a warranty that no harm will occur. Its adequacy depends on the quality of inquiry, integration, action, tracking, communication, and correction. Nor is disclosure a substitute for performance. A report can improve comparability and accountability, but it can also become selective narrative unless definitions, denominators, methods, boundaries, and negative outcomes are visible. The author's synthesis is that the strongest evidence is a closed loop: a risk was identified, authority and resources were assigned, behavior changed, outcomes were measured, affected people could challenge the result, and unresolved harm triggered remedy or escalation.

### Voice, Whistleblowing, and Remedy Convert Values Into Accountability

Employees and contractors often observe misconduct before boards, regulators, or investors. An ethical system therefore needs accessible reporting channels, confidentiality, anti-retaliation protection, independent investigation, escalation outside the implicated chain of command, and feedback to the reporter. Stubben and Welch analyzed nearly two million internal reports submitted to more than 1,000 publicly traded U.S. firms and found that report volume was associated with fewer and lower amounts of government fines and material lawsuits [12]. Because the study reports associations rather than randomized effects, the defensible implication is not that more complaints automatically cause better outcomes; it is that silence is not evidence of integrity and active use can reveal an open information channel.

Whistleblowing is only one form of voice. Worker committees, unions, customer complaints, supplier escalation, community grievance mechanisms, ombuds functions, and independent audits can surface different harms [5][6][7]. Their credibility depends on whether concerns reach an actor with authority, whether evidence is preserved, whether retaliation is detected, and whether outcomes are explained. A hotline routed back to the manager named in the complaint is a communication channel without accountability.

Remedy completes responsibility. Prevention is preferable, but a firm that caused or contributed to harm may owe restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, apology, reinstatement, guarantees of non-repetition, or another form suited to the injury [5]. Remedy should not be conditioned on public silence or designed only to extinguish liability. The author's synthesis is that a company has not learned from harm until the affected party receives a credible response and the organizational mechanism that produced the harm has changed.

## Evidence

### Close-Call Shareholder Proposals Provide Quasi-Experimental Evidence

Caroline Flammer studied corporate social-responsibility proposals that passed or failed by a small margin around the shareholder-vote threshold. The regression-discontinuity design treats firms immediately on either side of the threshold as more comparable than firms with widely different vote shares, providing a quasi-experiment on proposal adoption [9]. The study found positive announcement returns and improved subsequent accounting performance for close-call proposals that passed, with increases in labour productivity and sales growth among the investigated channels [9].

The method supports a bounded instrumental finding: some marginal CSR proposals approved in close votes were value enhancing in the studied setting. Flammer explicitly cautions that close proposals differ from non-close proposals, so the result does not establish that every CSR expenditure benefits shareholders [9]. It also does not establish the normative proposition that firms should respect stakeholders only when an event study predicts a gain. The evidence weakens a blanket claim that responsibility necessarily destroys value; it does not turn market value into the source of moral duty.

### Employee-Reported Integrity Differs From Advertised Values

Guiso, Sapienza, and Zingales conducted two related analyses. They coded advertised values for S&P 500 firms, then separately examined confidential Great Place to Work data for U.S. applicants from 2007 through 2011. In the survey dataset, 1,072 organizations completed the Culture Audit; after exclusions, the final for-profit sample comprised 679 companies, 1,367 firm-year observations, and 410,521 full-time employee observations [10]. Advertised values showed little relation to financial performance, while perceived managerial integrity was positively associated with Tobin's q and, less consistently, return on sales [10].

The study is observational and cannot eliminate every selection, halo, or reverse-causality explanation. Its contribution is therefore diagnostic rather than dispositive. It measures the difference between saying integrity and being experienced as acting with integrity. That difference supports a governance rule: culture evidence should come from protected employee experience, observed decisions, promotion patterns, and responses to bad news, not only from codes and public statements.

### Meta-Analysis Finds a Positive but Heterogeneous Business Case

Orlitzky, Schmidt, and Rynes conducted a meta-analysis of 52 prior studies containing 33,878 observations on corporate social or environmental performance and corporate financial performance [11]. They reported a positive average association, with results moderated by how social performance and financial performance were measured; accounting measures and reputation indices showed different relationships from market measures and other indicators [11].

Meta-analysis improves precision across a fragmented literature, but aggregation does not automatically cure endogeneity or inconsistent constructs in the underlying studies. Financially successful firms may have more resources for social initiatives, responsible practices may improve performance, and both may reflect management quality or industry conditions. The author's interpretation is narrow: the literature does not support a universal trade-off in which responsibility is necessarily a cost, but it also does not justify selling every ethical duty through a promised financial return.

### Internal Reporting Systems Reveal the Difference Between Voice and Silence

Stubben and Welch examined 1,992,334 reports filed with 1,135 publicly traded U.S. firms through a major internal-reporting provider [12]. They described report content and management review, documented substantial variation across firms and industries, and found that companies encouraging internal reporting received more reports. Higher report volume was associated with fewer and lower amounts of subsequent government fines and material lawsuits [12].

The evidence is associational and report counts depend on firm size, workforce, channel design, awareness, trust, and the types of concerns included. A low count can mean few problems, but it can also mean fear, ignorance, or futility. The practical finding is that boards should not reward a declining complaint number without examining substantiation, retaliation, closure quality, recurrence, time to investigation, and external outcomes. Voice is evidence about the system; suppressing it improves the metric while worsening knowledge.

### The Business Roundtable Pledge Tests Voluntary Commitment

In 2019, the Business Roundtable announced a statement signed by 181 chief executives committing to customers, employees, suppliers, communities, and shareholders [13]. The statement was a primary-source declaration of stakeholder purpose, not an enforceable allocation of rights. Bebchuk and Tallarita later reviewed hand-collected governance documents for 128 U.S. public signatories, including guidelines, bylaws, proxy statements, and responses to stakeholder proposals [14]. They found little evidence that the pledge changed governance commitments, board incentives, or the treatment of stakeholder proposals and concluded that the statement was largely unaccompanied by material governance change [14].

The study is a document analysis centered on U.S. public companies and does not prove that no operating practice changed anywhere. It does provide a demanding test of institutionalization: if a stated purpose is meant to govern, one should expect evidence in board-approved policy, decision rights, executive incentives, disclosure, stakeholder voice, or remedy. The gap between pledge and governance supports the author's accountability principle that voluntary language is weak evidence until it changes who can demand what from whom.

### Bangladesh Factory Safety Shows the Value of Multi-Party Enforcement

After the Rana Plaza collapse, public and private initiatives inspected thousands of export-oriented ready-made-garment factories in Bangladesh for structural, fire, and electrical hazards. An ILO progress report stated that 3,780 export-oriented factories had been inspected by December 2015 and reported remediation progress of 84 percent for Accord factories and 91 percent for Alliance factories by 2018, while also identifying the need to transfer capacity and responsibility to national institutions [15]. The initiatives combined harmonized inspections, public summary reporting, corrective plans, brand participation, repeated follow-up, and worker-reporting channels rather than relying only on supplier promises [15].

These figures are program reports, not a randomized estimate of prevented deaths, and completion percentages depend on definitions and reporting systems. The author's synthesis is that the case illustrates an institutional mechanism: global buyers can convert leverage into inspection, finance, transparency, worker voice, and time-bound correction. It also exposes limits. Private initiatives cannot permanently substitute for capable public enforcement, and responsibility is incomplete if remediation depends on purchasing practices that continue to reward unsafe cost cutting.

### Evidence Supports Systems, Not Moral Automation

Across the studies, responsible conduct can coincide with value, experienced integrity matters more than advertised values, active reporting can reveal organizational learning, and voluntary pledges can fail to alter governance [9][10][11][12][14]. The evidence does not identify one universal business case or one score that resolves conflicts among rights, welfare, distribution, and legitimacy. Empirical research answers whether a mechanism worked under specified conditions; normative analysis answers whether the purpose, burdens, and residual harms are justified.

The author's synthesis is that strong evidence changes the form of business ethics. It moves analysis from intention to mechanism: close-call adoption, employee experience, aggregated outcomes, actual report use, document-level governance change, inspection, and remediation. Ethical claims should therefore state the affected population, decision process, comparison, time horizon, outcome, uncertainty, and remedy. A corporation cannot validate moral responsibility by citing reputation alone, and a critic cannot establish irresponsibility from one adverse event without tracing control, contribution, response, and recurrence.

## Implications

### For Boards and Owners

Boards should translate corporate purpose into an explicit hierarchy of objectives, constraints, and escalation rules. Economic viability and long-run value belong in the objective; human rights, truthful dealing, anti-corruption, safety, and other justified limits belong in the constraints; unresolved conflicts belong in a process that identifies affected parties, independent evidence, and the actor authorized to decide [4][5][6]. A purpose statement without this architecture expands rhetoric more than accountability.

Oversight should begin with the firm's severe impacts rather than with a generic list of fashionable issues. The board needs reliable information about workforce conditions, product and customer harm, supply-chain pressure, environmental exposure, corruption, technology misuse, complaints, retaliation, and remediation. It should test whether incentive plans reward outcomes inconsistent with stated duties and whether management can suppress bad news. The author's synthesis is that the worst governance design combines broad stakeholder promises with concentrated managerial discretion and no stakeholder enforcement rights.

Owners should distinguish long-run value creation from permission to externalize costs. Engagement can ask whether the board has mapped cause, contribution, direct linkage, leverage, and remedy; whether capital allocation funds controls needed to honor commitments; and whether reported progress includes failures and denominators [5][6]. Divestment can protect a portfolio from exposure, but it does not necessarily protect affected people. Where voice and leverage can reduce harm, exit should be compared with responsible engagement rather than treated as automatic moral cleansing.

### For Executives and Managers

Executives should make moral expectations operational before a crisis. This requires decision rights, budgets, expertise, pre-launch review, supplier terms, product safeguards, protected escalation, investigation standards, and stop authority. Policies must specify who owns a risk and what happens when schedule, sales, or cost targets conflict with a constraint. The evidence on proclaimed versus perceived values indicates that employees infer culture from these decisions, not from the vocabulary of the code [10].

Managers need a repeatable decision record. State the business objective; identify affected stakeholders and rights; estimate benefits, harms, distribution, uncertainty, and reversibility; test complicity through incentives and relationships; identify alternatives; document dissent; assign monitoring; and define remedy. The process should be more demanding when the affected party lacks exit, the firm holds specialized information, the harm is irreversible, or the decision scales through software or supply chains. This is the author's synthesis of the normative and due-diligence frameworks [1][5][6].

Compensation should not place employees in a position where success requires violating the policy the company publicly endorses. A sales target that presupposes deceptive selling, a sourcing target incompatible with safe production, or a launch date that excludes adequate testing is an organizational instruction even if no manager writes "break the rule." Ethical leadership therefore includes revising the target, absorbing the cost, or abandoning the activity. Tone at the top matters only when resource allocation and promotion decisions make the tone credible.

### For Employees and Professionals

Employees retain moral agency inside a hierarchy, but their responsibility should be assessed with attention to authority, information, coercion, and retaliation risk. A junior employee may be unable to redesign the system yet still possess a duty to avoid direct deception, preserve evidence, seek advice, and use credible escalation where proportionate and safe. Licensed and technical professionals may have stronger obligations when specialized knowledge reveals a risk that ordinary decision-makers cannot evaluate.

Organizations should not shift the burden of integrity onto courageous individuals. The empirical reporting evidence supports treating employee voice as a governance resource, while the due-diligence frameworks require engagement and correction [5][6][12]. Multiple channels, anonymity, independent investigation, anti-retaliation monitoring, board access, and feedback reduce the dependence of ethics on one supervisor. A reporter should not have to choose between silence and career destruction for the company to learn what its own system is doing.

### For Suppliers and Global Value Chains

Buyer codes should be matched by commercial terms that make compliant performance feasible. Lead time, order volatility, price, forecasting, design changes, audit duplication, and payment delay can each alter a supplier's safety and labour choices. The author's synthesis is that a company contributes to a foreseeable violation when its purchasing model materially incentivizes the violation and it continues the model after warning. Audit alone cannot correct a contradiction designed into the contract.

Risk-based due diligence should prioritize severity rather than inspect every supplier identically [5][6]. High-risk operations require worker-informed assessment, independent verification, transparent corrective plans, financing or pricing support where the buyer helped create the constraint, and escalation tied to outcomes. Termination is appropriate when the supplier refuses correction or continued connection cannot be justified, but abrupt exit can also remove income and leverage. Responsible disengagement plans for affected workers and communities rather than treating contract termination as the end of responsibility.

The Bangladesh factory-safety experience illustrates both the power and limit of coordinated buyer leverage [15]. Coordinated commitments, standardized inspection, disclosure, worker reporting, and repeated remediation can improve conditions that supplier assurances alone do not test. Durable responsibility still requires capable local institutions, lawful worker organization, and purchasing practices consistent with safe production. The ethical unit is the system that produces the garment, not only the legal boundary of the brand.

### For Customers, Communities, and Technology

Customers are owed products and services whose safety, claims, privacy practices, and foreseeable uses can be justified. Disclosure is necessary but insufficient when the customer cannot understand, negotiate, or avoid the risk. A consent screen cannot legitimize an exploitative design merely because the user clicked it. The stronger the information asymmetry and dependency, the stronger the firm's duty to test comprehension, reduce avoidable harm, and provide correction and appeal.

Communities often bear effects that contracts do not price: pollution, congestion, displacement, emergency risk, tax-base changes, and dependence on one employer. Consequential analysis should include these externalities, while legitimacy requires participation and remedy where private decisions reshape shared conditions [5][6]. Community investment can be beneficial, but it must not purchase silence or substitute for prevention. The author's synthesis is that a benefit offered by the firm does not cancel an unrelated right or harm without the affected party's justified agreement.

Technology expands both scale and distance. A design decision can affect millions, while supply chains and automated systems obscure who made the choice. The OECD Guidelines expressly extend due-diligence expectations to technology, data, products, services, and their use [6]. Governance should therefore examine intended purpose, foreseeable misuse, affected groups, data provenance, distribution of errors, human authority, monitoring, and shutdown or remedy. "The system decided" is not an answer to moral responsibility; it is a sign that responsibility was poorly allocated.

### For Regulators and Public Institutions

Voluntary standards can articulate expectations, diffuse practices, and operate across jurisdictions, but the Business Roundtable evidence shows why declarations without governance change can remain symbolic [13][14]. Regulation becomes relevant where harms are externalized, victims lack bargaining power, firms face collective-action problems, or responsible companies are disadvantaged by competitors that cut costs through abuse. Public rules can create common minimums, investigation powers, sanctions, disclosure standards, and enforceable remedies.

Regulation is not automatically effective. A formal duty without capacity, data, independence, protection for reporters, or access to remedy can reproduce the same gap as a corporate code. Rules should identify the responsible entity, required process, prohibited outcome, evidence standard, supervisory authority, stakeholder standing, remedy, and treatment of business relationships. The author's synthesis is that public law should set enforceable floors while due diligence supplies an adaptive process for risks that detailed rules cannot anticipate.

Business ethics should remain distinct from legal analysis. The ethical question is why a duty is justified and how power should be used; the legal question is what a jurisdiction currently requires and how that requirement is enforced. Law can properly settle some conflicts through democratic authority, but compliance cannot prove that every affected person was treated justly. Conversely, corporate leaders should not use private moral preference to displace legitimate public rules. The boundary is disciplined complementarity: ethics criticizes and guides conduct beyond the legal floor, while law supplies public authorization and enforceability.

### A Practical Accountability Standard

The author's synthesis is an eight-part standard for corporate moral responsibility. First, define the legitimate purpose and economic objective. Second, identify every materially affected group and distinguish rights, duties, risks, preferences, and benefits. Third, map organizational and individual control, contribution, knowledge, and leverage. Fourth, apply moral constraints before aggregating consequences. Fifth, compare feasible alternatives, including non-action, delay, redesign, and responsible exit. Sixth, assign authority, resources, independent challenge, and protected voice. Seventh, track outcomes with measures that cannot be improved merely by suppressing reports or moving harm outside the legal entity. Eighth, provide remedy and alter the mechanism that produced the harm [1][5][6][12].

This standard does not promise one formula for every conflict. It makes disagreement auditable. A shareholder advocate can ask whether managers have a determinate objective; a stakeholder advocate can ask whether affected interests have standing; a rights theorist can test constraints; a consequentialist can test outcomes and distribution; a virtue ethicist can test culture; and a legitimacy theorist can test voice and authority. The decision is strongest when it can answer all six without changing the facts or hiding the burden in a slogan.

The final implication is that corporate responsibility is neither unlimited benevolence nor profit subject only to law. It is disciplined answerability for how organized power creates value, allocates risk, shapes other actors' choices, and responds when those choices harm people. Economic viability bears on whether an enterprise can continue; legal compliance addresses whether it met an applicable public rule; ethical responsibility asks the remaining question: whether the way it continued can be justified to those who bore its consequences.
