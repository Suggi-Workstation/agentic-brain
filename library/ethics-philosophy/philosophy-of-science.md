# Philosophy of Science -- No Single Rule Justifies Science, but Organized Criticism Makes It Reliable

Philosophy of science asks what distinguishes scientific inquiry from non-science, how evidence can support theories that outrun observation, and what it means for science to progress. Its central lesson is not that one rule guarantees truth, but that testability, comparative explanation, historical learning, and organized criticism together make scientific knowledge more reliable while keeping every conclusion open to revision [1, 2, 10].

## Background

Philosophy of science emerged from a problem internal to knowledge itself: observations are finite, while scientific laws usually make general claims that extend beyond what anyone has observed. An investigator can examine many instances of a phenomenon, but no finite series of favorable cases deductively proves a universal law. The history of scientific method therefore includes repeated efforts to explain how observation, experiment, mathematics, and inference can justify conclusions without turning provisional evidence into certainty [1, 6].

Early modern accounts emphasized induction and controlled experience. Francis Bacon opposed reliance on inherited authority and promoted the disciplined collection of observations, while later philosophers attempted to formulate rules by which repeated instances could support general conclusions. David Hume's problem of induction exposed a basic difficulty: an inference from observed regularities to unobserved cases assumes that relevant regularities will continue, but experience cannot justify that assumption without using induction again. Twentieth-century philosophy of science inherited this problem and shifted much of its attention from proving theories to comparing, testing, and revising them [1, 3].

Logical empiricists sought to reconstruct scientific knowledge through relations between theoretical statements and observation. Their programs differed, but confirmation became a central concern: what logical or probabilistic relation makes evidence count for or against a hypothesis? Confirmation theory remains the study of non-deductive evidential support. It includes hypothetico-deductive accounts, on which successful consequences support a hypothesis, and Bayesian accounts, on which evidence changes the probability assigned to competing hypotheses. Neither approach turns empirical support into deductive proof [6].

Karl Popper proposed a sharper response to the problem of induction. Instead of asking how repeated observations verify a universal theory, he emphasized the logical asymmetry between confirmation and refutation. A universal claim can survive many favorable tests without being proved, while an observation that genuinely conflicts with it can show that something in the tested system is wrong. Popper made falsifiability a demarcation criterion: a scientific theory must expose itself to possible observations that conflict with it. He also distinguished falsifiability, a logical property, from an actual methodological decision to reject a theory [2, 3].

Popper's framework captured the importance of risky prediction and criticism, but historical scientific practice created difficulties for a simple theory-by-theory account. Scientists commonly retain a central theory when an experiment produces an anomaly because the failure may lie in an auxiliary hypothesis, an instrument, an initial condition, or the interpretation of the result. Pierre Duhem had argued that hypotheses in physics are tested only as part of larger theoretical systems. W. V. O. Quine later generalized this holist challenge, helping to establish what is now called the Duhem-Quine problem of underdetermination [6, 7].

Thomas Kuhn changed the unit of analysis from isolated hypotheses to historically situated scientific communities. In The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, he described mature science as long periods of normal science conducted within a paradigm or disciplinary matrix, interrupted by crises and revolutionary change. Normal science is productive partly because researchers usually treat their framework as given and solve the puzzles it defines. An anomaly does not automatically overthrow the paradigm; only a deeper crisis and a viable alternative can make wholesale change possible [4, 9].

Imre Lakatos tried to preserve rational comparison while accommodating the historical lessons of Kuhn and the holism emphasized by Duhem. His methodology of scientific research programmes treats inquiry as sequences of related theories. A programme contains a relatively stable hard core, a revisable protective belt of auxiliary assumptions, and heuristics that guide further work. The relevant question is not whether one result instantly falsifies the hard core, but whether revisions generate novel, corroborated predictions or merely protect the programme after repeated failures [5].

These debates also changed the meaning of scientific progress. If theories are not conclusively verified, if tests involve auxiliary assumptions, and if standards can change during revolutions, progress cannot be identified simply with the accumulation of proven facts. Philosophers instead debate several candidates: increasing predictive and explanatory success, solving more significant problems, approaching truth, improving empirical adequacy, or sustaining progressively successful research programmes. The realism-antirealism dispute asks whether the success of mature science warrants belief in the observable and unobservable entities described by its best theories, or only confidence in their empirical performance [8, 9].

The modern field consequently studies both logic and practice. It examines the formal relation between evidence and hypotheses, the historical development of disciplines, the role of instruments and models, the values used in theory choice, and the institutions that distribute criticism. Scientific objectivity is no longer adequately described as the absence of all judgment. It is also analyzed through procedures that expose claims to independent scrutiny, manage conflicts among epistemic and social values, and make errors detectable [1, 10].

## Core Concepts

### Demarcation Is a Problem, Not a One-Word Test

The demarcation problem asks where science ends and non-science or pseudoscience begins. These categories must be kept distinct. Mathematics, logic, ethics, and metaphysics can be non-scientific without pretending to be empirical science; pseudoscience typically presents itself as scientific while failing important standards of inquiry. A criterion that excludes every non-empirical discipline does not by itself identify deception or defective inquiry, and a criterion that identifies one defect may not capture every way a practice can be unreliable [2].

Popper's falsifiability criterion remains powerful because it asks a concrete question: what conceivable observation would conflict with this claim? A theory that permits every possible outcome gives investigators no empirical way to discriminate between its success and failure. Falsifiability also rewards informative risk. A precise theory rules out more possible observations than a vague one, so its survival under severe tests carries more methodological significance. Popper opposed the accumulation of agreeable examples when a theory had been insulated from genuine criticism [2, 3].

Falsifiability is not sufficient for good science and is not identical to falsification in practice. An empirically testable claim can be poorly supported, methodologically careless, or embedded in a degenerating programme. Conversely, a failed prediction does not specify which assumption in a complex test should be rejected. Scientists must decide whether the target theory, an auxiliary model, an instrument calibration, a background assumption, or the reported observation is responsible. Popper's mature account therefore relied on methodological rules and critical practice, not on a mechanical instruction to discard a theory after one contrary datum [3, 7].

Contemporary treatments often regard demarcation as multidimensional. Relevant features include empirical testability, responsiveness to contrary evidence, methodological transparency, connection to established knowledge, willingness to revise, and participation in a community capable of criticism. The author's synthesis is that this plural approach should not be mistaken for permissiveness. Its purpose is to diagnose different failure modes rather than force every failure into one logical category [1, 2].

### Confirmation Compares Expectations

Evidence confirms a hypothesis when the evidence gives some reason to increase confidence in it, but several different relations can produce that support. In a hypothetico-deductive pattern, a hypothesis combined with auxiliary assumptions yields an observable consequence; observing that consequence counts in its favor. The weakness of a naive version is that many hypotheses can entail the same result when paired with suitable assumptions, and a successful consequence may have been easy to accommodate after it was already known [6, 7].

Predictivist accounts assign special weight to novel or independently generated predictions. Evidence that was not used to construct a theory can provide a more demanding test because the theory had less opportunity to be fitted to the result. Yet novelty is not the only epistemic virtue. Old evidence can strongly support a new theory when the theory explains why the evidence occurs, unifies previously separate findings, or derives a precise result without being tailored to it. Confirmation therefore depends on the relation among hypothesis, alternatives, auxiliaries, and evidence, not simply on whether a result came before or after a proposal [6].

Bayesian confirmation represents rational uncertainty with probabilities. Evidence confirms a hypothesis when it is more expected under that hypothesis than under relevant alternatives, causing the hypothesis's posterior probability to exceed its prior probability after updating. This framework clarifies why surprising evidence can be powerful and why the same evidence can produce different posteriors from different priors. It does not automatically choose priors, determine the relevant alternatives, or eliminate disputes about model specification [6].

Confirmation is contrastive. A result that fits one theory matters less if every serious rival predicts it equally well. A result that sharply separates alternatives matters more. The author's synthesis is that good testing should therefore record not only whether evidence agrees with a preferred theory, but which live competitors were considered, what each predicted, and which auxiliary assumptions connected those predictions to observation. That record makes evidential support inspectable rather than rhetorical [6, 7].

### The Duhem-Quine Problem Makes Tests Holistic

A theory rarely meets observation alone. To derive a prediction, researchers also rely on assumptions about initial conditions, measurement, statistical models, instrument behavior, sample construction, and other background theories. When prediction and observation conflict, logic identifies a problem in the conjunction of those commitments but does not isolate the guilty member. Duhem developed this point for physical theory, and Quine expanded confirmational holism into a broader account of knowledge [7].

Holism explains why a single anomaly does not rationally compel immediate abandonment. Revising an auxiliary assumption can be legitimate when there is independent reason to doubt it or when the revision produces new tests. The same logical flexibility can also shield a failing programme through ad hoc changes made only to absorb contrary evidence. The methodological challenge is therefore not to prohibit auxiliary revision but to distinguish fertile revision from rescue without independent payoff [5, 7].

Underdetermination has more than one form. Holist underdetermination concerns which component of a tested system should change after a failure. Contrastive underdetermination concerns rival theories that fit the available evidence while making different claims, especially about unobservable reality. The historical problem of unconceived alternatives adds that investigators may compare only the theories they have managed to imagine, while a future theory could explain the same evidence better. These problems support fallibilism, but they do not establish that all theories are equally warranted [7, 8].

The author's synthesis is that underdetermination converts theory appraisal from an isolated verdict into a portfolio of tests. Independent instruments, different experimental designs, cross-domain predictions, replication, and successful novel applications can constrain different auxiliaries. No single result removes every alternative, but convergent evidence can make the set of credible alternatives much narrower [7, 10].

### Kuhn Explains Normal Science and Revolutionary Change

Kuhn used paradigm in several related ways, later preferring the more precise idea of a disciplinary matrix: shared symbolic generalizations, models, values, and exemplars that organize a scientific community. Exemplars are solved problems that train researchers to recognize legitimate questions and solutions. A paradigm is therefore more than a proposition. It is a working inheritance that shapes perception, standards, instruments, and professional judgment [4].

Normal science is puzzle-solving under that inherited framework. Researchers extend measurements, improve agreement between theory and observation, and articulate the paradigm rather than continuously attempting to destroy it. This commitment can look dogmatic, but Kuhn argued that sustained work on difficult puzzles is possible because scientists do not reopen foundational questions after every setback. The same stability that enables depth can later delay recognition that anomalies are structural rather than local [4].

A crisis begins when important anomalies resist ordinary repair, especially when an alternative framework can reorganize the field. Revolutionary change alters not only answers but also concepts, standards, and the classification of problems. Kuhn's idea of incommensurability does not mean that rival paradigms cannot communicate at all. It identifies the absence of a complete neutral translation or algorithm that determines theory choice independently of the competing frameworks [4, 9].

Kuhn did not reduce scientific change to arbitrary conversion. Accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness can guide theory choice, but scientists can legitimately weight these values differently. Progress can also be understood through increased problem-solving capacity and predictive precision. His account shows why history matters to philosophy: an adequate norm of scientific rationality must describe how successful inquiry actually survives anomalies and changes its organizing commitments [4, 9].

### Lakatos Evaluates Sequences Rather Than Snapshots

Lakatos's research programme joins a hard core of central commitments to a protective belt of revisable hypotheses. A negative heuristic tells researchers which core commitments not to abandon during routine work; a positive heuristic proposes directions for extending the programme and dealing with problems. This architecture makes persistence rational when premature rejection would destroy a developing framework before its resources were explored [5].

The critical distinction is between progressive and degenerating problem shifts. A programme is theoretically progressive when a new version has excess empirical content, including novel predictions. It is empirically progressive when at least some of that content is corroborated. A programme degenerates when revisions mainly accommodate already known failures, reduce testability, or repeatedly promise future success without delivering new corroborated results [5].

This standard reframes falsification as competition through time. A programme may rationally survive anomalies if it continues to generate discoveries, while an apparently tidy programme may deserve less confidence if its successes are retrospective adjustments. Abandonment also depends on whether a more progressive alternative exists. Lakatos therefore offers a middle position between instant Popperian rejection and a purely sociological description of paradigm allegiance [5].

Lakatos does not supply a mechanical deadline for declaring degeneration terminal. The absence of such an algorithm leaves room for judgment and historical disagreement. The author's assessment is that the framework is most useful as an audit trail: record which changes were made, whether they were independently motivated, what new predictions followed, and whether those predictions succeeded. It does not eliminate judgment, but it makes the basis of judgment more explicit [5].

### Realism and Instrumentalism Disagree About What Success Warrants

Scientific realism is commonly characterized by commitments in three dimensions. Semantically, theoretical claims are interpreted literally rather than merely as calculation devices. Metaphysically, the world described by science exists independently of investigators. Epistemically, successful mature theories are taken to be true or approximately true, including at least some claims about unobservable entities and processes [8].

Realists argue that the predictive and explanatory success of science would be difficult to explain if its central theoretical structures did not track reality. This family of arguments is often called the no-miracles argument. Realism can also explain why instruments designed through theory allow intervention in entities that are not directly observable with unaided senses. These arguments seek to move from sustained success to warranted belief, not to infallibility [8].

Antirealist positions restrict what success justifies. Instrumentalist approaches treat theories primarily as tools for organizing observations and producing predictions. Constructive empiricist approaches can accept a theory as empirically adequate without believing every claim it makes about unobservable entities. Historical changes in successful theories motivate the pessimistic induction: earlier theories achieved important successes while making claims later rejected, so current success alone may not establish approximate truth [8].

Underdetermination sharpens the dispute because empirically equivalent rivals can agree about observations while disagreeing about hidden structure. Realists answer in several ways, including inference to the best explanation, selective commitment to the parts responsible for success, and appeals to evidence that eventually distinguishes rivals. No response has ended the debate. The lasting result is a disciplined distinction between using a model, accepting its empirical adequacy, and believing its ontology [7, 8].

### Objectivity Is Achieved Through Structured Criticism

The older ideal of objectivity as a view from nowhere treats knowledge as free from perspective and value. Philosophy of science has shown why this is too simple. Observations depend on instruments and background concepts; theory choice uses epistemic values that can conflict; and decisions about acceptable evidence can involve judgments about the consequences of error. These facts create risks of bias but do not imply that evidence is powerless [10].

Objectivity can also be institutional and procedural. Explicit methods, accessible data, criticism from differently situated investigators, conflict-of-interest disclosure, independent replication, and correction mechanisms make claims less dependent on one person's preferences. The author's synthesis is that objectivity is better understood as an achievement of error-detecting arrangements than as a psychological state of perfect neutrality. The relevant question is not whether judgment entered the process, but whether the judgments and their consequences can be examined and challenged [10].

## Evidence

### Popper's Contrast Between Risky and Accommodating Theories

Popper's demarcation proposal grew from comparing theories that exposed themselves to failure with systems that appeared able to explain every outcome. His method was logical and comparative: identify what observations a theory prohibits, then examine whether supporters accept adverse results as genuine criticism or reinterpret every result as confirmation. Relativity supplied his positive model because it generated specific observational consequences that could have conflicted with the theory; psychoanalytic and historicist systems supplied negative models when their interpretations expanded to absorb contrary cases [2, 3].

The finding was not that one successful observation proves a theory. Popper described survival under severe testing as corroboration, a provisional status rather than verified truth. The case supports two narrower conclusions: empirical content increases when a theory rules out possible results, and a practice loses critical force when no result can count against it. Later criticism showed that falsifiability alone does not settle actual theory rejection, but the requirement to identify potential failure remains a durable test of whether a claim takes empirical risk [2, 3].

### Duhem's Analysis of Physical Testing

Duhem analyzed experiments in physics to show that a test joins the target hypothesis to auxiliary theories and assumptions. The method was a logical reconstruction of prediction: trace the premises required to move from a theoretical claim to an expected observation, then ask what follows when the observation fails. The result is limited. The conjunction is in trouble, but deductive logic does not identify which premise should be changed. Quine later extended this holist insight beyond physics [6, 7].

This finding explains a recurrent feature of laboratory work. Investigators check calibration, sample preparation, background models, data processing, and experimental conditions before treating an anomaly as a refutation of a fundamental theory. Such checks are not automatically evasive because any link in the inferential chain can fail. The same flexibility becomes epistemically dangerous when auxiliaries are altered only after failure and yield no independent prediction. Duhem's analysis therefore supplies evidence against both mechanical falsification and unrestricted accommodation [7].

### Kuhn's Comparative History of Scientific Revolutions

Kuhn examined episodes such as the Copernican, chemical, and relativistic revolutions rather than beginning with an idealized rule of method. His historical method compared textbook narratives of cumulative progress with records of how communities defined problems, treated anomalies, and changed standards. He found a recurring pattern: stable puzzle-solving under a shared matrix, mounting strain around significant anomalies, proliferation of alternatives, and reorganization under a new framework [4, 9].

The historical evidence challenged the idea that scientists normally try to falsify their deepest commitments. During normal science, anomalies are often assigned to the researcher's technique or to unresolved puzzles, while the paradigm is protected. Revolutionary change also cannot be reduced to one decisive experiment because the competing frameworks can classify observations and problems differently. The finding is not that evidence is irrelevant. It is that evidence is interpreted through practices and standards that may themselves change, so rational appraisal includes historical and community-level dimensions [4, 9].

### Lakatos's Reconstruction of Research Programmes

Lakatos used historical sequences including Newtonian mechanics and relativity to test a methodology focused on programmes rather than isolated theories. His method tracked how a stable hard core was surrounded by changing auxiliary hypotheses and asked whether those changes led to novel, corroborated content. This comparison distinguished protective revisions that enabled discovery from retrospective repairs that merely absorbed known trouble [5].

The analysis found that theories commonly regarded as exemplary science would have faced serious anomalies without being immediately discarded. Their rational strength came from the progressive development of a wider programme, not from uninterrupted agreement with every observation. Conversely, formal falsifiability could coexist with degeneration if a programme stopped generating successful novel predictions. Lakatos's evidence supports a temporal and comparative standard: judge whether a line of inquiry is learning faster than its alternatives [5].

### Confirmation Theory Tests Measures of Evidential Support

Work in confirmation theory uses formal counterexamples and probabilistic models to evaluate proposed rules of inference. A simple hypothetico-deductive rule says that evidence confirms a hypothesis when the hypothesis helps entail it, but formal analysis shows that suitable auxiliary assumptions can make weak or irrelevant evidence appear confirmatory. Bayesian models instead compare how probable the evidence is under different hypotheses and update prior probabilities, clarifying the roles of surprise and alternatives [6].

The finding is that no bare count of confirming instances measures support adequately. Evidential force changes with the competing hypotheses, the auxiliary assumptions, prior commitments, and whether a result was independently predicted or fitted after observation. These analyses justify practices such as explicit model comparison and out-of-sample prediction, while also showing why those practices are not universal algorithms. A formal score inherits the assumptions built into the model that produced it [6, 7].

### The Realism Debate Uses the History of Success and Replacement

Arguments about scientific realism treat the history of theory success as evidence in opposing directions. Realists emphasize sustained prediction, explanation, and intervention: if theories repeatedly guide successful action involving entities beyond direct observation, approximate truth appears to explain that record. Critics emphasize that past theories also achieved empirical success before important ontological claims were rejected, and that alternative theories can share observable consequences [8].

The shared method is inference from historical patterns, but the parties select different explanatory targets. The realist asks why science succeeds; the antirealist asks why success has repeatedly coexisted with later replacement. The evidence does not deliver a deductive verdict. It establishes that empirical success, literal truth, and warranted acceptance are different concepts and that movement from one to another requires an additional philosophical argument [8].

Taken together, these cases support a bounded conclusion. Science is not justified by one decisive rule, and historical practice does not fit a simple cycle of conjecture followed by automatic rejection. Reliability instead arises from interacting constraints: claims must encounter possible failure, evidence must discriminate among alternatives, revisions must earn independent success, communities must preserve criticism, and conclusions must remain revisable. This final statement is the author's synthesis of the evidence above [2, 5, 6, 10].

## Implications

### For Scientific Practice

Researchers should separate logical testability from the practical decision to reject a claim. A result can conflict with a prediction while leaving open whether the theory, an auxiliary assumption, or the measurement process failed. The appropriate response is not to ignore the anomaly or to declare immediate revolution, but to map the full inferential chain and design follow-up tests that vary its components. This implication follows from the Popperian demand for risk and the Duhem-Quine account of testing [3, 7].

The author's practical synthesis is a four-part record for consequential tests: state the target claim, list the material auxiliaries, specify the outcomes that would shift confidence, and compare the live alternatives before observing the result. Afterward, classify changes as independently motivated revisions or accommodations introduced because of the outcome. This record does not remove judgment, but it makes selective hindsight and indefinite rescue easier to detect [5, 6].

Research programmes also need evaluation across time. A temporarily unsuccessful line of inquiry may deserve continued work when it has a coherent positive heuristic and produces new testable questions. A prestigious programme may deserve declining confidence when each revision follows known failures and yields no new corroborated result. Funding and review systems can therefore ask whether a programme is generating independently testable content rather than counting publications or isolated positive findings alone. This application is an interpretation of Lakatos's framework, not a mechanical decision rule [5].

### For Reproducibility and Open Criticism

Underdetermination makes methodological diversity valuable. Replicating one design checks stability under similar assumptions; testing the same claim with a different instrument, population, model, or method challenges a different set of auxiliaries. Convergent findings are stronger when their likely errors are not shared. The author's synthesis is that a portfolio of partially independent tests is more informative than repeated use of one highly optimized pipeline [7, 10].

Objectivity should be designed into institutions. Data access, clear methods, registered analysis plans where appropriate, adversarial collaboration, post-publication correction, and protection for dissent all widen the routes by which error can be found. None guarantees neutrality, and each can become ceremonial. Their value lies in making claims answerable to critics who do not share the originating team's incentives or assumptions [10].

Kuhn's account adds a caution: communities can mistake the stability of normal science for proof that their framework is complete. Review processes should preserve deep expertise while leaving routes for anomalies and rival approaches to receive serious examination. Constant foundational disruption would prevent cumulative puzzle-solving, but complete closure would prevent recognition of crisis. The institutional problem is to sustain that tension rather than maximize either conformity or novelty [4, 9].

### For Public Evaluation of Scientific Claims

The public question "Is it scientific?" should not be answered by searching for one surface marker. Technical vocabulary, numerical output, institutional affiliation, and even a nominally falsifiable statement can coexist with weak practice. More diagnostic questions are whether the claim risks failure, whether contrary evidence changes it, whether methods are transparent, whether serious alternatives were considered, and whether the programme has produced novel successes rather than post hoc explanations [2, 5].

This plural test also prevents misuse of uncertainty. Fallibility does not make expert conclusions equivalent to unsupported opinion. A mature conclusion can be provisional and still be much better supported than its rivals. Underdetermination means that evidence rarely dictates one belief without assumptions; it does not mean that every set of assumptions is equally coherent, fertile, or empirically successful. The comparative record matters [5, 7].

Public communication should distinguish confidence in observations, models, and ontological interpretations. A measurement can be robust while its theoretical explanation remains contested. A model can be predictively useful while claims about the entities it posits remain uncertain. The realism-instrumentalism debate supplies vocabulary for stating these differences without forcing a false choice between absolute truth and total skepticism [8].

### For Artificial Intelligence and Automated Science

AI systems can generate hypotheses, fit models, search literature, and propose experiments, but these capabilities do not settle the philosophy of justification. An automated pipeline can optimize confirmation within a fixed model class while ignoring unconceived alternatives, hidden auxiliaries, or values embedded in its objective function. The scientific-method question is therefore not only whether an AI predicts accurately, but what the prediction tests, which alternatives were available, and how failures alter the system [6, 7].

The author's synthesis is that scientific AI should preserve an auditable separation among data, background assumptions, generated hypotheses, selection criteria, and post-result revisions. Systems should be evaluated on novel predictive performance and error discovery, not only on retrospective fit. This applies Popper's empirical risk and Lakatos's progressive-shift standard to machine-assisted inquiry; it does not establish that those philosophies alone define an engineering specification [3, 5].

AI ethics enters when decisions about acceptable error have unequal consequences. Choosing a threshold for medical, environmental, or public-risk claims can distribute harms between false positives and false negatives. Scientific objectivity does not eliminate these value judgments. It requires that they be disclosed, contested, and separated from claims that the evidence alone fixed the decision [10].

Automated consensus can also amplify a dominant paradigm. If training data and evaluation benchmarks encode the same disciplinary assumptions, apparently independent systems may reproduce correlated judgments. The author's assessment is that epistemic diversity must be measured at the level of assumptions and methods, not by counting model instances. This implication connects Kuhn's disciplinary matrices with procedural accounts of objectivity [4, 10].

### For Epistemology and Rational Decision-Making

Philosophy of science is applied epistemology because it studies justification under conditions of incomplete evidence, theory-laden observation, and costly error. It replaces the demand for certainty with comparative questions: which claim takes more empirical risk, predicts the evidence better, survives more independent criticism, and belongs to the more progressive programme? These questions can guide rational inquiry without pretending to provide infallible foundations [3, 5, 6].

The realism debate adds discipline to decisions that use models. A decision-maker can rely on a model's demonstrated range without assuming that every element is literally true. Conversely, treating a model as a mere instrument does not excuse ignoring the mechanisms on which extrapolation depends. The author's synthesis is to match commitment to evidence: trust observed performance within validated conditions, assign additional confidence when independent mechanism and explanation support transfer, and retain uncertainty about untested structure [8].

The deepest implication is institutional humility. Science deserves confidence not because scientists escape human judgment, but because well-designed inquiry makes judgments visible, exposes them to evidence and rivals, and preserves ways to correct error. Confidence should therefore attach to a living critical process as well as to its current conclusions. This is compatible with realism, instrumentalism, and fallibilism even though those positions explain the warrant differently [8, 10].
