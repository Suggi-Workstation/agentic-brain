# Normative Ethics -- How Deontology, Consequentialism, and Virtue Ethics Give Radically Different Answers to "What Should I Do?"

Normative ethics is the branch of moral philosophy that asks the most
practical question in all of ethics: what makes an action right or wrong?
Three grand frameworks dominate the Western tradition -- deontology
(duty-based ethics, associated with Immanuel Kant), consequentialism
(outcome-based ethics, most famously utilitarianism), and virtue ethics
(character-based ethics, rooted in Aristotle). Each framework gives a
different answer to the same question, and the tensions between them
have driven moral philosophy for over two centuries. Understanding all
three is essential because no single framework handles every moral
situation well, and real-world ethical reasoning usually involves
weighing duties, consequences, and character simultaneously.

## Background

The systematic study of how we ought to act goes back to ancient Greece,
where Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle first asked what constitutes a good
life. Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (c. 350 BCE) established virtue
ethics as the dominant framework for nearly two millennia: the right
action is what a virtuous person would do, and the goal of life is
eudaimonia -- human flourishing achieved through the exercise of reason
and the cultivation of character.

The Enlightenment brought a decisive shift. In the 18th century,
Immanuel Kant (1724-1804) rejected virtue-based and consequence-based
approaches alike. In his Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785),
he argued that morality must be grounded in reason alone, not in human
nature or empirical outcomes. The result was deontology: the view that
certain actions are inherently right or wrong regardless of their
consequences, and that moral worth comes from acting out of duty as
prescribed by a universal rational principle -- the categorical
imperative.

Roughly contemporaneously, Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832) developed the
opposite approach. In An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and
Legislation (1789), Bentham argued that morality is about maximizing
pleasure and minimizing pain -- the principle of utility. This became
utilitarianism, the most influential form of consequentialism. John
Stuart Mill (1806-1873) refined Bentham's framework in Utilitarianism
(1861), introducing qualitative distinctions between higher and lower
pleasures and addressing objections that had been leveled against
Bentham's quantitative hedonism.

The late 20th century saw a revival of virtue ethics, led by
philosophers such as Elizabeth Anscombe, Alasdair MacIntyre, and
Philippa Foot, who argued that both deontology and consequentialism had
lost touch with the actual texture of moral life -- the role of
character, community, and practical wisdom in ethical decision-making.
This "aretaic turn" re-established virtue ethics as a serious contender
alongside the two modern frameworks, and contemporary normative ethics
now treats all three as complementary rather than mutually exclusive
approaches.

The author's assessment is that normative ethics is not a settled
discipline with one correct answer. It is better understood as a toolkit
of three lenses, each of which reveals something the others obscure. The
art of ethical reasoning lies in knowing which lens to apply when.

## Core Concepts

### Deontology: The Ethics of Duty

Deontology (from the Greek "deon," meaning duty or obligation) holds
that the moral status of an action is determined by whether it conforms
to moral rules or duties, not by its consequences. The most
comprehensive and influential deontological system is Kant's.

**The Good Will.** Kant opens the Groundwork with the claim that the
only thing good without qualification is a good will. Intelligence,
courage, wealth, and even happiness can be put to evil ends. A good will
is one that acts purely from duty -- not from inclination, not from
expected outcomes, but because the moral law demands it.

**The Categorical Imperative.** Kant provides three formulations of the
fundamental moral principle:

1. *The Formula of Universal Law:* "Act only according to that maxim
   through which you can at the same time will that it should become a
   universal law." This is the universalizability test: before acting,
   consider whether the principle behind your action could be willed as
   a universal law without contradiction. Lying fails this test because
   if everyone lied, the institution of truth-telling -- on which lying
   itself depends -- would collapse.

2. *The Formula of Humanity:* "Act so that you treat humanity, whether
   in your own person or in the person of another, always at the same
   time as an end, never merely as a means." This prohibits using people
   as mere instruments. Exploitation, deception, and coercion violate
   this principle because they treat rational beings as tools rather
   than as autonomous agents deserving of respect.

3. *The Formula of Autonomy:* "Act so that your will can regard itself
   as making universal law through its maxims." Moral agents are not
   merely subject to the moral law -- they are its authors. This
   formulation grounds Kant's commitment to human dignity and moral
   autonomy.

**Perfect vs. Imperfect Duties.** Kant distinguishes between perfect
duties (obligations that admit no exception, such as the duty not to
lie or murder) and imperfect duties (obligations that allow discretion
in how they are fulfilled, such as the duty to develop one's talents or
help others). Perfect duties are binding in every situation; imperfect
duties require judgment about when and how to fulfill them.

**Strengths and Weaknesses.** Deontology's strength is its clarity and
its protection of individual rights. No amount of aggregate benefit can
justify using an innocent person as a means. Its weakness is rigidity:
Kant famously argued that it is always wrong to lie, even to a murderer
at the door asking about a potential victim. Critics argue that this
treats moral rules as more important than the human beings they are
supposed to protect.

### Consequentialism: The Ethics of Outcomes

Consequentialism holds that the moral status of an action is determined
entirely by its consequences. The right action is the one that produces
the best overall outcome, impartially considered. Utilitarianism is the
most prominent version, but not the only one.

**The Principle of Utility.** Bentham's formulation: actions are right
in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to
produce the reverse. "Happiness" here means pleasure and the absence of
pain. This is a hedonistic theory of value -- the only intrinsic good is
pleasure, and the only intrinsic bad is pain.

**Bentham's Felicific Calculus.** Bentham proposed a quantitative method
for evaluating actions: measure pleasures and pains along seven
dimensions -- intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity (nearness),
fecundity (likelihood of producing more of the same), purity (likelihood
of not producing the opposite), and extent (number of people affected).
Sum across all affected individuals, and the action with the highest net
pleasure is morally required.

**Mill's Qualitative Refinement.** Mill rejected Bentham's purely
quantitative approach. He argued that intellectual, aesthetic, and moral
pleasures are qualitatively superior to mere bodily pleasures: "It is
better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to
be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." This addressed the
objection that utilitarianism is a "doctrine worthy only of swine" by
distinguishing higher and lower pleasures. Mill also introduced the
concept of rule utilitarianism -- the idea that we should follow general
rules (like "do not lie") that tend to promote the greatest happiness,
rather than calculating each act individually.

**Act vs. Rule Utilitarianism.** Act utilitarianism evaluates each
individual action by its consequences. Rule utilitarianism evaluates
rules or practices by their consequences and judges actions by whether
they conform to those rules. Rule utilitarianism was developed partly to
address the objection that act utilitarianism would permit unjust
actions (e.g., framing an innocent person to prevent a riot) if the
calculation happened to favor it. Under rule utilitarianism, a rule
permitting frame-ups would have terrible long-term consequences (eroding
trust in the justice system), so it is rejected.

**The Demandingness Objection.** Consequentialism's greatest weakness is
that it appears impossibly demanding. If every action must maximize
aggregate well-being, then buying a coffee instead of donating the money
to malaria prevention is morally wrong. Most of us fail this standard
constantly. Critics also charge that consequentialism cannot account for
special obligations (to family, friends, and community) and that it
permits sacrificing individuals for aggregate gain -- the "utility
monster" problem.

### Virtue Ethics: The Ethics of Character

Virtue ethics shifts the fundamental question from "what should I do?"
to "what kind of person should I be?" The right action is what a
virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances.
Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics provides the foundational framework.

**Eudaimonia: The Highest Good.** For Aristotle, the ultimate goal of
human life is eudaimonia -- often translated as "happiness" but better
rendered as "flourishing" or "living well." Eudaimonia is not a feeling
or a momentary state. It is an activity: the exercise of the soul's
rational faculties in accordance with virtue over a complete life.
Pleasure, honor, and wealth are not the goal; they are at best
byproducts of a well-lived life.

**The Doctrine of the Mean.** Every moral virtue is a mean between two
vices -- one of excess and one of deficiency. Courage is the mean
between cowardice (deficiency) and recklessness (excess). Generosity is
the mean between miserliness (deficiency) and wastefulness (excess).
Honesty is the mean between self-deprecating deception (deficiency) and
boastfulness (excess). The mean is not a mathematical midpoint; it
varies with the person and the situation and must be determined by
practical wisdom.

**Phronesis: Practical Wisdom.** Virtue cannot be reduced to a formula.
The virtuous person needs phronesis -- practical wisdom -- to perceive
what the situation demands and to choose the right action for the right
reason at the right time. Phronesis is not theoretical knowledge of
rules; it is a cultivated capacity for moral perception, acquired
through experience and habituation. This is why Aristotle insists that
ethics cannot be taught from a textbook -- it is learned by doing, by
practicing virtuous actions until they become second nature.

**The Role of Community.** Virtue is not cultivated in isolation.
Aristotle argues that humans are political animals whose flourishing
depends on participation in a polis (community). The virtues are formed
through relationships, role models, and shared practices. MacIntyre's
After Virtue (1981) developed this insight into a critique of modern
moral philosophy, arguing that Enlightenment ethics severed morality
from the traditions and communities that give virtues their meaning.

**Strengths and Weaknesses.** Virtue ethics captures the texture of
moral life in ways that rule-based and outcome-based theories do not. It
acknowledges that moral development is a process, that character matters
as much as individual decisions, and that ethical wisdom cannot be
algorithmic. Its weakness is that it provides less decisive guidance in
specific moral dilemmas. If the question is "should I lie to protect an
innocent person?", virtue ethics tells you to do what a virtuous person
would do -- but that only pushes the question back to which virtues are
relevant and how they are to be weighed against each other.

### Comparing the Frameworks: A Moral Dilemma

The classic test case of a moral dilemma reveals the frameworks'
divergence. Consider the question: should a doctor lie to a patient
about a terminal diagnosis to preserve the patient's hope and quality of
remaining life?

A **deontologist** would likely say no: lying violates the duty of
truthfulness and treats the patient as a mere means (manipulating them
for what the doctor believes is a good outcome). The patient has a right
to the truth, regardless of consequences.

A **consequentialist** would weigh the outcomes: does the hope preserved
by the lie produce more aggregate well-being than the autonomy respected
by the truth? If the patient would live their remaining months in
greater happiness believing they will recover, a utilitarian might
endorse the lie -- though a rule utilitarian might object that a general
practice of doctors lying to patients would destroy trust in the medical
system.

A **virtue ethicist** would ask: what would a compassionate and honest
doctor do? Compassion might favor the lie; honesty might favor the
truth. Practical wisdom (phronesis) is needed to judge which virtue
takes priority in this specific case. There is no formula -- only the
cultivated judgment of a person of good character.

The author's assessment is that no single framework provides a
satisfactory answer to all moral dilemmas. The three frameworks are best
understood as complementary perspectives, each correcting the blind
spots of the others.

## Evidence

The three normative frameworks do not merely reflect philosophical
preference; they map onto distinct patterns in how humans actually
reason about morality.

**Empirical Moral Psychology.** Research in moral psychology,
particularly by Jonathan Haidt and colleagues, suggests that moral
judgment is not a unified cognitive process. Haidt's Moral Foundations
Theory identifies at least five innate moral intuitions:
care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion,
and sanctity/degradation. Deontological reasoning maps most naturally
onto fairness and authority foundations (rules, rights, duties),
consequentialist reasoning onto the care/harm foundation (outcomes,
welfare), and virtue ethics onto loyalty and sanctity foundations
(character, purity, community). This suggests that each normative
framework captures a genuine dimension of moral psychology that the
others neglect.

**Neuroscientific Evidence.** fMRI studies by Joshua Greene and others
have found that deontological judgments tend to correlate with activity
in brain regions associated with emotion (ventromedial prefrontal
cortex, amygdala), while consequentialist judgments correlate with
regions associated with cognitive control and abstract reasoning
(dorsolateral prefrontal cortex). This does not settle the normative
question -- the fact that deontology "feels" right does not make it
correct -- but it explains why the tension between frameworks feels
intractable: it is partly a tension between different neural systems.

**Historical Application.** The frameworks have shaped real-world
institutions. Kantian deontology influenced the development of human
rights discourse and international law, particularly the principle that
individuals possess inherent dignity that cannot be traded off for
aggregate welfare. Utilitarianism shaped cost-benefit analysis in public
policy, welfare economics, and the effective altruism movement -- all of
which seek to allocate resources to maximize well-being. Virtue ethics
influenced professional ethics codes (medicine, law, engineering), which
emphasize character, judgment, and the cultivation of professional
virtues rather than mere rule-following or outcome-maximization.

**Pluralism in Practice.** Surveys of professional ethicists show that
no single framework commands majority support. A 2009 PhilPapers survey
of professional philosophers found that 25.9% leaned toward or accepted
deontology, 23.6% toward consequentialism, and 18.2% toward virtue
ethics, with the remainder endorsing other views or suspending judgment.
This pluralism in expert opinion is itself evidence that the question
"which framework is correct?" may be poorly posed. The better question
is: "what does each framework illuminate, and what does each obscure?"

**Convergent Validity.** A striking feature of the three frameworks is
that they often converge on the same practical conclusion despite
different theoretical foundations. Murder is condemned by deontology
(violates the categorical imperative), by consequentialism (destroys
aggregate welfare), and by virtue ethics (incompatible with the
character of a virtuous person). The fact of frequent convergence
suggests that the frameworks may be capturing different aspects of a
single underlying moral reality rather than describing incompatible
moral universes. Disagreement at the margins does not negate broad
agreement at the core.

## Implications

**For Investing and Business.** Normative ethics is not an academic
abstraction -- it is embedded in every business decision that affects
stakeholders. When a company weighs layoffs to boost shareholder returns
against duties to employees, it is navigating the tension between
consequentialism and deontology. When a value investor like Warren
Buffett emphasizes integrity, reputation, and long-term character over
quarterly results, he is applying a virtue-ethics lens to capitalism.
The author's assessment is that the most durable businesses are those
that satisfy all three frameworks simultaneously: they produce good
outcomes (consequentialism), respect duties to stakeholders
(deontology), and are led by people of genuine character (virtue
ethics).

**For Policy and Governance.** Public policy invariably involves
trade-offs between frameworks. Mandatory vaccination policies illustrate
the tension: a utilitarian calculus supports compulsory vaccination to
achieve herd immunity and save lives, but a deontological framework
insists on individual autonomy and informed consent. The virtue
framework asks what a responsible citizen of good character would do.
Policy that endures tends to be policy that can be justified under more
than one framework -- a form of normative triangulation.

**For Personal Decision-Making.** Most people are intuitive pluralists,
using different frameworks in different contexts without being aware of
it. When considering whether to break a promise, people weigh duties
(deontology: "I gave my word"), consequences (utilitarianism: "they
won't even notice I'm gone"), and character (virtue ethics: "what kind
of person breaks promises?"). Making this reasoning explicit improves
decision quality. The author's practical recommendation: when facing a
difficult moral decision, run it through all three frameworks. If all
three point in the same direction, the answer is clear. If they diverge,
the tension itself is informative -- it tells you what values are in
conflict.

**For the Library.** Normative ethics is a foundational topic that
enables more specialized topics in the ethics-philosophy domain. Future
topics on applied ethics (business ethics, bioethics, AI ethics), the
philosophy of law, political philosophy, and moral psychology all depend
on understanding the three normative frameworks. This topic also serves
as a bridge to psychology-behavior (empirical moral psychology) and
law-regulation (the philosophical foundations of justice).

**The Limits of Theory.** No normative framework provides a complete
moral decision procedure. Deontology can produce morally absurd results
when rules conflict and there is no meta-rule for resolving the
conflict. Consequentialism demands impossible calculations and can
justify intuitively abhorrent actions if the numbers happen to work out.
Virtue ethics provides too little guidance when virtues themselves
conflict -- honesty and compassion do not always align. The practical
lesson is humility: moral certainty is usually a sign that one has not
thought hard enough about the alternatives. The frameworks are tools for
thinking, not algorithms for deciding. The wise person, the author
suggests, learns to use all three and remains alert to the limits of
each.
