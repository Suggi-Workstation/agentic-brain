# Aesthetics and Artistic Value Depend on Form, Interpretation, Experience, and Institutions

A work matters artistically through no single property: perceptible form, expressive or cognitive achievement, informed experience, historical context, and practices of recognition can each supply reasons for valuing it. Beauty remains important, but conceptual art, cross-cultural traditions, technological reproduction, and generative AI show why neither beauty, popularity, scarcity, price, nor an institution's approval can serve as a complete measure of artistic value [1][4][5][7][8].

## Background

Philosophical aesthetics asks about beauty, art, aesthetic experience, judgment, interpretation, and value. The modern term entered European philosophical vocabulary in the eighteenth century, but reflection on representation, form, feeling, and artistic practice is much older and is not confined to the European tradition [1][4][7][8]. Plato treated beauty as a wide metaphysical concern while regarding poetry and pictorial imitation with ethical and epistemic suspicion; later European theories variously made art imitation, expression, formal organization, sensuous truth, or a source of distinctive experience [4]. This history matters because "aesthetics" does not name one doctrine. It names an inquiry whose central concepts have repeatedly changed when new practices exposed what older accounts omitted [1][4].

The eighteenth-century debate about taste established a problem that still structures aesthetic judgment. If liking is a feeling, disagreement may seem irresolvable; yet judgments such as "this is beautiful" ordinarily claim more than "I happen to enjoy this." David Hume accepted that sentiment is indispensable but argued that practice, comparison, perceptual delicacy, good sense, and freedom from prejudice make some critics more reliable than others. He also acknowledged persistent variation arising from age, temperament, and historical situation [9]. Immanuel Kant likewise treated judgments of beauty as grounded in pleasure rather than determinate concepts, while maintaining that they claim a kind of shared validity. His account distinguished the agreeable, which merely gratifies an individual, from the beautiful, whose pleasure is presented as merited and communicable [3][10]. Neither view turns taste into a mechanical rule; both try to explain why reasons, education, and criticism matter even though aesthetic judgment cannot be deduced like a theorem.

The modern philosophy of art then widened beyond beauty. Photography, readymades, abstraction, performance, and conceptual art challenged definitions based only on skilled imitation or attractive perceptible form. Arthur Danto's examples of perceptually indiscernible objects made context decisive: visually identical items can occupy different artistic roles because one is situated within an art-historical interpretation and another is not [1][4]. George Dickie's institutional approach emphasized presentation to an artworld public, while historical accounts emphasized intended relations to prior art [4][5]. Conceptual art intensified the challenge by treating an idea, instruction, event, or critical intervention as the work's center and by assigning cognitive value greater importance than conventional beauty [5]. These developments did not eliminate perceptual form; they showed that what a viewer is perceiving, and under which category, can alter the significance of the same visible features [1][2].

A global account cannot treat the modern Western category of autonomous fine art as universal. The Stanford Encyclopedia's account of Chinese painting and calligraphy describes aesthetic evaluation as extending through intention, meditation, brush movement, incompleteness, allusion, and the beholder's imaginative completion rather than concentrating only on a finished object [7]. Its account of Japanese aesthetics connects artistic practices to impermanence, pathos, austerity, suggestion, disciplined practice, and continuities among art, philosophy, religion, and ordinary life [8]. These traditions do not supply one alternative "Eastern" theory. They demonstrate that relations among maker, process, object, audience, ritual, and daily practice can be organized differently from a museum-centered model [7][8]. Comparative aesthetics therefore requires translation between categories without assuming that every tradition divides art, craft, religion, nature, and life in the same way.

Technological reproduction created another turning point. Walter Benjamin argued that mechanical reproduction detaches an object from the domain of tradition and transforms the authority of its unique presence, which he called aura. Photography and film do more than make copies: they change access, scale, viewpoint, exhibition, performance, and the social function of art [11]. His account does not establish that reproduced art is valueless. It identifies a shift from unique presence and ritual location toward reproducibility and exhibition, while showing that authenticity is partly historical and relational rather than simply visible [11]. The author's synthesis is that digital files and generative systems extend this problem because a work may have many equivalent instances, uncertain provenance, distributed human contributions, and no scarce physical original [11][13][16][19].

Generative AI makes old questions newly testable. Philosophical work commonly analyzes creativity through novelty and value, often adding surprise, agency, intention, choice, evaluation, or understanding [12]. Text-to-image systems can produce novel artifacts, but the artistic achievement may be distributed among training-data creators, model designers, a prompting or fine-tuning artist, curators, editors, and institutions. Empirical studies also show that viewers change judgments when identical or comparable images receive human or AI authorship labels, so provenance can affect perceived profundity, worth, effort, and creativity even when visible content is held constant [14][15]. The philosophical task is not to settle copyright or market ownership. It is to specify which features of a process, artifact, experience, and practice supply reasons for calling something art, creative, authentic, or artistically valuable.

## Core Concepts

### Aesthetic value is broader than beauty and narrower than every value

Beauty is a central aesthetic value, but it is neither the whole of aesthetics nor a necessary aim of every artwork. Aesthetic vocabulary also includes sublimity, elegance, grace, balance, delicacy, tension, ugliness, the comic, the uncanny, and other qualities that organize attention and response [1][3]. An artwork can deliberately repel, unsettle, bore, or frustrate while succeeding in its project. Conversely, a sunset, proof, garden, face, or well-designed tool may be aesthetically valuable without being an artwork [2][3]. This asymmetry prevents two reductions: art cannot simply be defined as whatever is beautiful, and aesthetic value cannot simply be defined as value possessed by art.

Artistic value is the value of a work as art. One influential view makes it the value of an informed experience in which the work is understood, while pluralist accounts allow aesthetic, cognitive, moral, religious, political, historical, and other achievements to contribute in different practices [5][20]. The useful distinction is functional rather than lexical. A painting's auction price is market value, its importance to a community may be historical or sacred value, and its capacity to reorganize perception may be aesthetic or artistic value. The values can interact without becoming identical. High price does not prove artistic merit; popularity demonstrates uptake rather than correctness; institutional acquisition supplies evidence of recognition but not a conclusive verdict [4][5].

The author's synthesis is that an artistic-value claim should name the achievement being credited. "This is important art" is incomplete until it identifies whether the work realizes a demanding form, articulates experience, transforms a medium, discloses a subject, enables insight, revises a tradition, or sustains an unusually rich informed experience [2][5][6]. Different achievements may resist a single common scale [20]. That does not make evaluation arbitrary; it means comparisons require stated dimensions and reasons rather than an unexplained total score.

### Taste is subjective in basis but answerable to reasons

Aesthetic judgment begins in response, but it need not end in preference. Hume's critic develops through repeated practice, comparison among works, sensitivity to fine distinctions, good sense, and efforts to reduce prejudice [9]. Kant's judgment of beauty rests on feeling yet presents itself as worthy of agreement, not as a report about appetite [3][10]. Both approaches explain ordinary critical practices: viewers revisit works, learn conventions, compare performances, discover previously unnoticed relations, and revise judgments. The possibility of improvement would be unintelligible if taste were only a private reflex.

Reasons in aesthetics are commonly defeasible rather than algorithmic. A critic may point to compositional rhythm, economy, expressive precision, an unresolved contradiction, the transformation of a convention, or the fit between means and purpose. These observations direct attention, but they do not guarantee the same response from every competent viewer [1][3]. A feature that creates productive tension in one work may create incoherence in another because medium, genre, intention, and history differ. Context is therefore not decorative information. It helps determine the category under which a feature is perceived and the comparison class against which an achievement becomes intelligible [1][2].

Expertise improves access without creating infallibility. Training can sharpen discrimination and expand comparison, but institutions can reproduce fashion, exclusion, and inherited hierarchy. Hume's own acknowledgement of prejudice and historical situation makes critical authority conditional rather than absolute [9]. The author's synthesis is a double test: ask whether a judgment responds closely to the work, and ask whether the standards used are themselves defensible. Deference to an expert may justify further attention, but testimony alone cannot substitute completely for the first-person engagement that aesthetic appreciation ordinarily requires [3][18].

### Form, representation, expression, and cognition identify different achievements

Form concerns organized relations among perceptible or otherwise apprehensible elements: line, color, shape, rhythm, duration, spatial arrangement, narrative order, sonic structure, movement, contrast, repetition, and variation. Formalism gives such relations explanatory priority. Its strength is disciplined attention to how a work is made rather than only what it depicts. Its weakness appears when subject, function, intention, history, or social meaning changes the experienced character of the same form [1][2]. Danto's indiscernible-object cases and conceptual art show that perceptual organization cannot by itself explain every artistic difference [1][5].

Representation concerns what a work depicts, describes, enacts, or makes available as a possible world. Resemblance is one mechanism, not a complete theory: diagrams, stylization, abstraction, fiction, and symbolic convention represent without duplicating appearances. Expression concerns how a work makes emotion, attitude, stance, or sensibility intelligible. Expression need not be a transparent report of the maker's private state; a performance can be mournful even if its performer is not presently sad, and a novel can articulate perspectives its author rejects [4][6]. Cognition concerns what engagement makes thinkable or understandable. Conceptual art foregrounds this dimension by using ideas and discourse as primary materials [5].

These categories overlap but should not be collapsed. A formal device can carry a representation, organize an expression, and direct thought at once. Gadamer's hermeneutical aesthetics treats art as presenting a subject matter rather than merely copying it: interpretation participates in the work's realization because the work configures a space in which meaning can appear [6]. The author's synthesis is that strong criticism traces the route from specific means to an achieved effect. It does not infer value merely from a serious theme, and it does not isolate form from the interpretive work form performs.

### Interpretation joins work, intention, context, and audience without reducing one to another

Interpretation asks what a work means, shows, performs, or makes possible. Artist intention is relevant because it can identify the project, category, constraints, and choices against which an achievement is assessed. It is not automatically decisive because intentions may be incomplete, internally inconsistent, unrealized, strategically described after the fact, or exceeded by what the work makes available [5][6]. An audience likewise contributes knowledge, attention, memory, and imagination, but an idiosyncratic association does not become an equally warranted interpretation merely because someone experiences it.

Context enters at several levels. Art-historical context can identify a quotation, convention, rejection, or innovation. Social context can explain why an image carries unequal risks or why a symbol is not freely interchangeable across communities. Medium context establishes which choices were available and what counted as technically or conceptually difficult. Reception context explains why interpretations change as later audiences ask new questions [1][5][6]. None of these levels should be used as a shortcut. A biography does not replace analysis of the work, and formal description does not erase the history through which form became meaningful.

The author's synthesis is a constrained-pluralist model. A defensible interpretation must fit salient features of the work, use relevant contextual evidence, explain more than rivals with fewer arbitrary assumptions, and remain open to counterevidence. Several interpretations can pass because complex works organize multiple relations, but not every reading fits equally well [5][6]. This model avoids two failures: reducing meaning to the maker's declaration, and treating interpretation as unconstrained projection.

### Institutions identify, preserve, and rank art, but recognition is not value itself

Institutional theories explain why artworld practices matter. Museums, galleries, academies, critics, publishers, festivals, archives, markets, and communities create roles through which objects and performances are presented, preserved, compared, and debated [4]. They can make an ordinary object available for artistic attention, maintain provenance, supply interpretive frames, and stabilize genres. Danto's artworld and Dickie's institutional approach respond directly to cases in which visible form does not distinguish artwork from non-artwork [1][4].

Institutional recognition nevertheless answers a classification question more readily than a merit question. An institution can successfully confer or recognize art status while presenting weak art; an unrecognized practice can possess artistic value before dominant institutions notice it. Institutions also differ among cultures. The modern gallery is not the universal form of artistic life, and many traditions embed aesthetically significant artifacts and performances in ritual, education, governance, craft, or ordinary use [4][7][8]. A theory that makes one institutional structure necessary risks excluding the practices it should explain.

The author's synthesis is to treat institutions as epistemic and practical infrastructure, not oracles. Their judgments deserve weight when they rest on preservation, sustained comparison, scholarship, community knowledge, and transparent criticism. Their authority weakens when selection is driven by market power, closed networks, inherited exclusion, or a demand that every tradition resemble the institution's own categories. Institutional history is evidence about a work's status and reception; it is not proof of the work's final value.

### Authenticity concerns provenance, achievement, and appropriate relation, not mere uniqueness

The author's synthesis distinguishes several meanings of authenticity. Object authenticity concerns whether an item is the artifact or instance it is claimed to be. Performative authenticity concerns fidelity to a practice, score, role, or expressive commitment. Personal authenticity concerns whether an artist's work presents an owned rather than strategically borrowed stance. Cultural authenticity concerns relations to a tradition and can become dangerous when it freezes living cultures into a single authorized form. These questions are related but not interchangeable.

Benjamin's analysis of mechanical reproduction shows why perfect resemblance does not settle authenticity. A reproduction may share visible content with an original while lacking its history, material continuity, location, and relation to tradition; at the same time, reproduction can reveal details, widen access, and create forms such as film whose artistic logic presupposes reproducibility [11]. Photography similarly challenges a simple original-copy hierarchy because a negative, file, print, exposure, crop, or edition can occupy different roles. The value at stake may lie in singular presence, controlled realization, documentary relation, selection, or circulation rather than handmade uniqueness alone [11].

The author's synthesis is an achievement-sensitive approach. Ask what kind of work this is, which history constitutes its identity, which human or collective decisions are being credited, and which relation an audience has reason to care about. Scarcity can affect price without adding artistic merit. An exact forgery can fail as the claimed historical achievement while retaining perceptible qualities. A print in an authorized edition can be authentic despite multiplicity. A restored or performed work can be authentic through disciplined continuity rather than untouched material.

### Cross-cultural judgment requires translation, participation, and limits on appropriation

Chinese theories of painting and calligraphy described in the SEP shift attention from autonomous product to creative process, cultivated intention, the irreversible brush stroke, suggestiveness, the unfinished, and imaginative continuation by the beholder [7]. Japanese concepts such as mono no aware, wabi, yugen, and kire organize attention around impermanence, austerity, mysterious depth, disciplined cutting, and relations among practice, nature, religion, and daily life [8]. These concepts do not map cleanly onto a narrow opposition between formal beauty and conceptual meaning. They expand the objects and relations an aesthetic theory must explain.

Comparison therefore requires more than importing a term. A viewer must ask what practice trains perception, which distinctions participants make, how an artifact functions, and what translations lose. Cultural context does not prohibit outsiders from appreciating or creating across traditions, but it changes the burdens of knowledge, attribution, reciprocity, and responsibility. Philosophical analysis of cultural appropriation distinguishes descriptive borrowing from wrongful cases and examines possible harms such as exploitation, profound offense, distorted representation, and violations of group intimacy, while also recognizing that cultures are historically dynamic and internally contested [17].

The author's synthesis is that respectful cross-cultural evaluation is neither uncritical relativism nor one universal ranking system. It begins with participant concepts and historical relations, tests interpretations against informed voices and the work itself, and states where comparison becomes uncertain. The worst failure is to extract a visible style while deleting the practice, people, and asymmetry that made it meaningful. Attribution, consultation, provenance, and openness to correction are therefore part of aesthetic competence, not external public-relations additions.

### Creativity and authorship concern agency across a process

A common philosophical account treats a creative product as new and valuable, sometimes adding surprise. Accounts of creative agency further emphasize choice, evaluation, understanding, judgment, and the ability to guide or endorse a process [12]. These conditions explain why accidental novelty is not automatically credited as creativity and why technical skill alone is insufficient. They also leave room for improvisation, collaboration, rule use, chance procedures, and tools. A process need not be fully controlled to express agency; the relevant question is how an agent selects constraints, responds to results, and takes responsibility for the achieved work.

Authorship can be distributed. Workshops, orchestras, film crews, performance ensembles, editors, software tools, and commissioning institutions already complicate the lone-genius model. Generative AI increases the number and opacity of contributors. The model's training data and design shape a possibility space; a user may prompt, generate, reject, revise, sequence, combine, fine-tune, or contextualize outputs; an institution may frame the result as art [13][16]. Credit should follow documented contribution rather than a metaphysical all-or-nothing label.

The author's synthesis is a process ledger. Record who set the purpose, selected data or references, designed constraints, made consequential choices, evaluated alternatives, altered outputs, arranged presentation, and accepted accountability. Then assess the work independently: meaningful human labor does not guarantee artistic success, and a compelling artifact does not by itself prove a particular author's achievement. This separation clarifies generative AI without deciding legal ownership, which belongs to law and regulation rather than philosophical aesthetics.

## Evidence

### Philosophical stress tests show why no single criterion is sufficient

Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste" begins from observable disagreement and uses comparative criticism rather than deduction. Its method identifies conditions under which perception and judgment improve: practice, comparison, delicacy, good sense, and reduced prejudice. Its bounded finding is that sentiments are not equally well formed even though no a priori rule mechanically derives beauty, and it retains irreducible variation across temperament and historical situation [9]. Kant's analysis begins from the grammar and phenomenology of judgments of taste. It distinguishes agreeable pleasure from judgments of beauty and explains the latter through feeling, disinterest, and a claim to shared validity; his theory of genius addresses exemplary artistic production that cannot be reduced to following a prior rule [10]. These arguments do not produce a universal rating formula. They explain why aesthetic judgments can be subjective in basis and still make normative demands.

Readymades and conceptual art supply counterexamples to simple aesthetic definitions. If an art object and an ordinary object can be perceptually indistinguishable, visible form alone cannot determine art status. Institutional and historical relations become relevant, while conceptual works test whether an artwork must offer traditional aesthetic pleasure at all [1][4][5]. The method is philosophical case analysis: construct or examine a hard case, identify what a proposed definition classifies, and test whether that result survives informed judgment. The result is not that form never matters. It is that theories based only on perceptible form, beauty, or craft exclude recognized practices or fail to explain their differences.

Gadamer's hermeneutical approach supplies a different test. Rather than treating aesthetic experience as isolated subjective pleasure, it examines how a work addresses an audience and discloses a subject matter through interpretation [6]. Conceptual art similarly reveals tension between artist intention and audience interpretation: if the idea fully fixes the work, interpretive freedom narrows; if audiences complete the idea without constraint, the work's identity becomes unstable [5]. These analyses support a bounded conclusion: interpretation is constitutive of engagement but requires a work, practice, and evidence that constrain it.

### Cross-cultural sources expose category error in supposedly universal theories

The SEP account of Chinese painting and calligraphy reconstructs a body of theory in which intention precedes execution and endures after the trace, while the aesthetic object remains bound to meditation, brush movement, unfinishedness, suggestiveness, and the beholder's imagination [7]. Its method is historical and comparative interpretation of indigenous concepts and art-theoretical texts. The finding is not that all Chinese art follows one rule, but that a product-centered theory misses dimensions explicitly valued within major Chinese practices. In particular, originality need not mean separation from tradition, and completion need not mean filling every visible space [7].

The SEP account of Japanese aesthetics uses philosophical texts and practices to show how impermanence, pathos, austerity, suggestion, cultivated spontaneity, and cutting organize artistic experience [8]. Its cases include poetry, tea, landscape painting, gardens, theater, and film. The account's emphasis on disciplined practice prevents "simplicity" from being confused with lack of skill; its emphasis on impermanence prevents incompleteness from being classified automatically as defect [8]. Together, the Chinese and Japanese materials falsify a careless universal inference from one modern Western institution to art as such. They do not justify a reverse stereotype in which Asia is spiritual and the West formal. Each tradition is internally historical and contested.

The cultural-appropriation literature adds an ethical test to cross-cultural interpretation. It distinguishes borrowing as a descriptive fact from wrongful appropriation and evaluates possible offense, exploitation, lack of reciprocity, distorted representation, and group intimacy [17]. The method is normative analysis of cases and proposed rights, not a survey proving that every member of a culture agrees. Its finding is conditional: cultural exchange can be valuable, but power, permission, benefit, and representation can change the moral and therefore sometimes the artistic assessment of a work [17].

### Photography and reproduction separate appearance from historical presence

Benjamin's essay compares traditional objects with photography and film to analyze how technical reproducibility alters presence, authority, exhibition, reception, and politics. His claim that aura withers under reproduction is a historical-philosophical thesis, not an experiment measuring viewer preference [11]. Its evidential force comes from explaining observable changes: a copy can travel where an original cannot, a camera can disclose views unavailable to unaided perception, and film is assembled for reproducible exhibition rather than derived from one complete public performance [11].

The case shows why visual identity does not imply identity of value. A faithful reproduction can carry compositional and representational qualities while differing in provenance and relation to tradition. Yet Benjamin also shows why reproduction is productive, not merely subtractive: it expands access and creates new perceptual and political conditions [11]. The author's synthesis is that photography and mass reproduction disprove both extremes. Material uniqueness is not required for all art, but provenance and mode of production can remain aesthetically relevant even when appearances coincide.

### Generative AI experiments separate artifact features from authorship beliefs

Zhou and Lee analyzed more than four million artworks from over 50,000 users on an art-sharing platform, including known adopters of text-to-image systems. Using an event-study difference-in-differences design, they estimated that adoption increased monthly creative productivity by 25 percent and favorites per view by 50 percent. Mean content and visual novelty declined over time, while maximum content novelty increased, suggesting a larger but inefficiently explored idea space [13]. Their value measure reflects platform response and cultural context, not philosophical artistic value. The study therefore supports claims about productivity, peer evaluation, and measured novelty; it does not prove that AI made the works better art.

Horton, White, and Iyengar conducted six experiments with 2,965 participants and manipulated human-made and AI-made labels. Participants devalued works labeled AI-made across several dimensions even when they regarded the images as indistinguishable and even when collaboration with a human was described. Direct comparison with AI-labeled work also increased perceived creativity of human-labeled work [14]. Because labels rather than visible properties drove the contrast, the study demonstrates that source attribution can enter evaluation. It does not establish that every provenance-sensitive judgment is irrational; human achievement may itself be a legitimate object of appreciation.

Bellaiche and colleagues randomly assigned "human-created" or "AI-created" labels to paintings that were all AI-generated. In Study 1, human-labeled images received higher ratings for liking, beauty, profundity, and worth, with larger differences for profundity and worth than for surface-level liking. Study 2 found that perceived emotion, story, meaning, effort, and creation time helped explain label-sensitive judgments [15]. This method isolates top-down authorship belief from bottom-up image content. Its bounded implication is that aesthetic response is multi-level: viewers evaluate perceptible qualities and inferred communication or achievement, not only pixels.

The findings fit experimental philosophy reviewed by the SEP: people may classify AI-generated paintings as art while hesitating to classify AI systems as artists, partly because they attribute less intention or other mental states to the systems [18]. They also fit the philosophical creativity literature, which distinguishes novelty and value from agency, evaluation, and judgment [12]. The combined evidence does not settle whether an AI can be creative. It identifies the disputed variables and shows that authorship information has measurable effects on evaluation.

### Generative systems test diversity, not only authorship

The UNESCO Independent Expert Group on AI and Culture reports that AI can extend creative capability, accelerate ideation, and widen access while also concentrating infrastructure, reproducing bias, and reinforcing dominant aesthetic norms through common datasets and platform feedback loops [16]. The report is an expert policy synthesis rather than a controlled experiment. Its claims about cultural monoculture and underrepresentation identify governance risks that require continued measurement, especially for non-Western and less digitally represented traditions [16].

An interdisciplinary review argues that generative models can expand participation while embedding the geographic, racial, gender, and stylistic biases of web-scale training data. It also notes that artists may move intention and rigor into data selection, prompting, curation, and downstream transformation [19]. The author's synthesis is that generative AI is a stress test for every major aesthetics theory. Formalism asks about outputs; intentionalism asks who guided them; institutionalism asks how they are presented; historical theories ask how they relate to prior art; achievement views ask what agency deserves credit; cross-cultural aesthetics asks which traditions were represented, flattened, or excluded.

## Implications

### Critics should separate classification, interpretation, and evaluation

A disciplined criticism begins with three different questions. First, what kind of object, event, instruction, performance, or process is under consideration, and why does it count as art within the relevant practice? Second, what does it present, express, disclose, or invite an audience to do? Third, how well does it achieve those ends, and which values are involved [4][5][6]? Collapsing the questions creates predictable errors. Institutional acceptance can establish status without merit; a compelling interpretation can explain a weak work; beauty can support value without defining art.

Critics should then identify the evidence for each claim. Perceptible features support formal description. Artist statements and production records support claims about intention and process, though not necessarily success. Historical records support provenance and influence. Audience studies support claims about specified populations, not universal reception. Community testimony supports cultural context, while disagreement within a community should remain visible [7][8][14][15]. The author's recommended test is simple: if removing the work's price, fame, or institutional label destroys the evaluation, the critic should ask whether evidence about the artistic achievement was ever supplied.

Comparison should be dimension-specific. One work may exceed another in formal integration but not in historical innovation, expressive force, ethical insight, or participatory achievement. A final ranking can remain indeterminate because the dimensions do not share a natural unit [3][5][20]. This is not an excuse for vague praise. It is a demand to state tradeoffs and uncertainty rather than conceal them in a number.

### Artists should make the location of agency legible

Artists working in traditional, collaborative, digital, or generative processes benefit from a process record. It can identify the purpose, references, constraints, materials, iterations, rejected alternatives, chance procedures, tool settings, human collaborators, and final editorial decisions. Such a record does not prove quality, but it lets audiences and institutions assess achievement, intention, and responsibility [12][13]. It also protects against the false choice between total control and no authorship. Many artistic practices use contingency; agency appears in designing, interpreting, selecting, and answering for what contingency produces.

For generative AI, a bare prompt is not a stable unit of authorship. Some works may involve extensive dataset construction, model adaptation, repeated generation, compositing, painting, sequencing, installation, performance, or critical framing; others may involve accepting the first output. These histories can yield visually similar artifacts but different achievements [12][13]. The relevant philosophical question is not whether a tool touched the work. It is whether the claimed artist exercised consequential judgment and whether the presented result warrants the interpretation attached to it.

Artists borrowing across cultures face an additional burden. Research, attribution, participation, permission where appropriate, reciprocity, and benefit sharing can alter both the ethical and artistic adequacy of a project [17]. Surface imitation without semantic or social understanding weakens interpretation even before legal questions arise. The worst outcome is a work that claims universality while treating another community's living practices as anonymous raw material.

### Audiences should distinguish perceptual response from inferred achievement

An immediate response is evidence about experience, not a complete verdict. A viewer can record what attracts, repels, confuses, or moves before learning provenance, then ask how context changes the judgment. AI-label experiments show why this sequence is useful: authorship information can alter ratings even when image content is held constant [14][15]. The change may expose bias, or it may reflect a legitimate interest in human effort, intention, or communication. The next question is which inference the label supports.

A provenance-sensitive judgment is strongest when provenance bears on the value claimed. If a work is admired for an artist's risky transformation of a medium, evidence that the process was largely automated is relevant. If it is admired only for a color relation, the same fact may matter less. If a ritual object is assessed within a living tradition, the maker's standing and use may be constitutive. If a photograph is valued as documentation, causal connection to its subject matters. The author's synthesis is that audiences should attach context at the point where it changes an identified achievement, not use context as generalized prestige or stigma [7][8][11].

Repeated engagement and comparison remain essential. Hume's emphasis on practice and comparison, and Kant's distinction between appetite and a claim to shared validity, imply that mature judgment must survive attention and reasons [9][10]. Audiences need not suppress pleasure, but they should ask what in the work merits it, which alternatives clarify it, and what evidence would revise the response.

### Cultural institutions should treat authority as accountable stewardship

Museums, galleries, schools, publishers, platforms, and archives influence what becomes visible, comparable, and preservable. Their responsibilities therefore exceed selection. They should document provenance, version, medium, collaboration, cultural context, restoration, and generative methods where relevant; disclose uncertainty; retain contested interpretations; and provide routes for affected communities to correct or supplement descriptions [4][7][8][17]. These practices strengthen institutional authority because they make its evidential basis inspectable.

Institutions should also resist equating visibility with value. Market price, attendance, engagement, and recommendation rank measure different forms of uptake. UNESCO warns that shared datasets and platform curation can reinforce dominant aesthetics and marginalize less represented cultural traditions [16]. A platform that optimizes only predicted engagement can create a feedback loop in which prior visibility becomes evidence for future visibility. Curatorial pluralism therefore requires deliberate comparison across languages, regions, media, and institutional forms, not merely a larger volume of similar content.

The author's synthesis is that reversibility is a useful governance principle. Metadata can be corrected; attributions can be expanded; disputed displays can be contextualized; community access can be renegotiated; and algorithmic ranking criteria can be audited. The single worst institutional failure is irreversible erasure: destroying provenance, treating one interpretation as the artifact's only history, or allowing a dominant technical system to make unrepresented practices undiscoverable. Preservation should maintain both the object and the relations needed to understand it.

### Generative AI should be evaluated as a workflow, artifact, and institution

A three-level audit prevents confusion. At the workflow level, examine data, model design, prompting, fine-tuning, selection, editing, collaboration, labor, and accountability. At the artifact level, examine form, content, expression, interpretive richness, and achieved experience. At the institutional level, examine labeling, platform ranking, market incentives, training-data governance, cultural representation, and access [13][14][15][16]. A judgment at one level does not settle the others. An ethically disputed workflow can produce a formally compelling image; a responsible workflow can produce poor art; institutional promotion can confer visibility without merit.

Claims about AI creativity should specify the criterion. Novelty and value may be present; surprise may be present for users and designers; agency, understanding, intention, and responsibility remain disputed [12]. Empirical evidence that people prefer a human label does not prove that human authorship is necessary, while evidence that users cannot distinguish outputs does not prove that the underlying achievements are equivalent [14][15]. Indistinguishability is a perceptual finding, not a complete theory of art.

Disclosure should therefore be informative rather than stigmatizing. A binary AI label can conceal whether a system performed incidental retouching or generated most of the material. Process-level disclosure lets audiences decide which facts bear on their reasons for valuing the work. It also supports research on bias by preventing hidden category changes [14][15]. Legal ownership, licensing, and infringement require separate legal analysis; aesthetics can clarify authorship and value without pretending to decide rights.

### A practical evaluation protocol should preserve plural reasons

The following protocol is the author's synthesis from the cited sources. First, identify the candidate work and its medium, instances, versions, and relevant practice [2][4]. Second, describe perceptible and structural features before relying on reputation. Third, identify representation, expression, cognitive content, and intended function. Fourth, reconstruct maker and production evidence without treating declared intention as final [5][6]. Fifth, place the work in art-historical, social, and cultural contexts, using participant concepts where possible [7][8]. Sixth, separate aesthetic, artistic, moral, historical, sacred, market, and popularity values. Seventh, identify the particular achievement being credited. Eighth, compare relevant alternatives and disclose the critic's competence and limits [9]. Ninth, test whether institutional recognition is functioning as evidence or as an unsupported substitute for reasons [4]. Tenth, record provenance, appropriation risks, and distributed agency [11][12][17]. Eleventh, seek counterinterpretations and state what evidence would change the judgment. Twelfth, leave an audit trail that another critic could contest.

The author's assessment is that this protocol does not promise consensus; it prevents disagreement from becoming opaque. One critic may give greater weight to formal unity, another to cognitive disclosure, and a participant in a tradition may identify a relation outsiders missed. Their claims become comparable when each names the work, evidence, standard, and tradeoff. Aesthetics is valuable precisely because it makes felt importance answerable to reflection without pretending that art can be reduced to one formula.
