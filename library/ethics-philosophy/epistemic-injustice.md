# Epistemic Injustice -- Credibility, Interpretation, and Institutions Can Exclude Knowers

Epistemic injustice occurs when people are wronged in their capacity to know, interpret experience, communicate evidence, or participate in inquiry. Its central lesson is that knowledge practices are not insulated from power: credibility judgments, shared concepts, institutional routines, and automated systems can exclude a person's contribution even when no rule explicitly commands exclusion [1][2].

## Background

Epistemology traditionally asks what knowledge is, what justifies belief, and when testimony transmits knowledge. Social epistemology adds that most knowing depends on other people and on institutions that collect, certify, preserve, and circulate information. Miranda Fricker's 2007 book gave a canonical name and structure to a further normative question: can a person be treated unjustly specifically as a knower? Fricker answered yes and distinguished testimonial injustice from hermeneutical injustice. The first concerns prejudicially distorted credibility; the second concerns unequal access to the interpretive resources needed to understand and communicate socially significant experience [1][2].

This framework joined ethics to epistemology without reducing either field to the other. A false belief is an epistemic failure, and discrimination is a moral and political failure, but epistemic injustice identifies conduct or structure in which the two are connected: power distorts how a person's epistemic agency is recognized or exercised. Fricker described social power as a socially situated capacity to control action and emphasized identity power, which operates through shared conceptions of social identity. On this account, a hearer can use an identity-prejudicial stereotype to assign a speaker less credibility than the evidence warrants, thereby harming the speaker as a giver of knowledge [1][2].

The idea developed from earlier feminist and social-epistemological work that treated knowers as socially situated rather than interchangeable observers. Testimony, conceptual resources, professional authority, and standards of relevance are sustained by communities. They can therefore reflect unequal participation in the practices that decide whose reports are intelligible, which experiences receive names, and which forms of evidence count. The field does not claim that social location makes every belief true or that marginalized speakers are infallible. It asks whether the procedures used to test claims give people fair standing as epistemic agents while retaining ordinary standards of evidence and correction [2][18].

Fricker's two-part account quickly generated extensions. Kristie Dotson examined silencing as a failure of hearers, owing to pernicious ignorance, to meet speakers' vulnerabilities in linguistic exchange. Dotson distinguished testimonial quieting, in which an audience does not recognize a speaker as a knower, from testimonial smothering, in which a speaker truncates testimony because the audience appears unable or unwilling to receive it competently [3]. Dotson later introduced contributory injustice to describe cases in which marginalized communities possess interpretive resources, but dominant hearers will not use or acknowledge them, compromising the marginalized group's ability to participate in shared knowledge production [4].

Gaile Pohlhaus Jr. developed the related concept of willful hermeneutical ignorance. Knowers are both situated and interdependent: they encounter different parts of the world, yet rely on others' epistemic tools. Injustice can occur when dominantly situated knowers refuse tools developed from marginalized experience, allowing them to misunderstand or ignore parts of the social world that those tools disclose [5]. These extensions corrected a possible overreading of hermeneutical injustice as always involving a concept that no one yet possesses. Sometimes the resource exists in a counterpublic or marginalized community; the injustice lies in blocked uptake, circulation, or institutional recognition [4][5].

The credibility side of the framework also expanded. Fricker treated identity-prejudicial credibility deficit as the central case because it directly disadvantages the speaker. Jose Medina argued that credibility judgments must be understood as socially and historically extended patterns and that undeserved credibility excess can sustain another person's deficit. Later work also showed that positive stereotypes can typecast a person as a token or spokesperson, restricting which contributions others will hear from that person [2][6]. Credibility is therefore not a single quantity that can be corrected by mechanically believing every marginalized speaker more. The normative task is to assign authority for relevant reasons, notice relational patterns of excess and deficit, and preserve each participant's individuality [2][6].

A further shift moved from interpersonal virtue to institutional design. Fricker proposed reflexive testimonial and hermeneutical virtues: responsible hearers monitor how identity power affects their perceptions, remain alert to expressive difficulty caused by an unjust interpretive environment, and adjust their judgments accordingly. Elizabeth Anderson argued that individual correction is insufficient when segregation, shared-reality bias, or institutional routines produce cumulative exclusion even through locally innocent transactions. She treated integration of diverse people and institutions engaged in inquiry, along with structural devices such as blind review, as institutional forms of epistemic justice [2][7].

The resulting field is philosophical in its governing question: what makes treatment of a person as a knower unjust? It uses empirical findings from medicine, law, education, workplaces, and technology to identify mechanisms and consequences, but those findings do not settle the normative judgment by themselves. A measured group disparity can arise for several reasons; an epistemic-injustice diagnosis additionally requires an account of the relevant epistemic standing, the source and fairness of the disadvantage, the power relation, and the available justification [18]. This boundary keeps the topic within ethics-philosophy while allowing evidence from adjacent domains to test and refine the framework.

## Core Concepts

### Epistemic agency and the distinctive wrong

A person acts as an epistemic agent when asking questions, offering testimony, interpreting experience, testing reasons, learning from others, or helping revise shared concepts. Epistemic injustice targets one or more of these capacities. The primary wrong is not exhausted by a bad practical outcome. A report may be ignored without changing a final decision, yet the speaker can still be treated as though they were not a competent participant in inquiry. Conversely, a harmful decision caused by an honest, evidence-responsive mistake is not automatically an epistemic injustice [1][2][14].

This distinction separates injustice from ordinary error or disagreement. Human credibility judgments are fallible, and interlocutors may reasonably dispute relevance, competence, sincerity, or interpretation. Fricker's central testimonial case adds identity prejudice that unfairly deflates credibility. Byskov's analysis likewise emphasizes that epistemic disadvantage can be legitimate in some circumstances and that injustice requires more than exclusion alone [2][18]. The author's synthesis is a four-question diagnostic: Was a person disadvantaged in an epistemic capacity? Was the reason for that disadvantage responsive to relevant evidence? Did social power, prejudice, or an unfair structure shape the disadvantage? Could the procedure offer reasons, correction, and meaningful participation? A negative answer to the first question ends the inquiry; weak answers to the others require further evidence rather than an automatic verdict.

The wrong can produce secondary harms. A court may reach an inaccurate finding, a patient may receive inappropriate care, an employee's warning may never reach management, or a student may abandon a line of inquiry. Repeated exclusion can also weaken epistemic confidence and reduce later participation. These practical and developmental consequences matter, but the distinctive wrong remains the denial or deformation of the person's standing as a knower [1][2].

### Testimonial injustice is prejudicial miscalibration of credibility

Testimonial injustice occurs when prejudice causes a hearer to give a speaker less credibility than the speaker deserves. Credibility has at least two components: competence, or capacity to know and communicate the matter, and sincerity, or honesty in reporting it. A stereotype can distort either component without appearing as an explicit belief. Fricker treats credibility assessment as a socially trained perception, so a hearer may sincerely endorse equality while inherited social imagery continues to influence whom the hearer finds authoritative, rational, confused, emotional, or deceptive [1][2].

The mechanism is relational. A speaker offers information; a hearer evaluates it in a context structured by roles, status, and prior expectations. Testimonial injustice does not require conscious hostility. It requires an unjust deficit connected to identity prejudice in Fricker's central account, while later institutional accounts allow patterns in which no single transaction displays explicit prejudice but the system repeatedly discounts the same groups [1][2][7]. Because competence is domain-specific, justice does not require equal credibility on every subject. A patient's first-person knowledge of symptoms, a clinician's diagnostic expertise, and a statistician's knowledge of model performance are different epistemic assets. The task is to avoid turning one kind of expertise into unlimited authority over all others [8][14].

Credibility excess complicates the picture. An expert, official, manager, or automated score may receive more authority than its evidence warrants. Medina argues that such excess matters when it forms part of a social pattern that produces deficits elsewhere [6]. An excess can also typecast its apparent beneficiary: a person may be heard only as a representative of an identity or presumed expert in a stereotyped field rather than as an individual [2]. The author's synthesis is that excess should be analyzed through its relational effects. Ask whose testimony becomes comparatively less visible, whether the favored source is competent in the relevant domain, and whether the excess constrains the favored person's agency. Not every compliment or deference is unjust; the issue is unjust epistemic authority.

### Hermeneutical injustice concerns unequal intelligibility

Hermeneutical injustice arises when an unfair defect in shared interpretive resources prevents someone from understanding or communicating a significant social experience. Fricker's canonical example is sexual harassment before a widely available concept connected recurring workplace conduct into a recognizable pattern. The person may feel confusion, distress, or wrongness yet lack language that institutions and peers can use to recognize the experience. The disadvantage is situated: the interpretive gap burdens the person experiencing the conduct more than those benefiting from or unaffected by it [1][2].

The mechanism is structural because concepts and categories are collective tools. Some groups have more access to the settings in which those tools are created and authorized. Professional vocabularies, legal classifications, diagnostic manuals, curricula, and data schemas can make an experience legible while obscuring another. Hermeneutical justice therefore requires more than patient listening after a person speaks. It requires attention to who participates in building, revising, and distributing the resources through which speech becomes understandable [2][7].

A missing concept is only one form. Marginalized communities may already possess an apt concept, narrative, or practice, while dominant institutions treat it as anecdotal, ideological, unprofessional, or outside scope. Dotson's contributory injustice and Pohlhaus's willful hermeneutical ignorance identify this condition: the problem is not universal absence but refusal of uptake [4][5]. This distinction changes the remedy. When no one has an adequate resource, collaborative concept formation is needed. When a resource exists but is blocked, the task is to redistribute attention and authority, remove gatekeeping barriers, and require institutions to engage the available resource on its merits [4][5].

### Silencing can occur before, during, or after speech

Silencing is not limited to physically preventing speech. Testimonial quieting occurs when an audience fails to identify a speaker as a knower. Testimonial smothering occurs when a speaker withholds or edits content because competent uptake appears unlikely or dangerous. A further form occurs after speech when an institution records the words but excludes them from the categories that influence a decision. These mechanisms can reinforce one another: repeated failures of uptake teach speakers which experiences are unsafe or futile to report, and the resulting sparse record can later be cited as evidence that no problem exists [3][4].

This framework explains why an open microphone or formal invitation does not by itself establish epistemic inclusion. Participation requires that contributions can enter the inquiry, encounter reasons rather than status dismissal, and alter shared understanding when warranted. It also explains why asking marginalized people to repeatedly educate an institution can impose unequal epistemic labor. The author's synthesis is that a just process must provide both access and uptake without treating any participant's testimony as beyond scrutiny [3][4][7].

### Institutions distribute epistemic standing

Institutions determine who may speak, which forms accept evidence, what categories databases contain, how much time a hearing permits, which credentials confer authority, and who can appeal. These rules can improve inquiry, but they also distribute epistemic standing. Anderson's institutional account identifies segregation and shared-reality bias as structural causes: when advantaged groups deliberate mostly with one another, their convergent perspective can appear complete while experiences outside the group remain unintelligible or incredible [7].

Institutional epistemic injustice can therefore arise without a prejudiced decision-maker. A standardized intake form may omit a relevant experience; a meeting may solicit frontline knowledge only after plans are fixed; a court may apply unequal burdens to different accounts of racialized reality; a workplace hierarchy may make managerial interpretation authoritative by default; or a curriculum may define legitimate inquiry through a narrow disciplinary lineage [11][12][13]. Each case still requires domain-specific evidence. The philosophical claim is not that all hierarchy or standardization is unjust, but that rules for knowledge production should be assessed for who can contribute, how contributions are evaluated, and whether affected people can challenge the interpretive frame.

### Automated systems can become epistemic authorities

Data-science and AI systems classify, predict, rank, retrieve, and generate information. Symons and Alvarado argue that these systems can diminish a person's standing as a knower when an opaque output receives authority over the person's testimony or when the system obstructs epistemic participation. They also stress that not every epistemic harm is unjust and that epistemic injustice can be conceptually distinct from downstream material harm [14]. This distinction prevents algorithmic error from being relabeled automatically as injustice while preserving harms to agency that occur even when no allocation changes.

Generative AI adds mechanisms beyond decision scores. Kay, Kasirzadeh, and Mohamed distinguish amplified testimonial injustice, manipulative testimonial injustice, hermeneutical ignorance, and access injustice. Their framework describes how generated content can reproduce socially biased viewpoints, enable fabricated attacks on credibility, omit or distort marginalized interpretive resources, and distribute access to knowledge unevenly [15]. These are philosophical categories, not proof that every output instantiates them. Demonstrating a particular injustice requires evidence about training and evaluation data, the system's use, the authority users assign it, affected groups, available alternatives, and routes for correction.

Automation can intensify credibility excess because numerical or fluent outputs may appear independent of human judgment. Yet the system's categories, labels, objectives, deployment rules, and thresholds arise from human and institutional choices. The author's synthesis is that an AI system should be treated as one component in a sociotechnical credibility economy, not as a neutral hearer. Its output needs calibrated evidential weight, provenance, contestability, and a procedure through which a person's contrary evidence can receive competent review [14][15].

### Normative diagnosis and empirical measurement are different tasks

Empirical research can measure disparities in language, credibility assessments, participation, or outcomes. It can test whether a pattern persists after adjustment and compare mechanisms across settings. Philosophy asks whether the measured difference constitutes an unjust wrong to people as knowers. The questions inform one another but are not interchangeable [18]. A disparity may be justified by relevant differences in expertise or evidence; a uniform outcome may conceal an unjust process; and an individual case cannot always be inferred from a population-level association.

A disciplined diagnosis therefore states the unit of analysis, comparison group, relevant epistemic capacity, evidence of prejudice or structural exclusion, practical and epistemic consequences, and plausible alternative explanations. It also distinguishes a speaker's claim from the speaker's entitlement to a fair hearing. Epistemic justice protects standing and procedure; it does not guarantee that every claim is accepted. This boundary is essential to avoid turning the concept into a label for any disagreement while retaining its power to identify patterned exclusion [2][18].

## Evidence

The evidence base combines conceptual analysis, qualitative research, systematic reviews, experiments, and observational records. These methods answer different questions. Philosophical cases isolate the structure of a wrong; experiments test mechanisms under controlled conditions; records identify patterned language or decisions; interviews show how participants experience and navigate institutions; and reviews assess recurrence across studies. No single method establishes the whole diagnosis, so the strongest applications align a normative account with empirical evidence and state the limits of each [8][17][18].

### Pain assessment links a stereotype to credibility and treatment judgment

Hoffman, Trawalter, Axt, and Oliver conducted two survey experiments on false beliefs about biological differences between Black and White people. Study 1 retained 92 White U.S.-born native English-speaking adults; 73 percent endorsed at least one false item. Study 2 retained 222 White medical students and residents; about 50 percent endorsed at least one false belief. Participants who endorsed more false beliefs rated a Black patient's pain lower than a White patient's and made less accurate treatment recommendations for the Black patient. Racial bias in pain ratings correlated with racial bias in treatment-recommendation accuracy at r = 0.46, although the authors reported small effect sizes for key findings and noted the limits of survey scenarios [9].

The study did not test the philosophical concept of epistemic injustice directly. It supplies evidence for a mechanism relevant to testimonial injustice: false racial beliefs can affect how observers interpret another person's report of pain and can alter a clinical recommendation. The normative diagnosis requires the additional judgment that a patient's first-person pain testimony received less weight for an identity-prejudicial reason rather than a medically relevant one [8][9]. This separation between measured bias and normative classification prevents the empirical result from carrying more philosophical weight than its method supports.

### Clinical notes reveal a large-scale credibility pattern

Beach and colleagues used natural-language-processing models and generalized-estimating-equation logistic regression to analyze 13,065,081 notes written from 2016 through 2023 about 1,537,587 adult patients by 12,027 clinicians across four clinical fields in a mid-Atlantic U.S. health system. Their models identified terms that undermined or supported competence and sincerity. Credibility-undermining language appeared in 106,523 notes, or 0.82 percent, and supporting language in 33,706 notes, or 0.26 percent [10].

After adjustment, notes about non-Hispanic Black rather than White patients had higher odds of credibility-undermining language (adjusted odds ratio 1.29, 95 percent confidence interval 1.27-1.32) and lower odds of supporting language (0.82, 0.79-0.85). The disparity was larger for terms undermining competence (1.50, 1.47-1.54) than for sincerity (1.16, 1.14-1.19). The authors limited the analysis to explicit terms, studied one health system, and cautioned that demographic documentation and group representation were imperfect [10].

These records provide population-level evidence of a patterned credibility difference, not proof that every phrase or clinician was prejudiced. The result is nonetheless unusually direct evidence for the credibility economy described by testimonial-injustice theory because the measured language concerned whether patients were competent or sincere informants. It also shows an institutional transmission mechanism: a credibility judgment entered into a durable record can shape later readers even after the original encounter ends [10].

### Healthcare analysis identifies both interpersonal and structural mechanisms

Carel and Kidd analyzed patient complaints and healthcare structures through Fricker's framework. They argued that stereotypes of ill people and features of contemporary healthcare can produce testimonial frustration and hermeneutical marginalization. Their analysis distinguishes factual information that a system invites from lived experience that it may classify as irrelevant, and it treats patients' vulnerability as contingent rather than as a reason to deny epistemic agency [8]. This is a philosophical synthesis of empirical literature rather than a single causal study, so its contribution is explanatory structure.

Cote's 2024 critical systematic review searched four databases, identified 629 records, and included 35 articles on strategies for epistemic justice in healthcare. It grouped recommendations into virtuous, structural, narrative, cognitive, partnership, and resistance approaches and judged a patient-partnership approach based on the Montreal Model promising across levels of healthcare. The wording matters: the review maps and critiques proposed strategies; it does not establish that one intervention has definitive comparative effectiveness [17].

Liabo and colleagues provide a smaller qualitative test of participation. They analyzed three meetings and 11 interviews involving public collaborators and researchers in three U.K. health-research studies. Public collaborators contributed through stories, comments, questions, answers, and accounts of their roles, while some adapted their contributions to fit the research setting. The study identified hierarchy and jargon as obstacles and described practices such as advance meetings, acronym lists, community settings, and researchers' efforts to create inclusive reception [16]. Its small, contextual sample limits generalization, but it shows why invitation alone does not guarantee uptake.

### Education shows recurrence across disciplinary settings

Gonzales, Pasque, Farris, and Hansen conducted a systematic review of U.S. doctoral education covering teaching, advising, peer interaction, socialization, and related experience across humanities, social-science, and science disciplines. They retained and analyzed 107 manuscripts. Their review identified testimonial, hermeneutical, and contributory injustice and described rules through which prestige, disciplinary boundaries, and evaluative authority shaped who counted as a legitimate knower. In their reported coding, 16 papers contained testimonial, 13 hermeneutical, and seven contributory examples [11].

A review of prior studies cannot determine the prevalence of epistemic injustice among all doctoral students, and coding diverse qualitative literature through one framework depends on interpretive judgment. It nevertheless supplies cross-study evidence that epistemic exclusion is not confined to one discipline or one interpersonal relationship. The institutional mechanism is especially relevant: faculty and peers do not merely evaluate isolated propositions; they help determine which questions, methods, styles, and intellectual lineages receive legitimacy [11].

### Law and workplaces show how status structures evidence

Legal proceedings formalize credibility through evidence rules, witness roles, burdens, and findings. Gonzales Rose argues that racialized fact-finding can impose disparate evidentiary burdens: White accounts of racialized reality can receive implicit acceptance while Black and Brown accounts face greater scrutiny or suppression. Her analysis treats this as an epistemic problem in evidence law, not simply as interpersonal disbelief [13]. Later work on criminal trials connects credibility excess granted to in-group prosecution witnesses with deficits imposed on minoritized defendants, showing why excess and deficit should sometimes be assessed together [6][13]. These are legal-philosophical analyses; particular courts and cases still require case-specific proof.

Kwok's analysis of workplace hierarchy argues that traditional hierarchies can reinforce testimonial and hermeneutical injustice because status determines who is heard and whose interpretive framework governs work. He contrasts traditional hierarchy with contestatory arrangements that grant workers a right to be listened to [12]. The claim is normative rather than a measured estimate of workplace prevalence. Its value is to identify decision rights, escalation paths, and the distribution of interpretive authority as epistemic features of organization, not merely management technique.

### Data science and generative AI can scale exclusion

Symons and Alvarado use examples from criminal justice, workplace hierarchy, and education to argue that data-science technologies can diminish a person's standing as a knower or source of testimony. Opacity can make a system difficult to contest, while institutional deference can grant its output credibility in excess of its warrant. They explicitly warn that not every epistemic harm is unjust, preserving the need for normative diagnosis [14].

Kay, Kasirzadeh, and Mohamed extend the analysis to generative AI through four dimensions: amplified and manipulative testimonial injustice, hermeneutical ignorance, and access injustice. Their work is a conceptual taxonomy supported by examples, not a population-level causal estimate. It identifies testable questions: whose testimony is represented or distorted, which languages and concepts receive adequate coverage, whether fabricated media manipulates credibility, who can access corrective tools, and whether affected people can contest an output [15].

Across these studies, the evidence is strongest for the existence of measurable credibility patterns in selected healthcare settings and for recurring exclusion mechanisms in reviewed educational literature. Evidence for remedies and for some AI-specific categories is less mature and more conceptual [10][11][14][15][17]. The author's assessment is therefore bounded: epistemic injustice is a useful explanatory framework with empirical support for several mechanisms, but each application must state whether it is a normative argument, qualitative finding, observational association, experimental result, or tested intervention.

## Implications

### For individual hearers: replace automatic deference with reflexive calibration

A responsible hearer should ask what kind of knowledge the speaker is positioned to have, which evidence supports or challenges the report, and whether identity-linked expectations are changing the burden of proof. Fricker's testimonial virtue requires critical awareness of the relation between hearer and speaker identities, while hermeneutical virtue requires patience with speech made difficult by an unjust interpretive environment [1][2]. The aim is not compensatory credulity. It is to remove irrelevant prejudice, seek clarifying evidence, and suspend judgment when the available framework may be inadequate.

Reflexive listening should include attention to credibility excess. Institutional title, professional fluency, numerical presentation, or system output may warrant some deference without warranting unlimited authority. A clinician can possess diagnostic expertise while a patient possesses first-person symptom knowledge; a manager can know strategy while a frontline worker knows a process failure; a model can detect a statistical pattern while lacking contextual facts that a person can supply [6][8][14]. A fair inquiry specifies the domain of each source's competence and records why one source receives more weight.

Speakers also need protection from unequal epistemic labor. An institution should not treat members of a marginalized group as permanent educators, demand personal disclosure as the price of participation, or assume one person represents a whole group. Testimonial smothering predicts that speakers will rationally edit what they say when uptake is unsafe or futile [3]. Confidential channels, multiple ways to contribute, clear anti-retaliation rules, and demonstrated response to prior reports can change that expectation, although their effectiveness must be evaluated rather than assumed.

### For institutions: design participation, uptake, and correction as separate functions

Epistemic access asks whether a person can enter the process. Uptake asks whether the contribution is evaluated through relevant reasons. Correction asks whether a person can challenge a credibility judgment, category, record, or automated output. Institutions often satisfy the first function symbolically while failing the other two. The author's synthesis is that all three should have named owners, documented procedures, and evidence of operation [7][16].

Procedural safeguards can include blinded review where identity is irrelevant, structured criteria for credibility, independent corroboration requirements, reason-giving, preserved dissent, appeal, and audit of patterned outcomes. Anderson treats blind review and integration as structural remedies because they change the environment in which individual judgment operates [7]. These tools are not universally suitable: identity can be substantively relevant, blind review can conceal context, and a formal appeal can be inaccessible. Each safeguard should therefore be tied to a specified mechanism and checked for new exclusion.

Integration means more than demographic presence. People with different social locations must participate on terms that let their evidence influence problem definition, categories, methods, and decisions. Liabo and colleagues' findings show that public collaborators can be present yet still face jargon, hierarchy, and pressure to translate experience into professional form [16]. Anderson's institutional account explains why sustained, equal-status interaction matters: it interrupts segregated shared realities and expands the pool of interpretive resources [7]. The practical test is whether participation can change the inquiry, not whether attendance can be counted.

Counterpublic and community-developed resources should be treated as candidates for evaluation, not as automatically authoritative or automatically parochial. Contributory injustice arises precisely when dominant institutions refuse resources that already exist [4][5]. A fair procedure makes the refusal answerable: it identifies the concept or evidence offered, states the relevance and quality criteria, gives reasons for rejection, and permits revision. This preserves evidentiary standards while preventing professional convention from functioning as an unexamined veto.

### For medicine, law, education, and workplaces: audit the credibility economy

Healthcare organizations can examine credibility language in records, compare how patient reports are documented across groups, and remove labels that substitute character judgment for clinical evidence. The PLOS One study shows that explicit credibility language is measurable at scale, while its limitations show why an audit should combine quantitative patterns with chart review, patient perspectives, and clinical context [10]. Patient partnership should involve agenda-setting and knowledge production, not only feedback after decisions have been made [16][17].

Courts and legal institutions can distinguish legitimate scrutiny from identity-linked burdens. Evidence rules, jury instructions, interrogation practices, expert testimony, and appellate review all allocate credibility. A record should make visible when one account receives implicit acceptance while another must prove the background reality that makes it intelligible [13]. This does not require accepting testimony without cross-examination; it requires applying evidentiary demands consistently and considering how credibility excess for one source transfers harm to another [6][13].

Educational institutions can audit who defines legitimate questions, methods, citation lineages, and standards of professional expression. The doctoral-education review suggests that epistemic injustice can be embedded in socialization and disciplinary rules, not only in overt insults [11]. Transparent evaluation criteria, plural supervisory input, protected routes for methodological dissent, and review of exclusion patterns address structure. Mentoring and bias education address individual practice. Neither level substitutes for the other [7][11].

Workplaces can separate formal decision authority from exclusive epistemic authority. Managers may retain responsibility while creating contestatory processes through which workers can require a reasoned hearing, preserve warnings, and escalate unresolved evidence. Kwok's account supports a right to be listened to as a less epistemically unjust alternative to traditional hierarchy [12]. The author's synthesis is that after-action reviews should examine not only whether a decision was correct but which reports entered the decision, which were filtered out, and whether status rather than evidence explains the difference.

### For AI governance: preserve human epistemic agency

An AI impact assessment should include epistemic questions alongside accuracy and allocation. What claims does the system make or imply? Whose data and concepts structure its categories? Which users are likely to assign it excess credibility? Can a person inspect relevant inputs, offer contrary evidence, reach a competent reviewer, and obtain correction? Are language, disability, geography, or cost barriers creating access injustice? These questions follow directly from treating AI as an epistemic technology embedded in institutions [14][15].

System interfaces should communicate evidential status rather than simulate final authority. A score should be labeled as a model output with stated scope and uncertainty; generated text should preserve source provenance where claims require verification; operators should have independent evidence and authority to depart from recommendations. Appeals should test both data accuracy and the appropriateness of the interpretive category. Otherwise, a person may correct a factual input while remaining trapped in a classification that cannot represent the experience being contested [14][15].

Participatory design can improve category formation, evaluation cases, and failure reporting, but participation should be assessed for uptake and influence. Inviting affected groups after objectives and labels are fixed can reproduce the same injustice under a consultative form. A stronger process includes affected knowledge in problem definition, documents unresolved disagreement, compensates epistemic labor, and publishes how contributions changed or failed to change the system. The evidence base for the comparative effectiveness of these remedies remains limited, so monitoring and revision are part of the remedy rather than evidence that it has already succeeded [15][17].

### A practical epistemic-justice audit

The author's synthesis is a seven-part audit. First, identify the epistemic activity: testimony, interpretation, classification, inquiry, or decision support. Second, identify participants and the specific knowledge each may possess. Third, map credibility allocation, including excess as well as deficit. Fourth, inspect shared concepts and ask whether relevant resources are absent, inaccessible, or refused. Fifth, trace institutional and technical channels that admit, transform, preserve, or discard contributions. Sixth, test disparities and alternative explanations with methods suited to the domain. Seventh, require reasons, correction, remedy, and continued monitoring [2][4][6][7][14][18].

The audit's worst failure would be to convert epistemic justice into a demand that identity decide truth. That would replace one unjust shortcut with another. The framework instead requires evidence-responsive inclusion: people receive standing as epistemic agents, claims remain open to criticism, and institutions must justify how power shapes the route from testimony to accepted knowledge [1][2][18]. Epistemic justice is therefore neither politeness nor automatic belief. It is the disciplined design of knowledge practices in which credibility, interpretation, and participation are allocated for reasons that affected people can examine and contest.
