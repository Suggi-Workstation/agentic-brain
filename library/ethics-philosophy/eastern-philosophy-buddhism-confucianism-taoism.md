# Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism Are Distinct Traditions, Not a Single Eastern Philosophy

Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism offer different accounts of suffering,
ethical cultivation, social order, knowledge, and human flourishing; grouping
them as one "Eastern philosophy" conceals their internal debates and their long
histories of interaction [1][2][3][4][7][8]. A defensible comparison therefore
starts with each tradition's texts and contested interpretations, then compares
specific problems rather than treating either "East" or "West" as a single
viewpoint [4][12]. These traditions matter not because they supply one exotic
alternative to Western thought, but because they provide several disciplined
ways to examine attachment, character, relationships, perspective, and action.

## Background

The three traditions did not begin as a coordinated system. The historical
Buddha probably flourished around 450 BCE in northern India, but the surviving
Nikaya and Agama collections were transmitted for centuries, preserved in
languages other than the one he probably spoke, and sometimes disagree [1].
Confucius is traditionally dated 551-479 BCE, while the received *Analects* is
a layered record whose relation to the historical teacher remains debated [3].
The *Laozi* and *Zhuangzi* likewise emerged through textual histories more
complicated than the old picture of two books written in finished form by two
founders: scholars disagree about the identity of Laozi and the date and
composition of the *Laozi*, while the *Zhuangzi* includes material associated
with more than one hand or school [7][8]. These uncertainties do not make the
texts unusable. They require claims about origins and authorship to be stated as
tradition, scholarly reconstruction, or interpretation rather than as settled
biography.

Karl Jaspers's label "Axial Age" is often used for the broad first-millennium
BCE period in which major philosophical and religious texts appeared in several
regions. It is a useful comparative prompt, not a settled causal explanation.
A systematic cross-cultural assessment found that traits proposed as uniquely
"axial" appeared in some societies before the conventional period and that the
evidence did not support one sharply bounded global age, although some proposed
features did occur across the regions studied [13]. The original claim that
Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism arose within a single 150-year intellectual
window was therefore too precise. The safer conclusion is that early Buddhist
and classical Chinese debates developed during overlapping portions of the
first millennium BCE under different political, linguistic, and religious
conditions [1][3][7][8][13].

Buddhist traditions organize practice around liberation from suffering. The
Four Noble Truths summarize suffering, its origination, its cessation, and a
path to cessation; non-self, impermanence, karma, rebirth, disciplined conduct,
meditation, and wisdom are interpreted differently across Buddhist schools
[1][2]. Buddhism is not one ethical theory. Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana
lineages differ in canonical corpora, ideals, practices, and philosophical
systems, and the Stanford account of Indian Buddhist ethics explicitly treats
the tradition as varied rather than reducible to one formula [2]. The Pali
Tipitaka is the complete canon of Theravada and an important witness to early
Buddhism, but it is not the sole canon for all Buddhist traditions [15].
Mahayana texts and later Indian, Tibetan, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese
traditions substantially expanded the philosophical record [2][15].

Classical Confucian thought arose within debates about how people could restore
ethical and political order through cultivated conduct, ritual, learning,
family roles, and exemplary government. Confucius's teachings were developed
and contested by later thinkers. Mencius argued that humans possess tendencies
or "sprouts" that can grow into virtue under proper cultivation, whereas Xunzi
argued that untrained dispositions require deliberate transformation through
ritual, education, and conscious effort [5][6]. Translating this as a simple
dispute between an innately good species and an innately evil species loses the
different meanings each thinker gives to human nature and the fact that both
allow moral development [5][6]. Confucianism also changed after the classical
period. Han synthesis, Buddhist influence, and Song Neo-Confucian projects,
including Zhu Xi's influential curriculum and metaphysics, produced traditions
that cannot be read back into every passage of the *Analects* [3][4][5].

"Daoism" is also a retrospective category. It can name philosophical readings
of the *Laozi* and *Zhuangzi*, later religious institutions, and multiple
movements that share a concern with dao, naturalness, cultivated responsiveness,
and critiques of imposed norms without forming a single uniform doctrine
[7][8][9]. The historical Laozi may be a composite or legendary figure, and the
*Laozi* has been read as a work of ethics, politics, self-cultivation,
metaphysics, or religion [7]. The *Zhuangzi* uses stories, paradoxes, humor,
and perspective shifts to question rigid distinctions and unexamined claims to
a privileged standpoint [8]. Calling both texts "Taoism" is convenient, but
comparison must not erase their differences or the later development of
organized religious Daoism [7][8][9].

Interaction is as important as origin. Buddhism entered China and developed in
conversation with Chinese vocabularies and practices; Chinese Buddhist and
Daoist traditions influenced one another, and Neo-Confucians criticized while
also responding to Buddhist and Daoist problems and concepts [3][4][5][8][9].
The familiar image of one person using Confucianism for public duty, Daoism for
private retreat, and Buddhism for death is an interpretive summary, not a rule
that describes every historical person. The evidence supports coexistence,
competition, borrowing, and institutional change, not three sealed systems or
a timeless cultural division [3][4][5][8][9].

The phrase "Eastern philosophy" is therefore best used as a loose cataloguing
term, not an explanatory category. India and China are not one intellectual
culture; neither are Buddhism, Confucianism, and Daoism exhaustive of Asian
philosophy. Even within China, classical debates included Mohists, thinkers
later grouped as Legalists, and other positions alongside Confucian and Daoist
texts [4][9]. A sound comparison names the text, school, period, problem, and
level of analysis. It asks, for example, how early Buddhist accounts of
non-self compare with a particular theory of personal identity, or how
Confucian role ethics compares with a specified account of autonomy. It does
not infer what all "Eastern" or all "Western" people believe.

## Core Concepts

### Buddhism: suffering, conditioned existence, and liberation

The Four Noble Truths provide a practical structure rather than four isolated
propositions. They identify a problem, trace conditions that produce it, claim
that ending those conditions can end the problem, and specify a path of
training [1]. Dukkha includes obvious pain but also the instability and
unsatisfactoriness of relying on conditioned things as if they could provide
permanent security. The path is conventionally organized through right view,
intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration;
ethical restraint, meditation, and insight function together rather than as
independent techniques [1][2]. Calling this only a therapy understates its
traditional setting in karma, rebirth, monastic and lay discipline, and a goal
of liberation [1][2].

Non-self is not simply the claim that persons are unreal in every sense. Early
Buddhist analysis rejects a permanent, independent self that owns or controls
changing physical and mental processes; it also must explain agency,
responsibility, karma, and rebirth without such a self [1]. Different Buddhist
philosophers developed different answers. The doctrine is connected to
dependent origination: phenomena arise through conditions rather than through
self-sufficient essences. In practice, this analysis is intended to weaken
clinging to experiences as "I" or "mine," not merely to win a metaphysical
argument [1][2]. It is therefore inaccurate to present modern neuroscience as
having proved non-self. Neuroscience can study processes involved in self-models
and self-reference, while the Buddhist doctrine belongs to a broader
soteriological and philosophical argument [1][11].

Karma in traditional Buddhism concerns intentional action and its consequences
within an account that commonly includes rebirth. Motivation affects the moral
quality and karmic significance of action, but calling karma an impersonal
scientific law of cause and effect obscures doctrinal commitments and
interpretive disputes [1][2]. Buddhist ethics also includes precepts,
compassion, loving-kindness, generosity, and distinct ideals of sainthood or
buddhahood. Scholars have compared parts of Buddhist ethics with virtue ethics,
consequentialism, and moral particularism, but the Stanford survey shows that
no single Western category captures the whole tradition [2]. The comparison is
a philosophical interpretation, not a discovery that Buddhism is secretly one
familiar theory.

The common claim that the Buddha told followers to accept nothing on authority
and test everything personally overstates the *Kalama Sutta*. A close scholarly
reading argues that the discourse answers a practical question about competing
teachers and recommends an ethical assessment involving consequences and the
judgment of the wise; it is not a general recipe for deriving every truth by
private experiment [14]. Buddhist traditions use testimony, teachers, texts,
reasoning, contemplative experience, and communal discipline in different
combinations [1][2][14]. The more defensible epistemic lesson is narrower:
authority alone does not settle a claim, but neither does an individual's first
impression.

### Confucianism: cultivated persons in structured relationships

Confucian ethics places moral formation within families, rituals, education,
friendships, offices, and political relationships. Ren is translated as
humaneness, benevolence, or authoritative moral excellence; the *Analects*
uses it in more than one way rather than supplying one exhaustive definition
[3][4]. Li includes ritual, ceremony, etiquette, and socially embodied forms of
conduct. Yi concerns fitting or righteous action, while xiao concerns filial
conduct, and the junzi is an exemplary person formed through learning and
practice [3][4]. These concepts are mutually conditioning: ritual without
humaneness can become empty performance, while good intention without formed
habits and situational judgment may fail to guide action [3][4].

The graphic claim that the written character for ren simply combines "person"
and "two" to prove that humaneness exists only between people is not a secure
etymological argument and should not bear philosophical weight. Relational
readings instead rest on the texts' treatment of care, ritual, roles, and
interaction [3][4]. Likewise, the familiar list of five relationships is most
clearly formulated in the *Mencius*: affection between parent and child,
righteousness between ruler and minister, distinction between spouses,
precedence between elder and younger, and trust between friends [5]. Later
Confucian traditions often made these roles more rigid and hierarchical. The
classical materials also contain demands on rulers, ministers, parents, and
children, so reciprocity and remonstrance must be examined alongside deference
[3][4][5].

Mencius's four sprouts are incipient tendencies associated with compassion,
shame or aversion to wrongdoing, deference, and judgment. They require a
supportive environment and deliberate cultivation; "human nature is good" does
not mean everyone automatically acts well [5]. Xunzi's statement conventionally
translated as "human nature is bad" uses a different account of what belongs to
nature and emphasizes the transforming work of conscious activity, teachers,
ritual, and music [6]. Both thinkers explain how moral excellence can be
cultivated, but they disagree about the starting materials and mechanisms.
Treating Mencius as a democrat and Xunzi as a simple authoritarian is not
supported by the complexity of their arguments [5][6].

Confucian political thought links personal cultivation and institutional order,
but it does not reduce politics to the private virtue of a ruler. Classical
texts discuss exemplary rule, material welfare, education, ritual, ministerial
remonstrance, and conditions under which a ruler loses moral standing
[3][4][5][6]. Later Confucianism contains both defenses and criticisms of hierarchy,
including debates over gender and political authority [4]. It is therefore
misleading to infer a single modern regime type from "Confucianism." A
contemporary argument for role-based duties must still address coercion,
unequal power, rights, accountability, and the possibility that inherited roles
are unjust.

### Daoism: dao, naturalness, noncoercive action, and perspective

Dao in early Chinese philosophy can mean a path, way, or guiding practice. The
*Laozi* makes dao central while warning that naming and fixed distinctions do
not exhaust what guides the world's transformations [7][9]. De can name the
potency or excellence through which a way is embodied. Ziran, often translated
as naturalness or "so of itself," concerns processes that are not forced into
an alien pattern. These terms have ethical, political, practical, and
cosmological dimensions, and interpreters disagree about their exact relation
[7][9]. Treating dao as a single supernatural substance or as a synonym for
modern ecology imposes conclusions that the texts do not by themselves settle.

Wu wei is commonly rendered "non-action," but literal inactivity is usually a
bad guide. In *Laozi* interpretations it can describe action that does not
coerce, overmanage, compete for display, or impose a rigid design against the
conditions at hand [7]. Water is a recurring image for responsiveness,
low position, and efficacy without contention. Political passages often favor
restraint and warn that aggressive intervention can generate the disorder it
purports to cure [7]. Yet these are not a universal empirical law that less
government always produces better outcomes. Applying wu wei to management or
public policy is an analogy requiring independent evidence about the actual
system.

The *Zhuangzi* intensifies questions about perspective, language, skill, and
transformation. Its stories show that judgments arise from situated ways of
classifying and valuing, while skilled agents can respond without constant
self-conscious calculation [8]. The butterfly dream destabilizes confidence in
a fixed standpoint, but it does not by itself prove that all beliefs are equally
true. Scholars debate whether the text is skeptical, relativist, pluralist,
naturalist, therapeutic, or some combination [8]. The responsible lesson is
not "nothing can be known." It is that a claimant should examine the dao,
training, language, interests, and limitations that make a judgment seem
obvious.

Daoism includes more than philosophical readings of two classical texts.
Organized religious movements, ritual communities, practices of cultivation,
commentarial traditions, and interactions with Buddhism all became part of its
history [7][8][9]. The later category also joins thinkers who did not call
themselves members of one "Daoist school" in the modern sense [8][9]. A review
must therefore specify whether it means the *Laozi*, the *Zhuangzi*, later
philosophical interpretation, or religious Daoism. Otherwise claims about
"what Daoism says" can silently move among different sources.

### Comparative architecture without false unity

A limited comparison can clarify the different projects. Early Buddhist texts
diagnose conditioned suffering and prescribe a path toward liberation [1][2].
Classical Confucian texts ask how cultivated persons and practices can sustain
ethical relationships and political order [3][4][5][6]. The *Laozi* and
*Zhuangzi* question coercive striving, fixed distinctions, and confidence in
socially authorized ways, while exploring responsive action and transformation
[7][8][9]. These are differences of problem, method, and goal, not three
versions of one Asian doctrine.

There are also overlaps. All three traditions contain disciplines of
self-cultivation, challenge untrained desire or egoistic fixation, and connect
personal conduct with a larger order [1][2][3][4][7][8]. Yet the larger order
is not the same: dependent origination and liberation are not Confucian ritual
roles, and neither is identical to dao or ziran. Similar vocabulary in a modern
translation can conceal different arguments. "Harmony," "nature," "self," and
"virtue" must be interpreted within the source tradition before they are used
as bridges.

## Evidence

Evidence for a philosophical tradition is heterogeneous. It includes textual
transmission, manuscript history, philology, arguments reconstructed from
texts, records of institutions and practices, and empirical studies of modern
interventions or populations. These forms answer different questions. A
manuscript can change a claim about when a passage existed; it cannot by itself
show that the passage's ethical conclusion is true. A clinical trial can test a
standardized intervention in a defined population; it cannot validate karma,
non-self, nirvana, ren, or dao. Keeping those evidential levels separate is the
central safeguard for this topic.

The Buddhist record illustrates source limits. The Buddha entry in the Stanford
Encyclopedia dates the historical teacher approximately to 450 BCE and notes
that the surviving teachings were written down centuries later in languages he
probably did not speak and that the textual witnesses disagree in important
respects [1]. This supports careful reconstruction, not either total skepticism
or the claim that every canonical sentence is a verbatim transcript. It also
corrects the original topic's treatment of the Pali Canon as the single
foundation for all Buddhism. The Pali Tipitaka is the Theravada canon, while
Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions preserve and authorize additional bodies of
text and interpretation [2][15].

The Chinese record requires similar caution. Confucius is a historical and
symbolic figure mediated by texts compiled and interpreted across centuries;
modern scholarship can reconstruct coherent themes, but the exact boundaries
of "the philosophy of Confucius" are underdetermined [3]. The traditional
sixth-century BCE Laozi and single-author model of the *Laozi* are disputed,
and the oldest excavated witnesses show that the received text has a manuscript
history [7]. The *Zhuangzi* is a compilation containing strata associated with
more than one authorial setting [8]. These findings correct neat founder
stories while leaving the arguments available for philosophical evaluation.

The Axial Age supplies a case study in testing a large historical
interpretation. Mullins and colleagues compared proposed markers of axiality
across ten regions using a historical databank and quantitative analysis. They
found little support for a single, sharply localized transformation because
several markers occurred earlier or outside the canonical regions, although
some expected features did appear in the cases studied [13]. The study also
acknowledged that its twelve proxy measures were incomplete and sometimes
coarse [13]. The appropriate conclusion is conditional: "Axial Age" can
organize questions, but it should not be used as a precise date, a demonstrated
single cause, or proof that separate traditions made the same discovery.

Modern mindfulness research tests a much narrower claim than "Buddhism works."
Goyal and colleagues reviewed 17,801 citations and included 47 randomized trials
with active controls and 3,320 participants. Mindfulness programs showed small
improvements in anxiety and depression, with effect-size estimates of 0.22 to
0.38 for anxiety and 0.23 to 0.30 for depression across the reported follow-up
periods; evidence for several other outcomes was low, absent, or insufficient
[10]. The review identified weak registration and reporting, attrition,
allocation, blinding, and power problems, and it found no evidence that
meditation programs were better than exercise, drugs, or other active
behavioral therapies [10]. This corrects the original topic's unsupported
"moderate-to-large" summary and supports a modest clinical claim, not a verdict
on Buddhist liberation or metaphysics.

Neuroimaging evidence also requires restraint. Fox and colleagues combined 21
morphometric studies involving about 300 meditation practitioners and reported
an overall medium association between meditation and differences in brain
morphology, with candidate regions related to attention, awareness, memory,
and regulation [11]. The authors also described publication bias and
methodological limitations as strong concerns and called for more rigorous
research before a definitive causal link could be made [11]. Cross-sectional
differences cannot establish that meditation caused the anatomy, and a brain
correlate of self-referential processing does not prove the Buddhist doctrine
of non-self. The evidence supports research questions about trained attention
and self-processing, not metaphysical equivalence.

Cross-cultural psychology provides a direct test of the topic's East-West
binary. Vignoles and colleagues first studied 2,924 students in 16 nations and
then more than 7,000 adults from 55 cultural groups in 33 nations. Their
seven-dimensional model found that cultural groups combine different forms of
independence and interdependence; a single opposition between an independent
West and interdependent East did not adequately describe global variation [12].
The work supports some cultural variation while rejecting the claim that all
Western selves are autonomous and all East Asian selves relational. It also
shows why a philosophical text, a national average, and an individual person's
psychology must not be treated as the same level of evidence.

A final evidential case concerns the *Kalama Sutta*. Popular summaries cite it
as a charter of unrestricted private empiricism. Evans's close reading finds
that the audience asks which teacher is truthful and that the discourse offers
an ethical-practical test involving harmful consequences, knowledgeable
judgment, and eventual commitment, rather than a general method for discovering
all truth without authority [14]. This does not eliminate the text's challenge
to passive deference. It narrows the claim to what the passage can support and
shows why a memorable paraphrase cannot replace contextual reading.

Taken together, the evidence supports five conclusions. The traditions are
internally diverse; founder biographies and canons require source criticism;
classical concepts cannot be validated by loose modern analogies; some
contemplative interventions have modest measured effects; and broad East-West
psychological binaries fail empirical scrutiny [1][2][3][7][8][10][11][12].
None of these conclusions decides whether liberation, ren, or dao is the best
ideal. They determine which descriptions and inferences are defensible before
normative evaluation begins.

## Implications

For ethics, the traditions expand the unit of analysis. Buddhist ethics asks
how intention, craving, ignorance, compassion, and disciplined attention shape
suffering [1][2]. Confucian ethics asks how repeated practices and structured
relationships form character [3][4][5][6]. Daoist texts ask when forceful,
self-advertising, or rigidly classified action defeats its own purpose [7][8].
A practical framework can use all three questions without pretending that the
traditions agree: What mental conditions drive this action? What relationships
and practices formed it? Where might coercive effort create resistance or
unintended consequences? These are diagnostic prompts, not automatic answers.

For individual agency, the material challenges a false choice between an
isolated self and no agency at all. Buddhist non-self questions a permanent
controller while retaining ethical training and responsibility [1][2].
Confucian role ethics treats persons as formed within relationships but still
requires judgment, cultivation, and sometimes remonstrance [3][4][5].
*Zhuangzi* questions fixed perspectives while portraying skilled and adaptive
action [8]. A contemporary theory can learn from these models only if it also
protects persons from roles or institutions that deny voice, mobility, or
remedy. Relationality describes dependence and obligation; it does not by
itself justify every inherited hierarchy.

For families, schools, and organizations, Confucian cultivation directs
attention to the environments that make conduct habitual. Values become
credible when leaders model them, practices repeatedly embody them, and roles
include reciprocal duties [3][4][5][6]. This helps explain why a written code
without incentives, examples, correction, and practice often fails. The same
framework exposes a risk: ritual can normalize domination as easily as
excellence. The governing question is not merely whether a practice is
traditional or harmonious, but what capacities and relationships it builds,
whose interests it protects, and whether those subject to it can challenge it.

For leadership and policy, wu wei offers a warning against confusing visible
activity with effective action. Good intervention can be enabling, timed,
minimal, and responsive to the structure of a system rather than imposed for
display [7]. That insight resembles ideas in systems engineering and management,
but the resemblance is interpretive. Whether a regulation, control, or policy
is excessive must be tested with domain evidence. Some harms persist precisely
because authorities do too little. Daoist restraint is most useful as a
question about fit and second-order effects, not as a universal argument for
laissez-faire government.

For mental health, Buddhist-derived practices should be represented with both
respect and clinical precision. Standardized mindfulness programs are modern
interventions selected from and adapted out of larger ethical and contemplative
traditions. Evidence supports small improvements in some symptoms and does not
show superiority to every active treatment [10]. Clinical use should therefore
specify the population, protocol, comparator, expected magnitude, instructor
competence, and possible adverse or null outcomes. Conversely, reducing
Buddhism to stress management removes its ethical discipline, communal forms,
and liberation-oriented aims [1][2]. Both romanticization and extraction lose
important context.

For epistemology, the traditions recommend different checks on confidence.
Buddhist sources analyze how craving and ignorance distort experience [1][2].
Confucian texts emphasize learning, correction by others, and the formation of
judgment through practice [3][4]. *Zhuangzi* exposes dependence on perspective
and vocabulary [8]. Combined as an explicitly modern synthesis, these yield a
three-part audit: examine the motivation attached to the belief, seek correction
from competent relationships and records, and vary the perspective or framing.
This synthesis is the reviewer's interpretation; it is not a doctrine jointly
taught by the three traditions.

For comparative philosophy, the correct unit is a problem and an argument, not
a civilization. Buddhist no-self can be compared with a specified account of
personal identity. Confucian ren and li can be compared with a specified
virtue-ethical or role-ethical theory. Wu wei can be compared with a specified
account of skilled action or political restraint. Each comparison must preserve
asymmetries and counterexamples. Vignoles and colleagues show empirically that
even psychological models of selfhood require several dimensions, making a
one-axis East-West contrast inadequate [12]. Philosophical comparison deserves
at least the same discipline.

For political thought, none of the traditions licenses a shortcut from an
ancient concept to a modern state. Confucian exemplary rule does not by itself
settle constitutional design, individual rights, administrative accountability,
or democratic authorization [3][4][5][6]. Daoist suspicion of coercion does not
settle the need for public goods or regulation [7][8]. Buddhist compassion does
not specify a budget, legal institution, or distribution rule [2]. Ancient
frameworks can clarify values and failure modes, but institutional conclusions
require legal, economic, historical, and empirical evidence from the setting in
question.

For decision-making, the most durable lesson is procedural humility. A decision
maker should distinguish tradition from reconstruction, primary text from later
commentary, philosophical claim from empirical prediction, and analogy from
evidence. Sources should be chosen for the exact question: textual scholarship
for authorship, argument for coherence, trials for interventions, and
cross-cultural data for population claims. This prevents the central error of
the earlier account, which moved from classical ideas to modern neuroscience,
politics, and cultural psychology as if similarity of language established
identity or causation.

For investors and other forecasters, these distinctions can be converted into a
pre-decision check. A Buddhist lens asks whether attachment to an earlier thesis
is distorting the update; a Confucian lens asks whether incentives, exemplars,
and institutional routines are forming reliable judgment; a Daoist lens asks
whether an intervention is fighting the structure of the system and creating a
second-order cost. This is the reviewer's comparative application, not a claim
that the classical texts taught modern portfolio management. It becomes useful
only when paired with base rates, financial evidence, explicit alternatives,
and a record that can later show whether the analogy improved the decision.
Without those controls, philosophical vocabulary can rationalize a prior belief
as easily as it can correct one.

## Eastern and Western Philosophy: Divergent Paths to Wisdom

The contrast between "Eastern" and "Western" philosophy can orient a first
conversation, but it fails as a map of either side. Greek and Roman philosophy
included therapeutic ways of life, not only abstract truth seeking. Indian
Buddhist traditions developed rigorous argument, logic, and metaphysics, not
only practice. Chinese philosophers debated language, knowledge, human nature,
and political standards as well as harmony [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8][9]. The
claim that Western philosophy asks only whether an argument is sound while
Eastern philosophy asks only whether a practice works reverses a difference of
emphasis into a false exclusion.

A narrower comparison remains useful. Many modern European and North American
moral theories begin analytically with an agent, a choice, a rule, or an outcome.
Classical Confucian ethics often begins closer to roles, cultivated dispositions,
and concrete relationships [3][4]. Buddhist analysis can decompose the person
into conditioned processes and ask how identification with them produces
suffering [1][2]. Daoist texts can question the stable classifications and
purposive control assumed by the problem statement itself [7][8]. These moves
can reveal premises that another framework leaves implicit, but none represents
all thought in a hemisphere.

Comparisons with Western schools should therefore be treated as hypotheses.
Confucian cultivation and Aristotelian virtue ethics both emphasize formed
character, but their accounts of family, ritual, practical judgment, and the
good life differ [3][4]. Buddhist ethics can resemble virtue ethics in one
interpretation, but Buddhist traditions also contain consequential,
particularist, monastic, devotional, and bodhisattva-centered elements [2].
Daoist responsive skill may resemble Stoic accommodation or modern theories of
flow, but similarity does not establish common metaphysics, causal mechanisms,
or aims [7][8]. Each analogy clarifies only if the differences remain visible.

The category "West" is no more uniform than "East." Plato, Aristotle, the
Stoics, medieval theologians, empiricists, Kantians, pragmatists,
phenomenologists, and analytic philosophers disagree about self, reason,
practice, nature, and the purpose of philosophy. The same is true within every
tradition examined here. A comparative claim should name both comparands, cite
the relevant texts, and state whether it concerns historical influence,
conceptual similarity, practical function, or empirical consequence.

The reviewer's synthesis is that the three traditions are most valuable in
comparison when they resist compression. Buddhism makes conditioned suffering
and liberation central; Confucianism makes cultivated relationships and roles
central; Daoist texts make responsive naturalness and criticism of rigid ways
central [1][2][3][4][7][8]. Their disagreements create the learning value. A
reader gains more from asking which diagnosis fits a particular problem, what
evidence the diagnosis requires, and what each framework neglects than from
choosing a civilizational team.
