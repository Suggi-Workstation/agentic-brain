# Just War Theory -- Moral Permission Requires More Than a Just Cause

Just war theory asks when organized force can be morally initiated, how it may be used, and what justice requires when fighting ends. Its central constraint is cumulative: just cause does not excuse indiscriminate conduct, legal compliance does not by itself establish moral permissibility, and victory does not erase duties to surrendering fighters, civilians, or a defeated society [2][7][9]. The framework is therefore best understood as a demanding discipline of authority, necessity, proportionality, discrimination, responsibility, and just peace rather than a moral license for war [1][2][4].

## Background

Moral reflection on war developed from the recognition that two simple positions leave an important practical gap. Political realism doubts that ordinary morality can meaningfully govern contests for collective survival and power, while pacifism holds that war is always wrong or that actual wars cannot satisfy the conditions for justified violence. Just war theory occupies the disputed middle: it accepts a strong presumption against killing but asks whether force can sometimes defend people against wrongful aggression or atrocity, and, if so, which limits continue to bind every participant. The contemporary Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy account emphasizes that this middle path is not automatically successful; the possibility of a justified war may be real in principle yet nearly impossible under actual conditions [2][4].

The classical tradition did not begin as one fixed checklist. Augustine connected the use of force to public responsibility, correction of wrong, and the restoration of peace. Thomas Aquinas later gave an influential compact formulation: war requires public authority, just cause, and right intention. In Aquinas's account, a sovereign may use force to protect the common good, but authority and cause are not sufficient when the agent acts from cruelty, aggrandizement, or another corrupt intention [1]. These criteria placed war within ethics rather than outside it. They also created enduring questions: who possesses authority when institutions are unjust, what injuries are grave enough to justify war, and whether a stated intention to restore peace can be trusted when incentives reward conquest [1][2].

Later writers and state practice separated the moral evaluation of war into distinct but connected fields. Jus ad bellum evaluates the resort to and continuation of war. Jus in bello evaluates particular conduct within war. Jus post bellum evaluates termination, settlement, accountability, occupation, and reconstruction. The separation prevents a just cause from cleansing unjust methods and prevents an unjust cause from removing protections owed to its soldiers and civilians. The ICRC explains the corresponding legal distinction in especially strict terms: international humanitarian law applies to parties regardless of which side has the just cause, because conditioning protection on agreement about aggression would paralyze the humanitarian rules [9]. Moral theory can dispute whether the separation is ultimately defensible, but the legal neutrality of jus in bello serves a protective institutional purpose [5][9].

The modern legal order overlaps with, but does not duplicate, the moral tradition. Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter prohibits interstate threats or uses of force against territorial integrity or political independence, while Article 51 preserves individual and collective self-defense after an armed attack and Chapter VII gives the Security Council authority to act for international peace and security [8]. These are positive legal rules. Just war reasoning asks additional questions about moral justification, including whether lawful authority is legitimate, whether a defensive response is necessary and proportionate, whether a legally available operation is ethically reckless, and whether a settlement respects the rights of affected people. Conversely, an actor's sincere moral claim does not cancel legal obligations [2][8][9].

Twentieth-century just war theory was shaped by efforts to explain both the moral equality of combatants and civilian immunity. The traditional view treats soldiers on opposing sides as legal and, in an important institutional sense, moral equals when they obey the rules of war. That position allows a soldier who lacks reliable access to high-level political facts to fight without personally resolving every question of jus ad bellum, while making deliberate attacks on civilians impermissible. Revisionist theorists, led prominently by Jeff McMahan, challenge this symmetry. McMahan argues that war does not create a separate morality: unjust combatants normally lack permission to attack people who are defending against wrongful aggression, even though practical reasons may still support a legally neutral battlefield code [5]. The dispute exposes a central tension between deep moral liability and rules that ordinary institutions can administer fairly [2][5][6].

Contemporary conflict intensifies that tension. State and non-state forces fight through cities, coalitions, proxies, digital infrastructure, remotely operated systems, and increasingly autonomous functions. These conditions blur political authority, distribute causal responsibility, compress decision time, and entangle military objectives with civilian systems. They do not make the old principles obsolete. They make the factual predicates of authority, necessity, distinction, proportionality, and responsibility harder to establish. The ICRC's work on proxy support, cyber operations, urban explosive weapons, and autonomous weapons repeatedly identifies the same danger: capability can be distributed among actors while accountability and civilian protection fall through the gaps [13][14][15][16].

The author's synthesis is that just war theory should be treated as a sequence of burdens of justification rather than as a box-ticking permission structure. The burden begins before force is used, continues through each target and tactic, and persists through surrender, occupation, settlement, and repair. Failure at one stage cannot be cured merely by success at another: a lawful defensive war can contain wrongful attacks, a tactically restrained campaign can pursue an unjust aim, and a justified victory can be corrupted by revenge or negligent abandonment [2][7][9].

## Core Concepts

### Jus ad bellum: authority, cause, intention, prospects, proportionality, and necessity

Just cause identifies a wrong serious enough to make organized force potentially permissible. Defense against wrongful aggression is the paradigm case in both just war theory and the UN Charter framework, although philosophers dispute humanitarian intervention, defense of others, recovery of territory, and prevention of imminent attack [2][8]. Just cause supplies a reason in favor of force; it does not prove that force is the best or permissible response. An unlimited campaign can therefore be unjust even when its initiating grievance is real. The moral object must be specified narrowly enough to determine when the cause has been secured and when continued fighting ceases to be necessary [2][7].

Legitimate authority asks who may commit a political community to organized lethal force. Aquinas assigned this power to the sovereign because private persons can seek ordinary adjudication and cannot rightfully mobilize the whole community [1]. Modern theory cannot simply equate legitimacy with effective control or formal office. A regime may possess legal command while lacking representative legitimacy; a resistance movement may defend a population without recognition as a state; a coalition may divide command and responsibility; and a sponsor may enable a proxy while denying control. Authority is therefore both institutional and moral: the decision-maker needs a defensible mandate, competence, accountability, and a relationship to the people whose lives are placed at risk [1][2][16].

Right intention requires the war to be directed toward the just cause and a defensible peace rather than vengeance, territorial aggrandizement, domination, or profit. Mixed motives are common, so right intention cannot mean psychological purity. It should instead discipline objectives, operational plans, public commitments, and termination conditions. If the declared purpose is defense but the campaign is designed for permanent conquest or collective punishment, the mismatch is morally relevant even if some leaders also hold a defensive motive [1][2][7].

Reasonable prospects of success prevent futile violence. The criterion does not require certainty or equal military strength. It asks whether the proposed force has a plausible path to the just aim and whether failure, escalation, or state collapse would impose harms without compensating moral gain [2]. The assessment must be dynamic. A war that was initially capable of achieving a limited defensive objective can become impermissible when the objective is unattainable or when further fighting only postpones an inevitable settlement at mounting cost. This is why the ethics of war includes decisions to continue, limit, negotiate, and exit, not only the first decision to attack [2][7].

Ad bellum proportionality compares the morally weighted goods reasonably expected from the war with all relevant harms, including deaths, injury, displacement, institutional breakdown, escalation, environmental loss, and burdens transferred to future people. It is not a simple body-count subtraction. Rights, responsibility, distribution, probability, and reversibility affect moral weight. A defensive benefit for one population cannot automatically absorb unlimited harm to another, and speculative benefits cannot be valued like near-certain harms [2]. The author's synthesis is that an honest proportionality analysis should disclose scenarios, confidence, distribution, and excluded values rather than hide them in one aggregate score.

Last resort is better understood as necessity than as a chronological requirement to try every imaginable alternative. Force is unnecessary when a less harmful feasible option can secure the just cause with comparable reliability and acceptable delay. Diplomacy, sanctions, evacuation, defensive deployment, mediation, inspection, or limited protection may sometimes substitute for war; at other times they may be ineffective, slower than the threatened harm, or harmful in different ways. Necessity therefore requires comparison among real alternatives, not ritual proof that talks occurred. It also requires selecting the least harmful military means capable of achieving the legitimate objective [2].

### Jus in bello: distinction, necessity, proportionality, and precautions

Distinction separates persons and objects that may be attacked from those protected against direct attack. Customary international humanitarian law requires parties at all times to distinguish civilians from combatants and forbids attacks directed against civilians [10]. The ethical foundation is not that every combatant is guilty and every civilian blameless. Roles, threats, responsibility, coercion, and knowledge vary. The operational rule uses publicly recognizable categories because lethal decisions need a common protective standard under uncertainty. Civilians retain protection unless and for such time as they directly participate in hostilities, while wounded, captured, unconscious, shipwrecked, or clearly surrendering persons are hors de combat and may not be attacked [10][12].

Military necessity permits only force connected to a legitimate military purpose and does not override specific prohibitions. Destroying an object because it is inconvenient, frightening a population, or inflicting suffering for revenge does not become necessary through military labeling. At the tactical level, necessity asks whether the target and degree of force contribute to a defensible military aim and whether a less harmful effective method is available. At the moral level, revisionists add a harder question: whether the aim served by the particular attack is itself just. McMahan argues that an attack cannot gain moral proportionality merely by efficiently advancing an unjust cause [5].

In bello proportionality prohibits an attack expected to cause incidental civilian harm excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. The legal formulation protects civilians without claiming that every incidental death is unlawful [11]. Ethical proportionality can be more demanding because it asks how uncertain intelligence, distribution of risk, prior choices, alternatives, and the moral value of the military objective affect the comparison. Precision does not settle proportionality: a weapon can hit the selected point accurately while the selected attack remains excessive, unnecessary, or directed at the wrong object [2][11][15].

Precautions turn civilian protection from an after-the-fact ratio into a planning duty. Verification of targets, choice of timing and weapons, warnings when feasible, reassessment during execution, cancellation when assumptions fail, and attention to indirect effects all matter [3][15]. The doctrine of double effect helps explain why intentionally using civilian deaths as a means differs from foreseeing incidental civilian harm. Its traditional conditions require a permissible act, a good effect not produced through the bad effect, no intention of the bad effect, and a proportionately grave reason [3]. Yet foresight without intent is not sufficient. An attacker who predicts civilian deaths, ignores safer alternatives, and takes no precautions cannot obtain moral permission merely by describing those deaths as collateral [3].

Surrender marks a decisive change in moral status. A person who clearly expresses an intention to surrender, abstains from hostile acts, and does not attempt escape is hors de combat; attacking that person is prohibited under the customary rule identified by the ICRC [12]. This rule constrains revenge at the moment of greatest tactical dominance. It also shows why responsibility must be individualized. The fact that a unit, government, or movement committed grave wrongs does not erase protection for a person who no longer poses a battlefield threat [12].

### Responsibility from political leaders to individual operators

Responsibility is distributed but not diluted. Political leaders answer for the decision to initiate and continue war, commanders for plans and orders, intelligence personnel for the quality and presentation of information, operators for choices within their control, and institutions for training, weapons review, discipline, and investigation. Causal contribution alone does not establish identical blame: knowledge, authority, coercion, alternatives, and foreseeability matter. But complex chains should not become moral laundering in which every participant claims that someone else chose the target, objective, model, or escalation policy [2][5][14].

The traditional separation of ad bellum and in bello responsibility protects ordinary soldiers from being made judges of secret state policy and preserves equal humanitarian rules. Revisionists correctly note that obedience cannot make participation in aggression morally neutral in every case [5]. The author's synthesis is a layered standard. Soldiers are not presumed to possess leaders' information or freedom, but they retain duties to question manifestly wrongful orders, refuse direct attacks on civilians or surrendering persons, and seek clarification when the factual basis for lethal action is materially uncertain. Leaders bear a heavier burden because they define the cause, create the information environment, and decide whether subordinates face impossible moral choices [2][5].

### Competing moral frameworks and critiques

Consequentialism evaluates war by its expected effects on well-being and other valuable outcomes. It gives necessity, prospects of success, and proportionality central roles and can compare action with omission. Its strength is refusal to ignore predictable consequences. Its danger is that sufficiently large claimed benefits can be used to trade away individual rights, especially when decision-makers discount distant civilians or overestimate political gains. Rule-consequentialist defenses of distinction respond that stable protections and reciprocal restraint produce better results than case-by-case permission to target innocents [2][4].

Deontological approaches emphasize rights, duties, intention, and constraints. They explain why persons may not be used merely as instruments and why direct attacks on civilians, torture, or killing those who surrender remain wrong even under strategic pressure. Double effect is one attempt to distinguish intended harm from foreseen side effect without adopting absolute pacifism [3]. The difficulty is resolving conflicts among rights and explaining when grave threats permit overriding, though not erasing, a right. Revisionist just war theory is often deontological in this sense: liability to defensive harm depends on responsible contribution to an unjust threat, not on uniform alone [2][5].

Virtue ethics asks what practical wisdom, courage, justice, temperance, mercy, honesty, and fidelity require from people who wield organized force. It adds moral character to rules and outcomes. Courage without justice can become aggression; loyalty without practical wisdom can protect wrongdoing; restraint without courage can abandon people facing attack. Virtue cannot replace public criteria, because adversaries can praise incompatible warrior ideals. Its contribution is to explain why good institutions need agents able to perceive morally relevant facts, resist revenge and group hatred, accept risk for civilian protection, and stop when the just aim has been secured [1][2].

Pacifist critiques challenge the framework at its foundation. Absolute pacifism rejects war or lethal violence categorically. Consequentialist pacifism argues that war's actual costs and escalation dynamics defeat expected benefits. Deontological pacifism emphasizes the rights of combatants and civilians, including conscripts and others who bear little responsibility. Contingent or just-war pacifism accepts that force might be justified in theory but concludes that modern war, especially when civilians cannot be reliably protected, rarely or never meets the tests [4]. These critiques keep the presumption against war active; they prevent a list of criteria from becoming a routine administrative pathway to permission.

Realist critiques move in the opposite direction, questioning whether moral rules can govern states under severe insecurity. Descriptive realism stresses uncertainty, power, and survival; prescriptive realism can argue that leaders ought to prioritize state interest. Just war theory replies that prudence and morality need not be enemies and that even existential pressure cannot make every method permissible. The practical evidence for restraint is not perfect, but the existence of reciprocal legal rules, military doctrine, and repeated condemnation of atrocities shows that war is not treated institutionally as a wholly norm-free domain [2][9][10][11].

### Jus post bellum: ending war without converting victory into domination

Justice after war begins with termination. Once the just cause can be secured through settlement or the legitimate military objective has been achieved, continued violence requires a new necessity and proportionality justification. Gary Bass argues that the aims declared at the start of war generate obligations after combat and that revenge, collective punishment, unlimited conquest, and forced cultural transformation are inconsistent with a limited just war [7]. A settlement should distinguish responsible leaders and perpetrators from the population, protect basic rights, release persons no longer lawfully detained, and avoid demands whose severity plants the next conflict [7].

Occupation illustrates the difference between control and ownership. The law of occupation treats effective control as temporary and imposes duties toward the protected population, including humane treatment, respect for persons and family life, and maintenance of public order and essential conditions [17]. Moral duties can exceed minimum legal compliance. An occupying power whose actions destroyed institutions or essential services may owe repair, security, or assistance, but reconstruction must not become a license for indefinite rule or social engineering. Authority should move toward legitimate local institutions capable of protecting rights [7][17].

Accountability and reconciliation can conflict. War-crimes trials affirm individual responsibility and reject collective guilt, while amnesties or negotiated inclusion may sometimes be considered necessary to stop fighting. Jus post bellum does not supply one automatic formula. It requires transparent reasons for punishment, restoration, compensation, institutional reform, and compromise, with special attention to victims and people who lacked power over the war [7]. The author's synthesis is that a peace is not just because shooting stops; it is just to the extent that it removes the wrongful threat without institutionalizing revenge, domination, impunity, or foreseeable renewed violence.

## Evidence

### Classical criteria became a framework of cumulative constraints

Aquinas's Question 40 uses a structured disputation rather than empirical measurement. It states objections, cites contrary authority, and offers a reasoned resolution identifying sovereign authority, just cause, and right intention as conditions of a just war [1]. The method matters because the criteria are conjunctive: public authority cannot redeem wicked intention, and right intention cannot authorize a private war. Contemporary analytic philosophy expands the questions to prospects, necessity, proportionality, discrimination, and postwar duties while retaining the idea that several independent failures can defeat permission [2]. The textual record therefore supports a bounded historical finding: just war theory developed as an effort to constrain force by reasons, agents, means, and ends, not merely to identify a righteous side [1][2].

### Revisionist analysis exposes the responsibility problem

McMahan's 2004 article tests traditional combatant equality through analytic comparison with ordinary self-defense. His method asks whether a person defending against wrongful aggression loses the right not to be attacked merely by becoming a soldier, and whether an attacker gains permission because the violence occurs within war. He concludes that unjust combatants generally cannot satisfy deep moral requirements of necessity and proportionality when their acts advance no just cause, although he preserves pragmatic reasons for neutral laws of war [5]. The finding does not settle the debate, because institutionalists dispute whether interpersonal self-defense models can govern collective war. It does establish a durable challenge: a theory must explain why uniform, membership, or state authorization changes liability without making civilian immunity collapse [2][5].

Weiner applies this conflict to asymmetric warfare between states and non-state groups. His method compares the traditional moral equality of soldiers with legal and institutional conditions facing non-state fighters. He argues that some non-state fighters should receive war rights when violence has reached armed conflict and the opposing state cannot claim an uncontested monopoly of legitimate force, while insisting that attacks on civilians and other violations remain prosecutable [6]. This is a conditional argument, not evidence that every insurgent group possesses legitimate authority. It shows why legal status, moral cause, governance, and conduct must be assessed separately rather than inferred from the state or non-state label [6].

### Customary-law research shows institutional convergence around minimum protections

The ICRC Customary IHL Study uses treaties, military manuals, legislation, official statements, judicial decisions, and operational practice to identify rules accepted as customary law. Its Rule 1 survey finds broad support for the requirement to distinguish civilians from combatants and for the prohibition on directing attacks at civilians in international and non-international armed conflicts [10]. Rule 14 traces proportionality in attack through Additional Protocol I and state practice [11]. Rule 47 identifies the prohibition on attacking people recognized as hors de combat, including those in an adversary's power, rendered defenseless by wounds or sickness, or clearly surrendering [12].

This method does not prove that the rules are perfectly obeyed or philosophically sufficient. State statements can diverge from conduct, and legal categories do not resolve every moral dispute about liability. The finding is narrower and important: diverse institutions have converged on administrable minimum protections that do not depend on agreement about which side is just [9][10][11][12]. That convergence supports the institutionalist claim that neutral rules can protect people amid epistemic and political disagreement, while repeated violations show why doctrine must be translated into planning, training, investigation, and accountability.

### Urban warfare reveals the difference between nominal distinction and foreseeable protection

The ICRC's 2022 report on explosive weapons with wide-area effects combines first-hand evidence from multiple conflicts, technical analysis of blast, fragmentation, accuracy, and munition effects, review of IHL, and examination of military policy and practice [15]. It reports a recurring pattern: when heavy explosive weapons are used against objectives in populated areas, civilian deaths, injury, infrastructure damage, service disruption, displacement, and unexploded ordnance extend beyond the immediate target. The report documents, among other cases, 42 incidents involving heavy explosive weapons in Fallujah in 2016 that killed at least 115 civilians and injured 150, and it recommends an avoidance policy unless mitigation can sufficiently limit wide-area effects [15].

A separate ICRC follow-up study compared available fatality data and battle trends in four governorates in Iraq and Syria from March 2017 to July 2018. Its best estimates attributed 6,485 civilian deaths, or 78 percent of civilian fatalities in the studied areas, to urban offensives and found those offensives eight times more deadly for civilians than fighting elsewhere or ongoing lower-intensity fighting [18]. The study is geographically bounded and relies on available conflict data, so it does not establish a universal multiplier. Together, the studies support the ethical finding that foreseeable environment and weapon effects matter. Selecting a military object is not enough if the method predictably defeats distinction or imposes excessive and avoidable civilian harm [11][15][18].

### Cyber operations test what counts as harm, attack, and precaution

The ICRC's 2019 cyber position paper applies existing IHL categories to digital operations and separates an IHL "attack" from an "armed attack" under Article 51 of the UN Charter [13]. Its method is doctrinal and scenario-based: it examines civilian infrastructure, the military use of civilian networks, spread, attribution, and operations that disable functionality without physical destruction. The paper finds that IHL applies to cyber means during armed conflict while acknowledging unsettled questions, including the treatment of civilian data and whether loss of functionality alone qualifies as damage or attack [13].

The ethical implication reaches beyond legal classification. A cyber operation against power, water, medical, financial, or communications systems can impose distributed and delayed harm while appearing bloodless at the point of execution. Necessity and proportionality therefore require mapping dependencies, propagation, recovery time, and vulnerable populations, not only physical destruction. The author's synthesis is that digital distance cannot reduce responsibility when civilian effects are foreseeable; it instead raises the burden of technical understanding and containment [3][13].

### Autonomous weapons test whether responsibility can survive delegated selection

The ICRC's 2021 position analyzes weapon systems that select and apply force after activation without human intervention in the specific target decision. It evaluates predictability, limits on effects, civilian protection, treatment of persons hors de combat, and the ethical significance of human control. The ICRC recommends legally binding rules that prohibit unpredictable autonomous weapons, rule out systems designed or used to target persons, and restrict other systems by target type, duration, geographic scope, scale, situation, supervision, intervention, and deactivation [14].

This is a normative and technical risk assessment, not a finding that every automated function is unlawful. Its contribution is to identify a responsibility threshold: an operator cannot make a context-sensitive distinction, proportionality, surrender, or precaution judgment if the system's target set and effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted, or bounded [12][14]. The evidence supports a general principle for just war theory: delegation is permissible only when human institutions retain enough knowledge and control to own the moral decision rather than attributing it retrospectively to software.

### Proxy relationships show how support can diffuse or extend responsibility

The ICRC defines a support relationship as assistance that increases a party's capacity to conduct armed conflict and examines political backing, partnered operations, arms transfers, finance, hosting, training, and other forms of support [16]. Its method draws on operational engagement and uses a decision framework to identify how supporters can influence preparation, conduct, and aftermath. The finding is two-sided: poorly coordinated relationships can diffuse responsibility and increase risk to civilians, but leverage over a partner can also improve compliance and protection [16].

For just war theory, the important result is that distance from the trigger does not end moral inquiry. A sponsor should assess the recipient's cause, authority, record, target process, detention practice, end state, and the sponsor's capacity to condition, monitor, suspend, or repair support. The author's synthesis is that responsibility tracks knowledgeable contribution and control: supplying intelligence, weapons, finance, or political cover can create duties even when the supporter does not issue every tactical order [5][16].

## Implications

### For decisions to begin or continue war

Political leaders should publish a bounded account of the just cause, legal basis, intended political end state, feasible alternatives, expected civilian and regional harms, success conditions, and termination conditions. Security-sensitive facts can require protected review rather than full disclosure, but secrecy should not erase accountable evaluation. The comparison must include non-action, delay, diplomacy, defensive measures, and limited force rather than contrasting the preferred campaign with an unrealistically passive alternative [2][8].

A decision record should separate law from ethics. A Security Council authorization or self-defense claim addresses legal authority; it does not prove that the proposed scale, timing, objectives, or aftermath are morally proportionate. Conversely, a humanitarian moral argument does not itself establish a lawful exception to the Charter. Keeping the claims separate prevents decision-makers from borrowing the authority of one domain to conceal a failure in the other [2][8][9].

Continuation should be reviewed at specified thresholds. Material changes in achievable aims, civilian harm, alliance structure, adversary conduct, escalation risk, or settlement opportunities can alter necessity and proportionality. The worst failure is to treat the original justification as a permanent authorization after its factual basis has changed. A review should identify which objective remains just, whether force is still causally connected to it, whether a less harmful settlement can secure it, and who bears the cost of waiting [2][7].

### For commanders and civilian-protection systems

Commanders should treat civilian protection as part of mission design rather than as a final collateral-damage calculation. Target development should include civilian presence, time patterns, infrastructure dependencies, displacement routes, medical capacity, indirect effects, and uncertainty. Weapon selection should consider blast, fragmentation, accuracy, failure modes, reverberating effects, and whether another tactic can achieve the same military aim at lower expected harm [11][15]. Warnings and evacuation routes do not turn remaining civilians into lawful targets, and a defender's violation does not cancel the attacker's own duties [10][11][15].

Rules of engagement should make surrender observable and actionable. Forces need training for ambiguous gestures, language barriers, wounded adversaries, remote sensors, and situations in which accepting surrender creates tactical risk. Autonomous or highly automated systems should not be used where they cannot reliably recognize protected persons or where operators cannot intervene in time [12][14]. Recording the basis of target and engagement decisions improves learning and accountability, but records must show the actual alternatives and uncertainty rather than merely certify a conclusion.

Urban operations require an explicit presumption against weapons with wide-area effects unless mitigation narrows their impact and the operation still passes distinction, necessity, proportionality, and precaution. This is stronger than assuming that technical accuracy equals ethical precision. Civilian infrastructure is interconnected: damaging power can disable water, health care, communications, and sanitation. Plans should model these chains, preserve repair access, and reassess an attack when the civilian system has already been degraded by prior operations [15][18].

### For soldiers, intelligence personnel, engineers, and advisers

Individuals need role-specific moral questions. A soldier asks whether the person remains a threat or is surrendering. An intelligence analyst asks whether uncertainty and alternative hypotheses are visible to the decision-maker. A weapons engineer asks whether effects can be predicted, limited, explained, and interrupted. A legal adviser asks what law permits; an ethical adviser also asks whether the permitted choice is necessary and proportionate under the best available moral reasons. A political leader asks whether the entire chain serves a just and attainable peace [2][3][12][14].

Moral courage includes refusing manifestly wrongful action, but institutions must not make refusal the only safeguard. Clear doctrine, realistic training, protected reporting channels, independent investigation, and command climates that reward uncertainty reporting reduce the pressure to choose between obedience and conscience. Virtue matters because no rule anticipates every battlefield fact; systems matter because character alone cannot correct incentives, secrecy, fatigue, or distributed technical decisions [1][2].

The author's synthesis is that responsibility should be proportional to knowledge, control, and role while never becoming zero merely because a chain is long. Engineers and data teams who know that a targeting system cannot perform as represented have duties to surface the limitation. Intelligence partners who know that a proxy repeatedly misuses support have duties to change the support decision. Commanders who inherit a lawful plan have duties to cancel or modify it when battlefield facts defeat its assumptions [5][14][16].

### For proxy warfare and coalitions

Supporters should establish conditions before providing arms, intelligence, training, finance, hosting, or operational assistance. Conditions should cover target verification, civilian-harm tracking, detention, surrender, investigation, information access, suspension triggers, and post-conflict obligations. Monitoring should follow the support to its operational use. A clause that exists only in an agreement does not discharge responsibility when credible evidence shows predictable misuse [16].

Coalitions also need clear ownership of cumulative effects. One actor may select targets, another supply coordinates, another provide the weapon, and another approve the political objective. Each can perform its assigned task correctly while the combined operation is wrongful. Joint review should therefore reconstruct the full causal and decision chain, including who could stop the attack and who possessed decisive information. The author's synthesis is that shared action should increase scrutiny rather than divide accountability into fragments [5][16].

Non-state status should neither automatically deny moral standing nor confer it. A movement's representative relationship, internal accountability, objectives, treatment of civilians, compliance capacity, and willingness to accept reciprocal restraint are relevant. Legal combatant status remains a distinct question governed by positive law. Weiner's asymmetric analysis shows why the ethics cannot be resolved merely by awarding all legitimacy to the recognized state, while civilian immunity and the prohibition on atrocities remain binding regardless of the movement's political claim [6][10].

### For cyber operations and autonomous systems

Cyber target review should map civilian dependencies, propagation routes, recovery assumptions, data integrity, and effects that arise without physical damage. Operations designed to remain below an armed-attack threshold can still be morally serious when they disable hospitals, water, finance, or communications. Reversibility is relevant but must be demonstrated: a claimed temporary disruption may destroy trust, corrupt backups, or cause irreversible physical consequences [13]. Where effects cannot be bounded, necessity favors narrower tools, delayed execution, or non-cyber alternatives.

Autonomous functions should be constrained by the contexts in which their effects can be understood and supervised. Geographic limits, duration limits, target-type constraints, human confirmation, abort mechanisms, testing, auditability, and post-use review are not substitutes for moral judgment; they are conditions that can make judgment operational [14]. Systems that target persons without reliable contextual assessment create special problems because combatancy, surrender, incapacity, and civilian status can change faster than a generalized profile can represent [12][14].

The worst-case failure is a lethal effect that no accountable person understood, intended in its specific form, or could stop, followed by an investigation in which each participant points to another layer. Preventing that failure requires assigning a named human authority for the operational envelope, the target policy, the decision to activate, monitoring, intervention, and termination. If those assignments cannot be made honestly, the system should not be used [14].

### For war termination, occupation, and peace settlement

Planning for the aftermath should begin before force is authorized. The political objective determines which institutions must survive, what security must be restored, which displaced people can return, how detainees will be handled, and when authority will transfer. A plan built only for battlefield victory can make success self-defeating by producing avoidable disorder, revenge, or indefinite dependency [7][17]. Resource estimates for repair, policing, courts, essential services, and demobilization belong in the original proportionality assessment.

Settlement terms should remain connected to the just cause. Defense can justify ending aggression and securing credible safeguards; it does not automatically justify annexation, permanent occupation, regime exploitation, or collective impoverishment. Accountability should focus on responsible persons and fair procedures rather than populations. Restoration should prioritize people harmed and institutions needed for a rights-respecting peace, while cultural and political transformation requires local legitimacy and strict limits [7][17].

Negotiation is not necessarily betrayal of justice. Continuing war can become unnecessary when a settlement secures the defensible objective at lower cost, and compromise may be required to stop ongoing harm. Yet peace at any price can entrench conquest or abandon vulnerable people. The author's synthesis is a two-part test: the settlement must remove or sufficiently constrain the wrongful threat, and its terms must not impose new domination or predictable renewed violence. Where those aims cannot both be fully achieved, decision-makers should state the unresolved injustice and create review, monitoring, protection, and remedy mechanisms rather than declare a morally complete victory [2][7].

### A practical just-war review

A disciplined review can use four linked records. First, the ad bellum record states the wrong, authority, aim, alternatives, prospects, expected harms, and exit conditions. Second, the in bello record states the target, military contribution, intelligence confidence, civilian and indirect effects, alternatives, precautions, and cancellation criteria. Third, the responsibility record identifies who knew, decided, enabled, supervised, and could intervene. Fourth, the post bellum record covers termination, surrender, detention, occupation, repair, accountability, political transfer, and the conditions of a stable peace [2][7][10][11][12].

No record supplies moral certainty. Its purpose is to make reasons testable and to prevent one favorable label -- self-defense, precision, collateral, autonomous, proxy, victory -- from doing the work of an argument. The author's assessment is that a proposed war or attack is strongest when it survives independent review under consequentialist, deontological, virtue-ethical, legal-institutional, and pacifist objections, and weakest when its permissibility depends on optimistic forecasts, hidden civilian costs, diluted responsibility, or an undefined end state [2][3][4][5].

Just war theory ultimately imposes a paradoxical discipline: force can be permissible only when those who wield it remain oriented toward the peace in which their exceptional permission ends. Authority must be accountable, necessity must be comparative, proportionality must include those least able to shape the decision, distinction must survive fear and anger, and victory must terminate rather than expand the license to harm. These conditions do not make war good. They specify why even a morally serious resort to force remains tragic, limited, and continuously answerable to the people exposed to it [1][2][7].
