# Free Will and Determinism -- Why the Debate About Whether We Choose Our Actions Remains Philosophy's Most Consequential Unresolved Question

The free will debate asks whether human beings genuinely choose their
actions or whether every decision is the inevitable result of prior
causes beyond our control. This is not an academic curiosity -- it is
the foundation upon which moral responsibility, criminal justice,
praise, blame, and our deepest sense of self all rest. Four centuries
after the modern debate was joined, philosophers, neuroscientists, and
legal theorists remain divided across four irreconcilable positions,
and the question is no closer to resolution than it was when Spinoza
first declared free will an illusion.

## Background

The free will problem in its modern form crystallized during the
Scientific Revolution. As Newtonian mechanics revealed a universe
governed by deterministic physical laws -- every state following
inexorably from the previous state -- philosophers recognized an
uncomfortable implication: if the laws of physics determine every
physical event, and human decisions are physical events in the brain,
then human decisions are determined. There is no room for a
"free choice" that could have gone differently.

The ancient Greeks had already glimpsed the problem. Aristotle worried
about the "sea battle" paradox -- if it is true today that there will
be a sea battle tomorrow, does that mean the sea battle is inevitable?
The Stoics resolved this by embracing determinism while insisting that
human attitudes remained within our control: we cannot choose what
happens, but we can choose how we respond. Epicurus, by contrast,
introduced an element of randomness -- the "swerve" (clinamen) of
atoms -- to create space for free will in an otherwise deterministic
atomic physics.

The Enlightenment sharpened the debate into its modern form. Baruch
Spinoza (1632-1677) articulated a rigorous determinism: "Men think
themselves free because they are conscious of their volitions and
desires, but are ignorant of the causes by which they are led to wish
and desire." David Hume (1711-1776) took the opposite view, arguing
that freedom and determinism are compatible -- what matters for freedom
is not whether our actions are caused, but whether they are caused by
our own desires and character rather than external coercion. Immanuel
Kant (1724-1804) attempted to split the difference: in the phenomenal
world (the world as it appears to us), determinism reigns; but in the
noumenal world (the world as it is in itself), we may be free. Kant's
solution preserved moral responsibility at the cost of making freedom
unknowable.

The 20th century saw the debate transformed by three developments.
First, quantum mechanics undermined Laplacean determinism -- at the
subatomic level, events are probabilistic rather than deterministic,
raising the question of whether quantum indeterminacy provides the
opening that libertarian free will requires. Second, neuroscience
entered the fray: Benjamin Libet's experiments in the 1980s appeared
to show that the brain initiates action before we become consciously
aware of deciding, threatening even compatibilist accounts of freedom.
Third, P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) reframed the
debate around interpersonal attitudes rather than metaphysical
questions, arguing that we cannot give up holding each other
responsible regardless of what physics says.

## Core Positions

The free will debate is structured by two questions: Is determinism
true? And is free will compatible with determinism? The answers
produce four major positions, though the boundaries between them are
contested.

### Hard Determinism

Hard determinists answer "yes" to the first question and "no" to the
second. Determinism is true, free will is incompatible with
determinism, and therefore free will does not exist. Every human
action is the inevitable product of genetics, environment, and prior
causes stretching back before the agent was born. The hard determinist
position traces from Spinoza through the French materialist Baron
d'Holbach (1723-1789), who wrote in "The System of Nature" (1770):
"Man's life is a line that nature commands him to describe upon the
surface of the earth, without his ever being able to swerve from it,
even for an instant."

In contemporary philosophy, hard determinism has evolved into "hard
incompatibilism," most rigorously defended by Derk Pereboom in "Living
Without Free Will" (2001). Pereboom argues that even if determinism is
false -- even if quantum indeterminacy introduces randomness at the
micro level -- the kind of free will required for moral responsibility
is still impossible. Randomness does not give control; it replaces
determination with chance, which is no basis for holding someone
responsible. Pereboom's hard incompatibilism thus holds that free will
and moral responsibility are impossible regardless of whether
determinism is true.

The hard determinist faces a difficult practical question: if no one
is morally responsible, what do we do with the criminal justice
system? Pereboom advocates replacing retributive punishment (which
requires the offender to "deserve" suffering) with a forward-looking
quarantine model: we detain dangerous people to protect society, just
as we quarantine contagious patients, but we do not blame them. Galen
Strawson goes further in a 1986 paper to argue that true moral
responsibility is logically impossible -- you would need to be
responsible for the way you are, which would require you to have
created yourself, an infinite regress. Saul Smilansky takes the
pragmatic position that while free will is an illusion, we should
maintain the illusion because society functions better when people
believe they are responsible for their actions.

### Libertarianism (Metaphysical)

Libertarians about free will answer "no" to determinism and "yes" to
free will. They hold that genuine free choice requires indeterminism
-- that at the moment of decision, the future is genuinely open, and
the agent, not the prior causal chain, settles which way it goes. This
is the position closest to the ordinary person's intuition about free
will.

Libertarianism splits into two major varieties. Event-causal
libertarianism, defended by Robert Kane (1996), holds that free
actions are caused by mental events (beliefs, desires, deliberations)
in a probabilistic rather than deterministic way. Kane locates
indeterminism in "self-forming actions" -- rare, character-defining
moments where an agent faces conflicting motivations and either choice
would be consistent with their values. In these moments, the
indeterminacy is not randomness but genuine authorship: the agent
resolves their internal conflict and in doing so creates the self that
will face future choices more deterministically. The objection to
event-causal libertarianism is the "luck problem": if the action is
genuinely undetermined by prior mental states, then which way it goes
is a matter of luck, and luck is not freedom.

Agent-causal libertarianism, defended by Roderick Chisholm (1964) and
Timothy O'Connor (2000), takes a more radical approach. It posits that
agents themselves -- not events in their brains -- are irreducible
causes. An agent-cause is a substance (the person) directly causing an
effect without being caused to do so by prior events. This avoids the
luck problem because the agent, not a random event, settles the
choice. But it introduces a new difficulty: how does agent-causation
work? It seems to require a non-physical self that intervenes in the
physical causal chain, a position many philosophers find
metaphysically extravagant.

Both forms of libertarianism face the problem of explaining why
indeterminism at the neural level would produce freedom rather than
randomness. As the philosopher Alfred Mele has argued, merely showing
that brain processes are indeterministic does not show that agents
control them -- and control is what distinguishes freedom from chance.

### Compatibilism

Compatibilists answer "yes" to determinism and "yes" to free will.
They argue that free will and determinism are compatible because free
will does not require the ability to do otherwise in exactly the same
circumstances -- it only requires that the action flows from the
agent's own desires, character, and reasoning, unconstrained by
external coercion.

Classical compatibilism, articulated by Hobbes and Hume, defines
freedom simply as the absence of external impediments: "a free man is
he that in those things which by his strength and wit he is able to do
is not hindered to do what he has a will to." On this view, a
determined action is free as long as no one is holding a gun to your
head. The water flows downhill by necessity, but it flows freely if
the channel is not blocked.

Contemporary compatibilism is more sophisticated. Harry Frankfurt's
(1971) hierarchical account distinguishes between first-order desires
(impulses, cravings) and second-order desires (desires about which
desires we want to act on). A drug addict who wants to quit but cannot
resist the craving is not free, even though no one coerces them,
because their second-order desire (to quit) is ineffective against
their first-order desire (to use). A free agent is one whose
second-order volitions -- the desires they want to be effective --
actually govern their actions. Freedom, on this account, is about the
structure of the will, not about breaking the causal chain.

Frankfurt also attacked the "Principle of Alternative Possibilities"
(PAP) -- the idea that an agent is morally responsible only if they
could have done otherwise. In his famous counterexample, Jones decides
to shoot Smith, but unbeknownst to Jones, a neuroscientist named Black
has implanted a device that would force Jones to shoot Smith if Jones
hesitated. Since Jones shoots Smith on his own, without Black's
intervention, Jones is responsible -- even though, with Black's device
in place, Jones could not have done otherwise. If this argument
succeeds, it severs the link between alternative possibilities and
moral responsibility, undermining a key incompatibilist intuition.

P.F. Strawson's (1962) "Freedom and Resentment" offered a different
compatibilist strategy. Strawson argued that the debate had become
trapped in abstract metaphysics and had lost sight of what actually
matters: our interpersonal practices of holding each other
responsible. We cannot, as a matter of psychological fact, give up the
"reactive attitudes" -- resentment, gratitude, indignation, forgiveness
-- that constitute our moral relationships. These attitudes do not
depend on any metaphysical belief about determinism; they depend on
whether the person acted from ill will, negligence, or good faith. The
question "is determinism true?" is irrelevant to whether Jones should
resent Smith for a betrayal, because resentment is a natural human
response to perceived ill will, not a philosophical conclusion.

Daniel Dennett's compatibilism takes an evolutionary approach: the
kind of freedom worth wanting is not metaphysical contra-causal
freedom but the practical capacity to anticipate consequences, learn
from experience, and adjust behavior. Evolved creatures develop
increasingly sophisticated control systems, and human beings sit at
the apex of this development with language, foresight, and culture.
Dennett argues that libertarians want a kind of freedom that is not
only impossible but incoherent -- "a puppet with no strings is not a
free agent; it is a puppet that has fallen down."

### Hard Incompatibilism

A fourth position, distinct from classical hard determinism, has
gained prominence. Hard incompatibilism, associated with Pereboom and
Galen Strawson, holds that free will is incompatible with both
determinism and indeterminism. If determinism is true, we lack
alternative possibilities. If indeterminism is true, our actions are
partly random, and randomness is not freedom. Either way, the kind of
free will required for "basic desert" moral responsibility -- the idea
that someone genuinely deserves to suffer for a wrong act -- is
impossible. This position is distinct from determinism because it does
not depend on determinism being true; it claims that the very concept
of ultimate moral responsibility is incoherent regardless of how
physics turns out.

## Neuroscience and the Free Will Debate

The empirical turn in the free will debate began with Benjamin Libet's
experiments in the early 1980s. Libet asked subjects to flex their
wrist at a time of their own choosing while watching a rapidly
rotating clock and recording when they became aware of the "urge" to
move. Simultaneously, he measured brain activity via EEG. Libet found
that a "readiness potential" -- a slow buildup of electrical activity
in the supplementary motor area -- began approximately 550
milliseconds before the movement, while the conscious awareness of the
decision occurred only about 200 milliseconds before the movement. The
brain, it seemed, had already begun preparing the action roughly 350
milliseconds before the subject consciously "decided."

Libet himself interpreted the results cautiously. He noted that while
the brain initiates the action unconsciously, the conscious mind
retains a "veto power" -- the ability to abort the action in the final
200 milliseconds before execution. Free will, on Libet's view, is not
the initiation of action but the capacity to say no.

Later experiments appeared to strengthen the case against free will.
John-Dylan Haynes (2008) used fMRI to show that the outcome of a
simple decision (pressing a left or right button) could be predicted
from brain activity up to 7-10 seconds before the subject reported
making the decision. This suggested that even the content of a
decision -- not just the timing -- is determined by unconscious brain
processes before we become aware of it. However, the prediction
accuracy was only about 60%, significantly above chance (50%) but far
from deterministic.

The neuroscientific case against free will has faced substantial
criticism. First, Libet-style experiments study trivial, arbitrary
decisions (when to flex a wrist, which button to press) that bear
little resemblance to the deliberative, reason-responsive choices that
matter for moral responsibility. No one's criminal trial turns on when
they decided to flex their wrist. Second, Schurger et al. (2012)
showed that the readiness potential may not represent a pre-conscious
"decision" at all, but rather the natural behavior of a neural
accumulator crossing a threshold -- spontaneous fluctuations in neural
activity that are time-locked to the movement not because they cause
it, but because they happen to coincide with it in the analysis.
Third, neurosurgical evidence from patients who had their supplementary
motor area (the source of the readiness potential) surgically removed
shows that they retain normal conscious decision-making capacity; the
SMA syndrome involves temporary motor deficits, not a loss of
volitional control. The readiness potential, in this view, is a motor
preparation signal, not a neural "decision."

A synthesized assessment: neuroscience has demonstrated that
unconscious brain processes influence and precede conscious awareness
of decisions in ways that challenge naive libertarian intuitions. But
it has not demonstrated that conscious deliberation is epiphenomenal
or that the kind of freedom compatibilists defend -- reason-responsive
action flowing from stable character -- is threatened by these
findings. The neuroscientific challenge is strongest against strong
libertarian positions and weakest against compatibilism and Strawsonian
accounts.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

The free will debate draws on evidence from philosophy, neuroscience,
and social psychology, though the "evidence" is often interpretative
rather than dispositive -- it shapes the debate without resolving it.

The philosophical evidence consists of arguments rather than data. The
Consequence Argument, formalized by Peter van Inwagen (1983), is the
strongest case for incompatibilism: if determinism is true, our acts
are the consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote
past, and we have no more control over the laws of nature or the
remote past than we have over the sunrise. Therefore, if determinism
is true, we have no control over our acts. Frankfurt cases (1969)
provide the strongest counterargument: if Jones would have acted the
same way regardless of Black's intervention, and Black did not
actually intervene, Jones is responsible despite lacking alternative
possibilities.

The neuroscientific evidence is reviewed above. Libet's (1983)
readiness potential experiments, Haynes's (2008) fMRI prediction
studies, and Schurger's (2012) stochastic accumulator reinterpretation
form the empirical core. A 2016 study by Soon et al. extended
Haynes's findings to more abstract decisions, showing that the brain's
"default mode network" activity patterns could predict choices between
addition and subtraction tasks several seconds before the conscious
decision. These findings collectively suggest that unconscious brain
processes set the stage for conscious decisions, but the interpretation
of this evidence remains fiercely contested -- it is compatible with
both hard determinism and sophisticated compatibilism.

The social psychology evidence addresses a different question: do
people's beliefs about free will affect their behavior? Kathleen Vohs
and Jonathan Schooler (2008) found that subjects who read passages
arguing that free will is an illusion were subsequently more likely to
cheat on a test and less likely to help a stranger. Subsequent
replication attempts have produced mixed results, with some studies
confirming the effect and others finding no relationship between free
will beliefs and moral behavior. A meta-analysis by Genschow et al.
(2021) found a small but significant effect: weakening belief in free
will modestly increases antisocial behavior and decreases helpfulness.
Additional studies by Baumeister et al. (2009) found that disbelief
in free will reduced self-control and increased aggressive behavior,
while Rigoni et al. (2012) showed that inducing free will disbelief
altered the readiness potential itself, suggesting that beliefs about
free will feed back into the very neural mechanisms that inform the
debate. This raises a pragmatic question with no clean answer: if
free will skepticism has negative social consequences, should we
promote belief in free will regardless of its metaphysical truth?
Saul Smilansky (2000) has argued that we should -- the "illusion" of
free will is socially indispensable.

## Implications

The free will debate matters because our entire system of moral and
legal responsibility assumes that people can choose their actions.
Four domains are particularly affected.

**Criminal justice.** Retributive justice -- the idea that criminals
deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrongdoing -- presupposes
that the criminal could have done otherwise. If hard incompatibilism
is correct, retribution is morally indefensible. This does not mean
abandoning the criminal justice system; it means transforming its
justification. We would still detain dangerous individuals (public
safety), but we would do so without the moral condemnation that
accompanies punishment. Pereboom's quarantine model and the public
health approach to crime -- treating criminal behavior as a social
problem to be managed rather than a sin to be punished -- follow
naturally from free will skepticism. The Norwegian and some Northern
European prison systems, which emphasize rehabilitation over
retribution, already approximate this model without explicitly
endorsing free will skepticism. In practice, most legal systems adopt
a compatibilist compromise: they hold people responsible when their
actions flow from their character and choices (as opposed to duress,
insanity, or automatism) while acknowledging that character and
choices are ultimately shaped by forces the person did not choose.

**Moral responsibility in everyday life.** If we accept hard
incompatibilism, what happens to gratitude, resentment, pride, and
guilt? These "reactive attitudes" are the fabric of our moral lives.
Strawson argued that we cannot and should not abandon them regardless
of what metaphysics says -- they are constitutive of human
relationships, not philosophical conclusions we can revise at will.
But this leaves a tension: we continue to resent and praise even while
acknowledging that resentment and praise may be metaphysically
unjustified. One resolution is to distinguish between "basic desert"
responsibility (the strong metaphysical claim that someone ultimately
deserves what they get) and "forward-looking" responsibility (the
practical claim that holding people accountable shapes future
behavior). We can abandon the first while retaining the second --
blame and praise become tools for shaping behavior rather than
judgments about ultimate desert.

**Personal identity and meaning.** The belief in free will is
intimately connected to our sense of self as authors of our lives. If
our choices are determined by factors we did not choose -- genes,
childhood environment, social context, random neural events -- then in
what sense are we the authors of anything? Compatibilists answer that
authorship does not require being the uncaused cause of oneself; it
requires that one's actions reflect one's values, reasoning, and
character. A painting is still "by" an artist even if the artist's
style was shaped by their teachers. The anxiety about authorship
persists, however, because incompatibilist intuitions run deep: the
sense that "I could have done otherwise" is phenomenologically vivid,
and compatibilist redefinitions can feel like changing the subject.

**Technology and manipulation.** The free will debate has practical
urgency in an age of algorithmic persuasion. If human choices are
determined by inputs, then whoever controls the inputs -- social media
algorithms, targeted advertising, recommendation engines -- exercises
a form of control over human behavior. The more we understand the
neural and psychological determinants of choice, the more precisely we
can manipulate them. This is the dark side of determinism: not just
that free will may be an illusion, but that the illusion can be
exploited. A society that takes determinism seriously must confront
the ethics of behavioral engineering.
