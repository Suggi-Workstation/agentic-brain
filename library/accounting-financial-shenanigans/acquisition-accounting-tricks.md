# Acquisition Accounting Tricks -- How Deal Structure, Purchase Price Allocation, and Earnouts Distort Reality

Acquisitions are the most complex single event in corporate accounting, and that complexity is precisely what makes them the richest hunting ground for earnings manipulation. When a company buys another business, the purchase price must be allocated across identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value, with any residual recorded as goodwill. Every allocation decision -- how much to assign to intangible assets versus goodwill, how to value contingent earnout payments, how aggressively to write down acquired inventory or reserves -- flows through the financial statements for years afterward. Howard Schilit identified acquisition accounting as one of the most persistent categories of financial shenanigans, and serial acquirers from Tyco to WorldCom demonstrated how the opacity of deal accounting can mask fundamental business deterioration for extended periods.

## Background

The history of acquisition accounting manipulation is inseparable from the evolution of the accounting standards that govern business combinations. Before 2001, U.S. GAAP allowed two methods for recording acquisitions: the purchase method and the pooling-of-interests method. The pooling method, which combined the balance sheets of the acquirer and target at historical book values with no goodwill recognition, was heavily abused. Companies engineered deal structures to qualify for pooling treatment, avoiding the goodwill amortization that the purchase method required. The Financial Accounting Standards Board eliminated pooling in 2001 with FAS 141, requiring all business combinations to use the purchase method (now the acquisition method under ASC 805). But eliminating one manipulation vector opened others.

Under the purchase method, the acquirer had to allocate the purchase price to the fair value of identifiable assets and liabilities, with the excess recorded as goodwill. Before 2001, goodwill was amortized over a period up to 40 years. Companies routinely used the maximum amortization period to minimize the annual earnings hit. In 2001, FAS 142 (now ASC 350) abolished goodwill amortization in favor of annual impairment testing. This change was meant to make goodwill reflect real economic value rather than a decaying accounting artifact. In practice, as documented in the companion topic on goodwill impairment games, it gave companies a powerful tool to defer the recognition of value destruction indefinitely.

The purchase price allocation process itself became a manipulation vector. ASC 805 (and its international equivalent, IFRS 3) requires acquirers to identify and value every identifiable intangible asset at fair value -- customer relationships, trade names, technology, in-process research and development, non-compete agreements. Only after all identifiable intangibles are valued does the residual become goodwill. This creates a critical choice: allocate more to identifiable intangibles, which are amortized and reduce future earnings, or allocate more to goodwill, which under ASC 350 is not amortized and is only tested for impairment annually. Research by Shalev (2009) and Shalev, Zhang, and Zhang (2013) documented that managers systematically over-allocate to goodwill to reduce post-acquisition amortization expense, particularly when their compensation is tied to reported earnings.

The contingent consideration rules added another layer. Before ASC 805 (under the legacy SFAS 141), acquirers could exclude contingent earnout payments from the purchase price until the contingency was resolved. This meant earnouts that were never paid never appeared on the balance sheet. ASC 805 changed this: contingent consideration must now be recognized at fair value on the acquisition date, included in the total consideration for goodwill calculation, and -- if classified as a liability -- remeasured at fair value each reporting period with changes flowing through earnings. This created a new manipulation vector: the initial fair value estimate of the earnout liability, set by management using Level 3 unobservable inputs, determines both the goodwill recorded at acquisition and the subsequent earnings volatility from remeasurement.

The serial acquirer phenomenon magnified all of these issues. Tyco International reported that it completed more than 700 acquisitions afforded purchase accounting treatment during 1999-2002 under CEO Dennis Kozlowski. The sheer volume and pace of deal-making buried accounting decisions in disclosures that few investors could fully disentangle. When a company is constantly acquiring, the purchase price allocations, restructuring reserves, and earnout estimates from prior deals blur into a continuous stream of adjustments. A Stanford Graduate School of Business case study separately counted more than 100 announced acquisitions through fiscal 2001 and described how Tyco's "growth on growth" strategy accompanied a tenfold stock-price increase while reported growth depended heavily on acquisition activity.

The regulatory response to these abuses was uneven. The SEC investigated Tyco's accounting practices multiple times but issued closing letters without action on several occasions. It was only after the broader post-Enron crackdown on corporate accounting that the full extent of acquisition-related manipulation at Tyco, WorldCom, and other serial acquirers came to light. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 strengthened internal controls and audit committee oversight, but the fundamental discretion embedded in purchase price allocation, contingent consideration valuation, and restructuring reserve estimation remained largely intact. The standards give management the benefit of the doubt on forward-looking assumptions, and that benefit is where the games live.

## Core Concepts

### Purchase Price Allocation as a Manipulation Lever

The purchase price allocation is the foundational accounting event of any acquisition. Under ASC 805 and IFRS 3, the acquirer must assign the total consideration paid -- cash, stock, assumed liabilities, contingent consideration -- to the identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed, each measured at fair value on the acquisition date. Whatever remains after all identifiable assets and liabilities are valued becomes goodwill.

This process involves enormous judgment. Customer relationships must be valued using methods like the Multi-Period Excess Earnings Method (MPEEM), which projects future revenue from existing customers, applies a churn rate, discounts to present value, and subtracts contributory asset charges for the brand, technology, and workforce that also contribute to generating that revenue. Each of these inputs -- revenue projections, customer attrition rates, discount rates, contributory asset charges -- is a Level 3 unobservable assumption set by management. Small changes in any input produce large swings in the assigned value.

The manipulation incentive is structural. Identifiable intangible assets with finite lives are amortized over those lives, reducing reported earnings. Goodwill, under ASC 350, is not amortized -- it is tested for impairment at least annually. A manager who wants to minimize post-acquisition earnings drag therefore has an incentive to under-identify finite-lived intangible assets and let more of the purchase price flow to goodwill. Shalev, Zhang, and Zhang (2013), writing in the Journal of Accounting Research, found that the proportion of acquisition price allocated to goodwill increased with the relative importance of earnings-based bonuses in CEO pay. The association diminished when bonus plans used measures less affected by goodwill allocation, such as cash flow, sales, or earnings growth.

The reverse manipulation also occurs. In asset deals where the buyer gets a new tax basis in acquired assets, the acquirer may prefer to maximize identified intangibles to capture tax amortization benefits. The tension between financial reporting incentives (minimize amortization, maximize goodwill) and tax incentives (maximize intangibles for tax deductions) means that the PPA can look very different from the tax allocation, creating a gap that forensic analysts can examine for evidence of gaming.

### Contingent Consideration and Earnout Accounting

Earnouts -- contingent payments that depend on the target's future performance -- are one of the most powerful and most misunderstood tools in acquisition accounting. They bridge valuation gaps between buyers and sellers: the buyer limits upfront payment, while the seller gains upside if performance targets are met. But under ASC 805, the accounting treatment creates multiple manipulation vectors.

At the acquisition date, contingent consideration must be recognized at fair value and included in the total consideration transferred for goodwill computation. This fair value is typically a Level 3 measurement requiring option pricing models or probability-weighted scenario approaches. The initial estimate determines the goodwill recorded: a lower fair value estimate for the earnout liability means lower total consideration and lower goodwill. A manager who wants to minimize goodwill -- perhaps because a large goodwill balance would attract auditor or analyst scrutiny -- has an incentive to underestimate the earnout liability at acquisition.

The subsequent remeasurement creates a second manipulation vector. If the earnout is classified as a liability (the more common treatment when settlement is in cash or variable shares), it must be remeasured at fair value each reporting period, with changes recognized in earnings. When the estimated payout increases, the company records a loss. When it decreases, the company records a gain. These are non-cash adjustments, but they flow through reported earnings and can be material. A company that initially underestimated the earnout liability will record a series of losses as the fair value is revised upward -- or, if the targets are missed, will record gains as the liability is written down. The timing and magnitude of these revisions are within management's control, particularly when the earnout metrics depend on operating decisions the acquirer makes post-close (revenue targets, EBITDA thresholds, customer retention).

The classification decision itself is consequential. Earnouts classified as equity (typically when settled in a fixed number of the acquirer's own shares meeting ASC 815-40 criteria) are not remeasured after initial recognition. This eliminates the earnings volatility entirely. The structuring choice between liability and equity classification -- made at the acquisition date based on the settlement mechanism -- determines whether the arrangement will produce ongoing P&L noise. The McLean Group, a valuation advisory firm, noted that this classification decision has "direct P&L consequences" and should be modeled at the term sheet stage, not discovered after close.

### Spring-Loading and the Stub Period

One of the most insidious acquisition accounting tricks does not involve the final purchase price allocation at all. It occurs in the period between signing and closing, when an acquirer pressures the target to accelerate expenses, defer revenue, or overstate reserves before the acquisition date. The target's pre-acquisition results look worse, but once the acquisition closes, the combined entity benefits from an artificially depressed comparison base.

Tyco's December 2002 Form 8-K provides primary evidence of this pattern. Internal materials urged managers to be aggressive in identifying acquisition balance-sheet exposures and to build support for reserves. A memorandum for the U.S. Surgical transaction proposed that the target "over-accrue expenses" before closing, and another acquisition schedule concentrated "financial engineering" entries in the month immediately before consummation. Tyco's own review concluded that management sometimes influenced targets to over-accrue expenses or otherwise exceed GAAP before closing, although the filing also distinguished aggressive accounting from proven fraud.

The tactic exploits the accounting boundary at the acquisition date. The target's pre-acquisition results remain outside the acquirer's post-close operating period, while later reserve releases or the absence of accelerated costs can improve the combined entity's reported results. The correct forensic question is therefore not whether every pre-close adjustment is improper, but whether estimates, expenses, or revenue recognition changed asymmetrically around the closing date without an economic explanation.

### Cookie Jar Reserves and Restructuring Charges

Acquisitions create a one-time opportunity to establish large reserves and take large charges that would be difficult to justify in the ordinary course of business. When a company acquires another business, it can record restructuring charges, integration costs, and asset write-downs as part of the acquisition accounting. These charges hit earnings at acquisition, but because they are framed as one-time, non-recurring items, analysts and investors often look past them. The reserves established -- for restructuring, for contingent liabilities, for asset impairments -- become "cookie jar" accounts that can be released into earnings in future periods.

Tyco was the canonical example. Short-seller David Tice accused Tyco of taking inappropriately large restructuring reserves for its acquisitions, and the CFO publication "Deconstructing Tyco" described how these purchase accounting liabilities became "cookie jar accounts -- accounts which Tyco management can use at its discretion to boost earnings in future quarters." The mechanism is straightforward: record a large restructuring reserve at acquisition (reducing current earnings, but investors forgive one-time charges), then release portions of the reserve in subsequent quarters when earnings need a boost. Each release flows through earnings as a gain, but because it is buried in cost of goods sold or operating expenses, it is rarely visible to outside analysts.

The SEC has long been concerned about this pattern. A Wall Street Journal report from 1999 noted that the agency "has been concerned about companies using such reserves as cookie jars to be dipped into when they need to boost earnings in future periods." But proving that a restructuring reserve was intentionally overstated is difficult: the reserve is based on management's estimate of future restructuring costs, and the range of reasonable estimates is wide enough to absorb substantial padding before it crosses into outright fraud.

### The Serial Acquirer as a Manipulation Platform

The individual techniques -- PPA manipulation, earnout games, spring-loading, cookie jar reserves -- become far more powerful when deployed by a serial acquirer. A company that makes one or two acquisitions per decade has a limited window for these games. A company that makes dozens of acquisitions per year, like Tyco under Kozlowski or WorldCom under Ebbers, operates in a continuous state of deal accounting that makes it nearly impossible for outside analysts to separate organic performance from acquisition-driven accounting effects.

The serial acquirer's core trick is using the pace of acquisitions to obscure organic deterioration. Revenue growth that appears impressive may be entirely driven by acquired revenue. The pro forma adjustments that are supposed to give investors a view of organic growth -- stripping out the contribution of acquisitions -- are themselves subject to management discretion. The SEC's Financial Reporting Manual specifies that pro forma adjustments should be limited to transaction accounting adjustments (reflecting the GAAP accounting for the deal) and management's adjustments for synergies and dis-synergies. But the line between a legitimate pro forma adjustment and one that flatters organic growth is blurry, and management has every incentive to push it.

WorldCom's case, documented in the SEC's Report of Investigation, illustrates how acquisition strategy and accounting fraud reinforced each other. WorldCom grew through scores of acquisitions in the 1990s, using its rising stock price as acquisition currency. When the acquisition strategy ended -- the Sprint merger was blocked on antitrust grounds in 2000 -- WorldCom could no longer mask its deteriorating organic performance with acquired revenue. The pressure to maintain the appearance of double-digit growth led directly to the accounting fraud: improperly capitalizing operating expenses, releasing accruals, and manufacturing revenue. The bankruptcy examiner's report noted that WorldCom "was constantly and feverishly in deal mode," and when the deals stopped, the fraud began.

### Red Flags for Acquisition Accounting Manipulation

Detecting acquisition accounting tricks requires looking at patterns rather than single transactions. The key red flags, synthesized from forensic accounting literature and SEC enforcement actions, include:

First, persistent intangible asset growth without corresponding revenue increases. When a serial acquirer's intangible assets and goodwill grow faster than its revenue, the purchase price allocations may be inflated or the acquired businesses may be underperforming. The ratio of intangible assets to revenue should be stable or declining as acquired assets are amortized; a rising ratio suggests either overpayment, under-identification of impairments, or both.

Second, aggressive earnout liability estimates. When a company consistently records earnout liabilities at the low end of the possible range and then revises them upward in subsequent periods, the initial estimates may have been deliberately understated to minimize goodwill. Conversely, when earnout liabilities are consistently revised downward -- producing gains in earnings -- the initial estimates may have been deliberately overstated to create a cookie jar of future gains.

Third, frequent goodwill impairment following acquisitions. When a company's acquisitions are routinely followed by goodwill impairments, the purchase price allocations may have been optimistic or the acquired operations may have deteriorated. The goodwill fraction is a useful prompt for investigation, not proof of overpayment. Potepa and Thomas (2023) found that post-acquisition accounting and market-performance declines, acquisition-year attributes, market-to-book ratios, segment data, and volatility jointly help identify acquisitions likely to impair; their evidence also showed that many impairment decisions occur with a defensible delay rather than immediately.

Fourth, revenue growth that largely disappears when acquisitions are stripped out. This is the serial acquirer's tell. If a company reports 20% revenue growth but organic growth (excluding acquisitions) is 2%, the acquisition strategy may be masking fundamental business weakness. The "acquisition addiction" red flag, as described in forensic accounting guides, includes frequent deals that boost revenue but erode margins, recurring goodwill write-downs, and pro forma results that exclude integration costs.

## Evidence

### Shalev, Zhang, and Zhang (2013): CEO Bonuses and Goodwill Allocation

Shalev, Zhang, and Zhang studied purchase price allocations for acquisitions completed from July 2001 through December 2008. Their summary measure combined the proportion of acquisition price recorded as goodwill and indefinite-lived intangibles; goodwill alone averaged 59 percent of purchase price in their sample. The design used compensation structure to test whether accounting-based bonus incentives affected allocation choices after controlling for economic determinants of the deal.

The proportion allocated to goodwill increased with instrumented CEO bonus intensity. Because goodwill is not amortized while finite-lived identifiable assets generally are, a larger goodwill allocation can increase later reported earnings and bonuses. The association weakened when bonus plans relied on cash flow, sales, or earnings-growth measures that are less affected by the allocation. These results support a specific incentive channel; they do not establish that every large goodwill balance is manipulated.

### The Tyco International Case

Tyco International under Dennis Kozlowski is the most extensively documented case of acquisition accounting manipulation. The Stanford Graduate School of Business case study describes how Tyco made over 100 announced acquisitions between 1992 and 2001, with total revenues exceeding $30 billion. The "growth on growth" strategy fueled a tenfold stock price increase, but the growth was substantially an accounting artifact.

Tyco's December 2002 Form 8-K documented the mechanics directly. Internal materials described "Acquisition Balance Sheet Opportunities," urged aggressive estimates, and proposed pre-merger accruals for U.S. Surgical and Carlisle Plastics. Tyco's review also identified $36.1 million of fiscal 2002 acquisition-accounting and reserve corrections. The filing did not characterize every aggressive entry as fraud, but it concluded that prior management's accounting was systematically oriented toward increasing reported results and sometimes crossed GAAP boundaries.

The "Deconstructing Tyco" analysis in CFO magazine described how Tyco's purchase accounting liabilities became cookie jar reserves. The company recorded large restructuring reserves at each acquisition, then released portions in subsequent quarters to smooth earnings. When the SEC began investigating, Tyco reportedly backed away from some aggressive moves, including an attempt to classify retention bonuses for fired employees as merger charges rather than expenses.

### WorldCom: When the Deals Stop, the Fraud Starts

WorldCom's trajectory, documented in the SEC's Report of Investigation and the bankruptcy examiner's reports, provides the clearest evidence of how acquisition accounting masks can become unsustainable. In the 1990s, WorldCom grew through scores of acquisitions, using its rising stock as currency. The MCI acquisition in 1998 -- a company more than two-and-a-half times WorldCom's size by revenues -- was the apex. When the Sprint merger was blocked on antitrust grounds in 2000, the acquisition strategy ended.

The SEC investigation found that WorldCom's accounting fraud took two principal forms: reducing reported line costs by improperly releasing accruals and capitalizing operating expenses, and exaggerating reported revenue. The fraud was directly linked to the end of the acquisition strategy: without acquired revenue to mask deteriorating organic performance, WorldCom resorted to manufacturing the growth it had promised Wall Street. The SEC identified over $958 million in improperly recorded revenue between 1999 and 2002. The bankruptcy examiner noted that WorldCom "was constantly and feverishly in deal mode" and found no evidence of a focused strategic plan -- the acquisitions were opportunistic, and the accounting followed suit.

### Potepa and Thomas (2023): Acquisition-Level Goodwill Impairment

Potepa and Thomas hand-collected data for 893 large acquisitions and tracked each for as many as ten post-acquisition years. Their model combined post-acquisition accounting and market-performance declines with acquisition-year attributes and identified 349 acquisitions as likely to impair. Of the full sample, 222 acquisitions recorded an impairment during the observation window.

Only 15 percent of the 349 at-risk acquisitions impaired in the first year the model classified them as likely to impair. When the authors allowed one or two additional years for management to determine whether a decline was permanent, their estimated compliance rate rose to about 65 percent. They concluded that the evidence showed high levels of compliance and little opportunism overall, while still identifying a minority of apparently noncompliant cases. The result is a warning against treating a delayed impairment or a large goodwill balance as conclusive evidence of manipulation without examining operating performance, segment data, and the reporting-unit context.

### Contingent Consideration Remeasurement Volatility

The accounting treatment of earnouts under ASC 805 creates measurable earnings volatility that several advisory firms have documented. The McLean Group's analysis of earnout accounting noted that liability-classified earnouts are remeasured at fair value each reporting period, with increases producing charges to earnings and decreases producing gains. These adjustments can be material and are non-cash, creating "potential confusion for stakeholders evaluating operating performance." For private equity-backed portfolio companies with leveraged balance sheets, the remeasurement volatility can affect covenant compliance -- the treatment of earnout remeasurement gains and losses in credit agreement definitions of Adjusted EBITDA is a frequent point of contention.

Sofer Advisors, a valuation firm, noted that earn-out disputes frequently arise from "metric manipulation, accounting policy changes, and ambiguous agreement terms." The buyer controls the acquired business post-close and can influence the metrics that determine earnout payouts -- changing accounting policies, altering expense classifications, or shifting resources between segments. This creates a structural conflict: the buyer has both the incentive and the ability to influence the earnout outcome, and the accounting rules require the buyer's management to estimate the fair value of the liability.

## Implications

### For Investors and Analysts

The primary implication for investors is that acquisition accounting is the single largest source of opaque adjustments in financial statements, and serial acquirers warrant heightened skepticism. An investor evaluating a serial acquirer should strip out acquisition contributions to assess organic growth, examine the trend of intangible assets and goodwill relative to revenue, and track the pattern of earnout liability remeasurements for evidence of systematic under- or over-estimation.

The pro forma adjustments that companies present alongside reported results are a critical but unreliable data source. Under SEC rules, pro forma adjustments should reflect the GAAP accounting for the transaction and management's estimates of synergies and dis-synergies. But management's adjustments can flatter organic growth by excluding costs that are arguably recurring (integration costs, retention bonuses, system migration expenses) or by including synergies that may never materialize. Investors should construct their own organic growth estimates by subtracting acquired revenue from total revenue growth, rather than relying on management's pro forma figures.

The Beneish M-Score, a forensic model for detecting earnings manipulation, incorporates several ratios that are directly relevant to acquisition accounting. The total accruals to assets ratio captures the gap between accounting earnings and cash flow -- a gap that widens when acquisitions generate large non-cash adjustments. The asset quality ratio captures the growth in non-current assets other than plant, property, and equipment -- a category that includes acquired intangibles and goodwill. Companies with rising M-Scores following an acquisition spree warrant deeper investigation of the PPA assumptions and restructuring reserve patterns.

### For Auditors and Standard-Setters

The auditing profession faces a fundamental information asymmetry in acquisition accounting. Management sets the assumptions -- revenue growth rates for customer relationship valuations, attrition rates, discount rates, earnout probability estimates -- and the auditor must challenge them. But management knows the acquired business better than any outside reviewer, and the range of "reasonable" assumptions is wide enough to absorb substantial optimism before it crosses into misrepresentation.

The PCAOB has increased scrutiny of goodwill testing and acquisition accounting in recent years, particularly the qualitative assessment ("Step Zero") that allows companies to skip quantitative impairment testing. But the core problem -- the discretion embedded in Level 3 fair value estimates -- remains. Standard-setters could reduce manipulation by requiring more granular disclosure of PPA assumptions (specific revenue growth rates, attrition rates, and discount rates used in intangible asset valuations) and by requiring retrospective comparison of initial PPA estimates to actual post-acquisition performance. If investors could see that a company's customer relationship valuations consistently assume 5% annual growth but the acquired businesses consistently deliver 2%, the manipulation would be visible.

The measurement period adjustment rules also deserve attention. ASC 805 allows acquirers to adjust the provisional PPA within one year of the acquisition date if new information is obtained about facts that existed at acquisition. These adjustments are retrospective -- they modify goodwill rather than flowing through current earnings. This creates a window in which management can revise the PPA to a more favorable allocation after the initial scrutiny has passed, with the adjustments buried in the following year's footnotes.

### For Corporate Governance and M&A Practitioners

The earnout structuring decision has implications far beyond the deal terms. The classification of contingent consideration as liability or equity determines whether the arrangement produces ongoing earnings volatility. The choice of earnout metrics -- revenue versus EBITDA versus gross profit -- determines how easily the buyer can influence the outcome through post-acquisition operating decisions. The McLean Group's analysis emphasized that these decisions should be modeled at the term sheet stage, not discovered after close.

For audit committees, the key governance question is whether the company has independent valuation specialists reviewing the PPA, or whether management's assumptions are being accepted without challenge. The presence of a Big 4 valuation team does not guarantee independence -- the valuation team is engaged by management, and their assumptions are calibrated to management's projections. Audit committees should require periodic retrospective reviews: comparing initial PPA assumptions to actual post-acquisition performance, and investigating systematic deviations.

The broader lesson from the Tyco and WorldCom cases is that acquisition strategy and accounting integrity are inseparable. A company whose growth narrative depends on continuous acquisitions is a company whose financial statements depend on continuous PPA judgments. When the acquisitions stop -- whether because of antitrust intervention, stock price collapse, or market saturation -- the accounting judgments that masked organic deterioration become visible, and the unwind can be catastrophic. Investors, auditors, and directors who understand this dynamic can identify the risk before the unwind begins.
