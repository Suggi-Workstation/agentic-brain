# Non-GAAP Metrics -- How Adjusted Earnings Became the Standard Story While GAAP Became the Footnote

Non-GAAP financial metrics -- "adjusted earnings," "pro-forma net
income," "adjusted EBITDA" -- began as a legitimate supplement to
help investors see through one-time distortions in GAAP accounting.
Over the past two decades they have metastasized into the primary
narrative companies use to present their performance, with the gap
between GAAP and non-GAAP earnings widening dramatically. The result
is a reporting environment where 97% of S&P 500 companies use non-GAAP
measures, 63% report higher earnings after adjustments, and investors
who rely on the adjusted numbers without scrutiny are systematically
overpaying for earnings that do not exist under any accounting standard.

## Background

The term "pro forma" entered the financial lexicon in the late 1990s
during the dot-com bubble. Internet companies with no GAAP profits
began reporting "pro forma" results that excluded inconvenient costs
-- stock-based compensation, acquisition amortization, restructuring
charges -- to present themselves as profitable on an "adjusted" basis.
The practice was so widespread and so misleading that it became a
contributing factor to the bubble's severity. When the dot-com crash
exposed companies whose pro-forma profits had never materialized into
actual cash, Congress included non-GAAP regulation in the Sarbanes-Oxley
Act of 2002.

Section 401(b) of Sarbanes-Oxley directed the SEC to issue rules
governing the use of non-GAAP financial measures. The SEC responded
in January 2003 with Regulation G and amendments to Item 10(e) of
Regulation S-K. Regulation G applies to any public disclosure of
non-GAAP measures -- earnings releases, investor presentations,
website postings, social media. Item 10(e) adds additional requirements
for documents filed with the SEC. Together they established the core
framework: companies must present the most directly comparable GAAP
measure, provide a quantitative reconciliation, and give GAAP measures
equal or greater prominence.

These rules were meant to tame the wild west of pro-forma reporting.
They did not. Instead of eliminating non-GAAP reporting, Reg G
legitimized it. Companies learned to comply with the letter of the
rules while continuing to use non-GAAP metrics as their primary
communication tool. The SEC's 2016 C&DIs (Compliance and Disclosure
Interpretations) tightened restrictions on individually tailored
accounting and misleading prominence, and the December 2022 updates
went further -- explicitly warning that excluding normal, recurring
cash operating expenses could be misleading even when accompanied
by detailed disclosures. Yet the gap between GAAP and non-GAAP
earnings has continued to grow, making this one of the most durable
and effective forms of financial statement distortion available to
public companies.

## Core Concepts

Non-GAAP measures are numerical financial metrics that either exclude
amounts included in the most directly comparable GAAP measure, or
include amounts excluded from it. If a company reports "net income"
that removes stock-based compensation, restructuring costs, and
amortization of acquired intangibles, that is a non-GAAP measure
subject to SEC rules. Operating statistics like unit sales or employee
count are not non-GAAP measures; neither are ratios calculated entirely
from GAAP figures.

The most common adjustments fall into several categories, and
understanding them is the first step in distinguishing legitimate
supplemental disclosure from manipulation.

Stock-based compensation (SBC) is the single largest adjustment.
Technology companies in particular report enormous gaps because they
pay a significant portion of employee compensation in stock. Under
GAAP, SBC is a real expense. Companies argue that excluding it shows
"cash earnings" or "economic earnings." The counterargument: SBC
dilutes existing shareholders, and paying employees with stock instead
of cash does not make the compensation free. A company that replaces
cash salaries with equity grants and then excludes the equity cost
from its "adjusted" earnings is presenting a cost structure that does
not reflect economic reality.

Amortization of acquired intangibles is another pervasive adjustment.
When a company acquires another business, GAAP requires it to allocate
part of the purchase price to identifiable intangible assets (customer
relationships, patents, trademarks) and amortize them over their useful
lives. Companies argue this is a non-cash charge unrelated to current
operations. The rebuttal: the acquiring company paid real money for
those assets, and if it must keep acquiring to sustain growth, the
amortization represents a genuine economic cost of the acquisition
strategy. Serial acquirers that always exclude acquisition amortization
present a recurring cost as a one-time item.

Restructuring charges are the classic "non-recurring" item that recurs
year after year. Companies announce a restructuring, take a large
GAAP charge, and then exclude it from adjusted earnings as a one-time
event. When the same company announces a restructuring every year,
the charges are part of its normal operating model. The pattern is a
red flag: if "one-time" charges appear in every reporting period, the
company's business model generates recurring costs that management
prefers investors not count.

Acquisition-related costs (legal fees, banker fees, integration costs)
are excluded as "transaction-specific." For acquisitive companies that
make multiple deals annually, these are effectively recurring operating
expenses.

Litigation settlements, asset impairment charges, and gains or losses
on asset sales are also commonly excluded. Each has a plausible
justification in isolation. The problem is aggregation: when a
company excludes seven categories of costs every single quarter, the
"adjusted" number represents a hypothetical business that does not
exist.

The regulatory framework rests on four principles from Regulation G
and Item 10(e). First, a company must present the most directly
comparable GAAP measure alongside any non-GAAP measure. For EBITDA or
adjusted EBITDA, the comparable measure is net income -- not operating
income. Second, the company must provide a quantitative reconciliation
showing exactly which items were excluded and the dollar amounts.
Third, the GAAP measure must be presented with equal or greater
prominence. The SEC considers it misleading to present a non-GAAP
income statement that gives more visual weight to adjusted numbers,
to headline a press release with non-GAAP EPS while burying GAAP
EPS in a footnote, or to discuss non-GAAP results at length in MD&A
while barely mentioning GAAP. Fourth, non-GAAP measures must not use
titles or labels that are the same as or confusingly similar to GAAP
measures. A metric calculated differently from GAAP "gross profit"
cannot be called "gross profit."

Despite these rules, enforcement is limited. The SEC's Division of
Corporation Finance issues comment letters challenging specific
disclosures, and non-GAAP comments have been the top comment letter
category for years. In 2023, approximately 30% of all SEC comment
letters contained at least one non-GAAP comment. Yet comment letters
only prompt companies to adjust disclosures -- they rarely result in
restatements. The Division of Enforcement has brought occasional
cases, including a 2023 action against a registrant for misleading
non-GAAP disclosures over multiple years. But systematic policing
of non-GAAP metrics is not occurring. The result is a disclosure
environment where the rules provide a framework for proper use but
the enforcement resources to detect and punish improper use are
modest relative to the scale of the practice.

The key red flags for analysts and investors include: non-GAAP
earnings that consistently and significantly exceed GAAP earnings,
with a widening rather than narrowing gap; "one-time" charges that
appear every quarter or year; adjusted EBITDA or adjusted earnings
that never reconcile to free cash flow -- if earnings are supposedly
high and growing but cash flow is flat or declining, the adjustments
are probably hiding real costs; frequent changes in the definition or
calculation of non-GAAP metrics, which makes year-over-year
comparisons impossible; metrics that are "individually tailored" --
custom-made for that company with no industry standard for comparison;
and non-GAAP measures presented on the first page of an earnings
release while the GAAP reconciliation requires scrolling to an
appendix.

The distinction between legitimate and deceptive non-GAAP use turns
on consistency, transparency, and economic substance. Legitimate
use: the adjustments are clearly explained, consistently applied
across periods, and the excluded items are genuinely non-recurring.
Deceptive use: the adjustments change every quarter, the excluded
"non-recurring" items recur, and the adjusted numbers tell a story
that cash flow does not confirm.

## Evidence

The academic evidence on non-GAAP reporting has evolved considerably
over two decades. Black, Christensen, Ciesielski, and Whipple (2018),
in their comprehensive review published in the Journal of Business
Finance and Accounting, documented the dramatic proliferation of
non-GAAP reporting and synthesized the research to date. They found
that while non-GAAP earnings can provide incremental information
beyond GAAP earnings -- particularly by stripping out truly transitory
items -- the growth of non-GAAP reporting has far outpaced any
commensurate increase in informativeness. More companies are reporting
more non-GAAP metrics that exclude more items than ever before, and
the marginal information content of each additional adjustment is
declining.

Black, Christensen, Joo, and Schmardebeck (2017), in Contemporary
Accounting Research, found a relationship between earnings management
and non-GAAP reporting: firms that engage in more accrual-based
earnings management also report non-GAAP earnings that exclude more
expenses. This suggests that non-GAAP adjustments are not purely
informational -- they correlate with other forms of financial
manipulation.

A 2021 study by Black et al. in Contemporary Accounting Research
examined the consistency and comparability of non-GAAP definitions
across time and firms. They found that when firms change their
non-GAAP definitions, the changes generally improve earnings
informativeness -- but such changes are rare. Most firms stick with
definitions that consistently present higher earnings, and the
authors found that non-GAAP adjustments generally result in greater
earnings comparability than GAAP alone. This study provides a nuanced
picture: non-GAAP reporting is not inherently misleading, but the
motivation behind adjustments matters enormously.

Brosnan (2023), in a 20-year systematic literature review published
in the Journal of Accounting Literature, characterized the non-GAAP
environment as having started as the "wild west" and gradually
maturing through regulation. The review found that current consensus
holds recent non-GAAP earnings to be informative to users, while
noting that the body of research lacks experimental studies and
qualitative disclosure research. The central tension in the academic
literature is between the "informative" view (non-GAAP earnings help
investors see through accounting noise) and the "opportunistic" view
(managers use non-GAAP adjustments to paint a rosier picture). Both
have empirical support; the reality is that non-GAAP reporting is a
tool whose value depends entirely on the user's integrity.

The quantitative evidence on the GAAP/non-GAAP gap is striking.
According to analysis by TKer (2025), 63% of S&P 500 companies
reported higher non-GAAP earnings than GAAP earnings, while only
7.8% reported lower non-GAAP earnings. The asymmetry is telling:
managers systematically adjust earnings upward, not downward. The
gap is not random noise -- it is a one-way ratchet.

Analysis by New Constructs (2022) of S&P 500 Core Earnings versus
GAAP earnings showed that GAAP earnings are consistently more volatile
but also consistently overstated relative to economic earnings in
expansion periods. The Financials sector showed the largest absolute
gap, with GAAP earnings exceeding Core Earnings by tens of billions
of dollars each quarter. A real-world example from Wells Fargo: after
adjusting for unusual items, Core Earnings were $15.8 billion versus
reported GAAP earnings of $19.3 billion -- a 22% overstatement.

The SEC enforcement record provides additional evidence of the
problem's scale. In the 2023 period analyzed by PwC, the SEC issued
1,346 comment letters, an increase from 863 in the prior year -- and
approximately 30% of comment letters in each period contained at
least one non-GAAP comment. The most common comment type (26% of all
non-GAAP comments in 2023, up from 22% in 2022) questioned the nature
of non-GAAP adjustments themselves. The SEC's 2022 C&DI updates have
triggered approximately 150 additional comments specifically referencing
those updates, with a focus on individually tailored accounting
principles and whether excluding normal recurring cash operating
expenses is misleading.

A concrete illustration comes from Tyler Technologies' SEC filings.
In its Q2 2025 earnings release, Tyler reported GAAP diluted EPS of
$7.40-$7.70 but non-GAAP diluted EPS of $11.20-$11.50 -- a gap of
roughly 50%. The adjustments included $3.47 per share of stock-based
compensation, $2.08 of amortization of acquired intangibles, and a
tax impact adjustment. The non-GAAP operating margin was 26.5% versus
a GAAP operating margin of 16.0% -- a 10.5 percentage point gap.
This pattern, repeated across hundreds of companies, means investors
using non-GAAP multiples are valuing a fundamentally different
business than the one that reports to the SEC on a GAAP basis.

## Implications

For investors, the practical implication is that non-GAAP metrics
must be treated as management's marketing material, not as neutral
financial data. Every non-GAAP adjustment represents management's
claim that an expense is "not real" or "not recurring." The investor's
job is to test that claim against evidence. The minimum standard of
diligence: always read the GAAP-to-non-GAAP reconciliation before
drawing any conclusion from an adjusted number; compare non-GAAP
earnings to operating cash flow and free cash flow -- if adjusted
earnings are growing but cash flow is not, the adjustments are
almost certainly hiding real costs; track whether "one-time" charges
recur -- a restructuring charge that appears in four consecutive
annual reports is not one-time; question why the company uses
non-GAAP metrics and whether management's own incentive compensation
is tied to the GAAP or non-GAAP numbers; and always value the company
using GAAP earnings and GAAP-based multiples as the baseline, with
non-GAAP as a supplementary perspective rather than the primary one.

For regulators, the non-GAAP conundrum presents a structural challenge.
The SEC's comment letter process catches the most egregious cases
but cannot review every filing from every company every quarter.
The 2022 C&DI updates tightened the rules but did not change the
fundamental dynamic: companies control the initial framing, and the
SEC can only react after the fact. The International Accounting
Standards Board (IASB) and FASB have both questioned whether the
proliferation of non-GAAP measures signals that GAAP/IFRS reporting
is failing to provide what investors need. One structural reform
under discussion: requiring non-GAAP metrics to be disclosed in a
standardized format (similar to the GAAP income statement) with
mandatory line-item reconciliation rather than the free-form
presentations common today. Another: requiring audit committee
oversight of non-GAAP metrics rather than leaving them entirely to
management discretion.

For the relationship between accounting and investing, non-GAAP
metrics represent the collision between two different purposes of
financial reporting. GAAP exists to enforce consistency, comparability,
and conservatism across all reporting entities -- it is designed for
the median user doing comparative analysis. Non-GAAP metrics exist to
tell each company's unique story in its own words. Both purposes are
legitimate, but the current system gives management nearly unlimited
discretion to choose which story to emphasize. The practical resolution
is for investors to treat non-GAAP reporting the way a forensic
accountant treats a subject company's narrative: listen carefully,
verify everything, and always return to the audited GAAP numbers as
the anchor of truth.

The compounding effect across the market is significant. If the
average S&P 500 company reports non-GAAP earnings 10-20% above GAAP
earnings, and the market prices stocks using analyst estimates that
are predominantly based on non-GAAP guidance, then the entire market
is systematically valued using inflated earnings. This does not mean
the market is "wrong" -- markets can price off whatever metrics they
choose -- but it does mean that reported P/E ratios based on GAAP
earnings are substantially higher than the headline P/E ratios
constructed from "operating" or "adjusted" earnings that dominate
financial media.

For the forensic analyst specifically, non-GAAP metrics are not an
enemy to be rejected but a signal to be interpreted. When a company's
non-GAAP adjustments are large, growing, and increasingly creative
in their labeling and composition, the adjustments themselves become
a red flag independent of any specific accounting rule violation. The
best forensic indicator is the trend: a company whose non-GAAP-to-GAAP
gap was 5% three years ago and is 25% today is sending a clear signal,
regardless of whether any individual adjustment violates a rule.
