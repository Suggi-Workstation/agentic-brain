# Stock-Based Compensation Shenanigans -- Non-Cash Adjustments Can Hide Dilution and Recurring Labor Cost

Stock-based compensation transfers value to employees for services, so its non-cash settlement does not make the labor free. The forensic problem arises when adjusted earnings remove the expense, operating cash flow adds it back, capitalized awards defer recognition, and repurchases are described as shareholder returns without reconciling the dilution they offset. A complete analysis follows the same compensation through expense, assets, cash flow, taxes, award activity, share count, and buybacks before judging whether the presentation clarifies performance or understates a recurring economic cost.[1][2][3][5]

## Background

Employee stock options, restricted shares, restricted stock units, performance awards, and employee stock purchase plans are different contracts, but each can exchange an equity-linked claim for labor or other services. FASB ASC Topic 718 rests on the principle that compensation cost from share-based payment transactions is recognized at fair value, while IFRS 2 likewise requires the effects of share-based payments to appear in profit or loss and financial position.[1][3][5] The accounting question is therefore not whether an equity award costs the issuing company anything. It is how to measure the award, when to recognize the service cost, where to present it, and how to disclose the residual claims on shareholders.

The modern rules followed a long dispute over employee options. FASB Statement 123(R), issued in 2004 and later incorporated into Topic 718, superseded the intrinsic-value method in APB Opinion 25 for employee awards and required a fair-value-based method for public entities. The FASB said the change would recognize the cost of employee services and reflect the economic consequences of share-based transactions in financial statements.[1] IFRS 2 had been issued earlier in 2004 with a similar fair-value-based requirement, improving international comparability even though the two systems retain detailed differences.[1][5]

For an equity-classified award, the accounting measurement commonly begins at grant-date fair value. Compensation cost is then attributed over the requisite service period, often the vesting period, subject to the award's conditions and the applicable forfeiture policy. FASB examples state that total recognized cost over the requisite service period equals the grant-date fair value of awards for which the required service is ultimately rendered. ASU 2016-09 retained that principle while permitting an accounting-policy election concerning whether forfeitures are estimated in advance or recognized when they occur.[1][2] Liability-classified awards differ because fair value is remeasured through settlement, so classification is essential to any period-to-period comparison.[1][3]

The same award can affect several statements at different times. Compensation cost reduces income as employees render service, unless the cost qualifies for capitalization into an asset under another accounting topic. Equity-classified cost generally increases additional paid-in capital rather than creating a cash liability. Under the indirect cash-flow method, recognized stock compensation is added back in reconciling net income to operating cash flow because granting equity for services is noncash; employee exercise proceeds are financing inflows, and cash used for tax withholding or settlement follows separate classification rules.[2][19] Later issuance or vesting can enlarge the share base, while open-market repurchases consume cash and may offset that dilution.

This separation creates the presentation opportunity. A company can report a GAAP labor expense, add it back in operating cash flow, exclude it again from adjusted profit, and cite repurchases as capital returned to shareholders. Each presentation can comply with its governing rule, yet the combined story can imply that the labor cost disappeared. Berkshire Hathaway's 2016 shareholder letter challenged precisely this logic, arguing that an item of value used to pay employees remains a payroll cost even when management adjusts it out of a non-GAAP measure.[15]

Regulation does not make every exclusion deceptive. The SEC permits non-GAAP performance measures, including some per-share measures, when the required reconciliation and other conditions are met. Its staff warns more generally that an adjustment can still make a measure misleading depending on the company's facts, and that inconsistent treatment between periods can also mislead.[4] The specific SEC example concerning exclusion of normal, recurring cash operating expenses does not by itself prohibit excluding a noncash stock charge. The analyst must instead test recurrence, economic substance, consistency, prominence, the stated purpose of the measure, and whether the company provides enough information to understand dilution and cash consequences.[4]

The domain boundary is important. Normal measurement under Topic 718 or IFRS 2 belongs to financial reporting. Valuing employee options as claims belongs partly to valuation. This topic concerns the forensic layer: repeated exclusions, aggressive assumptions, incomplete share-count bridges, misleading descriptions of buybacks, unexplained capitalization, selective tax presentation, and other choices that can make a recurring compensation program appear economically costless. No one indicator proves manipulation. The conclusion must rest on reconciled evidence and must distinguish transparent incentive design, aggressive presentation, accounting error, and intentional deception.[3]

## Core Concepts

### Equity changes the payment instrument, not the service received

An employee who accepts shares or options instead of cash still supplies labor. The company preserves current cash but transfers a claim on future equity value. Existing owners bear that transfer through a lower residual claim, a larger future share count, or cash later spent to repurchase shares. FASB and IFRS both recognize the cost of employee service, and IFRS research explicitly explains that recognizing share-based cost in the earnings numerator and potential dilution in the diluted-EPS denominator is not double counting. The numerator measures current or prior services received; the denominator measures potential sharing of future profits.[1][5]

This distinction prevents two symmetrical errors. Treating the award as if it were cash paid today can obscure the financing benefit and the uncertainty of the equity claim. Treating it as free because no cash left the company at grant ignores the value transferred. The author's synthesis is to separate the operating decision from the financing decision: first measure the labor or service cost; then analyze whether issuing equity, using cash, or using another claim was the best way to finance that cost.[1][5][12]

A useful economic identity is not an accounting entry but a control:

`employee service cost = value transferred through equity claims + any cash settlement - forfeited or unearned awards`

The accounting measure will not always equal the award's realized value to the employee or the ultimate cost perceived by shareholders. Equity-classified awards are usually fixed at grant-date fair value for compensation accounting even though the share price later changes, while options may expire worthless or become very valuable. That difference is a measurement feature, not evidence that the original service was free.[1][3][5]

### Grant-date fair value and recognition timing create a multi-period trail

Restricted shares and ordinary restricted stock units are often valued from the share price at grant, adjusted for relevant terms such as unavailable dividends. Options require a valuation technique using inputs such as current share price, exercise price, expected term, expected volatility, expected dividends, and the risk-free rate. Market-condition awards may require a simulation or another model that incorporates the condition into grant-date fair value. Performance conditions that are not market conditions affect whether or how much cost is recognized under the applicable rules rather than simply changing the option-pricing input.[1][3][5]

The expense period follows service. Straight-line recognition may apply to an award with one service period, while graded vesting, retirement eligibility, modifications, performance estimates, and accelerated vesting can change the pattern. Forfeitures matter because cost ultimately remains only for awards whose required service is rendered under U.S. GAAP's service-condition logic. A large expense reversal after employee departures may therefore reflect actual forfeiture experience rather than a hidden gain; an analyst should reconcile the award roll-forward before drawing a conclusion.[1][2]

Unrecognized compensation cost is the service cost attached to outstanding unvested awards that has not yet reached the income statement. It is not a contractual cash payable, and future forfeitures or performance outcomes may alter recognized cost. It is nevertheless a useful disclosed backlog: when a company has a large unrecognized balance and a short weighted-average recognition period, a claim that current stock expense is disappearing requires evidence from future grants, vesting, and retention rather than a single-period decline. Akamai disclosed $462.1 million of pre-tax unrecognized stock-award cost at December 31, 2023, expected to be recognized through 2027 over a weighted-average period of 1.6 years.[17]

### Income-statement placement can disperse the total

Stock compensation should follow the function of the employees whose services produced it. The amount can therefore appear in cost of revenue, research and development, sales and marketing, and general and administrative expense rather than on one line. A reader who searches only selling expense may miss a large share of the total. Akamai's 2023 filing allocated stock compensation across all four of those operating categories, while its earnings release separately adjusted both current stock compensation and amortization of capitalized stock compensation in non-GAAP measures.[16][17]

The analyst should distinguish compensation cost recognized in the period from expense visible in the period. Some employee services create qualifying inventory, software, or development assets under other accounting guidance. Their stock compensation can be capitalized first and reach expense later through cost of sales or amortization. EastGroup Properties disclosed that $693,000 of $3.252 million in employee stock-compensation cost for the first quarter of 2023 was capitalized into development costs.[19] Akamai separately disclosed stock compensation in current functional expenses and additional amortization primarily related to capitalized internal-use software.[16][17]

Capitalization is not automatically aggressive. It can be appropriate when the underlying employee labor would qualify for capitalization if paid in cash. The forensic risk is timing opacity: adjusted earnings may exclude current stock compensation and also exclude later amortization of capitalized stock compensation, while a management-defined capital-expenditure measure may exclude the noncash capitalized amount. The same labor cost can then disappear from both the performance measure and the apparent reinvestment requirement. The correct test follows the cost from employee function to asset addition, later amortization or cost of revenue, and any non-GAAP adjustment.[16][19]

### Cash-flow presentation can turn a real cost into apparent free cash flow

Under the indirect cash-flow method, noncash stock compensation recognized in net income is added back within operating activities. This is correct cash-flow mechanics: granting an equity claim does not itself use cash. Employee option exercise proceeds are financing inflows, while tax withholding, cash settlement, and award repurchases can create cash outflows under their specific rules.[2][19][20] The statement answers where cash moved; it does not answer whether issuing equity transferred value.

The analytical trap appears when free cash flow starts from reported operating cash flow and subtracts capital expenditures, but makes no deduction for stock compensation and no explicit adjustment for dilution. Depreciation is also added back, but a conventional free-cash-flow calculation later subtracts capital expenditure to represent asset replacement and growth. No standardized cash-flow line automatically reverses the stock-compensation addback. Bhojraj argues that this presentation can mislead internal and external valuation and discusses treating stock compensation as an operating outflow with an offsetting financing inflow, or adding disclosures that make the transfer explicit.[12]

There are two internally coherent valuation treatments. One can treat recurring stock compensation as an operating cost and avoid adding it back in normalized free cash flow. Alternatively, one can add it back as noncash but explicitly forecast award issuance, option claims, future dilution, and any cash required to neutralize that dilution. Adding back the expense while also assuming a stable share count without a repurchase outflow is incoherent because it keeps the cash benefit and deletes the ownership cost. This is the author's synthesis from the cash-flow and valuation evidence.[5][7][12][13]

### Diluted EPS is a disclosure control, not the full ownership model

Basic EPS uses the weighted-average basic shares outstanding. Diluted EPS adds dilutive potential common shares under prescribed methods. Topic 260 treats employee options, nonvested shares, and similar instruments as potential common shares, while IAS 33 likewise addresses options, warrants, nonvested shares, and contingent issuances.[2][6] The treasury-stock method generally assumes that option exercise proceeds are used to repurchase shares at the average market price, so only incremental shares enter diluted EPS.

Reported diluted shares can understate the economic claim for valuation. Out-of-the-money options may be anti-dilutive for current accounting EPS while retaining time value. A loss period can exclude potential shares because adding them would reduce the reported loss per share, even though the awards can dilute future profitable periods. Long-dated options also possess sensitivity to future share-price changes that the period-specific treasury-stock calculation does not fully capture. Core, Guay, and Kothari estimated that economic option dilution in their sample was substantially greater than treasury-stock-method dilution.[7]

Restricted units require a separate bridge because an RSU commonly carries no employee exercise price. Performance units require scenario analysis because target, threshold, and maximum outcomes can differ materially. Employee stock purchase plans, acquisition awards, convertibles, warrants, and net-share settlement for tax withholding can also affect the roll-forward. The analyst should reconcile beginning actual shares, issuance on vesting or exercise, tax-withholding shares, acquisition issuance, repurchases, ending actual shares, weighted-average basic shares, diluted incremental shares, outstanding unvested awards, and authorized but ungranted plan capacity.[2][6][7]

### Repurchases can neutralize dilution without becoming a free shareholder return

A repurchase uses corporate cash to acquire outstanding shares. If the shares are retired or held in treasury, the transaction can offset issuance under employee plans. The ending share count may remain flat even while the company grants substantial equity compensation. Flat shares therefore do not prove that stock compensation imposed no cost; they may show that cash was spent to prevent the ownership claim from expanding.[8][9]

The required reconciliation is gross, not net. Report award grants, vesting and exercises, gross share issuance, shares withheld for employee taxes, repurchased shares, average repurchase price, cash spent, and the net change in actual and diluted shares. Akamai states that one goal of its 2024-2027 repurchase program is to offset dilution from employee equity programs and disclosed $800 million of repurchases during the first half of 2025.[16] Airbnb similarly stated in 2023 that its repurchases enabled it to offset employee-plan dilution while also excluding a significant recurring stock-compensation expense from Adjusted EBITDA.[18]

Calling the whole repurchase amount a return of capital can therefore overstate what continuing owners received economically. Part of the cash may have funded compensation already excluded from adjusted profit. Whether the repurchase created value also depends on price: retiring shares above intrinsic value can destroy value even if EPS rises. The author's synthesis is to divide gross repurchase spending into an estimated dilution-offset amount and a net capital-return amount, then evaluate the price and financing of both.[8][9]

### Tax effects can disconnect book cost, cash tax, and realized employee gain

Book compensation for an equity-classified award is generally anchored to grant-date fair value, while tax deductions for nonqualified employee options can depend on intrinsic value at exercise. ASU 2016-09 requires tax effects from the difference between the tax deduction and cumulative book compensation to run through income tax expense or benefit, and it classifies excess tax-benefit cash flows with operating income-tax cash flows.[2] A rising share price can therefore create a tax benefit that differs sharply from the original book expense.

Graham, Lang, and Shackelford found that option deductions produced large aggregate tax savings in their Nasdaq 100 and S&P 100 sample and changed estimated marginal tax rates substantially for the Nasdaq firms.[14] The implication is not that tax benefits cancel compensation cost. It is that after-tax analysis must reconcile grant-date book cost, deferred tax, exercise-date deduction, excess benefit or deficiency, and cash taxes. A company can report a volatile tax rate because award settlement changed the tax deduction, even when operating pretax economics did not change.[2][14]

### Non-GAAP presentation becomes a shenanigan through omission and inconsistency

A reconciled non-GAAP measure can answer a useful question, such as comparing operating trends before volatile grant-date measurements. Akamai explains that it excludes stock compensation because grant-date fair value varies with stock price, methods, assumptions, award types, and performance outcomes, making comparisons difficult in management's view.[16] That explanation is evidence about the metric's purpose, not proof that the excluded cost lacks economic substance.

Concern rises when stock compensation is large, recurring, essential to hiring and retention, and excluded without a parallel ownership or cash adjustment. Additional red flags include definitions that change as the cost rises, exclusion from both adjusted earnings and free cash flow, exclusion of later amortization after capitalization, buybacks presented without award issuance, decreasing diluted shares caused only by expensive repurchases, optimistic option-model inputs, and performance awards re-estimated just before a target is met. The SEC's reconciliation, consistency, and misleading-presentation tests apply to the disclosed measure, while the forensic analyst adds economic recurrence and ownership transfer to the review.[3][4][11]

## Practical Forensic Framework

### 1. Fix the perimeter and period

Begin with at least three annual periods and the available interim periods. Record the accounting framework, award types, fiscal year, acquisition activity, restructurings, and changes in compensation programs. Define whether the objective is to test GAAP expense, management's adjusted measure, cash generation, dilution, capital allocation, or all five. A vague instruction to "add back SBC" or "deduct SBC" is not a method because the correct treatment depends on the question being answered.[1][3][12]

### 2. Build a compensation-cost bridge

Extract total stock-compensation cost by function from the footnote and tie recognized expense to the income statement. Add stock cost capitalized into inventory, internal-use software, contract costs, or development assets to obtain current-period compensation cost generated. Then reconcile prior capitalized cost released through cost of sales or amortization. The bridge should show: current cost generated, amount expensed immediately, amount capitalized, prior capitalized amount recognized, modifications or accelerations, tax effects, and after-tax expense.[16][17][19]

### 3. Reproduce the award roll-forward

For each material award class, record beginning outstanding and unvested units, grants, exercises or releases, forfeitures, expirations, cancellations, performance adjustments, and ending balance. Capture weighted-average grant-date fair value, exercise price, remaining term, intrinsic value, and maximum performance payout where disclosed. Reconcile grants to board authorization and plan capacity. A favorable expense change is explainable when the roll-forward supports forfeitures or lower expected performance; it is unresolved when units, assumptions, and cost move in inconsistent directions.[1][2][3]

### 4. Test valuation assumptions longitudinally

For options, compare expected term, volatility, dividend yield, risk-free rate, and valuation method across years and with observed outcomes. Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik found evidence that firms understated option-value estimates through assumptions, with stronger results for expected life and volatility, the inputs offering more discretion.[11] A change is not manipulation merely because it lowers fair value. The company may have different employee exercise behavior or volatility. The test is whether the assumption is supportable, consistently derived, disclosed, and symmetric when conditions move in the opposite direction.[3][11]

### 5. Construct the ownership waterfall

Start with beginning actual shares and add shares issued for vesting, exercise, employee purchase, acquisitions, and other purposes. Subtract shares withheld for taxes and shares repurchased. Tie the result to ending actual shares. Then reconcile weighted-average basic shares to diluted shares by instrument, and separately inventory awards omitted from diluted EPS because they were anti-dilutive. This waterfall prevents a flat ending count from hiding large gross issuance and prevents period-specific diluted EPS from being mistaken for a complete valuation claim.[2][6][7]

### 6. Separate dilution-offset buybacks from net distributions

Estimate the number of shares required to offset employee-plan issuance and multiply by the actual or a reasonable weighted-average repurchase price. Show this cash separately from repurchases that reduce the share count beyond compensation-related issuance. The estimate is an analytical allocation, not a GAAP number, so assumptions must be disclosed. Compare the company's stated repurchase purpose with the observed roll-forward; a program described as a shareholder return but consumed mainly by new employee shares warrants a corrected narrative.[8][9][16][18]

### 7. Recast free cash flow coherently

Start from GAAP operating cash flow and identify the stock-compensation addback. Under a cost approach, subtract recurring stock compensation or the estimated cash needed to neutralize associated dilution. Under a claim approach, retain the addback but forecast the expanding share base and value outstanding equity claims separately. Include cash tax withholding, exercise proceeds, award settlements, and repurchases in their actual cash-flow categories. Do not deduct the expense and a full dilution-offset buyback simultaneously unless the model explains why both represent separate burdens; that can double count the same compensation transfer.[2][12][19]

### 8. Inspect capitalization and later release

Reconcile capitalized stock compensation to the asset roll-forward and identify the employees and projects that generated it. Compare capitalization rates with project milestones, qualifying labor categories, and the treatment of cash payroll. Track amortization, cost-of-sales release, impairment, and abandonment. If adjusted measures exclude both current stock compensation and amortization of previously capitalized stock compensation, restore both at the appropriate periods before assessing margins and reinvestment.[16][19]

### 9. Map the future expense and dilution pipeline

Combine unrecognized compensation cost, weighted-average recognition period, unvested units, maximum performance units, outstanding options, remaining plan capacity, and the company's hiring or retention strategy. The result is not a deterministic forecast. It is a range showing cost already granted but not yet recognized, probable future grants needed to maintain the program, and shares that could enter the ownership base. This prevents a temporary fall in current expense after layoffs, forfeitures, or a grant cycle from being mistaken for a permanent structural improvement.[1][2][17]

### 10. Grade the conclusion

Use five outcomes: transparent, presentation risk, aggressive, likely error, or evidence consistent with manipulation. Transparent means the expense, ownership transfer, cash-flow addback, capitalization, tax effects, and buybacks reconcile and management states what its adjusted measure omits. Presentation risk means the accounting reconciles but the headline metric can mislead without additional work. Aggressive means assumptions or exclusions repeatedly favor performance but remain within an arguable range. Likely error requires evidence that the accounting or reconciliation fails. Evidence consistent with manipulation requires converging facts such as unsupported valuation inputs, concealed plan changes, inconsistent definitions, unexplained top-side entries, or deliberate omission of material dilution. The author's proposed grading rule follows the library's broader forensic method: no allegation from one ratio or one exclusion.[3]

## Evidence

### Standard setters treat service cost and dilution as different effects

FASB Statement 123(R) replaced an employee-option accounting model that could record little or no expense for at-the-money options with a fair-value-based method intended to reflect the economic consequences of employee services paid in equity.[1] Topic 718 and SEC SAB 120 extend the measurement and disclosure system across options, restricted shares, performance awards, share appreciation rights, and employee purchase plans.[3] ASU 2016-09 confirms that cost over the requisite service period is based on grant-date fair value for awards whose service is rendered, while also changing forfeiture, tax, and cash-flow details.[2]

The IASB's post-implementation research addressed the common argument that expense recognition and diluted shares count the same thing twice. Its project summary says the service cost belongs in the earnings numerator of both basic and diluted EPS, while potential future sharing of profits belongs in the diluted denominator.[5] IAS 33 separately treats fixed-term employee options and nonvested ordinary shares as options in diluted EPS, subject to its anti-dilution rules.[6] These independent frameworks support the narrower conclusion that analysts must inspect both cost and ownership rather than choose one and ignore the other.

### Economic dilution can exceed reported treasury-stock dilution

Core, Guay, and Kothari studied 731 firms and 1,787 firm-year observations from the mid-1990s. They estimated option claims using option sensitivity to stock price and compared that economic dilution with the treasury-stock method used in reported diluted EPS. Their paper reports that estimated economic dilution from employee options was, on average, as much as 200 percent greater than reported incremental-share dilution in the sample.[7] The finding is specific to the sample, period, option designs, and model; it does not provide a universal multiplier for current restricted-unit programs. It demonstrates that diluted EPS is not necessarily a complete measure of the outstanding option claim.

Bens, Nagar, Skinner, and Wong examined 357 S&P 500 industrial firms from 1996 through 1999. They found that executives increased repurchases as the dilutive effect of employee options on diluted EPS increased, and that reporting incentives helped explain repurchase behavior alongside excess cash, undervaluation, leverage, and preservation of option value.[8] The evidence links compensation dilution to a real cash-allocation decision. It does not show that every dilution-offset repurchase is opportunistic, but it rejects the assumption that repurchases can always be interpreted independently of employee awards.

Kahle examined thousands of open-market repurchase announcements from 1991 through 1996 and found evidence that employee options influenced repurchase decisions. Announcement returns were lower when nonexecutive options outstanding were higher, after controls, and option exercise or dilution supplied a distinct motive from classic signaling and free-cash-flow explanations.[9] Read with Bens and colleagues, the studies support a gross-share reconciliation: a repurchase can distribute cash, manage EPS, supply shares for exercises, and offset dilution at the same time.

### Markets recognize the cost, but reporting inputs and analyst models can still obscure it

Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik studied disclosed SFAS 123 expense before mandatory recognition. After controlling for net income, book value, expected earnings growth, and the mechanical relation between share price and option values, they found that equity investors treated the disclosed amount as an expense and that it was sufficiently reliable to enter valuation assessments.[10] This evidence undermines the claim that fair-value-based stock expense is categorically too artificial to matter.

The same authors later tested four option-pricing inputs: expected life, volatility, dividend yield, and risk-free rate. They found evidence that firms understated option values and disclosed stock-compensation expense, with the strongest findings for expected life and volatility and weaker or no findings for inputs offering less discretion.[11] The two studies are complementary rather than contradictory. A measure can be relevant and sufficiently reliable in aggregate while still permitting biased assumptions at particular firms.

Mohanram, White, and Zhao studied how market participants treat stock compensation. They found that firms with higher stock compensation had higher valuation ratios and lower subsequent returns, and that the pattern was stronger with greater analyst coverage. Their hand-collected analyst reports showed more optimistic target prices when analysts ignored stock compensation, while analysts who treated it as an expense were unbiased on average in their sample.[13] The study's associations do not prove that every high-stock-compensation firm is overvalued, but they provide direct evidence that omission from earnings or discounted-cash-flow models can affect valuation.

Bhojraj examined the cash-flow presentation problem and argued that treating stock compensation solely as a noncash addback can inflate valuation when no offset captures the shareholder transfer. Proposed solutions included presenting an operating outflow with an offsetting financing inflow or providing disclosures that expose the economic financing event.[12] The paper supports an analytical recast rather than a claim that the GAAP cash-flow statement is arithmetically wrong.

### Tax evidence shows why book expense and cash benefit must be reconciled

Graham, Lang, and Shackelford examined nonqualified option deductions for Nasdaq 100 and S&P 100 firms. They reported that the median estimated marginal tax rate for Nasdaq firms fell from 31 percent when option deductions were ignored to 5 percent when the deductions were incorporated; the effect was smaller for the S&P firms. They also found evidence consistent with option deductions acting as nondebt tax shields in capital-structure decisions.[14] This evidence explains why a company can show a material exercise-related cash-tax benefit that is not equal to grant-date book compensation.

ASU 2016-09 changed U.S. GAAP so excess tax benefits and deficiencies run through the income statement and related cash flows are classified with operating income taxes.[2] The resulting volatility can affect reported tax rates and earnings. A forensic model should therefore separate operating pretax performance from award-related tax remeasurement while retaining the genuine cash-tax effect. Excluding only unfavorable tax deficiencies or presenting favorable benefits as operating improvement would be asymmetric.

### Company disclosures show the complete pattern in practice

Akamai's third-quarter 2023 release reported GAAP diluted EPS of $1.04 and non-GAAP diluted EPS of $1.63. The reconciliation included $0.56 per share for stock compensation and another $0.06 for amortization of capitalized stock compensation and capitalized interest. The company also disclosed $113 million of quarterly repurchases and explained why it excluded stock compensation from its non-GAAP measures.[16] Its 2023 annual filing reported $328.5 million of current stock-compensation expense, additional amortization primarily from capitalized internal-use software, and $462.1 million of unrecognized compensation cost.[17] The example is not evidence of wrongdoing; it demonstrates why current expense, prior capitalization, adjusted profit, future cost, and repurchases must be analyzed together.

Airbnb's first-quarter 2023 shareholder letter disclosed $240 million of stock-compensation expense, excluded it from Adjusted EBITDA, added it back in operating cash flow, spent about $493 million on share repurchases, and said the program helped offset dilution from employee stock programs. The same letter explicitly described stock compensation as a significant recurring expense expected to continue and as an important part of compensation strategy.[18] This unusually direct disclosure makes the economic trade-off visible. The forensic concern would arise if an analyst repeated adjusted EBITDA and free cash flow but omitted the filing's own recurrence and dilution warnings.

EastGroup's 2023 filing illustrates capitalization: it disclosed that $693,000 of first-quarter employee stock-compensation cost was capitalized as development cost.[19] The example confirms that current expense alone can understate current compensation generated without violating Topic 718. It also supplies a concrete test: compare stock-cost capitalization with the accounting treatment of cash payroll for the same qualifying development activity.

## Implications

### For investors and valuation analysts

The first implication is that stock compensation cannot be normalized with a mechanical addback. An investor should choose an economic treatment and carry it through all statements. If recurring stock compensation is deducted as an operating cost, forecast earnings and free cash flow after that cost and avoid adding the same burden again through a full dilution-offset repurchase. If it is treated as noncash financing, model new claims, outstanding awards, per-share dilution, and repurchase cash explicitly. Both approaches can be made coherent; combining the most favorable element of each cannot.[5][7][12][13]

Per-share valuation requires more than dividing by the latest diluted share count. The analyst should value or scenario-test options, unvested units, performance ranges, employee purchase plans, convertibles, and likely future grants. A current loss can cause awards to be excluded from diluted EPS even though they remain outstanding. A treasury-stock calculation can omit time value that matters to equity valuation. A stable basic share count can be purchased with cash that would otherwise belong to owners.[6][7][8][9]

A useful output is a three-part bridge. The operating bridge starts with GAAP operating profit and shows stock cost generated, immediate expense, capitalization, prior-cost release, and non-GAAP exclusions. The cash bridge starts with operating cash flow and shows the stock-compensation addback, tax settlement, exercise proceeds, withholding, and cash used for dilution-offset repurchases. The ownership bridge starts with actual shares and shows gross issuance, tax withholding, repurchases, diluted instruments, and unrecognized awards. The author's synthesis is that a thesis depending on high adjusted margins or free-cash-flow conversion should fail review if those three bridges cannot be reconciled.

The second implication is to evaluate trend and scale. Stock compensation can be useful when it aligns employees, conserves scarce cash, and supports retention. The question is whether the cost grows faster than revenue, gross profit, operating cash flow before the addback, or value created per share. A falling ratio during rapid revenue growth may show operating leverage; a falling ratio caused by share-price collapse, forfeitures, or delayed grants may be temporary. The future-cost pipeline and award roll-forward identify which explanation is consistent with the disclosures.[1][2][17]

The third implication is price discipline in repurchases. Cash spent to offset dilution is not automatically wasteful; it can be the financing consequence of a deliberate compensation policy. But a company that grants equity when its shares are undervalued and repurchases the shares later at a higher price may transfer more value than the grant-date expense suggests. Conversely, issuing richly valued shares can be an efficient financing choice even though it dilutes ownership. The decision must be assessed from grant economics, employee value, market price, repurchase price, retention benefit, and per-share value rather than from EPS accretion alone.[8][9]

### For boards and compensation committees

Boards should approve an equity-compensation budget in both value and share terms. Grant-date accounting cost, maximum shares, expected vesting, realized employee value, dilution, and cash needed to neutralize issuance answer different questions. A plan can stay within an accounting expense budget but exceed a share budget after a price decline, because more units are needed to deliver a target value. It can stay within a share budget but produce an excessive transfer after a large price rise. The committee should see both dimensions and the sensitivity of performance awards.[1][3][5]

The compensation committee should also preserve the grant-date decision record. For options, record the source and rationale for expected term, volatility, dividends, and other inputs. For performance awards, record targets, probability assessments, maximum payout, modifications, and the evidence supporting changes. Aboody, Barth, and Kasznik's findings on expected-life and volatility discretion show why independently governed inputs matter.[11] The committee should review whether changes are symmetric and whether awards granted around material nonpublic information receive the scrutiny described in SEC SAB 120.[3]

Repurchase authorization belongs in the same governance packet. The board should know how much of each proposed authorization is expected to offset employee issuance, how much can reduce net shares, what valuation range governs purchases, and what investment or liquidity is displaced. Akamai and Airbnb openly state dilution offset as a repurchase purpose.[16][18] That transparency should be standard because it lets owners distinguish compensation financing from net cash distribution.

A board should not evaluate a plan solely by retention or share-price performance. Equity awards can encourage long-term ownership, but option-like pay can also increase risk-taking incentives, and broad market gains can reward results unrelated to management action. Those incentive-design questions extend beyond this forensic topic. The relevant accounting safeguard is narrower: report the full cost, preserve the share and cash consequences, disclose performance outcomes, and do not let an adjusted metric erase the compensation that the board intentionally granted.[1][3][5]

### For management and finance teams

Management can reduce suspicion by making the full reconciliation easy. Present stock compensation by function and award class; disclose current expense, capitalized cost, amortization of prior cost, unrecognized cost, forfeitures, performance adjustments, and tax effects; and show gross issuance against gross repurchases. If a non-GAAP measure excludes the expense, state that the item is recurring when it is recurring and explain the decision purpose served by the measure. Airbnb's explicit warning that stock compensation was significant, recurring, and central to its compensation strategy is more decision-useful than calling the exclusion simply noncash.[18]

Finance teams should align internal decisions with external reporting. A business unit evaluated before stock compensation may prefer labor financed with equity even when the company-level transfer is expensive. An adjusted margin target that excludes stock pay can reward replacing cash salary with awards without improving productivity. Internal capital budgeting and product profitability should therefore include a compensation charge or a centrally allocated equity cost. The author's assessment is that the worst control failure is a metric that makes the financing form, rather than the economic productivity of labor, determine whether a team appears profitable.[1][5][12]

Capitalization controls require special attention. The same eligibility rules applied to cash payroll should apply to stock compensation for the employees performing qualifying work. Time records, project milestones, placed-in-service dates, failed projects, and functional allocation should support the asset. Later amortization should remain visible even if management excludes it from a supplemental metric. A policy that removes current cost when granted and removes amortization when released can create a permanent adjusted-earnings exclusion from what was a recurring labor program.[16][19]

### For auditors, regulators, and forensic investigators

Auditors should reconcile compensation records, valuation models, payroll and human-resources data, board approvals, legal plan terms, transfer-agent records, tax settlements, and financial-statement disclosures. The central assertions differ by award: completeness of grants, measurement of fair value, classification as equity or liability, probability of performance conditions, service attribution, capitalization eligibility, tax measurement, and share-count presentation.[1][2][3] A high-level comparison of total expense with the prior year is insufficient when the award mix or capitalization rate changed.

Analytical procedures should look for linked anomalies. Examples include falling recognized expense while grants and unrecognized cost rise; a lower option value caused by unsupported expected life or volatility; a performance-probability change that supplies exactly the amount needed to meet guidance; capitalized stock cost increasing without corresponding project or headcount evidence; diluted shares falling only because cash repurchases exceed issuance; and non-GAAP margins expanding while GAAP labor cost and dilution rise. Each signal has innocent explanations. The investigator should state those alternatives and seek transaction-level evidence before escalating.[3][11]

Regulators face a presentation problem that crosses GAAP and non-GAAP boundaries. GAAP can correctly show expense and cash flow while a supplemental measure and investor narrative imply that the cost is irrelevant. Existing SEC guidance requires reconciliation and addresses misleading or inconsistent adjustments.[4] More comparable disclosure of stock cost generated, capitalized amounts, dilution-offset repurchases, and award backlogs would make the economic sequence easier to test without prohibiting management from presenting supplemental views. This is the author's policy inference from the standards, filings, and cash-flow research.[3][4][12][16][18]

### For credit analysts and employees

Credit analysis should not treat stock compensation exactly like near-term cash salary because equity settlement can preserve liquidity. It should also not ignore the cost. A company that depends on awards to retain employees may need cash compensation if its share price falls, dilution becomes unacceptable, or the plan lacks authorized shares. A recurring repurchase program used to offset awards can also become discretionary under stress, causing dilution to accelerate when cash is scarce. The credit model should therefore distinguish current cash protection from the longer-run labor and ownership obligation.[1][17][18]

Employees face a different measurement. Grant-date accounting fair value is not guaranteed realized pay. Vesting, forfeiture, performance conditions, exercise behavior, taxes, concentration, and share-price outcomes can make realized value lower or higher. That uncertainty can justify a compensation premium from the employee's perspective, but it does not eliminate the cost to owners. Transparent communication should distinguish accounting value, current intrinsic value, potential value, and after-tax realized value rather than present the grant as equivalent to a certain cash amount.[1][5][14]

### Limits and final test

No single stock-compensation ratio proves a shenanigan. Young firms may rationally conserve cash; mature firms may repurchase shares for several motives; grant-date fair value can diverge from realized value without error; capitalization can be required; and a non-GAAP exclusion can be clearly reconciled and useful for a defined comparison.[1][4][8][9] The correct conclusion depends on the whole chain and on contrary evidence.

The final inversion is simple: ask what would have to be hidden for the compensation to look free. The answer is some combination of recognized labor cost, capitalized labor cost, future unrecognized cost, option-model discretion, cash-flow addback, tax effects, gross issuance, potential dilution, or repurchase cash. A presentation is robust when every link is visible and reconcilable. It becomes a shenanigan when management or analysis preserves the benefits of equity financing while omitting the recurring cost and ownership transfer that made those benefits possible. This closing test is the author's synthesis of the standards, empirical studies, and filing examples.[1][3][5][7][12][13]
