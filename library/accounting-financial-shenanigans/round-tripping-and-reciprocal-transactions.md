# Round-Tripping and Reciprocal Transactions -- Circular Cash Can Manufacture Revenue Without Creating Sales

Round-tripping turns a company's own cash, a reciprocal purchase, or an offsetting obligation into reported revenue even though the linked arrangement creates little or no new economic activity. Detecting it requires treating all connected contracts and cash movements as one transaction, tracing who ultimately funded the apparent customer, and testing whether independent demand, control transfer, and commercial risk existed. [Sources 1-11]

## Background

Revenue normally records consideration earned by transferring goods or services to a customer. Topic 606 and IFRS 15 organize that conclusion around an enforceable contract, identified performance obligations, a transaction price, and transfer of control; both standards also exclude nonmonetary exchanges between entities in the same line of business when those exchanges merely facilitate sales to other customers. That exclusion illustrates a broader point: movement of goods, data, capacity, or invoices between counterparties does not by itself create customer revenue. The accounting must reflect what the parties obtained and surrendered as an economic whole. [Sources 1 and 2]

A reciprocal relationship is not inherently improper. Two businesses can independently buy from one another because each needs the other's product, negotiates on arm's-length terms, bears ordinary price and performance risk, and would complete its purchase even if the other transaction did not occur. A round-trip scheme removes that independence. One sale is conditioned on another purchase, the seller directly or indirectly supplies the buyer's funding, a buyback or side agreement reverses the apparent transfer, or several intermediaries circulate the original cash back to its source. The legal documents may describe separate purchases and sales, but the economic result can be no change in assets, risk, demand, or earning power. The author's synthesis is that dependency, rather than reciprocity alone, is the defining forensic question. [Sources 1-9]

The problem became visible during the late-1990s and early-2000s emphasis on top-line growth, trading volume, Internet subscribers, network capacity, and advertising revenue. SEC cases involving CMS Energy, Qwest, Homestore, and AOL alleged or found different versions of the same structure: simultaneous buy-sell energy trades, fiber-capacity swaps, multi-party advertising cash loops, and vendor purchases tied to advertising commitments. The mechanics varied, but each arrangement made reported activity appear larger than the independent market demand or economic change supported. [Sources 3-6 and 9]

Later cases show that the mechanism did not disappear with those industries. The SEC's Comscore order addressed nonmonetary data exchanges, linked monetary transactions, and side agreements used to increase or accelerate revenue. The SEC's Longfin complaint alleged fictitious commodity trades among entities controlled by the same executive, supported by forged contracts and phony bills of lading. These cases demonstrate that round-tripping can be built from cash, barter, data, capacity rights, inventory documents, related parties, or combinations of them. [Sources 7 and 8]

Round-tripping matters even when net income does not change. CMS recorded equal revenue and expense from zero-margin trades, so the scheme inflated gross revenue, gross expense, and trading volume without increasing net earnings. A larger revenue base can still change perceived scale, market share, growth, valuation multiples, credit analysis, executive incentives, and industry rankings. Other structures do increase earnings: the customer may return only part of the original funding, the company may capitalize or misclassify its reciprocal purchase, or the apparent sale may carry a margin while the related outflow is hidden elsewhere. [Sources 3-9]

The standards and enforcement record therefore require analysis beyond whether cash was received. Cash can be genuine and still be circular. A customer confirmation can verify a payment yet omit the linked purchase, financing, guarantee, referral, buyback, or side letter that made the payment possible. PCAOB fraud and related-party standards accordingly direct attention to unusual transactions, business purpose, management override, contradictory evidence, related-party identification, and corroboration of management explanations. [Sources 10 and 11]

The forensic objective is not to declare every reciprocal trade fraudulent. It is to reconstruct the entire arrangement, identify the source and final destination of consideration, and determine whether the apparent revenue represents an independent customer's demand for a transferred good or service. Where evidence is incomplete, the proper conclusion is an unresolved revenue-quality risk, not an accusation. [Sources 1, 2, 10, and 11]

## Core Concepts

### Economic substance outranks document count

A circular scheme often depends on presenting one economic arrangement as several unrelated legal contracts. The company may show a purchase order from a customer, a vendor invoice, a media-buy agreement, and separate bank transfers. Each document can be authentic. The misstatement arises because the documents were negotiated together, performance under one depends on performance under another, or one party funded the counterparty's apparent purchase. Reading each contract alone preserves the structure's camouflage. [Sources 3-9]

The first test is counterfactual: would each party have entered its contract on the same terms, at the same time, and in the same amount if the reciprocal transaction did not exist? The second test is economic change: after combining the linked legs, did either party obtain a new resource, transfer control, assume meaningful risk, or satisfy independent demand? The third test is funding: did the apparent customer pay from its own resources or from money, credit support, inflated vendor pricing, or an intermediary supplied by the reporting company? These are the author's synthesized tests from Topic 606, IFRS 15, the SEC cases, and PCAOB requirements. [Sources 1-11]

Contract combination is not merely a stylistic preference. If contracts are negotiated as a package, consideration in one depends on the price or performance of another, or the promises form one commercial objective, separate accounting can produce revenue that the combined arrangement does not support. Topic 606 and IFRS 15 contain contract-combination principles, while their scope exclusions for same-line-of-business nonmonetary exchanges prevent a facilitating exchange from being treated as ordinary customer revenue. [Sources 1 and 2]

### Direct two-party round trips

The simplest structure has two counterparties and two offsetting trades. Company A sells an item to Company B while Company A simultaneously buys the same or a similar item from Company B. Equal quantities, equal prices, matching dates, no contemplated delivery, and no profit strongly indicate that the trades are one circular arrangement rather than independent sales. CMS Energy's trades fit this pattern: the SEC found prearranged purchases and sales of the same energy volume at the same price, with no delivery contemplated and neither party earning a profit. [Source 3]

Gross accounting magnifies the effect. If each leg is recorded separately, Company A reports revenue on its sale and expense on its purchase, while Company B can do the same. Net income may be unchanged, yet both companies appear to conduct more business. Recording the arrangement net can reduce both lines to zero, but net presentation is not a cure if the arrangement also affects nonfinancial volume metrics or if disclosures still portray the activity as genuine customer demand. [Sources 3 and 9]

A direct round trip need not use identical assets. Qwest's IRU swaps paired sales and purchases of fiber-optic capacity. The SEC alleged that the assets were similar and that Qwest used the reciprocal purchases to obtain counterparties for its own sales while recognizing revenue up front. The economic question was not whether two contracts and two capacity rights existed; it was whether swapping similar capacity created a revenue-generating sale or merely exchanged comparable assets to fill a reporting gap. [Source 4]

### Multi-party circular funding

Intermediaries can separate the outgoing and returning cash so the transactions appear independent. A basic three- or four-party loop is: Company A pays Vendor B; Vendor B pays Intermediary C; Intermediary C buys advertising, data, or another service from Company A; Company A records the returning cash as revenue. The number of bank accounts changes, but Company A still funded its own apparent customer. [Sources 5 and 6]

Homestore illustrates the structure. The SEC alleged that Homestore paid vendors for products or services it generally did not need, the vendors bought advertising from media companies, and the media companies bought advertising from Homestore. During the first two quarters of 2001, Homestore paid vendors $49.8 million, vendors paid a media company $45.1 million, and Homestore recorded $36.7 million from the media company's related advertising purchases as revenue. The spread among the amounts represented fees and leakage through the loop, not independent demand for Homestore advertising. [Source 5]

Multi-party routing creates three detection difficulties. First, the apparent customer may not receive money directly from the reporting company. Second, each individual transfer may have an invoice and a plausible label. Third, ordinary vendor and customer master files do not reveal that different legal entities share owners, executives, addresses, bank accounts, referral relationships, or a common negotiator. The author's synthesis is that cash-flow tracing must therefore be paired with counterparty mapping. [Sources 5, 6, 8, 10, and 11]

### Vendor-funded and inflated-price reciprocity

A company can manufacture customer funding by overpaying a vendor or surrendering a discount, conditioned on the vendor buying something back. The outward payment is recorded as equipment, software, consulting, distribution, or another purchase; the return is recorded as revenue. If the vendor would not have bought the company's product without the inflated purchase price, the company has economically funded the sale. [Sources 6 and 9]

The SEC's AOL complaint described vendor transactions in which AOL allegedly paid inflated prices or forgone discounts in exchange for vendors' online advertising purchases. The complaint also described customers that lacked a need or budget for the advertising until the linked arrangement supplied the economics. The forensic test is to compare the purchase price with prior quotes, market benchmarks, normal discounts, and the timing and amount of the counterparty's later purchase. [Source 6]

This form can increase earnings rather than only revenue. If the reciprocal purchase is capitalized, amortized slowly, assigned to an acquisition, or hidden in a different expense line, current revenue can rise faster than current expense. Even when both legs are expensed, the arrangement can inflate gross revenue and a growth metric management has emphasized. The accounting entry must therefore be evaluated together with the commercial terms and the destination of the purchased item. This paragraph is the author's synthesis of the case mechanics. [Sources 4-7 and 9]

### Nonmonetary exchanges and barter

No cash is needed for a reciprocal inflation scheme. Two companies can exchange advertising, data, capacity, or other noncash assets and each report revenue based on asserted fair values. Fair value becomes especially vulnerable when the assets are unique, rarely sold for cash, unwanted by the recipient, or valued from circular list prices. Topic 606 and IFRS 15 exclude certain same-line-of-business nonmonetary exchanges from their revenue scope, and the broader analysis must still consider whether the exchange has commercial substance. [Sources 1, 2, 7, and 9]

The SEC's Comscore order found that Comscore entered nonmonetary data exchanges to increase reported revenue. The SEC press release stated that the company recognized revenue using values that had been improperly increased, and the order addressed transactions in which data supplied by the counterparty lacked commercial substance or a reliably determinable fair value. The order also addressed linked monetary transactions treated separately and side agreements that changed remaining performance obligations. [Source 7]

A noncash exchange can be legitimate when each party independently needs what it receives, the assets differ in a way that changes future cash flows, and the values are observable or otherwise supportable. The red flags are mirrored contract values, assets not requested by the counterparty, negligible use after receipt, no cash sales of comparable assets, and exchange volume that rises near reporting deadlines. These indicators initiate investigation; they do not establish a misstatement without the contract and use evidence. [Sources 1, 2, 7, 9, and 10]

### Repurchase promises and side agreements

A sale with a repurchase obligation may be financing in substance. The seller receives cash now but remains obligated, explicitly or implicitly, to return cash and take back the asset. A guaranteed resale, put right, porting right, return right, price protection, or oral buyback can prevent the apparent transfer from satisfying the conditions for revenue. [Sources 4, 9, and 10]

Side agreements are effective concealment devices because the main contract can appear compliant. Qwest enforcement materials describe portability and other side arrangements that were omitted from the contract reviewed by accountants. The SEC's revenue-recognition speech likewise stressed that companies and auditors must evaluate the substance of linked arrangements even when no single rule describes the entire structure. [Sources 4 and 9]

Detection requires collecting more than signed master contracts. Relevant evidence includes emails, term sheets, board materials, sales compensation files, referral agreements, vendor negotiations, credit support, subsequent returns, repurchases, amendments, and oral representations confirmed independently. A clean contract cannot overcome contradictory evidence showing that the customer had a protected exit or bought only because another promise existed. [Sources 4, 9, and 10]

### Related parties and controlled counterparties

Circularity is easier when the same person controls several entities. The entities can issue invoices to one another, route funds through different accounts, sign confirming documents, and create the appearance of external counterparties while remaining one economic group. Related-party identification is therefore part of revenue testing, not a separate disclosure exercise. [Sources 8 and 11]

The SEC's Longfin complaint alleged that Longfin recorded fictitious purchases and sales of commodity bills of lading among entities controlled by its chief executive. The complaint alleged that Longfin never held title to the underlying commodities and used forged contracts and phony bills of lading to support more than $66 million of reported revenue. Genuine-looking documents were not independent evidence because the same control network produced them. [Source 8]

Ownership is only one connection. The author's synthesis is that analysts should also map common directors, employees, family members, registered addresses, phone numbers, email domains, banks, auditors, legal advisers, introducers, and transaction negotiators. A counterparty can be legally unrelated yet economically dependent on the reporting company for funding or business. [Sources 5, 6, 8, and 11]

### Revenue, expense, cash, and balance-sheet signatures

Round trips do not produce one universal ratio. A zero-margin gross-up can raise revenue and cost of sales equally, leaving gross profit and operating cash flow nearly unchanged. A partially circular sale can inflate revenue and receivables while the related payment appears in another expense or asset account. A funded customer can produce genuine cash collections, while the outgoing funding appears earlier, later, in investing cash flow, or through a third party. [Sources 3-8]

Several patterns are nevertheless useful. Revenue and expense can rise together without a corresponding increase in gross profit. Receivables and payables can expand with the same counterparties or by similar amounts. Period-end sales can reverse shortly after year-end through refunds, purchases, repurchases, or credit notes. Transaction values can repeat in round amounts through several entities. Reported sales volume can diverge from physical delivery, customer use, capacity utilization, or external market demand. These patterns are the author's synthesized screens from the enforcement cases. [Sources 3-10]

Cash-flow analysis must use a net-source perspective. For every apparent customer receipt, trace preceding and subsequent payments to the customer, its affiliates, intermediaries, vendors, and financiers. The relevant measure is not merely cash received from customers but the company's net cash retained after linked outflows and obligations. Even that net amount is not automatically revenue: a retained fee can be a financing or arrangement fee rather than consideration for the product described as sold. [Sources 3-9]

### Legitimate reciprocity versus fabricated revenue

The strongest evidence of legitimacy is separability. Each transaction has a documented business purpose; independent personnel negotiated it; pricing is supported without reference to the other leg; the buyer had a pre-existing need and ability to pay; delivery or use occurred; risks and control changed; and either transaction would survive cancellation of the other. Subsequent conduct should match those facts. [Sources 1, 2, 9-11]

The strongest evidence of fabrication is dependency. Amounts and dates mirror one another; one contract refers informally to another despite formal separation; the customer lacks need or funds; the seller or its affiliate supplies the funding; an intermediary retains only a fee; goods are not delivered or used; side agreements remove risk; the same negotiator controls both legs; or cash returns to its origin. No one indicator is conclusive, but several aligned indicators narrow the innocent explanations. This is the author's synthesis of Sources 3-11.

## Evidence

### CMS Energy -- volume and revenue without delivery or profit

The SEC's 2004 CMS Energy order provides the clearest direct two-party case. It found that CMS's marketing subsidiary and counterparties entered massive prearranged transactions involving simultaneous purchases and sales of electricity or natural gas in the same volume and at the same price. No delivery was contemplated and neither party expected profit. The purpose was to increase reported trading volume and improve the subsidiary's industry ranking. [Source 3]

CMS recorded the trades gross. The SEC found that this treatment overstated revenue and expense by $5.2 billion over one year: $1.0 billion in 2000 and $4.2 billion during the first three quarters of 2001. Net earnings did not change, but the reporting created a false picture of business scale and robust trading activity. The case demonstrates why an analyst cannot dismiss a revenue overstatement merely because expense rises by the same amount. [Source 3]

The detection trail combined accounting and nonfinancial evidence. Internal variance reports attributed receivable and payable increases to buy-sell deals; the company's external auditor identified no-margin trades; and industry publications observed unusually large bilateral volumes. The transactions were visible when the same counterparties, quantities, dates, receivables, payables, and trading metrics were examined together. [Source 3]

### Homestore -- the company's cash returned as advertising revenue

The SEC's Homestore complaint alleged a multi-party cash loop designed to exceed analysts' revenue expectations. Homestore paid inflated amounts to vendors, the vendors bought advertising from media companies, and the media companies bought advertising from Homestore. Homestore recorded the returning cash as advertising revenue even though the arrangement's purpose was to generate that revenue and the parties generally lacked independent business need for the linked purchases. [Source 5]

The amounts make the structure measurable. In the first two quarters of 2001, Homestore paid vendors $49.8 million; the vendors paid $45.1 million to a media company; and Homestore recorded $36.7 million from the media company's related purchases. Across the first three quarters of 2001, the SEC stated that Homestore overstated advertising revenue by $46 million, or 64 percent. The difference between cash sent and revenue returned did not create an independent customer; it was the cost of routing the company's funds through the loop. [Source 5]

The case also demonstrates why ordinary customer confirmation can be insufficient. The media company could confirm that it paid Homestore, and vendors could confirm that they bought advertising. The missing fact was that Homestore initiated and funded the chain. A complete test had to ask where the customer's money came from, why each purchase occurred, and whether the intermediaries knew the transactions were linked. [Sources 5, 10, and 11]

### AOL Time Warner -- overpayments and foregone discounts funded advertising sales

The SEC's AOL Time Warner complaint alleged several round-trip forms. In vendor transactions, AOL paid inflated prices or surrendered discounts for goods and services while the vendor agreed to buy online advertising in an amount related to the markup or lost discount. In other arrangements, AOL supplied customers with funds that enabled advertising purchases they otherwise did not need or budget. The company documented the legs as separate arm's-length transactions while allegedly recognizing the return flow as advertising revenue. [Source 6]

The case broadens the detection rule. Cash need not return through the same entity or on the same day, and the outward payment need not be labeled customer financing. It may be embedded in an above-market equipment price, software license, acquisition payment, or surrendered discount. Price benchmarking and negotiation history are therefore part of revenue testing when a vendor is also a customer. [Source 6]

### Qwest -- reciprocal capacity purchases and concealed terms

The SEC's Qwest complaint alleged approximately $3.8 billion of spurious revenue across a broader accounting fraud. One mechanism was the use of IRU swaps: Qwest bought fiber-optic capacity from other telecommunications companies in exchange for their purchases of capacity from Qwest. The SEC alleged that similar assets were exchanged and that revenue should not have been recognized on the reciprocal sale. [Source 4]

The complaint also described secret side agreements, porting rights, incomplete delivery, backdating, and other terms inconsistent with immediate recognition. Qwest publicly portrayed nonrecurring capacity and equipment sales as recurring service growth. The case shows that round-trip analysis must address both whether revenue exists and what kind of revenue it is. Even a sale with some economic substance can mislead if it depends on a reciprocal purchase and is presented as sustainable customer demand. [Source 4]

### Comscore -- noncash exchanges, linked cash transactions, and side agreements

The SEC's 2019 order and press release found that Comscore used nonmonetary data exchanges and other arrangements to overstate revenue by approximately $50 million from 2014 through early 2016. Comscore and counterparties exchanged data without cash, while the company used asserted fair values and included data that counterparties had not requested. The order also addressed linked monetary transactions accounted for separately, including a pair that enabled recognition of $9.4 million in 2015, and side agreements that concealed future obligations. [Source 7]

The SEC stated that the conduct helped Comscore exceed consensus revenue targets in seven consecutive quarters and create an appearance of smooth growth. The evidence is important because it joins three mechanisms often reviewed separately: unsupported fair value in barter, separation of linked cash contracts, and undisclosed obligations. Each mechanism can produce revenue from a transaction whose combined commercial result does not support it. [Source 7]

### Longfin -- controlled entities and fabricated trade documents

The SEC's 2019 Longfin complaint alleged more than $66 million of fictitious commodity revenue, over 89 percent of the company's reported 2017 revenue. It alleged sham round-trip transactions among entities controlled by Longfin's chief executive, forged contracts, phony bills of lading, and commodity trades in which Longfin never held title. [Source 8]

This case demonstrates the limit of document vouching. An invoice, contract, bill of lading, and cash entry can all exist while failing to establish an external sale if the documents are fabricated or created by a common control group. Independent evidence must reach beyond the company's document set to carriers, ports, title records, banks, ultimate counterparties, and physical commodity movement. [Sources 8, 10, and 11]

### Standards and cases converge on the same test

Topic 606 and IFRS 15 require a contract with a customer and revenue that depicts transfer of promised goods or services for expected consideration. Their same-line-of-business exchange exclusions, contract-combination rules, and control framework prevent legal form from automatically creating revenue. The SEC cases apply the same economic reasoning to arrangements involving energy, capacity, advertising, data, and commodities. [Sources 1-9]

PCAOB standards reinforce the evidence method. AS 2401 directs auditors to respond to fraud risks, test unusual journal entries and adjustments, examine significant unusual transactions and their business purpose, and consider management override. AS 2410 requires sufficient appropriate evidence that related parties and their transactions were properly identified, accounted for, and disclosed. These procedures target the two facts round trips try to hide: that ostensibly separate transactions share one purpose, and that ostensibly independent counterparties share control or funding. [Sources 10 and 11]

The evidence base has a limit. Enforcement matters are selected cases, and complaints state allegations unless resolved through findings or admissions. They establish mechanisms and documented examples, not a population rate for all reciprocal transactions. A sound forensic conclusion should identify whether a cited fact is an SEC finding, a complaint allegation, a standard requirement, or the analyst's synthesis. [Sources 3-11]

## Implications

### For investors and analysts -- reconstruct net economics before valuing growth

The first implication is that gross revenue is not self-validating. A company can increase the top line without increasing gross profit, cash retained, customer demand, or competitive position. Revenue growth should therefore be reconciled to gross profit, operating cash flow, receivables, payables, customer concentration, physical volume, usage, and net cash retained after linked payments. A large rise in sales paired with equal expense, flat margin dollars, and unusual bilateral volume is economically different from broad customer growth. [Sources 3-9]

Build a counterparty-level bridge. For every material customer that is also a vendor, acquisition target, investee, borrower, lender, referral partner, or related party, combine all inflows and outflows over a multi-period window. Include payments through affiliates and intermediaries, noncash assets exchanged, guarantees, credit support, repurchase promises, discounts surrendered, and subsequent reversals. The author's synthesis is to compare reported gross revenue with three separate measures: gross consideration received, net cash retained after linked outflows, and revenue supportable after combining the arrangement under the applicable accounting framework. [Sources 1-11]

Normalize valuation metrics explicitly. For a zero-margin gross-up, remove equal revenue and expense; profit may remain unchanged, but revenue multiples, growth rates, gross-margin percentages, and market-share claims change. For a partially funded sale, remove unsupported revenue and its associated cost while separately reversing any hidden asset or deferred expense. For an arrangement that is financing in substance, replace sale proceeds and repurchase obligations with a borrowing and interest analysis. Each adjustment must follow the mechanism rather than applying one blanket haircut. [Sources 1-9]

The quality-of-business conclusion matters as much as the correction. A company that needs reciprocal trades to meet a sales target may have weaker organic demand than reported. A company whose apparent customers require its funding has customer-credit and liquidity exposure. A company that obscures linked terms has a governance problem that increases uncertainty around unrelated estimates. These are analytical implications, not automatic legal conclusions, and should be modeled as wider ranges or a higher required margin of safety when the underlying evidence remains incomplete. [Sources 3-11]

### A practical public-filings workflow

Begin with the footnotes and transaction descriptions, not a ratio screen. Search several years of filings for barter, nonmonetary, reciprocal, swap, exchange, vendor-customer, related party, repurchase, side agreement, consideration payable, referral, reseller, capacity, advertising, and gross-versus-net language. Read revenue policies together with related-party, commitments, acquisitions, receivables, payables, and cash-flow disclosures. The terms that reveal a loop may appear in different notes. This workflow is the author's synthesis. [Sources 1-11]

Next, rank counterparties by both sales and purchases. Compare names after accounting for subsidiaries, trade names, and spelling changes. Identify counterparties that move from vendor to customer near quarter-end, carry both receivable and payable balances, transact in matching amounts, or appear through several intermediaries. Compare each counterparty's payment capacity with its ordinary operations; a newly formed or thinly capitalized customer buying a large amount may be dependent on supplier funding. This is a risk screen, not proof. [Sources 5, 6, 8, and 11]

Then trace timing. Match outgoing payments with incoming receipts over days, weeks, and reporting periods rather than only on the same date. Test whether purchases, sales, credit notes, returns, repurchases, or contract amendments occur immediately before and after quarter-end. A loop can be deliberately staggered to defeat exact-amount matching. Use ranges and cumulative amounts, and investigate shared approvers or negotiators. This method is the author's synthesis from the case patterns. [Sources 3-10]

Finally, seek external corroboration. Verify physical delivery, title, customer use, carrier records, independent funding, and subsequent settlement. Ask whether the customer would have purchased absent the company's payment or promise. Public analysts may not have access to confirmations or bank records, so they should state the limitation and avoid converting an unresolved pattern into a fraud allegation. [Sources 8, 10, and 11]

### For auditors -- test the arrangement, not only the invoice

Revenue confirmation should be designed to reveal linked terms. A confirmation that asks only whether an invoice balance exists can miss that the customer was reimbursed, protected by a return right, required to buy under another agreement, or funded through an intermediary. Where risk is elevated, confirmation should address contract terms, side agreements, source of funds, rights of return or exchange, future obligations, and whether another transaction was negotiated as part of the package. [Sources 4-11]

Audit procedures should connect sales and procurement. Different teams may test the outgoing vendor payment and incoming customer receipt without recognizing that one caused the other. A common counterparty register, linked-contract review, and shared unusual-transaction population reduce that fragmentation. PCAOB requirements concerning related parties, unusual transactions, journal entries, and management override provide the audit basis for connecting those records. [Sources 10 and 11]

Data analytics can prioritize testing. Useful tests include equal or near-equal sales and purchases with the same counterparty, mirrored quantities and prices, high gross revenue with near-zero margin, new customer-vendor pairs near period-end, receivable and payable balances moving together, payments that traverse a short counterparty path and return, and revenue entries posted through manual or senior-management journals. The author's synthesis is that network analysis is more informative than a one-account test because multi-party loops are relational by design. [Sources 3-11]

The evidence threshold remains high. A customer-vendor relationship can be legitimate, and identical commodity trades can serve location, timing, or logistics needs. The auditor must understand business purpose, inspect control and risk transfer, corroborate management's explanation, and evaluate contradictory evidence. Escalation follows unexplained dependency, not reciprocity alone. [Sources 1, 2, 10, and 11]

### For boards and audit committees -- control both ends of the loop

Boards should require a consolidated view of significant counterparties. Management reporting should identify entities that are simultaneously customers, vendors, investees, acquisition parties, financiers, or related parties; show gross and net activity; and explain why reciprocal transactions are independently necessary. Review should include the full commercial package, not only contracts routed through the revenue function. [Sources 6, 10, and 11]

Approval controls should focus on linked and unusual transactions. Require independent review when a sale depends on a company purchase, investment, guarantee, referral, discount concession, or side promise; when the counterparty lacks an evident business need; when noncash assets lack observable values; or when a transaction is material to a reporting target. Preserve negotiations and amendments so the accounting team receives the same terms as the commercial team. [Sources 4-7, 10, and 11]

Compensation design also matters. CMS sought a trading-volume ranking; Homestore, AOL, Qwest, and Comscore cases involved pressure around revenue growth or targets. A control system that rewards gross activity without testing net economics can encourage transactions that satisfy the metric while destroying information quality. The author's synthesis is that boards should compare incentive metrics with cash retained, gross profit, independent customer demand, and returns or reversals. [Sources 3-7]

### For management -- legitimate reciprocal business needs transparent support

Companies with genuine reciprocal relationships can reduce ambiguity by documenting separable business purposes, independent pricing, customer need, approval paths, and how the accounting team considered linked contracts. Disclose material barter, vendor-customer, and nonmonetary arrangements clearly enough for users to understand both gross and net economics. Do not rely on different legal entities, document sets, or reporting lines to imply independence. [Sources 1, 2, 9-11]

Controls should require procurement and sales systems to share counterparty identifiers, including ultimate parents and beneficial owners. Cash applications should flag customer receipts preceded by company payments to the customer or connected parties. The close process should review unusual period-end transactions, manual revenue entries, new counterparties, matching receivables and payables, and significant noncash consideration. These are the author's proposed controls based on the documented schemes and PCAOB requirements. [Sources 3-11]

The worst design failure is a process that verifies each leg while no one owns the combined transaction. Prevent it by assigning one independent reviewer responsibility for economic-substance analysis across contracts, entities, accounts, and periods. The reviewer should be able to stop recognition until contradictions are resolved and should report material disputes to the audit committee. This recommendation is the author's synthesis. [Sources 9-11]

### For regulators and standard-setters -- gross activity can mislead without changing profit

The CMS case shows that financial reporting can be materially misleading even when net earnings are unchanged. Revenue, expense, volume, and ranking data influence users' judgments about scale and competitive position. Enforcement and disclosure review should therefore consider gross presentation, nonfinancial metrics, and business-description claims together rather than treating zero-profit transactions as harmless. [Source 3]

Standard-setting cannot enumerate every circular structure because the intermediaries, assets, and labels change. The durable principles are contract combination, customer identification, transfer of control, commercial substance, related-party transparency, and faithful gross-versus-net presentation. Enforcement cases provide transaction examples; the standards provide the general test. [Sources 1-11]

For all users, the central rule is simple: follow the consideration until it stops. If the apparent customer's payment ultimately came from the reporting company and no independent demand, risk transfer, or net economic change remains after linked legs are combined, the top line may be measuring circulation rather than sales. That statement is the author's synthesis, to be tested against the applicable contracts, accounting framework, and evidence in each case. [Sources 1-11]
