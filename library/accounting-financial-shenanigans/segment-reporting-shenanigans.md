# Segment Reporting Shenanigans -- Aggregation and Allocation Can Hide Weak Businesses Without Changing Consolidated Earnings

Segment reporting can conceal a deteriorating business even when consolidated revenue, profit, and cash are correctly stated. The distortion arises when dissimilar operations are aggregated, costs or intersegment prices are shifted, management-defined profit measures omit burdens, or repeated reorganizations erase comparability, so the forensic task is to reconstruct the economic units beneath the published segments and reconcile every segment story to the consolidated accounts.[1][2][4][5][6]

## Background

Consolidated financial statements answer what the reporting entity earned, owned, owed, and generated in cash as a whole. They do not by themselves show whether a strong division subsidized a weak one, whether an acquisition is meeting its operating plan, whether a new product consumes more capital than it earns, or whether one geography produces most of the reported growth. A conglomerate can therefore report an accurate consolidated total while disclosing too little about the components that created it. Segment reporting was developed to expose those differences and let users assess the business activities and economic environments inside one legal entity.[1][3]

FASB Statement No. 131, issued in 1997 and now codified in Topic 280, replaced a more externally prescribed industry-line model with a management approach. An operating segment is a component for which separate financial information is available and whose results are regularly reviewed by the chief operating decision maker, or CODM, for resource allocation and performance assessment. The CODM identifies a function rather than a mandatory job title. Segment information is generally measured on the basis used internally, then reconciled to the corresponding consolidated amounts.[1][4]

The management approach has an important benefit. It can show investors the same units that senior management uses to allocate capital, evaluate managers, set plans, and discuss operating performance. It can also reduce the cost of producing information because the reporting system already generates it. The same design creates discretion, however, because external reporting depends on how management defines the decision-maker, structures internal reports, identifies components, chooses a segment profit measure, and applies aggregation criteria. The SEC staff has warned that the CODM report is only one data point: organizational structure, budgets, forecasts, compensation, information presented to the board, and the existence of accountable segment managers can also reveal the level at which the business is actually managed.[1][4]

A qualifying operating segment does not automatically become a separately disclosed reportable segment. Under Statement 131, a segment generally crosses a quantitative reporting threshold if its revenue, absolute profit or loss, or assets equal at least 10 percent of the corresponding combined segment amount. Additional segments must be reported until disclosed segments account for at least 75 percent of consolidated external revenue. Two or more operating segments may be aggregated only when aggregation is consistent with the standard's objective, the segments have similar economic characteristics, and they are similar in products and services, production processes, customers, distribution methods, and, where relevant, regulation.[1]

IFRS 8 uses a substantially similar management approach. It identifies operating components through CODM review, permits aggregation only when the core principle and specified similarity tests are met, applies comparable quantitative thresholds, and requires reconciliations from reportable-segment measures to entity totals. IFRS 8 also requires disclosure of management's aggregation judgments and the basis used to measure segment information. Although U.S. GAAP and IFRS differ in details, both frameworks seek to make internal operating structure visible without requiring a separate fully GAAP-compliant income statement for every component.[3]

The distinction between operating segments and reportable segments is where one class of shenanigan begins. A company can have several internally managed businesses but present fewer external segments if it concludes that some components may be aggregated or do not cross reporting thresholds. Permissible aggregation can simplify a genuinely homogeneous organization. Aggressive aggregation can average a low-margin or loss-making unit into a stronger business, suppress the dispersion in growth and returns, and delay questions about capital allocation, restructuring, or impairment.[4][5][8][9]

A second class of shenanigan concerns the reported measure of segment profit or loss. Because the management approach follows information supplied to the CODM, a segment measure can differ from consolidated GAAP operating income. Shared services, corporate overhead, stock compensation, restructuring, acquisition costs, depreciation, amortization, pension items, or litigation amounts can be allocated selectively, left in a corporate column, or excluded from the measure. Reconciliation preserves the consolidated total, but the placement of burdens determines which operation appears efficient. FASB issued ASU 2023-07 after investors said segment revenue and profit were available but segment expense information was often limited. The update requires disclosure of significant segment expenses regularly provided to the CODM and included in the reported segment profit measure, an amount and description for other segment items, additional interim information, and an explanation of how the CODM uses the measure.[2]

A third class concerns changes over time. A genuine reorganization, acquisition, divestiture, leadership change, or new product architecture can change how management reviews the company. Comparative segment information ordinarily must be recast when segment composition changes, subject to practicability rules, so users can compare the new structure across periods. Recasting improves comparability within the new presentation but can also overwrite the old analytical map. A company that changes segments repeatedly can make it difficult to follow the same economic business through time even when every individual recast complies with the standard.[1][2][10][11]

Academic evidence supplies a reason not to treat these judgments as neutral by default. Research comparing old and new segment regimes found that greater disaggregation revealed previously hidden information about diversification and changed market valuation and monitoring. Other studies found evidence consistent with managers having agency and proprietary motives to aggregate operations, including avoiding disclosure of weak abnormal profitability or commercially valuable strong profitability. These are population-level associations, not proof that a particular company's aggregation is improper, but they establish that external segment boundaries can reflect incentives as well as operating design.[7][8][9]

The domain boundary is therefore precise. Ordinary segment accounting explains how Topic 280 or IFRS 8 is applied. Valuation determines what disclosed businesses are worth. Segment-reporting forensics asks whether the public map faithfully reflects the units management actually runs, whether aggregation and allocation reduce transparency, whether changes are economically justified, and what corrected or disaggregated information would show. A suspicious pattern is a research trigger; a finding of manipulation requires converging documentary, numerical, organizational, and subsequent-event evidence.[4][5][6]

## Core Concepts

### The four layers of the segment-reporting map

A useful analysis separates four layers that published tables often collapse. The first layer is the economic activity: products, services, customer groups, geographies, facilities, acquired businesses, and projects that generate revenue and consume resources. The second layer is the internal operating component: the unit whose results are available and reviewed for decisions. The third layer is the reportable segment after thresholds and permissible aggregation are applied. The fourth layer is the consolidated entity after intersegment eliminations, corporate items, financing, taxes, and other reconciling amounts are included.[1][3]

A forensic review moves both downward and upward through those layers. Moving downward asks what businesses sit inside each reported segment and whether management, the board, or segment managers receive more granular results. Moving upward asks how the segment totals reconcile to consolidated revenue, operating income, assets, capital expenditure, and other material amounts. If the downward map is too coarse or the upward bridge contains large, unstable, or poorly described reconciling items, the published segment story requires more investigation.[1][2][4]

The consolidated statements remain the control total. Segment reallocation cannot create consolidated operating profit when only costs or intersegment prices move among components, and aggregation cannot change consolidated revenue when internal transactions are eliminated correctly. This is why segment shenanigans can be missed by conventional earnings-quality screens: total profit and cash may reconcile while the location, source, and sustainability of that profit are misrepresented. The affected claims are the quality of growth, performance of management units, economics of acquisitions, and allocation of capital rather than necessarily the entity-wide total.[5][6]

### Aggregation can erase economically important dispersion

Aggregation replaces several component results with a weighted average. If one business earns a 25 percent margin and another loses 10 percent, the combined margin can look ordinary while concealing both the high-return franchise and the capital-consuming operation. The average is mathematically accurate but analytically incomplete. The relevant accounting question is whether the businesses satisfy the economic and qualitative similarity criteria; the forensic question is whether separate information would materially change an informed user's understanding of future prospects.[1][4]

Long-term economic similarity should be tested with several measures rather than one favorable ratio. Revenue growth, gross margin, operating margin, return on identifiable assets, capital intensity, working-capital needs, cyclicality, customer concentration, pricing model, regulation, and exposure to technology or commodity risk can reveal different prospects. Similar products can have dissimilar economics when one is sold through subscriptions and another through projects. Similar margins in one year can result from a temporary price cycle. Conversely, different brands or locations can remain genuinely similar when their customers, processes, risk, and long-run returns converge.[1][3][4]

Quantitative thresholds are floors for separate reporting, not evidence that all sub-threshold units are economically homogeneous. Statement 131 permits information below the thresholds to be disclosed separately and restricts aggregation of smaller components to those sharing the required characteristics. The author's synthesis is that an analyst should preserve any component that is strategically important, rapidly deteriorating, acquisition-related, capital intensive, or central to management guidance even when it does not meet a formal threshold, because its effect on the investment thesis may exceed its current share of revenue or assets.[1]

### The CODM analysis must follow actual decisions, not one chosen document

The CODM function allocates resources and assesses segment performance. Identifying that function requires observing decisions: who approves budgets, reallocates capital, closes or expands facilities, evaluates forecasts, sets manager incentives, and responds to performance shortfalls. A title alone is not decisive, and neither is a single summary report. The SEC staff has explicitly cautioned against over-reliance on the CODM package and described efforts to remove information from a report to justify a desired segment conclusion.[4]

The internal evidence set should include recurring profit reports, budget-versus-actual reviews, forecasts, capital requests, board materials, executive compensation scorecards, acquisition integration reviews, and the reporting lines of business-unit leaders. External analysts rarely obtain those documents, but public traces exist. Earnings calls name the units management celebrates or repairs; investor presentations provide margins or key performance indicators below the financial-statement segments; job titles and organizational announcements identify accountable leaders; restructuring plans name affected businesses; and acquisition materials provide stand-alone targets. When public management communication is consistently more granular than the segment footnote, the difference deserves explanation.[4][5][11]

A company may legitimately centralize decisions and operate as one segment even if it sells several products. The author's assessment should therefore avoid inferring segment status from product variety alone. The stronger pattern is a component with discrete results, an accountable manager, recurring performance review, a budget or forecast, and resource decisions made on that basis. Evidence that the board or CODM uses those results outweighs a generic assertion that the business is managed only on a consolidated basis.[1][4][5]

### Segment profit is a management measure that needs a complete bridge

The segment profit measure can be gross profit, operating income, adjusted EBITDA, contribution margin, or another measure actually used by the CODM. ASU 2023-07 permits additional segment profit measures when the CODM uses more than one, but at least one disclosed measure must be the measure most consistent with the measurement principles applied to the corresponding consolidated amounts. The update also requires disclosure of the CODM's title and how the measure is used.[2]

The name of the measure is less informative than its components. Two companies can call a measure segment operating income while one includes depreciation, stock compensation, local marketing, and allocated technology costs and the other excludes them. Even within one company, a measure can change as management changes allocation policy or moves expenses into corporate. The analyst should reproduce the measure from segment revenue, significant expenses, other segment items, and the reconciliation. An unexplained residual should remain visible rather than being absorbed into a normalized margin.[2][3]

Corporate and unallocated columns require special attention. Some costs are genuinely central: public-company expenses, board costs, consolidated treasury, or enterprise-wide legal matters may not be controlled by a segment manager. Other costs are shared services consumed by the businesses, including information technology, human resources, procurement, property, distribution, insurance, and brand support. Leaving all shared costs outside segments can make every operating unit look more profitable than the entity that owns them. Allocating them through unstable or favorable drivers can move reported profit without changing consolidated economics.[2][6][11]

The author's synthesis is to keep three views. The reported view reproduces management's measure. A controllable view assigns direct and locally controlled costs but leaves genuinely central items separate. A full-cost view allocates recurring shared costs using transparent drivers such as headcount, transactions, space, shipments, revenue, or direct labor when those drivers reflect consumption. Differences among the views identify which claims depend on allocation rather than operations; they are analytical scenarios, not audited corrections.[1][2]

### Allocation changes can manufacture segment improvement

A cost-allocation policy determines which segment bears shared expenses. Changing the driver from headcount to revenue, excluding an acquired business during integration, moving a function into corporate, or reallocating supply-chain cost can improve one segment and weaken another with no consolidated effect. The signal strengthens when the beneficiary is the segment tied to guidance, executive compensation, a strategic narrative, or a promised acquisition return.[6]

Detection begins with a multi-year bridge by expense class. Record which expenses are direct, allocated, unallocated, eliminated, or excluded from the segment measure. Compare segment expense growth with operational drivers such as employees, facilities, shipments, customers, production, and capital employed. If a segment's allocated cost falls while its use of the shared function rises, request the rule and quantify the effect of the old rule. A transparent company can explain a change in the business and recast comparable periods; an aggressive presentation often offers only a high-level statement that management changed how it evaluates performance.[2][10][11]

Acquisitions create particular risk because integration costs, shared services, purchase-accounting effects, and synergies can be placed outside the acquired unit. A reported segment can meet its margin target only because central functions absorb new costs or because depreciation and amortization are excluded. The dedicated acquisition-accounting topic covers purchase price allocation and earnouts; segment forensics adds a different test: whether the acquired business's revenue, direct cost, allocated overhead, capital expenditure, working capital, and impairment indicators remain traceable after consolidation.[5]

### Intersegment transactions can transfer profit without creating consolidated value

Intersegment sales are eliminated in consolidation, but their prices determine where segment profit appears. A supplying segment can charge market, cost, cost plus a markup, or a management-set transfer price. A price increase moves profit toward the supplier; a rebate or reduction moves profit toward the buyer. When the total is eliminated correctly, consolidated profit does not change, yet management can make a favored segment meet a margin or growth target.[1][6]

The ADM enforcement order provides a direct example. The SEC found that executives used adjustments to intersegment transactions to improve the Nutrition segment's operating profit at the expense of other segments. The adjustments were targeted to dollar amounts needed to hit or mask shortfalls, were not on terms available to third parties, and contradicted ADM's disclosure that intersegment sales approximated market. Nutrition's annual 2021 operating profit growth would have been 17 percent rather than the reported 20 percent without the adjustment; 2022 annual growth would have been 5 percent rather than 7 percent.[6]

A forensic test reconstructs both legs. Compare internal prices with observable third-party sales or purchases, contemporaneous contracts, commodity benchmarks, freight and quality adjustments, volumes, and timing. Trace retroactive rebates, true-ups, transfer-pricing changes, management fees, royalties, and shared-service charges. A market-based policy must be tested against the actual terms, not repeated as proof. A change that lands near a reporting target is stronger evidence when it lacks a contemporaneous commercial rationale and reverses or migrates later.[6]

### Segment changes can be legitimate and still destroy trend visibility

A real reorganization can alter decision rights, reporting lines, budgets, product ownership, and resource allocation. Microsoft, for example, filed recast historical segment information after changing the composition of its segments to align with how management operated the business, and stated that the recast did not restate previously issued consolidated financial statements. This illustrates the normal distinction: segment presentation changes, while prior consolidated net income and cash remain the same.[10]

Forensic analysis should preserve both the old and new maps. Build a concordance showing which businesses moved, what measures changed, what costs were reallocated, and whether comparative periods were recast. Save pre-change filings because a later recast can make old segment labels difficult to reconstruct. Compare the announcement date with acquisitions, divestitures, executive departures, impairment reviews, compensation resets, and missed targets. Timing does not prove manipulation, but it identifies which alternative explanations require evidence.[1][2][10][11]

Repeated reorganization is especially important. A company may be adapting rationally to strategy, but each change can reset baselines, combine a weak unit with a strong one, or move costs to a different column. The author's proposed safeguard is a constant-structure series: preserve the last stable business definitions and estimate revenue, profit, assets, and capital spending on that basis for as long as disclosed data permit. Label approximations and do not invent unavailable allocations.[10][11]

### Geography, product, customer, and capital data can recover hidden economics

Entity-wide disclosures about products and services, geographic revenue and assets, and major customers can reveal concentration that reportable segments obscure. A reported global segment may contain a declining geography offset by a growing one; a product group can expose that headline growth came from one category; a major-customer disclosure can show that a segment's apparent diversification is limited. These disclosures are not substitute segment income statements, but they constrain explanations of reported growth.[1][3]

Capital data provide another constraint. Compare acquisitions, goodwill, identifiable assets, capital expenditures, headcount, facilities, impairments, and restructuring by business where available. A unit described as high growth should usually leave some trace in hiring, capacity, working capital, or investment unless its model is demonstrably asset light. A unit said to be shrinking should eventually show closures, lower assets, or reduced cost. The author's synthesis is that operational and balance-sheet evidence cannot prove a segment margin, but inconsistency across several independent measures makes the published allocation less credible.[5]

### Segment boundaries interact with impairment and restatement risk

Goodwill is assigned and tested at reporting-unit levels linked to the operating-segment structure under U.S. GAAP. The PowerSecure order found that failure to identify operating segments properly contributed to identifying too few reporting units, causing goodwill to be tested at a higher organizational level than required and potentially delaying recognition of impairment. The SEC did not state that a specific impairment loss had necessarily been missed; the finding was that the reporting-unit analysis was wrong and could have concealed one.[5]

This interaction makes segment changes relevant beyond disclosure. Aggregating a weak acquired business with a stronger operation can provide valuation headroom in impairment testing if the reporting-unit framework follows the broader unit. Separating or reorganizing components can expose impairment indicators. The forensic review should therefore read segment, goodwill, acquisition, restructuring, and discontinued-operation notes together rather than treating each as an isolated accounting topic.[5]

A later correction or restatement should be analyzed as a map of the prior failure. Identify which segments changed, which periods were recast, which allocations or transactions were corrected, and whether internal controls over segment identification were remediated. A change labeled only as a recast is not automatically an error; a correction following regulator inquiry, an internal investigation, or a material weakness carries different evidence. The restatement-analysis topic supplies the broader framework for grading those outcomes.[5][6]

## Evidence

### Standards define a high aggregation hurdle and a reconciliation discipline

Statement 131 provides a testable architecture. It defines operating segments from internal review, permits aggregation only when the objective, economic similarity, and specified qualitative characteristics align, imposes 10 percent revenue, profit-or-loss, and asset thresholds, and requires enough segments to cover at least 75 percent of external revenue. It also requires reconciliation of segment revenues, profit or loss, assets, and other disclosed amounts to consolidated financial statements. These provisions mean that neither a management label nor a single similar metric is sufficient evidence for aggregation.[1]

IFRS 8 applies a comparable structure and requires disclosure of the judgments used in aggregation, the basis of measurement, material reconciling items, and specified segment amounts. The official 2025 AcSB research presentation summarized prior user feedback that improper aggregation can obscure results or mask loss-making segments, while also recording preparer concerns about commercial sensitivity and the difficulty of allocating shared costs. Because that presentation reports preliminary outreach rather than a final standard-setting conclusion, it is evidence of stakeholder concerns, not evidence that any particular issuer violated IFRS 8.[3][11]

ASU 2023-07 documents a persistent information gap. FASB reported that investors considered segment information critical but often received limited detail about expenses. The update responds by requiring significant expenses included in the segment profit measure and regularly provided to the CODM, an explained residual for other segment items, broader interim disclosure, and information about the CODM's use of the measure. The amendment does not abolish the management approach or prescribe one allocation method, so the forensic need to analyze aggregation, shared costs, and reconciliations remains.[2]

### PowerSecure shows how internal granularity can be withheld from investors

The SEC's 2016 PowerSecure order is a direct case of under-disaggregation. The Commission found that PowerSecure presented continuing operations as one reportable segment from 2012 through the first quarter of 2014 when it should have identified multiple segments. PowerSecure had reasoned that discrete information did not exist below the combined level because some expenses were not allocated precisely below gross profit. The order states that gross profit was sufficient as a measure of profit or loss for identifying a component and that Topic 280 did not impose the allocation-precision requirement the company relied upon.[5]

The organizational evidence contradicted the one-segment conclusion. The chief executive received more disaggregated monthly financial results, met quarterly with business-unit leaders about operations, forecasts, and performance, and some leaders had unit budgets, forecasts, and incentive compensation. The board also received financial information below the consolidated level. When PowerSecure later reported an unexpected quarterly loss and attributed the gross-margin decline primarily to inefficiencies in one business unit, investors had not previously received the profitability metrics that management and the board possessed.[5]

The consequences extended beyond presentation. PowerSecure later identified more operating and reportable segments, disclosed a material weakness related to segment reporting, and revised prior-period disclosures. The SEC also found that the faulty segment analysis contributed to testing goodwill at too high a level, which could have delayed an impairment. PowerSecure lacked a segment-reporting policy and had limited documentation. The case demonstrates a complete evidence chain: internal reports showed managerial granularity, external reports suppressed it, a weak unit later surfaced in results, and the same boundary error affected another accounting estimate.[5]

### ADM shows how profit can be transferred among disclosed segments

The SEC's 2026 ADM order addresses a different mechanism. ADM disclosed three segments and reported that intersegment sales approximated market. The Commission found that when Nutrition risked missing operating-profit forecasts, executives pressured employees to identify adjustments that improved Nutrition at the expense of the other segments. The adjustments included retroactive rebates and price changes, targeted specific dollar amounts, and did not approximate market.[6]

The consolidated company did not need fictitious external revenue for the segment narrative to be false. Profit moved across segment boundaries through internal transaction terms. The order quantified the reporting effect: without the 2021 adjustment, Nutrition's annual operating-profit growth would have been 17 percent rather than 20 percent; without the 2022 adjustments, growth would have been 5 percent rather than 7 percent. The case shows why reconciling segment totals to consolidated profit is necessary but insufficient. A correct total does not validate the allocation among units.[6]

The ADM order also supplies a detection pattern. Management publicly emphasized a particular segment as a growth driver, set numerical expectations for its profit growth, and then used internal prices and rebates to close shortfalls. The author's synthesis is that segments tied to strategic re-rating narratives deserve heightened testing of intersegment prices, shared costs, acquisition effects, and excluded items. That is a prioritization rule, not a presumption of misconduct.[6]

### Research shows that disaggregation changes information and monitoring

Berger and Hann compared hand-collected restated Statement 131 data with the historical disclosures under the prior standard. They found that Statement 131 increased the number of reported segments and provided more disaggregated information. The newly revealed information changed analyst and market expectations, affected valuations, and was associated with behavior consistent with improved external monitoring. The study supports the proposition that hidden segment detail can matter even when users had partial clues before formal disclosure.[7]

Berger and Hann later examined managers' incentives to conceal segment profitability. Their 2007 study distinguishes proprietary cost, where revealing a highly profitable segment could attract competition, from agency cost, where revealing a poorly performing segment could expose unresolved managerial problems and invite monitoring. Using restated segment information around the change in reporting rules, they found results consistent with managers withholding relatively low abnormal-profit segments under the agency-cost hypothesis and mixed evidence for the proprietary-cost hypothesis. The result is a statistical association across a sample, not a test that classifies a particular company's intent.[8]

Bens, Berger, and Monahan used confidential U.S. Census plant-level data to compare economic groupings with published segment reports. They found that pseudo-segments were more likely to be aggregated when agency and proprietary costs of separate reporting were higher and when firm characteristics gave managers greater discretion under the segment rules. For multisegment firms, both agency and proprietary costs helped explain aggregation; the authors did not find the same agency-cost evidence among single-segment reporters. This qualification matters because the evidence does not justify treating every one-segment company as an attempt to hide weakness.[9]

Taken together, the standards, enforcement cases, and research support a bounded conclusion. Segment reporting is decision-useful precisely because disaggregation exposes differences in economics and managerial performance. The management approach can align disclosure with actual operations, but its judgments create opportunities to obscure those differences. A defensible finding therefore combines rule-based tests with internal-structure evidence, numerical reconstruction, incentive analysis, and subsequent outcomes; no ratio or reorganization date is enough by itself.[1][4][5][6][7][8][9]

## Implications

### Investors should build a constant-structure segment ledger

Begin with every available annual and interim segment table, not only the latest filing. Record for each period the segment names, products, geographies, revenue, profit measure, assets, capital expenditure, depreciation and amortization, significant expenses, intersegment sales, and reconciliation items that are disclosed. Preserve the exact definition of segment profit and the stated CODM. When a structure changes, add a concordance from the old segments to the new ones rather than replacing the old history.[1][2][10]

The ledger should contain three totals. First, sum the reported segments. Second, record eliminations, corporate or unallocated amounts, and every other reconciling item. Third, tie the result to the consolidated statement. The purpose is not merely arithmetic. Large or changing reconciling items identify where economic burdens sit outside the units management highlights. A segment that reports rising profit while the corporate column absorbs growing technology, compensation, integration, or restructuring costs may not have improved on a full-cost basis.[2]

For each segment, calculate growth, margins, return on disclosed assets, capital intensity, and the share of entity revenue, profit, and assets over time. Do not interpret those measures until the accounting perimeter is stable. Separate organic changes from acquisitions, disposals, foreign exchange, and reclassification. A margin increase created by moving expenses to corporate is not operating leverage; a decline caused by adding a low-margin acquired business is not necessarily deterioration in the legacy unit. Where disclosures do not permit separation, state the limit rather than inventing a precise adjustment.[2][5]

### Test aggregation with dispersion, not labels

List the businesses inside every reportable segment and compare their economics. Use revenue growth, gross and operating margins, asset turns, working capital, capital spending, customer class, distribution channel, regulation, and cyclicality over a full cycle where data exist. The objective is to test whether the components plausibly share long-term prospects, not to demand identical quarterly results. If one component has persistently different economics or is discussed as a distinct strategic unit, estimate the combined result with and without it.[1][4]

Public evidence can recover part of the hidden map. Use acquisition announcements for target revenue and margin, divestiture filings for disposed results, geographic and product revenue disclosures, major-customer information, restructuring charges, impairment notes, headcount, capacity, and management's own key performance indicators. Compare these traces with the reported segment. Several independent inconsistencies are more informative than one unusual ratio. The author's proposed output is an overlap table showing which facts support aggregation, which contradict it, and which remain unknown.[1][3][5]

The worst analytical error is to infer misconduct from diversity alone. A platform can centralize pricing, technology, distribution, and capital allocation across several products and operate as one economic unit. Conversely, identical products can contain distinct segments when they serve different customers, geographies, or regulatory systems and management reviews them separately. The evidence must follow both management process and economic characteristics.[1][4]

### Reconstruct shared-cost and intersegment-price sensitivity

For shared costs, classify each expense as direct, controllable allocated, recurring central, or event-specific. Reproduce management's segment measure first. Then prepare a sensitivity view using stable drivers that approximate consumption. Show the effect on each segment, the corporate column, and consolidated profit. The sensitivity is most useful when the conclusion changes: a high-margin segment that becomes ordinary after recurring shared cost is assigned has a different economic quality than one that remains exceptional under several reasonable allocations.[2]

For intersegment transactions, compare the disclosed policy with actual terms. Match prices, rebates, royalties, fees, freight, quality, credit, and timing against third-party transactions or market benchmarks where possible. Examine retroactive changes and quarter-end true-ups. Calculate segment profit before the disputed transfer while keeping the elimination and consolidated result unchanged. The ADM case demonstrates that a small-looking internal adjustment can materially change a promoted segment's growth rate without changing the entity total.[6]

Do not combine allocation and transfer-price adjustments into one unexplained haircut. Each mechanism has a different evidentiary basis and reversal pattern. A corporate cost allocation affects expense placement; an intersegment rebate affects the profits of both counterparties; an acquisition exclusion can affect segment expense and assets; a non-GAAP exclusion changes the reported measure. Keeping the bridges separate makes the analysis reversible and prevents the forensic model from becoming another opaque management metric.[2][6]

### Treat reorganization as a comparability event

When segments change, capture the filing date, effective date, stated operational rationale, new reporting lines, CODM information, and recast periods. Compare the change with executive appointments, acquisitions, divestitures, strategic reviews, compensation plans, impairment tests, and missed targets. A genuine reorganization should leave corroborating organizational evidence and a coherent explanation of resource allocation. A disclosure-only change with no corresponding change in management process deserves more skepticism.[4][10][11]

Retain both old and recast data. The recast view supports comparison under the current structure; the old view preserves what management reported contemporaneously. Differences between them can reveal which costs, businesses, and performance trends moved. If the company cannot recast an item, identify the missing series explicitly. Long-term analysis should not splice incompatible margins without a break marker.[1][2]

A repeated pattern can be more informative than one change. Watch for a weak unit disappearing into a larger segment, a strategic acquisition moving into corporate or all other, an expense category changing at the same time as a margin target, or a later impairment in a business whose segment performance was never separately visible. These facts support a hypothesis for investigation, not an allegation. Escalation requires documentary inconsistency, unsupported accounting, or evidence that the change did not reflect actual management reporting.[5][6]

### Boards and audit committees should govern the segment map as a control system

A board should require a written segment-reporting policy that identifies the CODM function, inventories recurring internal reports, maps accountable segment managers, documents aggregation criteria, defines segment measures, and states allocation and intersegment-pricing policies. The policy should be updated when reporting lines, systems, compensation, acquisitions, or strategy change. PowerSecure's lack of a segment-reporting policy and limited documentation show why informal conclusions can become an internal-control failure.[5]

The audit committee should receive a change log. It should show additions or removals from CODM materials, changes in business-unit budgets, manager incentives, allocation drivers, transfer prices, reportable thresholds, and impairment reporting units. Removing a metric from a management package cannot by itself erase the operating segment if other evidence shows that the component remains reviewed and managed. Controls should therefore cover the full evidence set rather than one designated report.[4][5]

Allocation governance should be symmetric. A rule adopted when it hurts a favored segment should not be changed only when it begins to help that segment. Intersegment prices should have contemporaneous support and independent approval where performance incentives differ across units. Retroactive rebates and target-sized true-ups require escalation. Board review should focus on who benefits, what third-party evidence exists, and whether the consolidated elimination hides a misleading transfer among reported units.[6]

### Auditors and regulators should connect disclosure to operations and impairment

Audit procedures should test completeness of the operating-segment population, not only the accuracy of the published table. Relevant evidence includes organizational charts, management and board reports, budgets, forecasts, compensation, capital approvals, business-unit meetings, and system-generated results. The identified CODM, segment managers, and discrete measures should agree with how decisions are actually made. Contradictory evidence should be resolved rather than excluded from the analysis.[4][5]

The audit should also connect segment conclusions to goodwill reporting units, asset impairment, revenue disaggregation, entity-wide product and geographic information, and non-GAAP measures. A boundary used to support one segment conclusion but ignored in impairment or management communication is inconsistent. PowerSecure demonstrates that a segment-identification error can propagate into goodwill testing; ADM demonstrates that correct segment identification does not prevent manipulation of the profit assigned to each segment.[5][6]

Regulators can compare filing disclosures with earnings calls, investor presentations, websites, compensation plans, and prior organizational statements. They can also use XBRL and longitudinal analysis to identify shrinking segment counts, growing corporate reconciling items, frequent recasts, or promoted segments whose profit rises through intersegment adjustments. These screens prioritize review but cannot establish intent without records and context.[4][5][6]

### Management can make legitimate judgment testable

Transparent reporting explains why businesses are aggregated, which economic indicators support similarity, what the CODM reviews, how segment profit is constructed, which costs remain unallocated, how shared costs are assigned, and how intersegment transactions are priced. When those policies change, management can quantify the effect and recast comparable periods. Disclosure of uncertainty is stronger than a conclusory statement that the segments are similar or that internal prices approximate market.[1][2][3][6]

Management should also separate operating change from reporting change. If the organization genuinely moved decision rights, budgets, and leaders, say so and identify the effective date. If only the presentation changed, explain why that better reflects existing operations. Preserve historical schedules for investors when formal financial statements cannot supply a long trend. A segment note that lets users reproduce the bridge reduces suspicion even when management uses a non-GAAP internal measure.[2][10][11]

The final forensic conclusion should be graded. "Transparent" means the internal structure, aggregation tests, measures, allocations, transfers, recasts, and reconciliations are coherent. "Presentation risk" means the accounting can be reproduced but a headline segment measure omits recurring burdens. "Aggressive" means judgments repeatedly favor the promoted narrative while remaining within an arguable range. "Likely error" means the operating-segment, aggregation, allocation, or reconciliation analysis does not meet the reporting framework. "Evidence consistent with manipulation" requires converging facts such as target-sized unsupported transfers, concealed internal granularity, contradictory management records, or a segment change that does not reflect actual decision-making.[4][5][6]

Only after the reporting map is corrected should valuation begin. Segment disclosures provide a starting map, not stand-alone companies: corporate costs, financing, taxes, shared assets, intersegment dependencies, and consolidation effects still require reconciliation. The forensic contribution is narrower and prior in sequence -- determine which businesses produced the consolidated result, which burdens belong to them, and whether the published segmentation preserves or conceals that economic truth.[1][2]
