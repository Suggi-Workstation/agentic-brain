# Supplier Finance Can Turn Ordinary Trade Payables Into Hidden Debt

Supplier finance can give suppliers earlier access to cash without changing a buyer's original payment date, but it can also extend that date and make bank funding look like ordinary trade credit. The forensic question is therefore not whether a program is called reverse factoring, but whether its terms, presentation, cash-flow effects, and withdrawal risk make the buyer's obligation economically closer to financing than to a normal payable.[1][2][3]

## Background

Trade credit begins with a commercial transaction: a supplier delivers goods or services, recognizes a receivable, and permits the buyer to pay after an agreed period. Supplier finance adds a third party after the buyer has approved the invoice. The financier may pay the supplier before the contractual due date, usually for a discount, while the buyer later settles the confirmed amount with the financier. The arrangement is commonly called supplier finance, supply chain finance, approved-payables finance, payables finance, or reverse factoring.[1][2][3][7]

The sequence can create a genuine financing benefit for the supplier. The financier relies substantially on the approved obligation and the buyer's credit quality rather than only on the supplier's standalone credit. A smaller supplier can therefore receive cash earlier and sometimes at a lower rate than it could obtain independently. The buyer retains cash until the agreed settlement date, while the financier earns a discount or fee. The Australian Parliamentary Joint Committee described the practical result as allowing the purchaser to use the full payment term while the supplier collects a discounted receivable earlier.[7]

This structure differs from conventional factoring in who organizes the program and whose credit supports the financing. In ordinary receivables factoring, a supplier sells or pledges receivables to obtain cash and the factor evaluates the supplier, the receivables, and recourse terms. In reverse factoring, the buyer usually initiates or facilitates the program, confirms approved invoices, and gives the financier a payment undertaking. That confirmation can reduce performance and dispute risk after approval, making the buyer's ability to pay the central credit exposure.[3][7]

The economic problem appears when early payment for the supplier is paired with materially later payment by the buyer. A program can then do two things at once: accelerate the supplier's receipt and extend the buyer's financing period. The first function supports the supply chain; the second supplies working capital to the buyer. Van der Vliet, Reindorp, and Fransoo modeled this interaction and found that longer payment terms impose nonlinear financing costs on suppliers, with the tolerable extension depending on demand uncertainty and the supplier's cost structure.[8] The label "supplier support" therefore does not establish that the economic benefit is shared fairly.

Financial statements can obscure this financing function because an increase in trade payables raises operating cash flow under the indirect method. If a buyer lengthens payment terms or moves invoices into a financed program while leaving the obligations within accounts payable, cash remains in the business longer and the working-capital reconciliation reports an operating inflow. If later settlement is presented as an operating outflow, the classification may remain symmetrical. If the trade payable is instead derecognized without cash and replaced by a finance payable, the eventual payment may appear in financing cash flows even though the earlier payable increase benefited operating cash flow.[2][3]

The disclosure gap became visible in corporate failures. Carillion used an early-payment facility alongside standard supplier terms reported by the UK Parliament as reaching 120 days. At collapse, the company had access to as much as GBP 500 million under the facility and was drawing about GBP 350 million.[5] S&P Global estimated that reverse-factoring amounts approximated GBP 500 million at the end of 2016 and concluded that limited transparency obscured weak operating cash flow and a substantial part of debt.[6] These amounts were estimates and facility data from the inquiry, not proof that every supplier-finance obligation is debt. They show why size, terms, and accounting presentation must be tested together.

Standard setters responded primarily with disclosure requirements. FASB issued ASU 2022-04 in September 2022 to require information about the nature, activity, changes, and potential magnitude of buyer-side supplier-finance programs. Its principal requirements became effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022, with the annual rollforward effective one year later.[1] The IASB amended IAS 7 and IFRS 7 in May 2023, with mandatory application for annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2024. The IFRS package requires information designed to expose effects on liabilities, cash flows, payment terms, and liquidity risk.[2]

Neither disclosure regime makes every program debt by definition. The IFRS Interpretations Committee concluded that existing standards provide a basis for judging balance-sheet presentation, derecognition, cash-flow classification, and note disclosure from the facts of each arrangement.[3] FASB's ASU adds disclosures but does not itself prescribe a universal reclassification. The forensic task remains one of economic substance: identify who is owed, when payment is due, which terms changed, what security or guarantees exist, how cash movements were classified, and how the company would fund itself if the program disappeared.[1][2][3]

## Core Concepts

### The three-party mechanism must be reconstructed invoice by invoice

A basic supplier-finance transaction has four stages. First, the supplier performs and invoices the buyer. Second, the buyer validates the invoice and communicates an approved obligation to the program administrator or financier. Third, the supplier decides whether to receive early payment, normally at a discount. Fourth, the buyer pays the financier according to the program terms. A financial institution may pay on the original invoice date or earlier, and the buyer may pay on the original date or a later one.[1][2][3][7]

The approved invoice is important because it separates performance risk from payment risk. Before approval, the buyer may dispute quantity, quality, delivery, or price. After an irrevocable approval, the financier expects payment from the buyer and can price the advance against that obligation. The author's synthesis is that an analyst should map the legal state of the invoice at each stage: disputed or approved, owed to the supplier or financier, subject to offset or not, and payable on the original or an amended date.[1][3][7]

Supplier participation can be voluntary even when the buyer controls the program's architecture. Voluntary early payment is evidence that the supplier chooses whether to accelerate cash, but it does not answer whether the buyer extended contractual terms before offering that choice. The economically relevant comparison is between program payment terms and comparable non-program trade terms in the same business, jurisdiction, and period. That comparison is explicit in the IASB's disclosure design because a long program term can be hidden if only the supplier's early receipt is described.[2]

### Trade payable or financing liability is a substance question

A normal trade payable arises from purchasing goods or services in the operating cycle and usually remains linked to customary commercial terms. A financing liability arises when capital is supplied to the buyer and repayment terms reflect a credit relationship. Supplier finance can occupy either position or fall between them. The IFRS Interpretations Committee identified facts such as additional security and terms substantially different from ordinary trade payables as relevant to deciding whether separate presentation is needed.[3]

Counterparty substitution alone is not conclusive. If the financier pays a supplier early but the buyer's amount, due date, rights, and obligations remain unchanged, the buyer may still have an obligation with the nature and function of a trade payable. The FRC illustrated this pattern with AstraZeneca's 2018 disclosure: suppliers could elect discounted early payment, Greensill Capital paid them, and AstraZeneca settled the original invoice amount at the original maturity date. AstraZeneca disclosed a potential early-payment balance of $166 million and retained the amount within trade payables.[4]

The conclusion changes as financing attributes accumulate. Relevant indicators include payment terms extended beyond customary trade practice, a new obligation owed directly to a bank, loss of normal rights of offset, buyer-paid financing fees, pledged assets, guarantees, interest-like pricing, scheduled amortization, and a facility that substitutes for a revolving credit line. S&P stated that its credit analysis would treat amounts as debt when reverse factoring materially extended payable days beyond normal company terms.[6] No single indicator supplies a universal accounting threshold, so the analysis should state both the facts and the judgment.

A false dichotomy should be avoided. An obligation can remain within trade payables under the reporting framework yet still be treated as debt-like in valuation or credit analysis. Conversely, a separately presented finance payable is not necessarily evidence of deception; transparent reclassification may improve faithful presentation. The author's assessment is that accounting classification, covenant classification, rating-agency treatment, and valuation treatment should be reconciled but not assumed to be identical.[1][2][3][6]

### Payment-term extension creates a one-time working-capital benefit

When a buyer extends payment from 60 days to 120 days, it retains cash for an additional 60 days on the affected purchases. During the transition, payables rise and reported operating cash flow improves. After the longer term is fully embedded, the same level of purchases no longer creates another transition benefit; further cash improvement requires purchase growth, another term extension, or a larger share of invoices inside the program. This is the author's synthesis of the cash-flow mechanics and the payment-term evidence.[2][4][8]

The stock of program obligations and the current-period cash benefit are different quantities. The ending obligation shows financing outstanding at one date. The increase in obligations can approximate new financing only after adjusting for purchases, seasonality, acquisitions, foreign exchange, noncash transfers, and payment timing. The total confirmed and paid during the year shows throughput, not incremental funding. FASB therefore requires an annual rollforward of beginning obligations, invoices confirmed, confirmed invoices paid, and ending obligations.[1]

Days payable outstanding can identify a broad change but cannot isolate a program by itself. The ratio can rise because purchase mix, seasonality, acquisitions, vendor bargaining, or payment failures changed. A program-specific analysis needs disclosed balances and payment terms, comparable non-program terms, quarterly or interim balances, and evidence about when suppliers and financiers were paid. Gelsomino and co-authors found evidence consistent with some firms reducing trade payables in the fourth quarter where interim audits were not mandatory, demonstrating why one year-end balance may not represent peak or average use.[9]

### Cash-flow classification can preserve or conceal the financing effect

Under the indirect method, the creation of a trade payable increases operating cash flow relative to immediate payment. When the payable is settled through operating cash flows, the creation and reversal stay in the same category across periods. A longer payment term still shifts cash between periods, but it does not automatically create a classification mismatch.[2][3]

A different pattern arises when a trade payable is derecognized and a finance payable is recognized without cash. The IASB illustrates the sequence as an operating working-capital inflow when the trade payable is created, a noncash transfer to finance payables, and a financing outflow when the finance payable is paid. Unless the noncash transfer is disclosed and understood, cumulative operating cash flow can appear higher because the reversal leaves the operating section.[2] IAS 7 requires disclosure of changes in liabilities arising from financing activities, including noncash changes.[3]

Actual cash movements must not be invented to force a preferred answer. The IFRS Interpretations Committee stated that when factoring an invoice causes cash to flow through the buyer, those cash flows must appear in the statement; when the financing transaction is noncash, it is excluded from the statement and disclosed elsewhere.[3] The author's synthesis is that analysts should reconcile bank movements, liability rollforwards, and noncash transfers before reclassifying any reported cash flow.

### Liquidity risk can rise while supplier liquidity improves

A supplier may receive cash sooner and face less collection risk after selling an approved invoice. At the same time, the buyer may become dependent on one or a few finance providers and on payment terms longer than suppliers would grant independently. The IASB identifies this concentration as a liquidity risk because withdrawal during financial stress can force the buyer to replace both maturing program balances and the working-capital gap created by shorter future terms.[2]

The risk is nonlinear at withdrawal. Continuing a facility requires funding only the normal settlements as they mature. Losing it can require the buyer to repay the financier, return to shorter supplier terms, and reassure suppliers that may demand cash on delivery. The FRC's model disclosure therefore asks companies to explain the viability effect if a reverse-factoring scheme is withdrawn and cannot be replaced.[4] Carillion's financier withdrew its discounted early-payment facility shortly before liquidation, illustrating that access can contract when the buyer is least able to replace it.[5]

Supplier concentration and financier concentration should both be mapped. A program may protect participating suppliers from a buyer's delayed payment because they have already received cash, while nonparticipating suppliers remain unsecured. The Australian parliamentary review noted this contrast in the Carillion case.[7] The author's assessment is that supplier benefit does not eliminate buyer rollover risk; the two can coexist.

### FASB and IASB disclosure packages answer different analytical questions

FASB ASU 2022-04 applies to buyers using supplier-finance programs for purchases of goods and services. Annual disclosures include key program terms, payment timing, the basis for determining terms, pledged assets or guarantees, the outstanding confirmed obligation, the balance-sheet line containing it, and a rollforward. The outstanding amount is also required at interim dates. The ASU's purpose is transparency; it does not make one balance-sheet or cash-flow classification mandatory for every program.[1]

The IASB amendments require terms and conditions; the carrying amount of liabilities in the program and their balance-sheet location; the subset for which suppliers have already received payment from finance providers; ranges of due dates for program liabilities and comparable non-program payables; noncash changes; and liquidity-risk information. The due-date comparison and supplier-paid subset expose facts that the FASB package does not require in the same form.[1][2]

The two systems therefore support different reconstructions. A U.S. GAAP rollforward helps trace program activity over the year, but it may not reveal how much the financier has already paid suppliers or how program terms compare with ordinary terms. IFRS supplies those comparisons but does not prescribe a single analyst adjustment because investors use different methods. The author's synthesis is that a cross-company model should build a common data set rather than equate formal compliance with economic comparability.[1][2]

### Legitimate working-capital management and manipulation are separated by evidence

Supplier finance is not inherently a shenanigan. A transparent program can reduce suppliers' financing costs, preserve original buyer terms, disclose balances and risks, and diversify funding. The FRC's AstraZeneca example and 3M's 2025 filing show disclosed programs in which the buyer stated that settlement occurred at original maturity dates. 3M reported approximately $0.3 billion outstanding at both year-ends and about $1.5 billion of invoices confirmed and paid during each year, with the obligations included in accounts payable.[4][10]

Risk rises when the program is described as supplier support while the buyer quietly lengthens terms, when liabilities are aggregated into payables without quantification, when financing outflows settle obligations whose creation benefited operating cash flow, or when year-end balances are temporarily reduced. Gelsomino and co-authors found that only 13 percent of reverse-factoring firms in their global sample disclosed the programs, often with little detail; they also reported year-end payable reductions consistent with avoiding disclosure or reclassification thresholds in settings without mandatory interim audits.[9]

The correct conclusion follows an evidence ladder. Program documents and disclosures establish terms; liability and cash-flow rollforwards establish accounting effects; supplier and financier data establish actual payment timing; covenant and rating materials establish external treatment; and subsequent facility changes test dependence. Suspicion based only on a high payable balance is not proof. The author's synthesis is that manipulation becomes supportable only when reported labels and cash-flow claims conflict with documented economic substance or omit material facts.[1][2][3][6][9]

## Evidence

### Van der Vliet, Reindorp, and Fransoo: supplier benefit depends on more than the discount rate

Van der Vliet, Reindorp, and Fransoo studied the price of reverse factoring with a periodic-review base-stock model that incorporated alternative financing sources. They used simulation optimization to ask how far a buyer could extend payment terms while leaving the supplier better off. Their model found that the supplier's financing cost increased nonlinearly as payment terms lengthened and that a tolerable extension depended on demand uncertainty and the supplier's cost structure.[8]

The study is analytical rather than a population estimate of actual misconduct. Its contribution is to disprove a simple inference that a lower factoring rate automatically compensates a supplier for any term extension. Inventory needs, uncertainty, and financing alternatives can make a nominally cheap program costly. For forensic analysis, the method supports separating the supplier's early-payment discount from the buyer's additional days of credit.[8]

### Gelsomino and co-authors: disclosure was rare and reporting behavior responded to oversight

Gelsomino, Paananen, Papadopoulos, and Elliot examined a global sample of 673 firms from 2011 through 2021. They identified reverse-factoring adopters, tested associations between disclosure and external pressures, and examined whether firms managed trade-payable balances in ways that could avoid disclosure or debt reclassification. The study was accepted in 2025 and published in Accounting Forum in 2026.[9]

The authors found that firms with greater financing needs were more likely to adopt programs. Firms in stronger enforcement environments and firms with more analyst following or institutional ownership disclosed more often, especially when programs were material. Even so, only 13 percent of identified program users provided any disclosure, and many disclosures contained little detail. In jurisdictions without mandatory interim audits, some firms significantly reduced fourth-quarter payables in a pattern consistent with staying below auditor thresholds; that pattern was absent where interim audits were mandatory.[9]

The design is observational, so it does not prove intent for every firm with a year-end reduction. It does demonstrate that formal year-end balances and voluntary disclosure can be shaped by enforcement and audit architecture. The forensic implication is to use interim balances, rollforwards, and payment-term evidence where available rather than treating one closing balance as a complete measure.[9]

### Carillion: facility scale, stretched terms, and classification obscured leverage

The UK Parliament's joint inquiry used company documents, testimony, correspondence, and public reporting to reconstruct Carillion's business model and collapse. It reported standard supplier terms of 120 days, access to as much as GBP 500 million under the early-payment facility at collapse, and drawings of about GBP 350 million. The inquiry concluded that the facility's treatment helped hide weak cash generation because borrowing-like support entered the working-capital presentation rather than being clearly shown as debt.[5]

S&P Global provided a separate credit-analysis perspective. Based on the parliamentary record and the increase in other creditors, it estimated approximately GBP 500 million of reverse factoring at the end of 2016. S&P stated that the lack of transparency obscured operating cash flow and debt and that its criteria would treat amounts as debt when payable days materially exceeded normal terms. It also cautioned that reverse factoring was not itself a cause of failure; opacity can conceal existing balance-sheet and cash-flow weakness.[6]

The case supports three inferences, each bounded by the evidence. Long terms can make a program financing-like; unquantified balances can frustrate leverage analysis; and withdrawal can expose a cash gap. It does not support treating every supplier-finance program as fraudulent or every accounts-payable classification as wrong.[5][6]

### FRC and 3M examples show what decision-useful disclosure can reveal

The FRC's 2019 report reproduced an AstraZeneca disclosure describing program mechanics, the financier, supplier choice, discount basis, original invoice maturity, supplier participation, and the amount within trade payables. The company reported 2,548 enrolled suppliers and a $166 million potential early-payment balance at December 2018. Because the stated buyer due date remained the original date, the disclosure let users distinguish supplier acceleration from buyer term extension.[4]

3M's 2025 annual filing applied the newer U.S. disclosure regime. It stated that confirmed invoices were generally due within 90 days of invoice date, that either 3M or the banks could terminate with notice, that suppliers could request early payment, that approximately $0.3 billion was outstanding at each of two year-ends, and that about $1.5 billion was confirmed and paid during each year. It also identified accounts payable as the balance-sheet location.[10]

These are disclosure examples, not external validations that every term was customary or every obligation was correctly classified. Their evidentiary value is narrower: named mechanics, dates, balances, activity, termination rights, and statement location make the analyst's judgment testable. Omission of those facts forces users to infer financing from aggregate payables.[4][10]

### Post-mandate working papers find capital-market and operating responses

Chuk, Lourie, and Yoo used the introduction of ASU 2022-04 to study real effects of mandatory supplier-finance disclosure. Their 2026 working paper reports that disclosed program users shortened payment periods relative to non-users after the mandate, adjusted other financing policies, and showed improved supplier receivable and investment outcomes. It also reports positive investor reactions and upward analyst cash-flow forecast revisions around initial disclosures.[11]

Berger, Li, and Liu used a difference-in-differences design to examine bond-market effects. Their 2025 working paper reports higher bond yields for revealed program firms after mandatory disclosure, with stronger effects among firms with higher default risk. The authors interpret the result as evidence that obligations to financial firms can reveal structural subordination risk for unsecured bondholders; they also report reduced trade-credit reliance after disclosure.[12]

Both sources are working papers rather than final peer-reviewed publications, so their findings should be treated as provisional. Together with the peer-reviewed disclosure study, they suggest that transparency can change payment behavior, financing choices, analyst expectations, and the price of credit rather than merely adding boilerplate to notes.[9][11][12]

## Implications

### Investors should separate operating payables from program funding

Begin with a payable map rather than a single accounts-payable number. Separate ordinary suppliers, approved invoices inside each program, amounts already paid to suppliers by financiers, finance payables created by noncash transfer, and overdue obligations. Record program and comparable non-program due-date ranges. The IASB disclosures provide much of this structure; FASB disclosures provide program terms, outstanding balances, and annual activity.[1][2]

Next, distinguish stock from flow. The ending program balance is the amount outstanding, while the current-period working-capital benefit depends on how the balance and payment terms changed. Confirmed invoices during the year measure activity, not financing retained. A flat ending balance can support substantial annual throughput, as 3M's disclosure illustrates, while a growing balance can reflect purchases, seasonality, acquisitions, or longer terms rather than one cause alone.[1][10]

Do not remove the entire ending balance from operating cash flow. Instead, reconstruct the portion of current-period cash retention attributable to a financing-like term extension or classification change. If evidence is incomplete, show a range and state the assumption. The author's assessment is that an adjustment hidden inside a normalized cash-flow total repeats the transparency problem it is meant to correct.[1][2][3]

### Value investors should adjust both debt and owner earnings without double counting

A program judged debt-like belongs in the enterprise-to-equity bridge if the operating valuation did not already capture its repayment. Add the relevant obligation to debt-like claims, test whether cash is available to offset it, and recalculate leverage, interest coverage, and common-equity value. This treatment connects supplier-finance forensics with claim-by-claim enterprise-value reconciliation.[6]

The cash-flow adjustment is separate. If a current-period increase in program funding inflated operating cash flow, subtract the incremental financing benefit from normalized operating cash or owner earnings. If future cash-flow forecasts already assume shorter normalized payment terms and repay the balance, subtracting the full obligation again from equity value can double count the burden. The author's synthesis is to show one schedule for normalized operating cash and another for debt-like claims, with an explicit cross-check between them.[1][2][6]

Margin of safety should reflect withdrawal risk. A company may need to repay current program balances, fund new purchases on shorter terms, and support suppliers simultaneously. Scenario analysis should therefore include an orderly runoff, partial reduction, and abrupt withdrawal. The FRC and IASB both identify loss of program access as a liquidity issue rather than merely an accounting issue.[2][4]

### Credit analysts and lenders should treat concentration as a refinancing exposure

Map each program by financier, committed or uncommitted status, termination notice, collateral, guarantees, currency, maturity, and supplier population. A broad group of suppliers may appear diversified even though the buyer's actual payment obligation has concentrated in one bank. IFRS 7 liquidity disclosures were amended specifically to make this risk more visible.[2]

Covenants should define whether supplier-finance obligations count as debt and whether related settlements reduce operating or financing cash flow. A covenant based on reported net debt can omit a program held in payables, while a cash-conversion covenant can benefit from term extensions. S&P's Carillion analysis demonstrates why rating models may recast the same balance even when the reported label remains unchanged.[6]

Stress testing should focus on cash timing rather than only annual totals. Estimate program balances at peak seasonal use, the replacement cost of shorter supplier terms, and the availability of bank lines after a credit deterioration. The worst case is not simply that the financier declines new invoices; it is simultaneous runoff of existing obligations and contraction of supplier credit. This scenario is the author's synthesis of the withdrawal and concentration risks documented by the IASB, FRC, and parliamentary inquiries.[2][4][5][7]

### Suppliers should evaluate the total bargain, not only the quoted discount

A low early-payment rate can be valuable when the buyer's credit is stronger than the supplier's and original payment terms remain unchanged. It can be less valuable when access requires accepting much later contractual terms, platform costs, loss of offset rights, or dependence on a single financier. The van der Vliet study shows why financing-rate savings and term extensions must be evaluated jointly.[8]

Suppliers should compare cash receipt under the program with receipt under the prior trade term, not with the buyer's newly extended due date alone. They should also test what happens if the financier withdraws, if the buyer delays approval, or if an invoice is disputed before confirmation. The Australian parliamentary review notes that fees vary with credit risk, term length, provider margin, and insurance cost, and that buyer market power can affect small suppliers' choices.[7]

The author's proposed supplier metric is net acceleration value: financing cost saved through earlier receipt minus the discount, fees, and economic cost of any term extension imposed as part of joining. This is a synthesis, not an accounting measure, and should be calculated from the supplier's own funding alternatives and operating cycle.[7][8]

### Boards and audit committees should control program design and reporting together

A board should approve why a program exists, how term changes are negotiated, which suppliers are eligible, who bears fees, and what funding substitutes it replaces. Treasury, procurement, accounts payable, and financial reporting should use one program inventory. A control that captures only balances supplied by treasury can miss procurement-side term extensions or platforms administered outside ordinary debt systems.[1][2]

Audit committees should receive quarterly balances, annual rollforwards, comparable payment terms, supplier-paid subsets, noncash transfers, and concentration by financier. They should ask whether balances fall unusually at year-end and whether management compensation uses operating cash flow, free cash flow, or net debt measures affected by the program. The Gelsomino study makes interim evidence especially relevant where only year-end statements are audited.[9]

Management should explain both benefits and risks. Calling a program supplier support without quantifying buyer liquidity effects is incomplete; calling every program borrowing ignores arrangements that preserve original trade terms. The author's assessment is that neutral disclosure states mechanics first and management's conclusion second.[1][2][4]

### Standard setters and regulators should test whether disclosure remains representative

FASB and IASB requirements materially increase available data, but they do not eliminate judgment about scope or classification. FASB does not require the supplier-paid subset and comparable due-date ranges in the same way as IFRS. IFRS provides those data but permits company-specific presentation and leaves investors to calculate effects under their own methodologies.[1][2]

Enforcement should therefore test completeness across program labels. The same economics may be described as approved payables, early payment, structured payables, vendor finance, or supply-chain finance. Program scope should be reconciled with bank confirmations, platform data, supplier communications, and financing-liability rollforwards rather than with note headings alone.[1][3][7]

Interim transparency also matters. A year-end balance can be unrepresentative when programs are seasonal or deliberately reduced before audit. The peer-reviewed evidence on fourth-quarter payable reductions does not prove universal window dressing, but it supports regulator attention to average, peak, and interim use when material.[9]

## Practical Forensic Reconciliation

### Step 1: Build the program inventory

Collect every note, accounting policy, cash-flow disclosure, debt schedule, liquidity-risk statement, covenant definition, and management discussion that refers to supplier finance or a synonym. For each program, record the buyer, administrator, finance providers, supplier eligibility, currencies, fees, security, guarantees, termination rights, original trade term, program term, and balance-sheet line. Reconcile management's list to bank confirmations and accounts-payable subledgers where those records are available.[1][2][3]

### Step 2: Reconcile obligations from beginning to end

Use the disclosed rollforward where available:

`Beginning confirmed obligations + invoices confirmed - obligations paid +/- other changes = ending confirmed obligations.`

Separate cash changes from noncash reclassifications, acquisitions, disposals, and foreign exchange. Under IFRS, also identify the subset already paid to suppliers and the carrying amount moved between trade and finance payables. A failure to reconcile can signal missing programs, aggregation of dissimilar arrangements, or an unexplained classification change.[1][2]

### Step 3: Compare terms and locate the financing increment

Compare program due dates with comparable non-program terms and with the company's own historical terms. Estimate the amount of cash retained because terms were extended beyond the normal operating cycle. Adjust for purchase growth and seasonality before treating a payable increase as financing. Document whether the result is an exact contractual calculation, a disclosed amount, or an analyst estimate.[2][6][8]

### Step 4: Reclassify cash flow only when the transaction evidence supports it

If a payable remained a trade obligation and settlement was an operating cash outflow, the analyst may normalize only the temporary term-extension benefit. If a trade payable was transferred without cash to a finance payable and later paid in financing, restore comparability by showing the noncash transfer and considering whether the eventual settlement should be included in normalized operating cash flow. Preserve the reported statement and present the analyst bridge separately.[2][3]

### Step 5: Adjust leverage and stress liquidity

Add the balance judged debt-like to gross debt or show it as a separate senior claim. Recalculate net debt, leverage, interest coverage, covenant headroom, and enterprise-to-equity value. Then model program runoff and withdrawal, including the replacement of extended terms and any supplier support needed to preserve operations.[2][4][6]

The following is the author's illustration, not a prescribed accounting entry. Suppose disclosed program obligations rise from 260 to 420, with no acquisition, foreign-exchange, or noncash effect. If term analysis shows that the entire 160 increase represents financing beyond normal trade terms, reported operating cash flow of 780 becomes 620 after removing that incremental benefit. If reported debt is 900, cash is 300, and EBITDA is 400, reported net debt is 600 and reported net leverage is 1.50 times; treating the 420 ending program balance as debt-like produces adjusted net debt of 1,020 and adjusted net leverage of 2.55 times. The operating-cash adjustment uses the 160 period increase, while the leverage adjustment uses the 420 ending claim, so the two quantities must not be confused.

### Step 6: State the conclusion at the right level of certainty

A supportable conclusion should distinguish four outcomes: ordinary trade payable with optional supplier acceleration; trade payable with a material but transparent term extension; financing liability presented and disclosed as such; or potentially misleading presentation requiring further evidence. State unresolved facts, alternative treatments, and sensitivity ranges. Supplier finance becomes a forensic finding only when evidence shows that its economics or risks conflict materially with the reported account, cash-flow classification, or disclosure.[1][2][3][6]
