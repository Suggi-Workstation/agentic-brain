# Nutrition and Cognitive Function -- What You Eat Becomes the Machinery of Thought

The brain is the most metabolically expensive tissue in the body: it runs
almost exclusively on glucose, cannot store meaningful fuel reserves, and
demands on the order of a fifth of resting energy expenditure (Muth & Park,
2021; "Role of Dietary Carbohydrates," 2025). Because thinking is a chemical
process, what a person eats -- and when -- measurably shifts attention,
memory, and executive function, with the strongest effects concentrated in
glucose regulation, omega-3 fatty acid status, micronutrient adequacy,
hydration, and overall dietary pattern. The evidence supports one durable
conclusion: fixing a deficient diet produces large cognitive returns, while
supplementing an already adequate diet produces small, domain-specific ones.

## Background

The idea that food shapes the mind predates science by millennia -- folk
traditions in nearly every culture link eating habits to mental acuity --
but controlled research began only in the 1970s and 1980s, when
experimental psychologists started manipulating meals and measuring
performance on objective cognitive tasks. The earliest influential work
examined breakfast: studies led by Ernesto Pollitt in the early 1980s
showed that skipping a morning meal impaired computational skill,
short-term memory, and problem solving in undernourished schoolchildren,
and later replications found that the effect depended heavily on the
child's baseline nutritional status and IQ (Zilberter & Zilberter, 2013).
In parallel, a laboratory tradition grew around glucose: administering
glucose drinks transiently improved memory performance in healthy
volunteers, establishing glucose as the brain's primary cognitive fuel
(Sunram-Lea & Owen, 2017). The first systematic neuroimaging review of
this literature found that the medial temporal lobe and frontal
regions -- the structures most involved in memory and executive
control -- are particularly sensitive to circulating glucose, especially
when cognitive demand is high ("Fuel for Thought?," PMC7305087).

By the late 1990s the field had discovered its most important
distinction: the absolute amount of glucose matters less than how well
the body regulates it. Kaplan and colleagues (2000) showed that elderly
people with better glucose tolerance performed better on memory tasks
even within the normal, non-diabetic range, and that their performance
could be improved with glucose or carbohydrate foods. This reframed the
question from "how much energy is available" to "how stable is the
supply," and it remains the organizing principle of the field
(Sunram-Lea & Owen, 2017; Muth & Park, 2021).

The field now operates on two distinct timescales. Acute effects --
the cognitive consequences of a single meal or beverage within hours --
are driven by glucose delivery, hydration, and postprandial signaling,
and they are modest, domain-specific, and conditional on individual
metabolic status (Sunram-Lea & Owen, 2017; Galioto & Spitznagel,
2016). Chronic effects -- the consequences of months and years of a
dietary pattern -- accumulate through structural mechanisms: membrane
composition, cerebrovascular health, neuroinflammation, and the slow
correction or accumulation of micronutrient deficits (Morris et al.,
2015; "Micronutrient balance and brain function," 2026). Most popular
confusion about "brain foods" comes from conflating these two scales:
a food that measurably sharpens the next hour says little about the
next decade, and vice versa.

The 2000s brought a second paradigm shift, from single nutrients to
dietary patterns. The PREDIMED trial's cognitive substudy, reported by
Martinez-Lapiscina and colleagues (2013), found that older adults
randomized to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with nuts or
extra-virgin olive oil maintained better cognition than a low-fat
control group (cited in Morris et al., 2015). Building on that finding,
Martha Clare Morris and her team at Rush University devised the MIND
diet -- a hybrid of the Mediterranean and DASH patterns that emphasizes
specifically neuroprotective foods such as leafy greens, berries, nuts,
whole grains, and fish -- and showed in a cohort of 960 older adults
that adherence was associated with a markedly slower rate of cognitive
decline (Morris et al., 2015).

The third shift was a correction. When the MIND diet was finally tested
in a rigorous randomized controlled trial (Barnes et al., 2023),
participants assigned to the MIND diet improved only slightly over
three years -- and no more than a control group that followed mild
caloric restriction with equally healthy dietary guidance. The honest
reading is that diet quality matters, but the marginal benefit of one
specific "brain diet" over another equally adequate diet is small
(Barnes et al., 2023). This mirrors a recurring pattern across the
field: large observational effects shrink or vanish when tested
against active, healthy control conditions.

Two methodological problems explain much of the field's inconsistency.
First, glucoregulatory status varies enormously between individuals, so
the same meal produces different cognitive outcomes in different
people -- Zilberter and Zilberter (2013) catalogued "sixteen effects in
nine populations" for breakfast alone. Second, many studies use small
samples and cognitive tests too insensitive to detect modest effects;
systematic reviewers repeatedly identify these limitations (Adolphus et
al., 2016; Linus Pauling Institute, 2023). These constraints are not
reasons to dismiss the field -- the mechanisms are real -- but they
explain why confident claims about single "brain foods" so often fail
to replicate.

## Core Concepts

### The Brain as a Metabolic Engine

The brain is a metabolic engine, not a neutral processor. It consumes
glucose at a high and roughly constant rate, and unlike muscle it
stores almost no glycogen, so it depends on a continuous arterial
supply; when blood glucose falls, cognitive function degrades within
minutes (Muth & Park, 2021). Under sustained carbohydrate restriction
the brain adapts by oxidizing ketone bodies and lactate, which is why
some individuals report stable or even improved mental clarity on
low-carbohydrate or fasting protocols -- but for the majority who eat
mixed diets, glucose stability is the variable that tracks day-to-day
cognitive performance ("Role of Dietary Carbohydrates," 2025).

### Glucose Regulation Matters More Than Glucose Itself

The key insight of modern nutritional neuroscience is that glucose
regulation -- the efficiency with which the body clears glucose from
the blood -- predicts cognitive performance better than absolute blood
glucose. Kaplan et al. (2000) demonstrated this in healthy elderly
adults: those with better glucose tolerance performed better on
memory tests, and carbohydrate foods improved their performance.
Because glucose tolerance declines with age, inactivity, and
ultra-processed diets, the same meal can support sharp cognition in one
person and a postprandial crash in another. The practical implication,
developed further below, is to choose foods that flatten the glycemic
curve -- low-glycemic-index, high-fiber, protein-containing meals --
rather than chasing quick energy (Sunram-Lea & Owen, 2017; "Role of
Dietary Carbohydrates," 2025). The glycemic index gives this principle
a concrete form: low-glycemic meals produce flatter postprandial
curves, and in children they better preserve accuracy of attention and
secondary memory two hours after eating, while high-glycemic meals
trade sustained accuracy for a short-lived vigilance peak (Zilberter &
Zilberter, 2013).

### Structural Fats: DHA and EPA Are Building Materials

Roughly half the dry weight of the brain is lipid, and omega-3
polyunsaturated fatty acids -- principally docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)
and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) -- are structural components of
neuronal membranes; DHA alone accounts for more than 40 percent of the
omega-3 content in gray matter (Effects of Omega-3 PUFAs on Brain
Functions, 2022). DHA supports membrane fluidity, synaptic integrity,
and plasticity, while EPA modulates inflammation; both must come from
the diet because humans synthesize them only in trace amounts. A 2025
dose-response meta-analysis of 58 randomized controlled trials found
that each 2000 mg/day increment in omega-3 supplementation was
associated with improvements in attention, memory, visuospatial
function, and global cognition, though the certainty of evidence was
graded low to moderate and the dose-response curves were non-linear
(Scientific Reports, 2025). The overview of systematic reviews on
omega-3 and cognitive decline concludes that benefit is plausible but
heterogeneous, strongest for early decline rather than established
dementia (PMC12472900, 2025). DHA and EPA act in part by competing
with pro-inflammatory omega-6 fatty acids, which is why the literature
repeatedly frames brain benefit in terms of the balance between fatty
acid families rather than absolute omega-3 intake alone (Scientific
Reports, 2025).

### Micronutrients as Cognitive Cofactors

Vitamins and minerals are the cofactors of cognition. Iron is required
for myelination and oxygen transport; iodine for thyroid hormone and
brain development; zinc for synaptic signaling; magnesium for hundreds
of enzymatic reactions including those that gate synaptic plasticity;
B vitamins -- folate, B6, B12 -- for methylation, neurotransmitter
synthesis, and myelin maintenance (Linus Pauling Institute, 2023;
"Micronutrient balance and brain function," 2026). The critical
pattern in the evidence: deficiency produces measurable cognitive
impairment -- attention deficits, slower processing, poorer memory --
while supplementation in already-replete people produces little or
nothing. The Linus Pauling Institute's review of intervention trials
finds conflicting results for multivitamin effects on attention and
memory in well-nourished populations, while deficiency correction in
deficient populations is consistently beneficial. Iodine and iron
deficiencies in early life cause lasting IQ losses, which is why the
first thousand days from conception are the most nutritionally
sensitive window of life ("Micronutrient balance and brain function,"
2026). The cofactor logic has a concrete aging corollary: B-vitamin
status in older adults tracks episodic memory in observational studies,
and intervention trials find benefits concentrated in those with low
baseline status -- the same correction-only pattern seen for iron and
iodine in children (Linus Pauling Institute, 2023; "Micronutrient
balance and brain function," 2026).

### Dietary Patterns Beat Single Nutrients

Nutrients act in concert, and whole-diet patterns predict cognitive
outcomes better than any single compound. The Mediterranean diet, the
DASH diet, and their hybrid the MIND diet share a common core -- plant
foods, fish, olive oil, limited red meat and refined sugar -- and all
three are associated with slower cognitive decline in observational
cohorts (Morris et al., 2015; "Micronutrient balance and brain
function," 2026). Morris et al. (2015) estimated that the top tertile
of MIND diet adherence was associated with a decline rate equivalent to
being 7.5 years younger. The pattern approach also explains why
isolated supplements frequently underperform: a fish-oil capsule
delivered to someone eating a Western diet cannot replicate the matrix
of polyphenols, fiber, and micronutrient balance of a dietary pattern
(Scientific Reports, 2025; Socci et al., 2017). The synergy
hypothesis -- that fiber, polyphenols, micronutrients, and fatty acids
act jointly on overlapping pathways of inflammation, vascular health,
and oxidative balance -- explains the pattern-level results and
cautions against expecting single-nutrient replacements to reproduce
them (Morris et al., 2015; "Micronutrient balance and brain function,"
2026).

### Meal Timing and the Postprandial Window

Cognition is measurably sensitive to the hours after eating. The
systematic review by Adolphus et al. (2016) of 45 studies in children
and adolescents found that breakfast relative to fasting produced
short-term, domain-specific benefits -- attention, executive function,
and memory were facilitated more reliably than language or psychomotor
function -- with stronger effects in undernourished children. In
adults, Galioto and Spitznagel (2016) reviewed 38 studies and found a
small but robust advantage for memory, particularly delayed recall,
with equivocal effects on attention and executive function. Crucially,
meal composition interacts with individual glucose tolerance: in
glucose-tolerant adults a high-carbohydrate breakfast improved
immediate recall, while glucose-intolerant adults showed no benefit
from the same meal (Zilberter & Zilberter, 2013). There is no single
"correct" breakfast; the effect depends on what is eaten, by whom, and
what task follows. Timing also interacts with task demand: breakfast
effects are easier to detect later in the morning, on demanding tasks,
and when error rates rather than raw speed are measured (Adolphus et
al., 2016).

### Hydration Is a Cognitive Variable

Even mild dehydration impairs cognition. The CogniDROP cluster-
randomized trial in 250 German schoolchildren found a non-linear
relationship between morning water intake and executive function:
drinking up to roughly one liter (about half of daily recommended total
water intake) improved performance on memory tasks, while intake
beyond that point reversed the benefit (Drozdowska et al., 2020).
Adults show similar patterns -- deficits in attention and working
memory appear at roughly 1-2 percent body water loss, a level that
produces no conscious thirst in many people (Drozdowska et al., 2020).
Hydration is therefore one of the cheapest, most reliable cognitive
interventions available.

### Polyphenols and the Cerebrovascular Effect

Plant polyphenols -- flavonoids and flavanols found in berries, cocoa,
tea, and dark-skinned fruit -- appear to improve cognition partly
through vascular mechanisms. Gratton et al. (2020) demonstrated in a
double-blind crossover trial that a high-flavanol cocoa drink improved
cerebral cortical oxygenation during a carbon-dioxide challenge and
improved performance on a cognitively demanding task, compared with
low-flavanol cocoa. In the CoCoA randomized trial, elderly adults who
consumed a high-flavanol cocoa drink daily for eight weeks improved
verbal fluency and executive function (Mastroiacovo et al., 2015).
The review by Socci et al. (2017) tempers enthusiasm: effects on mood
are more consistent than effects on cognition, magnitudes are small,
and benefits concentrate in older or cognitively stressed populations.
Mechanistically, flavanols act on the vasculature -- improving
cerebrovascular reactivity and cortical oxygenation -- rather than as
direct neuronal stimulants, which is exactly why their cognitive
effects concentrate in aging and cardiovascular-risk populations whose
endothelial function is already compromised (Gratton et al., 2020;
Socci et al., 2017).

## Evidence

### Glucose Regulation Predicts and Modifies Memory (Kaplan et al., 2000)

In a controlled study of healthy elderly adults with normal glucose
tolerance, Kaplan and colleagues measured glucose tolerance via blood
sampling after carbohydrate beverages, then administered cognitive
tests after glucose, sucrose, and other meals. Better glucose
regulation was associated with better memory performance, and glucose
or carbohydrate ingestion improved memory relative to fasting. The
finding matters because it established that individual metabolic
status -- not the meal alone -- determines the cognitive response to
food, and that the same carbohydrate load that helps a well-regulated
person can leave a poorly regulated person cognitively impaired
(Kaplan et al., 2000; Sunram-Lea & Owen, 2017). The study's enduring
significance is that it moved the causal question from "does glucose
help" to "for whom does glucose help," anticipating the individual-
differences theme that later reviews formalized (Sunram-Lea & Owen,
2017).

### The MIND Diet Observational Cohort (Morris et al., 2015)

Morris and colleagues scored dietary adherence in 960 participants of
the Rush Memory and Aging Project and tracked cognition over an average
of 4.7 years. In adjusted models, higher MIND diet scores were
associated with significantly slower decline in global cognition
(beta = 0.0092 per point, p < .0001) and in each of five cognitive
domains. The difference in decline rates between the top and bottom
tertiles of adherence was equivalent to being 7.5 years younger. This
is among the strongest observational associations in the nutrition-
cognition literature, but the design cannot rule out confounding --
people who eat MIND-style diets differ in education, income, and
lifestyle in ways that independently protect cognition (Morris et al.,
2015). The MIND score itself weighted natural plant-based foods and
fish positively and animal and high-saturated-fat foods negatively,
with cognition measured by an annually administered battery -- a
design strong enough to estimate decline rates precisely, but
observational all the same (Morris et al., 2015).

### The MIND Diet Randomized Trial -- the Honest Null (Barnes et al., 2023)

The definitive test came in 2023. Barnes and colleagues randomized 604
overweight older adults with a family history of dementia and
suboptimal diets to either the MIND diet with mild caloric restriction
or a control diet with the same mild caloric restriction, for three
years. Both groups improved on the global cognition composite by
roughly 0.2 standardized units, and the difference between groups was
not significant. The trial does not show that diet does not matter --
both arms ate better than baseline -- but it shows that a specifically
"brain-targeted" diet confers no measurable advantage over an equally
healthy control diet over three years, and it explains why
observational effect sizes should be read with caution (Barnes et al.,
2023). Because both arms carried the same mild caloric restriction,
the trial isolated the marginal effect of the MIND food pattern
itself -- a comparison far more stringent than any observational
contrast, and the reason its null result, rather than the earlier
cohort associations, is now the benchmark for claims about branded
brain diets (Barnes et al., 2023).

### Omega-3 Dose-Response Meta-Analysis (Scientific Reports, 2025)

A 2025 systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis pooled 58
randomized controlled trials of omega-3 supplementation in adults.
Per 2000 mg/day increment of supplementation, standardized mean
differences were 0.98 for attention, 0.87 for primary memory, 0.86 for
visuospatial function, 0.50 for perceptual speed, and 1.08 for global
cognition, with wide confidence intervals and GRADE certainty ratings
of low to moderate. Dose-response analysis revealed non-linearity:
episodic memory declined at moderate doses and rose again at higher
doses (P for non-linearity = 0.01), and global cognition followed an
inverted-U pattern (P = 0.008). The authors conclude omega-3s may
confer modest cognitive benefit, but the evidence does not support
precise dosing claims (Scientific Reports, 2025). Included dosages
spanned 230 to 4950 mg/day and durations 4 to 160 weeks, with the
Mini-Mental State Examination the most common outcome measure -- a
heterogeneity the authors identify as the principal reason certainty
ratings remain low to moderate (Scientific Reports, 2025). An umbrella
review of systematic reviews reaches the same guarded conclusion for
prevention of decline in non-demented adults (PMC12472900, 2025).

### Breakfast Systematic Reviews -- Children and Adults

Adolphus et al. (2016) synthesized 45 studies (43 articles) on
breakfast and cognition in children and adolescents. For acute
breakfast-versus-fasting comparisons, tasks requiring attention,
executive function, and memory were facilitated most reliably, with
effects more apparent in undernourished children and later in the
morning; evidence on breakfast composition was too inconsistent for
conclusions. In adults, Galioto and Spitznagel (2016) integrated 38
studies and found a small but robust advantage of breakfast for
memory -- particularly delayed recall -- with equivocal results for
attention and executive function and none for language. Both reviews
emphasize that test timing, meal composition, and participant
glucoregulation moderate the results (Adolphus et al., 2016; Galioto &
Spitznagel, 2016). Zilberter and Zilberter (2013) add the crucial
caveat that skipping breakfast harms undernourished children but is
inconsequential or even beneficial in well-nourished children and
some adults, which reframes breakfast as a conditional intervention
rather than a universal rule.

### Cocoa Flavanols, Oxygenation, and Cognition

Gratton et al. (2020) conducted a double-blind, within-subject,
placebo-controlled acute study comparing high-flavanol and low-flavanol
cocoa in healthy young adults. High-flavanol cocoa increased cortical
oxygenation during a hypercapnia challenge and improved performance on
demanding cognitive tasks, linking polyphenol intake to cerebrovascular
reactivity. In older adults, the CoCoA trial (Mastroiacovo et al.,
2015) found that eight weeks of daily high-flavanol cocoa improved
verbal fluency and executive function relative to low-flavanol cocoa.
The systematic review by Socci et al. (2017) confirms directionally
positive but small and population-dependent effects, with mood benefits
appearing more consistent than cognitive ones.

### Hydration: the CogniDROP Trial

Drozdowska et al. (2020) cluster-randomized 14 school classes (250
children) to either a water-promotion intervention or control and
measured executive functions with computerized tasks. Water intake up
to approximately 1000 mL during the school morning was associated with
better memory-task performance, while intake beyond that threshold
reversed the effect -- a non-linear dose-response suggesting both
dehydration and overhydration can impair performance. The effect sizes
were small to medium, but hydration stands out as an intervention with
essentially zero cost and risk.

## Implications

### For Knowledge Workers and Students

The actionable core of this literature is stability, not stimulation.
Because glucose regulation outpredicts glucose level, the practical
strategy for sustained attention and memory is to flatten the glycemic
curve: prefer low-glycemic-index, high-fiber, protein-containing meals;
avoid large refined-sugar loads before demanding cognitive work; and
maintain hydration in the roughly one-liter-during-work range indicated
by the CogniDROP data (Drozdowska et al., 2020; Sunram-Lea & Owen,
2017). Breakfast is worth eating for most people -- the adult evidence
shows a small, reliable memory advantage, and the child evidence is
stronger -- but composition should be matched to individual glucose
tolerance, and no universal prescription survives scrutiny (Galioto &
Spitznagel, 2016; Zilberter & Zilberter, 2013). Fatty fish or algae-
based DHA/EPA intake twice weekly, polyphenol-rich foods (berries,
cocoa, tea), and adequate iron, B12, and iodine status are low-cost,
low-risk complements (Scientific Reports, 2025; Socci et al., 2017).
The author's assessment: for a knowledge worker with an already
reasonable diet, expected gains are real but modest -- probably on
the order of small percentage improvements in memory and sustained
attention -- and are dwarfed by sleep, exercise, and training effects
documented in the sibling topics of this domain. The adult breakfast
data put a number on the stake: the memory advantage of eating
breakfast is small but robust, concentrated in delayed recall, and it
operates within the same morning -- a marginal edge that, deployed
daily across years of study and work, compounds the way small habit
effects do (Galioto & Spitznagel, 2016). A useful frame is the n-of-
one trial: track alertness and task performance for two weeks on a
protocol, then two weeks off, and keep only what measurably helps --
decision-journal logic applied to diet rather than to decisions.

### For Aging Adults and Dementia Risk

The strongest population-level signal is dietary pattern: MIND and
Mediterranean adherence associate with substantially slower cognitive
decline -- the 7.5-year-equivalent effect is the headline of the
literature -- but the randomized evidence shows the branded diet is no
better than any equally healthy pattern with caloric control (Morris et
al., 2015; Barnes et al., 2023). The honest implication for an older
adult is to adopt a plant-forward, fish-including, ultra-processed-
limited pattern for its established cardiovascular and metabolic
benefits, and to treat any additional cognitive protection as a
plausible bonus rather than a guaranteed return. Omega-3 supplementation
shows its clearest signal in early decline rather than established
dementia, and micronutrient correction matters most for those with
measured deficiencies (PMC12472900, 2025; "Micronutrient balance and
brain function," 2026). Practical sequencing follows this certainty
ordering: adopt the dietary pattern first, for its high-certainty
cardiovascular and metabolic benefits; correct measured deficiencies
second; and consider targeted supplements -- DHA/EPA in early decline,
flavanols in those with vascular risk -- last, at the low end of
certainty (Morris et al., 2015; PMC12472900, 2025; Socci et al.,
2017).

### For Education and Policy

School breakfast programs illustrate the conditional nature of the
evidence. The systematic reviews show breakfast helps attention and
memory most in undernourished children, with weaker or absent effects
in well-nourished ones (Adolphus et al., 2016; Zilberter & Zilberter,
2013). Policy should therefore target nutrition support where baseline
need is greatest -- the highest-yield use of public money -- rather
than universal mandates justified by universal benefit claims. The
same logic applies to workplace wellness: subsidizing quality food and
water access is defensible; mandating specific "brain food" programs
is not. The caution cuts both ways: a randomized controlled trial of
free school breakfast programs found no support for the widely
claimed academic-performance gains, so advocates should anchor
programs to nutritional adequacy for the underserved rather than to
universal cognitive-enhancement claims (Zilberter & Zilberter, 2013).

### For the Supplement Consumer

The supplement industry's central claim -- that pills replicate food --
is the weakest link in this literature. Intervention trials of
multivitamins, B vitamins, and antioxidants in well-nourished
populations are consistently conflicting or null, while deficiency
correction is consistently beneficial (Linus Pauling Institute, 2023).
The rational protocol is: test for deficiencies when risk factors
exist (age, restricted diets, malabsorption), correct what is found,
and otherwise obtain nutrients from a varied diet. The author's
assessment: money spent on omega-3 or polyphenol supplements by a
replete, healthy adult buys, at best, the small and uncertain effects
documented above -- a defensible bet only after the high-certainty
levers (deficiency correction, glycemic stability, hydration) are
already pulled. This ordering explains the common disappointment with
"brain supplements": most consumers are already replete, so most
trials among healthy adults find null or inconsistent effects on
attention, memory, and executive function, while the same nutrients
reliably help the deficient (Linus Pauling Institute, 2023).

Why small effects still matter. The measured effects in this
literature are small -- fractions of a standard deviation, reliable
mainly at the group level. The author's assessment is that their value
comes from compounding: an attention edge of a few percent, sustained
daily across a multi-decade career, is among the larger levers a
knowledge worker controls, and unlike pharmacological or
technological interventions it compounds with essentially no downside
risk. The catch is that the same compounding applies in reverse -- a
chronically deficient or glucose-unstable diet quietly taxes every
working hour -- which is why the first-order recommendation is always
to fix the baseline before optimizing the margins.

### Practical Protocols

Grounded in the evidence above, the following protocol is the author's
synthesis, not a source statement. First, fix the baseline pattern
before adding anything: plant-forward meals, fatty fish or algae
twice weekly, nuts and legumes daily, limited refined sugar and
ultra-processed food (Morris et al., 2015). Second, hydrate
preemptively: aim for roughly 500-1000 mL during a working morning
and do not wait for thirst (Drozdowska et al., 2020). Third, choose
low-glycemic, high-fiber, protein-containing meals before demanding
cognitive work; save high-glycemic treats for after (Sunram-Lea &
Owen, 2017). Fourth, include polyphenol foods -- berries, tea, dark
chocolate, or cocoa -- as part of the pattern rather than as
isolated supplements (Socci et al., 2017). Fifth, verify micronutrient
status when risk factors are present: B12, iron, vitamin D, and iodine
are the highest-yield tests ("Micronutrient balance and brain
function," 2026). Sixth, measure honestly: if a change does not
improve subjective or objective performance within weeks, revert --
individual glucoregulation makes every rule conditional (Zilberter &
Zilberter, 2013).
