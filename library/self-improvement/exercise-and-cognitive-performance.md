# Exercise and Cognitive Performance -- Benefits Are Real but Smaller and Less Universal Than Popular Claims

Exercise can improve some cognitive outcomes, but the average effects are usually small, vary by population and test, and do not prove that every workout makes a person think better [8][9][10]. The most defensible conclusion is that physical activity supports cognitive health while acute performance gains, structural brain changes, and proposed molecular mechanisms remain outcome-specific rather than guaranteed [6][7][10][12].

## Background

Physical activity and exercise are related but not interchangeable. The World Health Organization defines physical activity as bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure; it includes transport, work, recreation, and sport [1]. Exercise is the planned and structured part of that larger category. This distinction matters because observational studies of active lifestyles, randomized trials of training programs, and laboratory studies of a single exercise bout answer different questions. They also measure different outcomes: general cognition, memory, attention, processing speed, inhibition, working memory, and task switching should not be treated as one ability [8][10][14].

Modern interest in exercise and the brain grew from animal work that made neural plasticity experimentally visible. A 1995 rat study reported that voluntary running increased hippocampal and cortical expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, or BDNF, and a 1999 mouse experiment found that wheel running increased dentate-gyrus cell proliferation, enhanced long-term potentiation, and improved water-maze performance [3][4]. Those studies established plausible mechanisms in animals, not a direct prescription for human cognition. In particular, researchers can label dividing cells and manipulate signaling pathways in rodents in ways that are not normally possible in living people [3][4].

The human evidence therefore relies on converging but less direct measures: cognitive tests, blood biomarkers, cardiorespiratory fitness, and structural or functional neuroimaging. Adult human hippocampal neurogenesis itself remains methodologically difficult to measure. Postmortem and birth-dating studies support its occurrence, but estimates depend strongly on tissue handling, markers, health status, and other methodological choices [5]. Magnetic-resonance imaging can detect a change in hippocampal volume, but it cannot by itself show that new neurons caused the change; vascular, glial, dendritic, and fluid changes are alternative contributors [5][7].

A prominent human result came from Erickson and colleagues in 2011. In a randomized trial of 120 adults aged 55 to 80 without dementia, one year of moderate aerobic walking increased left and right hippocampal volume by 2.12 percent and 1.97 percent, while a stretching-and-toning control group declined by 1.40 percent and 1.43 percent [6]. Changes in serum BDNF correlated with volume changes within the aerobic group, but the intervention did not produce a significant between-group increase in serum BDNF [6]. The trial showed that hippocampal enlargement can occur under one well-defined protocol; it did not establish that aerobic exercise generally enlarges the hippocampus in all older adults.

Later synthesis changed the interpretation. A 2024 meta-analysis of eight supervised randomized trials involving 554 cognitively unimpaired older adults found no statistically significant effect of aerobic training on hippocampal volume, although cardiorespiratory fitness improved [7]. The authors emphasized that the evidence base was small and methodologically limited [7]. The correct historical lesson is therefore not that the 2011 result was false, but that a striking single trial did not become a stable pooled effect.

The same tension appears in broader cognition reviews. A 2025 umbrella review spanning clinical and healthy populations reported improvements in general cognition, memory, and executive function across 133 systematic reviews [8]. By contrast, a 2023 umbrella review restricted to randomized trials in healthy people estimated a small effect that became negligible after adjustment for publication bias [9]. These syntheses used different populations, eligibility rules, and statistical corrections, so their estimates are not interchangeable. Together they support a bounded conclusion: exercise can improve cognition, particularly in some clinical or lower-baseline groups, but effect size and certainty depend on who is studied and how bias is handled [8][9].

This more cautious account is still actionable. Exercise has established benefits beyond cognition, and public-health guidelines recommend regular aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity for substantial overall health benefits [2]. Cognitive benefit is a possible additional return, not a reason to convert broad health guidance into a precise claim that one modality, dose, or molecular pathway is optimal for every person.

## Core Concepts

### Separate the Outcome Before Estimating the Benefit

"Cognition" is an umbrella term. General cognitive screening scores, episodic memory, working memory, inhibition, attention, and reaction time rely on partly different tasks and can move in different directions after the same intervention [8][10][14]. A faster response does not necessarily mean a more accurate response, and a better laboratory score does not automatically imply better work, school performance, or daily decision quality. The 2024 acute-exercise meta-analysis, for example, found a small overall benefit in healthy young adults and clearer effects on reaction time than on accuracy [10]. Any practical claim must therefore name the population, exercise exposure, test, and time at which the test was administered.

This rule prevents a common inference error. A study showing a faster Stroop response after cycling cannot establish that the participant will write better code, remember more material months later, or make fewer judgment errors. Those outcomes may share components, but the transfer must be tested rather than assumed. The useful unit of evidence is not "exercise improves the brain"; it is a specified comparison such as "one acute bout changed reaction time on selected executive tasks in healthy young adults" [10].

### Acute Effects and Training Effects Are Different

Acute studies measure cognition during or after one bout. Training studies measure change after repeated sessions over weeks or months. Acute exercise changes arousal, temperature, blood flow, metabolites, neurotransmitter signaling, and fatigue on a timescale of minutes to hours [3][10]. Training can change fitness and may alter vascular function, network organization, or brain structure over longer periods [6][7]. Evidence for one timescale does not prove the other.

The best current acute estimate is modest. Garrett and colleagues synthesized 651 effect sizes, including imputed values used to address publication bias, from 113 studies and 4,390 healthy young adults. They estimated an overall Hedges' g of 0.13, with benefits concentrated more in reaction time than accuracy; executive-function analyses suggested improvements in working memory and inhibition [10]. The result supports a small average acute effect, not a universal productivity boost. Exercise type, test timing, participant fitness, and the cognitive task all varied across studies [10].

An individual experiment can show a larger or more durable result in a narrow setting. Marin Bosch and colleagues used a randomized within-person crossover design in 18 regularly exercising young men. Moderate exercise improved performance on their associative-memory task at an immediate test and a surprise retest three months later, and changes in BDNF and anandamide correlated with selected neural and behavioral measures [11]. The small, male-only, physically active sample and specialized task limit generalization. The study supports a possible mechanism and a testable timing effect; it does not prove that one workout usually improves long-term memory [11].

### BDNF Is a Plausible Mediator, Not a Complete Explanation

BDNF supports neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity and is central to many animal models of exercise-related learning [3][4]. Reviews organize proposed exercise-induced BDNF production into three interacting routes: activity-dependent neuronal signaling, hemodynamic signaling associated with cerebral blood flow, and humoral signaling from peripheral tissues through metabolites and exerkines [3]. Much of the pathway detail comes from rodents or cell models, while human studies often measure BDNF in serum or plasma rather than directly in brain tissue [3][12].

Human biomarker evidence shows association without settling mediation. A meta-analysis of 29 studies found a moderate rise in circulating BDNF after a single exercise session, a larger acute response after regular training, and a small increase in resting BDNF after a training program [12]. Peripheral BDNF is not a direct meter of hippocampal concentration, and blood measurements vary with sampling material, timing, sex distribution, and training status [12]. In the Erickson trial, change in serum BDNF correlated with change in hippocampal volume among exercisers even though the intervention did not significantly raise serum BDNF relative to the control group [6].

Causal language must be divided by species. Blocking BDNF signaling can remove exercise-related learning benefits in animal experiments, which supports necessity in those models [3]. Human genetic evidence is less decisive. A review of 26 studies and 11,417 participants found that the BDNF Val66Met variant did not consistently moderate cognitive response to physical activity or exercise; significant findings differed in direction, and both Val and Met carriers could benefit [13]. It is therefore inaccurate to say that BDNF is proven to be mechanistically necessary for exercise-related cognitive benefit in humans.

### Brain Volume Is Not the Same as Cognitive Function

Structural imaging is informative but easy to overread. The 2011 trial linked aerobic training to anterior hippocampal enlargement and spatial-memory improvement in one sample [6]. The later meta-analysis did not find a significant pooled hippocampal-volume effect in healthy older adults, despite improved fitness [7]. These results can coexist because single studies are noisy, protocols differ, and a measurable fitness improvement need not produce an MRI-detectable volume change over the observed period [7].

Even when volume changes, function cannot be inferred from size alone. MRI-defined volume combines multiple tissue and vascular processes; it does not identify newly generated neurons [5][7]. Likewise, adult human neurogenesis cannot be treated as the established explanation for any exercise-related MRI result. Rodent running experiments directly support neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and learning, while human evidence remains indirect [4][5]. The appropriate formulation is that neurogenesis is one plausible mechanism with strong animal support and unresolved human measurement.

### Dose Is Multidimensional and Does Not Reduce to an Inverted U

Exercise dose includes frequency, intensity, session duration, program length, modality, progression, and adherence. Cognitive outcomes add further dimensions: task type, assessment timing, baseline ability, age, health status, and control condition [8][9][10][14]. Because these variables interact, no single dose-response curve describes all cognitive effects.

The 2025 umbrella review reported that pooled gains were generally not altered by session frequency, duration, or intensity within studied ranges, while shorter interventions sometimes produced larger estimates [8]. The authors noted possible explanations such as attrition, adherence, and inadequate progression in longer trials [8]. A separate 2020 meta-analysis of executive function in older adults found a small overall benefit and larger estimates at three to four sessions per week than at one to two, but no significant moderation by intensity or session duration [14]. These are population- and outcome-specific findings, not a universal optimum.

Public-health targets should not be mislabeled as a cognition prescription. WHO recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination per week for adults, plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days [2]. Those recommendations integrate many health outcomes. Meeting them is a defensible health target, but evidence does not establish that 150 minutes is a threshold at which cognitive benefit begins or that more activity always yields more cognitive improvement [2][8].

### Modality Effects Are Selective and Uncertain

Aerobic exercise has the longest history in brain-imaging and BDNF research, but resistance, dance, mind-body, coordinative, and game-based activity can also contain cognitive demands and produce benefits [8][14]. In the 2025 umbrella review, exergames had the largest pooled estimate for general cognition and mind-body exercise performed well for memory, but subgroup rankings can reflect differences in populations, controls, and study quality as well as the exercise itself [8]. A category that combines movement with rules, coordination, and rapid decisions does not isolate the contribution of cardiovascular exertion.

Resistance training should not be described as a guaranteed executive-function intervention or as operating through one exclusive molecule. A 2020 synthesis of 33 randomized trials found a small executive-function benefit across exercise types in older adults, with resistance exercise among the beneficial categories [14]. A 2025 resistance-only meta-analysis of 17 trials and 739 older adults found improvements in overall cognition, working memory, verbal learning, and spatial memory span, but no significant pooled effect on executive function, attention, or processing speed [15]. The mixed pattern supports resistance exercise for general health and possibly selected cognitive domains, not a sharp aerobic-memory versus resistance-executive division.

### Stress Pathways Are Hypotheses, Not a Settled Chain

Exercise is itself a physiological stressor, and intensity, duration, training status, and recovery affect cortisol responses [16]. It is plausible that repeated exercise changes stress reactivity and thereby influences cognition, because the HPA axis and brain regions involved in memory and executive control interact [16]. Plausibility is not proof of mediation. A 2023 scoping review found insufficient evidence that chronic or habitual aerobic exercise improves neurocognitive health by altering cortisol or salivary alpha-amylase pathways; much of the available evidence was cross-sectional [16]. Claims that training simply lowers baseline cortisol, protects the hippocampus from structural damage, and thereby improves work performance exceed current human evidence.

### Baseline State and Control Conditions Matter

Benefits can be easier to detect in sedentary participants, people with lower baseline performance, or clinical populations with more room to improve [8][14]. Active control groups also matter. Comparing exercise with no contact or usual routine can capture expectancy, social interaction, and study attention, while comparison with stretching, health education, or another structured activity isolates a narrower effect. The 2023 healthy-population umbrella review found that publication-bias adjustment reduced the average estimate to near zero, whereas the broader 2025 synthesis retained significant effects after excluding lower-quality reviews [8][9]. The disagreement is evidence about sensitivity to design, not permission to select whichever estimate is more appealing.

## Evidence

### Broad Syntheses Reach Different Magnitudes

Singh and colleagues searched 11 databases for systematic reviews of randomized trials and included 133 reviews representing 2,724 trials and 258,279 participants across ages and health conditions [8]. Their pooled estimates were SMD 0.42 for general cognition, 0.26 for memory, and 0.24 for executive function. Heterogeneity was high, and the general-cognition estimate fell from 0.42 to 0.30 after adjustment for publication bias and to 0.23 when only high-quality reviews were analyzed [8]. Children and adolescents showed larger memory and executive-function estimates than adult groups, and ADHD samples showed a larger executive-function estimate than other diagnostic categories [8]. This is strong evidence that average effects can be positive across a broad evidence base, but the estimates should not be presented without their heterogeneity and quality sensitivity.

Ciria and colleagues asked a narrower causal question: what do randomized trials show in healthy people when meta-analytic overlap and publication bias are addressed? Their 2023 umbrella review found a small unadjusted average effect and an estimate near zero after publication-bias correction [9]. Restricting the population removes many groups with impaired baseline function, while stricter adjustment also reduces the estimate. The two umbrella reviews therefore do not establish a single universal effect size. They bracket a reasonable interpretation: small benefits are plausible in healthy populations, and larger pooled estimates partly reflect clinical populations, selective outcomes, and methodological choices [8][9].

### The Hippocampal-Volume Result Did Not Generalize Cleanly

Erickson and colleagues randomly assigned 120 older adults without dementia to aerobic walking or stretching and toning for one year [6]. The aerobic group increased left and right hippocampal volume by 2.12 percent and 1.97 percent, while the control group declined by 1.40 percent and 1.43 percent. Change was concentrated in the anterior hippocampus, spatial memory improved, and greater serum-BDNF change correlated with greater volume change within the exercise group [6]. The between-group effect on serum BDNF itself was not significant [6].

Balbim and colleagues later pooled eight supervised randomized trials with 554 healthy older participants. Aerobic training lasted three to twelve months and averaged about 130 minutes per week at moderate-to-vigorous intensity [7]. The pooled hippocampal-volume effect was SMD 0.10 with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.01 to 0.21 and p = 0.073, while cardiorespiratory fitness improved significantly [7]. The evidence therefore supports the fitness effect more consistently than the structural-volume claim. The single-trial percentage remains a valid finding for that experiment, not a general expectation for an individual program.

### Acute Benefits Are Small on Average and Larger Studies Limit the Claim

Garrett and colleagues included 113 studies of healthy young adults and modeled 651 effect sizes after trim-and-fill adjustment [10]. The pooled acute effect was g = 0.13, with a stronger result for reaction time than accuracy. Executive-function analyses supported small benefits for working memory and inhibition, and cycling and high-intensity interval categories showed positive estimates, although some modality subgroups contained much less evidence than others [10]. The result supports a short-lived average facilitation, not the claim that a particular workout reliably produces a large cognitive edge.

The Marin Bosch crossover experiment illustrates both promise and limitation. Eighteen regularly exercising young men completed rest, moderate-exercise, and high-exercise conditions, followed by associative-memory testing and fMRI [11]. Moderate exercise improved task performance at immediate and three-month tests, and selected BDNF and anandamide changes correlated with hippocampal signals or retention. The within-person design controlled stable individual differences, but the small and homogeneous sample, biomarker correlations, and specialized memory task prevent broad causal claims about daily learning [11].

A 2024 experiment in healthy older adults found that one short, moderate cycling bout improved selected inhibitory-control and spatial-working-memory outcomes [17]. Other outcomes, including one working-memory error comparison, did not differ significantly between groups, and the investigators did not record the heart-rate data used to maintain the exercise target [17]. This pattern is typical of the field: some measures improve while others do not, so a study-level conclusion must preserve the outcome detail.

### Molecular Evidence Is Strongest for Plausibility

The BDNF meta-analysis found that acute exercise increased circulating BDNF with Hedges' g = 0.46, that regular training amplified the acute response, and that training produced a smaller change in resting levels [12]. These findings verify a reproducible peripheral biomarker response. They do not establish that BDNF alone caused the cognitive change, because most included studies did not experimentally manipulate BDNF in humans and peripheral measures are imperfect proxies for brain concentrations [3][12].

The Val66Met review further limits a simple causal story. Across 26 studies, observational and interventional findings on genotype moderation were inconsistent in both presence and direction [13]. Some Val carriers responded more in selected studies, while some Met carriers responded more in others. The review concluded that neither genotype should be treated as unable to obtain cognitive benefit from activity [13]. Animal blockade studies remain important mechanistic evidence, but the human evidence supports a network of vascular, metabolic, neural, and behavioral pathways rather than a single BDNF switch [3][13].

### Resistance and Stress Evidence Reject Simple Modality Stories

Chen and colleagues found a small overall executive-function effect, g = 0.21, across 33 randomized exercise trials in adults older than 55 [14]. Estimates differed by exercise category and baseline fitness, with larger gains among initially sedentary participants, but did not differ significantly by intensity or session length [14]. Wu and Huang's resistance-only synthesis later found selective benefits but no significant pooled executive-function or attention effect [15]. These results do not support the claim that resistance training uniquely targets executive control through a distinct pathway.

The HPA-axis hypothesis is similarly incomplete. Molina-Hidalgo and colleagues reviewed aerobic exercise, cortisol, salivary alpha-amylase, and neurocognitive outcomes and found too little controlled evidence to conclude that stress-biomarker changes mediate cognitive improvement [16]. Acute exercise can raise stress hormones, and chronic patterns vary by dose and population. A practical account should therefore describe stress regulation as a research hypothesis and avoid promising that exercise will lower cortisol or reverse stress-related brain damage [16].

## Implications

### For Knowledge Work

Exercise should be treated as health-supporting infrastructure, not as a guaranteed nootropic. The acute meta-analysis implies that a workout may produce a small average improvement in reaction time and selected executive tasks, but accuracy and individual response can remain unchanged [10]. A person can reasonably test whether a familiar, non-exhausting bout before demanding work helps, while recognizing that fatigue, heat, scheduling cost, and task type may cancel the average benefit. The evidence does not justify translating g = 0.13 or g = 0.27 into claims that someone will hold more information in mind, resist distraction better, or solve complex problems faster without measuring those outcomes [10].

Timing advice should remain conditional. The Marin Bosch study supports moderate exercise around a specific memory-consolidation protocol in a small male sample, while broader acute evidence spans many timings and tasks [10][11]. A practical trial can compare the same cognitive task after exercise and after a matched non-exercise period across several sessions. The author's synthesis is to keep the protocol constant, record sleep and perceived exertion, and retain the timing only if task performance or work output improves consistently. This is an individual decision rule, not a population-level effect claim.

### For Aging and Cognitive Preservation

Regular activity is a defensible part of healthy aging because it benefits cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal, and mental health, and public-health reviews associate it with better cognitive health [2][18]. The cognitive case should not be reduced to hippocampal enlargement. One trial found enlargement, but the pooled randomized evidence in healthy older adults did not show a significant volume effect [6][7]. Fitness can improve without a detectable MRI change, and a person does not need evidence of brain growth for exercise to be worthwhile.

Dementia language requires similar discipline. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines identify lower risk of dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, among the benefits associated with regular physical activity [18]. Risk reduction at the population level is not a guarantee of prevention for an individual, and observational association is not identical to randomized evidence that a program prevents dementia. Exercise belongs in a broader risk-management approach rather than as a substitute for clinical assessment, treatment of vascular risk, sleep care, or evaluation of cognitive symptoms.

Older adults should scale activity to health status, function, medications, and fall risk. WHO recommends aerobic activity plus strength work and, for older adults, multicomponent activity emphasizing balance and functional strength [2]. These recommendations are grounded in broad health and functional outcomes; they should be followed for that reason even if the person's cognitive-test score does not change. Clinical guidance is appropriate when symptoms, unstable disease, or functional limitations make unsupervised exercise unsafe.

### For Program Design

The best-supported default is variety and adherence rather than a purported brain-optimization formula. WHO's adult range provides a public-health baseline: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination, with muscle strengthening on at least two days [2]. A program can combine aerobic, resistance, coordination, and balance activities because they serve different physical goals and because several modalities show cognitive signals [8][14][15]. The evidence does not establish a special synergy that guarantees broader cognition, so modality choice should also consider safety, access, enjoyment, and sustained participation.

Progression matters for physical adaptation, but cognition studies do not identify one reliable optimum for intensity, duration, or weekly frequency [8][14]. The author's synthesis is to start below a dose that disrupts recovery, progress toward public-health guidance, and judge the program first by adherence and established health outcomes. Cognitive outcomes can be monitored as secondary benefits. More exercise is not automatically better for an immediate task: high exertion can create fatigue, and acute studies show heterogeneous results by timing and measure [10][11].

Resistance exercise deserves inclusion without exaggerated mechanism claims. Public-health guidance includes muscle strengthening because it serves physical health and function [2][18], and randomized syntheses report possible benefits in selected cognitive domains [14][15]. Current evidence does not support saying that it primarily improves executive function, that IGF-1 uniquely explains the effect, or that heavier loads necessarily produce greater cognitive benefit. A balanced program can use resistance training for its established physical purposes and treat cognitive change as uncertain upside.

### For Education and Learning

The 2025 umbrella review found larger pooled memory and executive-function effects in children and adolescents than in adult age groups [8]. That result supports protecting opportunities for movement, but cognitive-test effects do not by themselves prove higher grades, better instruction, or a particular school timetable. Programs that combine movement with rules, coordination, or game demands also confound physical exertion with cognitive practice [8]. Education decisions should distinguish improvement on a measured cognitive task from transfer to academic outcomes.

For self-directed learners, exercise can be paired with study when it does not displace sleep, instruction, retrieval practice, or adequate study time. A short bout may facilitate selected tasks, but the evidence does not make pre-study exercise mandatory [10][11]. The author's synthesis is to use exercise as a stable health habit and test any learning-specific timing claim against delayed recall, not only immediate alertness.

### For Reading Exercise-Cognition Claims

Four checks prevent most overstatement. First, identify whether the exposure was one bout, a training program, or habitual activity. Second, name the population and control condition. Third, separate the measured cognitive domain from broader real-world performance. Fourth, inspect whether the claim comes from one study, a primary-study meta-analysis, or an umbrella review that may count overlapping trials more than once [8][9][10].

Mechanism claims need an additional species check. Direct evidence that running increases neurogenesis and that BDNF blockade changes learning comes largely from animals [3][4]. Human blood biomarkers, genotype associations, and MRI changes are valuable but do not provide the same causal access [5][6][12][13]. A responsible explanation can connect these levels while labeling the inferential step.

The resulting decision rule is simple. Exercise because its total health case is strong; expect cognitive effects to be small on average; prefer claims tied to a defined outcome and population; and treat individual response as something to measure rather than assume [2][8][9][10]. That standard preserves the practical value of exercise without turning a heterogeneous literature into a universal brain-upgrade promise.
