# Commitment Devices Work Best When Accountability Preserves Autonomy and Failure Remains Reversible

A commitment system makes a chosen future action easier to execute by specifying cues, exposing progress, or attaching consequences before temptation and inattention arrive. Evidence supports implementation intentions, recorded progress, reminders, and some voluntary contracts, but it also shows that public pressure and financial penalties can suppress enrollment, mis-target users, or make failure needlessly costly [1][2][3][9][11][12]. The practical task is therefore not to maximize pressure; it is to match the lightest reversible device to the actual failure mode while preserving consent, privacy, safety, and an exit route.

## Background

The central problem is an intention-action gap. A person can sincerely prefer exercise, saving, focused work, or regular study when considering the future, yet choose an easier competing action when the relevant moment arrives. Economic accounts describe present-biased choice and changing intertemporal preferences; psychological accounts emphasize failures to notice an opportunity, initiate action, protect an ongoing pursuit, or update a plan after feedback [1][2]. These accounts do not imply that every missed goal is a self-control failure. A plan may be unrealistic, the environment may change, the goal may cease to be valuable, or the person may lack time, money, health, authority, or skill. A commitment device is appropriate only when the person still endorses the goal and a predictable execution failure, rather than a bad goal or an external constraint, is the governing problem.

A commitment device is an arrangement chosen now that changes the choices, costs, or social consequences faced later. Bryan, Karlan, and Nelson distinguish hard commitments, which materially restrict future action or impose an economic consequence, from soft commitments, which rely on reminders, declarations, psychological costs, or informal social expectations [1]. A locked savings account and a refundable deposit contingent on completion are hard devices. A calendar appointment, a written promise, an if-then plan, a progress log, or a scheduled check-in is usually soft. The categories describe mechanisms rather than moral quality: a hard device can be voluntary and useful, while a soft public declaration can become humiliating or coercive.

Accountability is related but not identical. A commitment device alters the future choice environment; accountability makes conduct or progress answerable to an audience, rule, or review. The audience can be the same person through a record, one trusted partner, a small group, an employer, or a public network. Progress monitoring can operate without an audience, and an accountability conversation can operate without a penalty. This separation matters because the evidence for recording progress is broader than the evidence for exposing goals publicly, and because public exposure can deter people from making commitments at all [3][9].

Implementation intentions supply one low-cost bridge from intention to action. They take an if-then form that links a specified situation to a specified response, such as, "If I finish breakfast on a weekday, then I will open the study document before opening email." A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests reported a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment and described benefits for initiating action, shielding pursuit from disruption, disengaging from failing courses, and conserving capacity for later pursuit [2]. The device does not create a valuable goal. It operationalizes an already chosen goal at a point where noticing or initiation is likely to fail.

Progress monitoring supplies a second bridge. Harkin and colleagues synthesized 138 randomized studies with 19,951 participants. Interventions increased monitoring frequency strongly and improved goal attainment by a smaller but meaningful average amount; effects were larger when progress was physically recorded, reported to another person, or made public than when monitoring remained private [3]. This result supports visibility and review, but it does not establish that maximum publicity is best for every person. The studies covered varied behaviors and interventions, and a separate randomized field experiment found that public announcements through Facebook and email elicited support while also making participants less likely to form weekly commitments [9].

The modern evidence base developed through field experiments in saving, smoking cessation, exercise, and work. A commitment savings product in the Philippines restricted access to voluntarily deposited funds until a chosen date or balance was reached. Of 710 clients offered the product, 202 opened an account, and assignment to the offer increased average savings balances after twelve months relative to control [5]. A smoking-cessation contract allowed participants to deposit money for six months and recover it after passing a biochemical test; only 11 percent of those offered the contract enrolled, while the offer increased the six-month pass rate by 3 percentage points and the effect persisted in an unannounced twelve-month test [4]. These studies show both efficacy and selection: a device can help some users while most decline it.

The record also contains reasons for restraint. A gym-attendance experiment found that commitment-contract take-up did not reliably identify the people for whom the contracts were most welfare improving; approximately half of those choosing a contract for more attendance also chose one for less attendance in a diagnostic task, and the authors' structural estimates implied lower consumer surplus than an equally behavior-changing linear subsidy [11]. In a health experiment in Malawi, appointments more than doubled HIV testing, while many users of a financial commitment forfeited their deposits; the soft appointment produced a larger effect without financial loss [12]. A once-influential 2002 deadline study was retracted in 2026 after questions about its data, and a close replication with 124 participants found negligible effects of deadline structure on the original performance measures [14][15]. The scientific lesson is not that deadlines never work, but that a famous result cannot substitute for current, context-specific evidence.

The author's synthesis is that commitment should be treated as system design, not character correction. The system begins with a valued goal, diagnoses a repeated failure point, selects a proportionate mechanism, records enough evidence to learn, and includes a safe way to revise or stop. This framing keeps the topic within applied self-management. Clinical treatment, addiction care, eating-disorder management, medication changes, and other health decisions require qualified professional support; a self-imposed contract is not a substitute for diagnosis, informed care, or emergency flexibility.

## Core Concepts

### Diagnose the failure mode before choosing the device

Different failures call for different mechanisms. Forgetting calls for a salient cue or appointment. Failing to start despite remembering calls for an implementation intention, a reduced first step, or an environmental change. Stopping too early calls for a bounded work interval, progress feedback, or a check-in. Choosing an immediate temptation over a later benefit may justify friction, a deposit, or restricted access. Abandoning a goal because the plan was wrong calls for revision rather than stronger pressure. This classification is the author's synthesis of the implementation-intention, reminder, monitoring, and commitment evidence [1][2][3][8].

A useful diagnosis names one observable moment. "I lack discipline" is not testable. "On three evenings last week, I opened entertainment before starting the planned twenty-minute practice block" identifies a cue, a competing action, and a measurement opportunity. The more specific diagnosis also reveals when no device is needed. If practice failed because the task required equipment that was unavailable, the remedy is logistical. If it failed because pain appeared, the remedy may be medical evaluation. If the goal lost value, the correct action may be deliberate disengagement rather than escalation.

The worst design starts with the strongest available penalty and searches afterward for a problem it might solve. It converts uncertainty into risk before the person knows whether the target, metric, schedule, or device is workable. The reversible order is the opposite: test a reminder or if-then plan, add private recording, add a trusted review, and only then consider restricted access or a small stake. Evidence that appointments can outperform hard financial commitments, and that commitment selection can be noisy, supports this escalation ladder [11][12].

### Implementation intentions convert goals into cue-response rules

A goal intention states a desired end: "I intend to exercise." An implementation intention specifies the execution condition and response: "If it is 7:00 on Monday, Wednesday, or Friday and I am medically able, then I will begin the ten-minute warm-up." Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis reported an average effect of d = 0.65 across 94 independent tests, although effects necessarily varied across goals, populations, and designs [2]. The mechanism is not a threat. It reduces the need to notice, decide, and negotiate at the moment of action.

An effective if-then plan has four properties. The cue is specific enough to recognize, the response is under the person's control, the first action is small enough to begin, and an exception protects safety or changed conditions. Time can be a cue, but events are often more reliable: finishing breakfast, arriving at a desk, closing a meeting, or placing a phone on charge. A plan should also address a likely derailment: "If the session is interrupted, then I will write the next action before stopping and resume at the next available block." These are design recommendations derived from the evidence, not universal clinical prescriptions [2].

Implementation intentions overlap with existing goal-setting and habit practices but do not duplicate them. Goal setting chooses and specifies the target. Habit formation builds cue-linked automaticity through repetition. A commitment system governs the period in which the action is still fragile, attention is inconsistent, and future choice may conflict with present intention. The system can therefore use an if-then plan as its first layer, then reduce its own intensity as the behavior becomes reliable.

### Soft and hard commitment differ in consequence, not seriousness

Soft devices change salience, expectations, or social meaning without imposing a material loss. Examples include a calendar appointment, a written contract with oneself, a scheduled message, website blocking that can be overridden, a visible progress chart, or a promise to report results. Hard devices restrict access, impose a penalty, or make reversal costly: a locked account, a deposit forfeited after failure, automatic donation, or software that cannot be bypassed during a defined interval [1][16]. A device can combine both, such as a deposit contract verified by a partner.

Hard commitment can produce real changes. The smoking contract, savings product, exercise experiment, and worker contract all found behavior consistent with commitment effects in their settings [4][5][6][7]. In the workplace field experiment, data-entry workers voluntarily selected contracts that penalized low output without increasing the reward for high output; the contracts increased production, and contract choice varied substantially across workers [7]. These results show that some people knowingly value constraints on their future choices.

Hardness also creates failure cost. A penalty cannot distinguish procrastination from illness, caregiving, equipment failure, an unsafe condition, or a newly discovered flaw in the goal unless the contract explicitly does so. Evidence from gym attendance and health testing shows that people can select poorly targeted contracts or forfeit money even when a softer appointment would work better [11][12]. Hard commitment should therefore be small, bounded, voluntary, and preceded by a no-stakes pilot. Money needed for food, housing, health, debt service, dependents, or emergency reserves should never be put at risk by a personal productivity device; this is an ethical design conclusion, not a tested numerical threshold.

### Accountability should supply information and support, not surveillance

Private accountability means answering to a record and a prewritten review rule. It is useful when the person needs evidence but not social pressure. A weekly review can compare planned opportunities, completed actions, obstacles, and exceptions. Because the original record is preserved, the person can distinguish execution failure from a changed plan rather than reconstructing intent after the outcome. This design compounds with decision journals and personal experiments.

Partner accountability adds an audience. A partner can witness the goal, receive a brief progress report, ask a pre-agreed question, verify a result, or help remove an obstacle. A meta-analysis of 36 samples and 10,130 people in romantic relationships found a moderate positive association between partner support and goal outcomes, with responsive and practical support positive and negative support negative; the authors also reported methodological limits and substantial heterogeneity [13]. This evidence supports supportive involvement more directly than policing. It does not prove that any friend, coach, or colleague will improve any goal.

Public accountability expands the audience and the cost of failure. Harkin and colleagues found larger average effects when monitored outcomes were reported or made public [3]. Yet Munson and colleagues found that public announcements reduced the likelihood that participants would make commitments, even though the announcements elicited supportive replies [9]. The two findings are compatible: among commitments that exist, reporting can strengthen follow-through, while anticipated exposure can deter commitment formation or induce safer, less ambitious promises. Publicity is therefore a design variable, not an automatic upgrade.

An accountability agreement should define what is shared, with whom, for how long, and for what purpose. The minimum useful report is usually the target, the observation period, the result, and the next adjustment. Detailed logs, locations, health information, financial balances, or private communications should not be shared merely because a digital tool can collect them. The author's synthesis is that privacy should default to the smallest audience capable of supplying the missing function. Expansion should require explicit renewed consent.

### Progress visibility closes the control loop

A commitment without feedback cannot show whether the device is helping. Progress monitoring creates a control loop: set a reference, observe the current state, compare, and adjust action. The Harkin meta-analysis found that interventions designed to increase monitoring improved goal attainment on average, that the change in monitoring mediated the improvement, and that physically recorded information produced larger effects [3]. A visible record can be paper, a spreadsheet, a calendar mark, a device measurement, or a short message; the important property is reliable observation, not technological sophistication.

The metric must match the goal and remain hard to game. If the objective is focused writing, time with a document open can be inflated while no usable text is produced. If the objective is health, a step count can rise while pain or recovery worsens. A robust record pairs one action measure with one quality or outcome check and one safety condition. This is the author's design synthesis, informed by evidence that external targets and rewards can redirect attention and, when controlling, undermine intrinsic motivation [10].

Structured review turns data into adaptation. A daily signal should answer only whether the planned action occurred and why not. A weekly review can ask whether the cue fired, the task was feasible, the measure represented the goal, the consequence changed behavior, and any cost appeared. A monthly or end-of-contract review can retain, weaken, replace, or remove the device. Additional reminders are not automatically better: in three bank field experiments, goal- and incentive-linked reminders improved commitment attainment, but extra late reminders did not show an additive effect [8].

### Financial and nonfinancial consequences serve different functions

A financial stake makes failure immediately costly. It can counter an immediate temptation when the future benefit is too abstract, and voluntary deposit contracts have changed smoking, saving, exercise, and work behavior in field settings [4][5][6][7]. A stake is not free motivation: it transfers loss risk to the participant, can attract people who misjudge their future behavior, and may crowd out autonomous engagement when experienced as controlling [10][11][12]. The appropriate comparison is not "stake or nothing" but "stake versus the lightest alternative likely to solve the same failure."

Nonfinancial consequences include losing access to entertainment for a short interval, adding friction to a distracting action, rescheduling a missed session, sending a factual report, performing a small recovery task, or accepting that an appointment was missed. Their advantage is reversibility and lower material harm. Their weakness is that they may be too easy to ignore. A good consequence is immediate enough to matter, small enough not to threaten welfare, and closely connected to the behavior rather than to shame or identity.

Rewards differ from commitments. A reward adds a benefit for success; a pure commitment adds a cost or restriction for failure without increasing the success payoff. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan's meta-analysis found that expected tangible, task-contingent rewards could reduce post-reward free-choice behavior and self-reported interest, while informational positive feedback could operate differently [10]. This does not mean all rewards are harmful or that intrinsic motivation exists for every task. It means designers must ask whether the consequence supplies useful information and competence, or communicates external control.

### Digital tools should implement a contract, not become the goal

Digital tools can schedule cues, block access, record activity, display trends, request verification, and automate a bounded consequence. They can also generate notification fatigue, expose sensitive data, make cancellation obscure, or optimize engagement with the tool rather than progress on the user's goal. The system should therefore remain legible: the user should be able to state the trigger, tracked data, success rule, consequence, data recipients, duration, and exit procedure in plain language.

Automation should be strongest for observation and weakest for irreversible punishment. A calendar can send a reminder automatically; a tracker can calculate a weekly count; a blocking tool can enforce a short focus interval. A large financial transfer, public failure notice, or long lockout should require a cooling-off design, a verified condition, and a safe exception. This is the author's synthesis from the evidence on mis-targeting, financial forfeiture, autonomy, and public commitment suppression [9][10][11][12].

The minimal viable commitment system has seven parts: a valued goal, one diagnosed failure mode, one observable target behavior, one cue, one record, one review schedule, and one exit rule. Accountability, stakes, and automation are optional modules. This modularity prevents a common category error: treating more features as more commitment. A reminder plus a private log may be a complete system when inattention is the problem; adding public posting and money would create cost without adding function.

## Evidence

### Planning and monitoring have the broadest experimental base

Gollwitzer and Sheeran reviewed 94 independent tests of implementation intentions and reported a medium-to-large average effect of d = 0.65 on goal attainment [2]. Their framework distinguishes goal intention from execution planning: deciding to reach an outcome does not necessarily prepare a person to notice and respond to the critical situation. The review reported effects across initiation, protection of ongoing pursuit, disengagement from failing action, and preservation of capacity, supporting if-then plans as a general execution aid rather than a single-domain trick [2]. The evidence does not imply that every cue-response plan is correct; poor cues and infeasible responses can still fail.

Harkin and colleagues provide stronger direct evidence for the monitoring layer. Their systematic search found 138 randomized studies with 19,951 participants. Monitoring interventions increased the frequency of monitoring with d = 1.98 and improved goal attainment with d = 0.40; mediation analysis linked increased monitoring to improved attainment [3]. Reporting or publicizing monitored outcomes and physically recording information were associated with larger effects [3]. Because the intervention set and goal domains were heterogeneous, the pooled effect is an average, not a guaranteed return from any app, dashboard, or accountability meeting.

The reminder evidence is narrower but useful. Karlan and colleagues ran field experiments with three banks among clients who had opened commitment savings accounts. Reminder messages increased commitment attainment; messages mentioning both the savings goal and financial incentive were especially effective, gain-versus-loss wording did not produce significantly different effects, and additional late reminders did not add a detectable benefit [8]. The result supports timely, goal-linked cues while warning against equating notification volume with effectiveness.

Partner evidence is mainly associative rather than a direct test of accountability contracts. Vowels and Carnelley synthesized 195 effects from 36 samples involving 10,130 people in romantic relationships and estimated an overall association of r = 0.25 between partner support and goal outcomes [13]. Responsive support and practical support were positive, while negative support was negative; study methods and effects were heterogeneous, and some outcome measures lacked validated instruments [13]. The responsible inference is that supportive partners can matter, not that appointing a monitor will cause improvement.

### Field experiments show that voluntary constraint can change behavior

Ashraf, Karlan, and Yin randomly offered a commitment savings product to bank clients in the Philippines. Among 710 clients offered the product, 202, or 28.4 percent, opened it; the product restricted withdrawal until a chosen date or savings amount. After twelve months, assignment to the offer increased average savings balances by 81 percentage points relative to the control group's preintervention level, according to the authors' report [5]. The design demonstrates demand and impact in a specific financial context while also showing that most eligible clients did not choose the restriction.

Gine, Karlan, and Zinman tested a six-month smoking-cessation deposit contract. Participants deposited their own funds and recovered them after passing a urine test for nicotine and cotinine; otherwise, the funds went to charity. Eleven percent of smokers offered the product enrolled, and random assignment to the offer increased the six-month pass rate by 3 percentage points relative to control, with an effect that persisted in surprise testing at twelve months [4]. The intention-to-treat result includes nonparticipants, so the small average effect and low take-up coexist with a meaningful change for some users.

Royer, Stehr, and Sydnor tested incentives and self-funded commitment contracts for company-gym attendance. A four-week incentive produced only small lasting changes, while the group offered a commitment option afterward showed significant longer-run differences detectable years later [6]. The experiment supports the possibility that a voluntary contract can extend behavior beyond an initial incentive. It does not establish that contracts are generally superior, because the participants, target, workplace gym, and preceding incentive created a specific selection and support environment.

Kaur, Kremer, and Mullainathan studied full-time data-entry workers for a year. Workers sometimes chose dominated contracts that penalized output below a self-selected target without paying more above the target, and output increased under those contracts; effort also rose as randomly assigned paydays approached [7]. The authors reported substantial worker-level heterogeneity and argued that some workplace constraints can supply self-control benefits [7]. The same heterogeneity limits universal use: a feature helpful to a worker who wants it can impose a cost on a worker who does not.

### Publicity and pressure produce mixed effects

The monitoring meta-analysis found larger effects when outcomes were reported or public [3]. In contrast, Munson and colleagues randomized a technology-supported physical-activity intervention to private weekly commitments or public announcements by Facebook and email, with one public condition also announcing prior results. Public posts elicited supportive replies, but participants in both public conditions were less likely to make commitments, leading the authors to conclude that anticipated accountability can counteract its own benefits by suppressing commitment formation [9]. The difference between conditional effectiveness and take-up is central: a system can strengthen the commitments it retains while driving away people or goals that cannot tolerate exposure.

Self-determination research explains one plausible boundary. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan synthesized 128 reward experiments and found that expected tangible task-contingent rewards tended to reduce later free-choice engagement and, more modestly, reported interest; effects depended on reward type, contingency, age, and whether feedback conveyed competence or control [10]. Accountability can carry the same distinction. A partner who helps interpret progress may support competence and relatedness, whereas surveillance, threats, and identity-based criticism can shift the activity toward controlled compliance. This mechanism is not proof about every accountability design, but it supplies a testable reason to preserve choice and informational feedback.

The 2026 deadline correction strengthens the caution. Hyndman and Bisin recruited 124 participants for a close replication of the proofreading experiment in a famous 2002 paper and found that deadline structure had negligible effects on the original performance measures; evenly spaced external deadlines did not stand out [14]. The journal retracted the original paper after the replication raised questions about the underlying data and further investigation undermined confidence in the findings [15]. A practical framework should therefore avoid repeating the old claim that evenly spaced external deadlines are proven superior. Deadlines remain a tool whose value depends on task, enforcement, autonomy, and evidence from the user's own bounded test.

### Selection and welfare are the decisive limits of hard commitment

Carrera and colleagues studied commitment-contract demand in a gym experiment with 1,248 participants. Contracts increased exercise, but take-up had little association with measured actual or perceived time inconsistency, and approximately half of those choosing a contract for higher attendance also chose one for lower attendance in a diagnostic elicitation [11]. Their structural analysis estimated that the contract offer reduced consumer surplus and was less efficient than linear exercise subsidies producing the same average behavior change [11]. The result demonstrates that voluntary choice alone does not guarantee accurate self-targeting or welfare improvement.

Derksen and colleagues directly compared financial commitments with appointments for HIV testing in Malawi. Participants were randomly assigned to control, hard commitment, appointment, or both. Appointments had higher take-up, more than doubled testing relative to control, and caused no deposit loss; many men taking the hard commitment forfeited their money, and combining the devices added little beyond the appointment [12]. The authors note that the context was specific and that appointments may work through planning, reminders, and social expectation [12]. Even with that limitation, the comparison establishes an important design benchmark: a soft device can solve several execution failures while avoiding the principal harm of a hard one.

Taken together, the evidence rejects a one-dimensional scale from weak to strong. Implementation intentions solve cueing and initiation [2]. Reminders solve limited attention in some contexts [8]. Recorded progress supplies feedback [3]. Supportive partners can contribute practical and emotional resources [13]. Public reporting can strengthen some existing commitments while suppressing formation [3][9]. Financial stakes can change behavior but also select poorly and impose loss [4][11][12]. The author's assessment is that effectiveness must be evaluated jointly with take-up, burden, false verification, gaming, spillovers, and the cost of failure.

## Implications

### Build from the least coercive effective layer

For an individual, the first step is to write the goal and the reason it remains worth pursuing. The second is to identify the repeated failure moment using recent observations rather than a personality label. The third is to choose the smallest mechanism that addresses that moment: an if-then plan for initiation, a reminder for forgetting, friction for temptation, a visible record for feedback, or a check-in for support. This sequence is the author's synthesis of the planning, monitoring, reminder, and commitment evidence [1][2][3][8].

Run the first version without money or public exposure. A one- or two-week pilot can test whether the cue occurs, the target is feasible, the record is accurate, and the device creates an unintended cost. The review should preserve three legitimate outcomes: keep the system, redesign it, or stop because the goal or method is wrong. Treating stop as legitimate prevents a commitment device from becoming an escalation device. A device has succeeded when it improves chosen action at acceptable cost, not when it makes exit impossible.

If the pilot shows that attention and planning are sufficient, do not escalate. If the person still chooses a predictable immediate temptation, add reversible friction: log out, move the distracting device, delay access for a short period, or pre-position the desired materials. If private evidence is insufficient, add one trusted person and a minimal report. Consider a financial stake only after softer options fail, the target is under the person's control, the measure is hard to game, exceptions are specified, and the maximum loss is genuinely disposable. Evidence that appointments can outperform deposits and that contract choice can reduce welfare makes this conservative order rational [11][12].

### Define accountability as a protocol

An accountability partner should not be assigned the vague job of "making me do it." The parties should agree on the target behavior, reporting interval, evidence, response to a miss, confidentiality, duration, and exit condition. A useful check-in asks: What happened? What obstacle was present? What is the next adjustment? It does not infer laziness, demand private details unrelated to the target, or enlarge consequences after failure. Partner-support evidence favors responsive and practical support over negative involvement, although causal and measurement limits remain [13].

Private and public commitment should be distinguished explicitly. Private reporting to one person offers an audience with limited exposure. Group reporting can normalize effort and supply examples but also creates comparison pressure. Public posting creates durable reputational and privacy costs and may deter commitment formation [9]. The default should be private; wider sharing should serve a named function that cannot be achieved by a smaller audience. Consent to share a goal is not permanent consent to publish every result.

The partner must also have an exit. A system that turns a friend, spouse, or colleague into a permanent monitor can damage both the goal and the relationship. Set an end date and a brief renewal decision. Rotate from verification to support as reliability improves. If conflict rises, reduce or remove the accountability layer rather than interpreting conflict as proof that stronger enforcement is needed. This is an ethical design inference consistent with the negative-support findings and the autonomy evidence [10][13].

### Make consequences proportionate, recoverable, and non-humiliating

A good consequence is close to the behavior, capped in advance, and safe under foreseeable exceptions. A missed writing block might trigger rescheduling and a short factual report. A failed distraction limit might lengthen the next block by a small amount, not lock the person out of essential communication. A savings device should preserve emergency access or exclude emergency funds. A health behavior should never attach a penalty to symptoms, injury, treatment advice, or circumstances that require professional judgment.

Financial deposits require special safeguards. Use a small amount that cannot impair basic needs, a short contract, a verified and controllable behavior, explicit medical and emergency exceptions, and a cooling-off period before activation. Do not increase a stake after a miss. Do not allow a platform or accountability partner to benefit from failure, because that creates an incentive conflict. These are normative safeguards derived from the observed forfeiture, mis-targeting, and welfare results [11][12], not effect-size thresholds established by those studies.

Humiliation is not an acceptable penalty. Public failure posts, anti-identity messages, threats to relationships, and disclosures of private health or financial information can create costs far beyond the target behavior. Munson and colleagues show that anticipated publicity can reduce commitment formation [9], while self-determination evidence shows that controlling contingencies can reduce autonomous interest [10]. The universal design test is simple: the person should be able to fail, learn, and revise without losing dignity, essential resources, or control over unrelated parts of life.

### Design digital tools around data minimization and exit rights

A digital commitment tool should display its contract before activation: what event triggers the system, what data are collected, how success is calculated, who receives information, what consequence follows, how long data are retained, and how the user pauses or exits. Defaults should minimize collection and audience. A local calendar mark may be enough; location history, continuous sensor data, contacts, or message content should not be collected unless they are necessary for the chosen function.

Verification should be commensurate with stakes. Self-report can be enough for a private experiment with no external consequence. A modest deposit may require a timestamped record or partner confirmation. High-stakes automatic enforcement should not rely on a fragile sensor, an opaque algorithm, or a single missed synchronization. Because the evidence shows selection errors and costly commitment failure, ambiguous data should default to review rather than punishment [11][12].

Notification policy should be bounded. Start with one cue tied to a known opportunity and one review reminder. Measure whether the message arrives when action is possible and whether it changes behavior. Karlan and colleagues found that content tied to the goal and incentive mattered, while extra late reminders did not add a detectable effect [8]. More notifications can become background noise; the system should remove ineffective cues rather than accumulate them.

Digital systems should include a sunset. At the end of the agreed period, export or summarize the evidence, delete unnecessary personal data, and ask whether the user wants to renew. Renewal should not be automatic when money, publication, or access restriction is involved. The tool should make success possible without indefinite dependence on itself; otherwise, engagement with the platform can replace progress on the user's goal.

### Use a reversible seven-step design process

The author's synthesis is a seven-step process:

1. State the valued outcome and confirm that it remains voluntary.
2. Identify one repeated failure mode from recent evidence.
3. Define one controllable target behavior and one safety boundary.
4. Choose the lightest matching device and write an if-then plan.
5. Record the action with the smallest sufficient data set.
6. Review after a short pilot using prewritten keep, change, and stop rules.
7. Escalate only one layer at a time, with a capped cost and explicit exit.

The process is reversible by construction. A reminder can be changed without losing money. A private record can precede a social report. A partner can be added for a defined interval. A stake can be tested at trivial size before any meaningful amount is exposed. This ordering turns each layer into an experiment rather than a moral verdict. It also produces evidence about whether the actual bottleneck was attention, initiation, temptation, feedback, support, or an unrealistic plan.

Evaluation should separate four outcomes. First, did the target action occur? Second, did the broader valued outcome improve? Third, what burden or harm appeared? Fourth, would the person freely choose the same system again with current knowledge? A system that raises a count while degrading quality, health, relationships, or autonomy fails even if the count improves. A system that is effective only because the person fears humiliation also fails the autonomy and reversibility tests.

Use a prewritten review to limit gaming. Define the target and evidence before the observation period, preserve misses, and record exceptions rather than deleting them. If the metric becomes the object of optimization, add a quality check or replace it. If verification consumes more time than the behavior, simplify. If the person repeatedly changes the rule after outcomes, the next cycle should use a shorter period and a more observable target. These safeguards apply the same logic as a personal N-of-1 experiment: bounded question, observable comparison, explicit decision rule, and an inconclusive outcome that remains acceptable.

### Distinguish self-management from treatment and control over others

Commitment devices in self-improvement are self-chosen aids for ordinary goal pursuit. They are not treatment for addiction, depression, anxiety, eating disorders, attention disorders, or other clinical conditions. They do not authorize withholding medication, restricting food, risking injury, exposing confidential information, or overriding professional advice. Evidence from health-related contracts shows that devices can affect behavior, but it also shows low take-up, forfeiture, and context dependence [4][12]. Safety takes priority over contract completion.

A self-commitment system also does not justify imposing penalties on another person. Employer, teacher, parent, partner, or platform control changes voluntariness and power. The fact that some workers voluntarily choose a contract does not establish that an employer should impose it on everyone; Kaur and colleagues explicitly identify heterogeneity and potential costs to people without the relevant self-control problem [7]. The same categorical distinction applies at home: support requested by the goal owner is different from surveillance chosen by the observer.

The final principle is proportionality. Use planning when planning is enough, visibility when feedback is missing, support when isolation is the obstacle, and constraint only when a voluntary bounded constraint solves a demonstrated problem better than safer alternatives. The evidence supports commitment as a useful family of tools, not as a universal cure [1][3][11][12]. A well-designed system increases the probability of chosen action while preserving the person's capacity to reconsider, recover, and leave.
