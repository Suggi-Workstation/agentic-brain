# Deliberate Practice -- How Focused Training Improves Skill, and What It Cannot Explain

Deliberate practice is a demanding form of training organized around a specific performance weakness, an attainable next goal, repeated attempts, informative feedback, and revision. In its strictest formulation, a qualified teacher or coach selects individualized tasks and helps the learner interpret errors; broader formulations also include self-designed, improvement-focused practice. This difference is not semantic trivia: studies that count all structured training answer a different question from studies that count only individualized, supervised work [1][8][9][10].

The practical value of the idea is narrower and stronger than the popular slogan attached to it. Repeating a familiar activity, performing for an audience, or accumulating years of experience does not guarantee continued improvement. Practice works better when it converts an observed error into a task that can be attempted, measured, corrected, and attempted again [1][2]. But there is no universal 10,000-hour threshold, and accumulated practice time does not by itself explain all differences between performers. Current evidence supports practice as an important, modifiable contributor to skill while also implicating opportunity, instruction, task design, cognitive and physical characteristics, motivation, and genetic and environmental interplay [4][5][7][8][9][10][11].

## Background

The deliberate-practice framework grew out of research on a basic problem in expertise: experience and performance are not the same variable. People often improve rapidly when learning a task, then stabilize at a level that is adequate for ordinary work or recreation. Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Romer argued that this plateau need not be a fixed biological ceiling. Their 1993 review assembled laboratory and field evidence showing that experienced performers can improve when training is redesigned around explicit improvement rather than routine execution [1]. A later medical overview made the same distinction: professional reputation and years of experience correlated only weakly with observed performance, whereas improvement-oriented training used focused tasks, feedback, problem solving, evaluation, and repetition [2]. These sources support the claim that structured improvement can continue after routine experience stops producing visible gains; they do not establish that every plateau can be overcome or that practice erases all individual differences.

The pivotal field study in the 1993 paper examined violin students at the Music Academy of West Berlin. Faculty identified three groups of 10 students: those judged to have international-soloist potential, other good violinists in the performance department, and students in music education. Participants reconstructed weekly hours of solitary violin practice for each year of their development. The reported age-18 group means were 7,410 hours for the best group, 5,301 for the good group, and 3,420 for the less accomplished group. The study also used one-week diaries and activity ratings; students rated practice alone as highly relevant to improvement and effortful relative to many other activities [1][8]. These were retrospective, observational group comparisons based on small samples, not random assignment to different amounts or forms of practice. They showed an association between estimated practice histories and faculty-defined skill groups, not a universal dose-response law.

The original framework distinguished deliberate practice from both work and play. Work aims at a product, service, performance, or external reward; play is done primarily for enjoyment; deliberate practice is undertaken specifically to improve performance. Ericsson and colleagues described effective tasks as understandable at the learner's current level, repeatedly executable, and paired with informative knowledge of results. They also emphasized motivational, resource, and effort constraints: improvement-oriented work is demanding, access to instruction can be costly, and the amount that can be sustained is limited [1]. In the strict version later stated by Ericsson and Harwell, a qualified teacher evaluates the trainee, assigns individualized goals and methods, the trainee practices with concentration and feedback, and the next lesson evaluates the result [9].

Terminology changed across the literature. Ericsson and Pool later used naive practice for repetition that merely expects improvement, purposeful practice for self-directed work with specific goals and feedback, and deliberate practice for purposeful practice informed by an established training system and a qualified teacher. Hambrick, Macnamara, and Oswald documented that Ericsson's publications did not always use the teacher, solitude, and supervision criteria consistently [10]. Macnamara and Maitra separately measured practice alone and teacher-designed practice in their violin replication; the two measures were correlated, but teacher-designed hours did not predict group differences better than practice alone [8]. A careful reader should therefore ask how each study operationalized practice rather than treating every use of the label as equivalent.

This history also explains the 10,000-hour misconception. The original violin report supplied group averages, not a minimum threshold, and the age-18 mean for its best students was 7,410 hours [1][8]. By age 20, reported averages in the original work were near 10,000 hours for the best students, but individual estimates varied and the study did not test whether 10,000 hours was necessary or sufficient for expertise. Ericsson and Harwell explicitly rejected a magical number and noted that different domains, performance levels, training systems, and individuals require different amounts and kinds of work [9]. The durable conclusion is that advanced performance usually reflects extended preparation; the unsupported conclusion is that any person reaches mastery after the same number of hours.

## Core Concepts

### 1. Define a reproducible target

A practice cycle begins with a performance that can be observed repeatedly under comparable conditions. A vague aim such as "become better at writing" cannot tell the learner which behavior changed. A usable target specifies a task, an output, a scoring rule, and a time or difficulty condition: for example, diagnose ten calibrated cases, execute a passage at a stated tempo with a recorded error count, solve a defined class of positions, or explain a mechanism without notes and then score omitted causal links. Ericsson and Harwell argued that evidence about practice is interpretable only when attained performance is reproducibly measurable rather than inferred from reputation, years in a field, or broad group labels [9].

The target must also be narrow enough to connect error to correction. If a score combines many components, decompose it: timing versus pitch, calculation versus pattern recognition, factual recall versus causal explanation, or decision quality versus execution. This follows the original framework's requirement that a task be designed around the learner's existing knowledge and that results be available soon enough to guide the next attempt [1]. The target is not a permanent definition of excellence. It is the next measurable bottleneck within a larger skill.

### 2. Diagnose the limiting error

Practice should start from evidence of failure, not from a preferred exercise. Compare an attempt with a criterion, expert model, answer key, instrument trace, video, or other reliable record. Classify the error before selecting the drill. A chess loss caused by a missed tactical relation calls for different practice from one caused by poor time allocation; a laboratory explanation that omits the rate-limiting step calls for different work from one that confuses units. The original theory emphasized immediate informative feedback and repeated performance because an error must be visible before it can guide modification [1].

A coach is valuable when the learner cannot yet perceive the relevant difference. In the strict model, the teacher identifies an attainable improvement, assigns a method, and later evaluates whether the trainee reached it [9]. A learner working alone can approximate this loop when the domain supplies trusted examples, objective tests, recording tools, or delayed expert review. That is better described as purposeful practice unless it satisfies the stricter coaching and established-method criteria [9][10]. The label matters less for implementation than the audit trail: what failed, what evidence showed the failure, what task was chosen, and what changed.

### 3. Select a task just beyond reliable execution

An effective task isolates the diagnosed weakness while remaining solvable with focused effort. Work that is already automatic supplies too little error information; work far beyond current knowledge can produce failure without indicating a tractable correction. Ericsson et al. described practice tasks as adapted to preexisting knowledge, briefly instructable, repeatedly executable, and paired with knowledge of results [1]. "Outside the comfort zone" is therefore not a license to maximize difficulty. The operative standard is a challenge whose failure modes can be distinguished and corrected.

Adjust only enough variables to expose the targeted component. Reduce speed, shorten the sequence, remove distracting context, or use representative cases selected for one discrimination. Once the component is reliable, restore speed, length, uncertainty, and context. This staged progression is a practical inference from the task-design and feedback conditions in the deliberate-practice framework [1][9]. It should be validated against the real performance measure: improvement on an artificial drill is useful only if it transfers to the target task.

### 4. Attempt, obtain feedback, and revise

The core loop is short:

1. State the target behavior and success criterion.
2. Make one or a small number of fully attended attempts.
3. Record the output and compare it with the criterion.
4. Identify one consequential discrepancy.
5. Apply a specific correction or strategy.
6. Repeat a comparable attempt.
7. Test the corrected component in a more representative task.

This loop embodies the common elements in Ericsson's formulations: a specific improvement goal, concentrated execution, informative feedback, problem solving, and repetition [1][2][9]. Feedback is useful only when it changes the next action. "Wrong" is an outcome, not a diagnosis; "the denominator excludes dropouts" or "the third interval is consistently sharp" identifies a revisable feature. When feedback is delayed, preserve the attempt through notes, code, audio, video, or another record so that the diagnosis refers to actual behavior rather than memory.

Do not turn repetition into the objective. Once errors cease to be diagnostic, change the task: increase precision, add representative variation, shorten the response time, combine components, or test transfer. Conversely, if errors are chaotic, reduce complexity. The session should end with evidence about the next constraint, not merely an elapsed-hour total. This interpretation follows from the framework's emphasis on task quality and feedback, and from later criticism that total duration aggregates activities of different quality [9][10].

### 5. Build and test mental representations

Expert performance is domain-specific. Ericsson et al. summarized evidence that experts' superior memory for meaningful material from their field falls sharply when that material is randomly rearranged or removed from the domain [1]. The implication is not a general memory upgrade but acquired structures that organize relevant patterns, actions, and consequences. Ericsson and Harwell described these representations as supporting planning, execution, monitoring, and evaluation during music practice [9].

Practice develops a representation when the learner must predict before observing the answer, compare the prediction with the result, and revise the model that generated it. A chess player can state candidate moves and consequences before consulting an engine; a chemist can predict the direction and rate-limiting step before reading the result; a writer can mark the claim, warrant, and evidence in a paragraph before comparing it with an expert revision. These examples are applications, not evidence that one universal representation format exists. The representation must be judged by whether it improves reliable performance in that domain.

Serious solitary study provides one illustration. In two samples of tournament-rated chess players, Charness and colleagues collected retrospective histories of study, tournament play, and instruction. Log cumulative hours of serious study alone correlated with current rating at 0.54 and 0.48 and was the strongest measured predictor in both samples; the combined activity models explained about one third to two fifths of rating variance. The authors explicitly cautioned that their regressions were descriptive and did not establish causation [3]. The useful lesson is that analysis and reconstruction outside competition can be strongly associated with skill, not that solitary study is the only route or that its hours determine rating.

### 6. Separate acquisition, performance, and maintenance

Training to acquire a component is not the same activity as performing the integrated skill under real conditions. Competition, concerts, production work, or examinations sample integrated performance and expose weaknesses, but their rules often prevent immediate repetition of the same problem. Focused drills permit correction but may omit pressure, timing, coordination, or context. A complete system alternates the two: representative performance generates error data, focused practice changes a component, and another representative test checks transfer [1][3].

Maintenance is also distinct from acquisition. Hours used to preserve a current level should not automatically be interpreted as hours that produced that level. Ericsson and Harwell highlighted this problem when interpreting accumulated career totals, especially where older experts practiced to resist decline rather than acquire new skill [9]. A useful practice log therefore records the goal of each block -- acquire, correct, integrate, or maintain -- instead of treating all time as interchangeable.

### 7. Manage effort and recovery without inventing a universal quota

The 1993 violinists organized much of their solitary practice in the morning and afternoon and treated it as effortful; the paper argued that sustained deliberate practice is constrained by attention and recovery [1]. That evidence does not justify a universal four-hour daily ceiling, a mandatory 60-to-90-minute block, or one ideal rest schedule for every skill. Physical load, injury risk, cognitive demand, expertise, age, and task structure differ across domains [1][9].

Use performance quality to set the dose. Stop or simplify when error diagnosis degrades, technique becomes unsafe, or successive trials cease to test the intended component. Resume after recovery and compare the resulting quality with longer or denser schedules. Record focused minutes, but judge a block by valid attempts and corrected errors. This makes workload an empirical variable in the learner's system rather than a slogan imported from one elite sample.

### 8. Interpret hours and correlations cautiously

Accumulated time is an exposure measure, not a mechanism. Two learners can report equal hours while receiving different instruction, choosing different tasks, making different numbers of valid attempts, or allocating different shares to maintenance and performance. Retrospective totals also contain recall error. In both the original violin study and its preregistered replication, weekly estimates exceeded diary-recorded practice; Macnamara and Maitra reported that this overestimation did not differ significantly among groups, but the discrepancy still cautions against treating reconstructed lifetime hours as exact [8]. Ericsson and Harwell likewise reviewed declining reliability for distant practice histories and recommended richer records of goals, concentration, methods, and feedback [9].

A between-person correlation also answers a different question from a within-person learning curve. Training can improve every participant while accumulated hours explain only part of why participants differ from one another. Hambrick et al. illustrated this distinction directly: a positive within-person effect of training does not logically require a large between-person practice-performance correlation [10]. Observational associations can additionally reflect selection, access to coaching, persistence, prior ability, or the tendency of stronger performers to seek more practice. Causal claims require designs that track change, manipulate training where ethical and feasible, or otherwise address these alternatives.

### 9. Use the 10,000-hour figure only as history

The original violin evidence does not define a threshold. At age 18, the reported means were 7,410, 5,301, and 3,420 hours across the three student groups [8]. The 2019 replication found age-18 means of 8,224 hours for its best group, 9,844 for its good group, and 4,558 for its less accomplished group. The best and good groups did not differ significantly; individual ranges overlapped widely, and both more accomplished groups averaged above 10,000 hours only by age 20 [8]. These data refute a simple rule in which crossing one hour total produces expertise or in which the highest group must always have the most hours.

The number also ignores the attained standard. Conservatory admission, professional employment, national ranking, and international dominance are not interchangeable outcomes. Ericsson and Harwell stated that the original work showed extended preparation but no magical number, and they estimated that success at the highest levels of piano competition could require far more than 10,000 hours [9]. The scientifically defensible questions are: which performance, which learner population, which activities, which measurement method, and which uncertainty interval?

### 10. Treat expertise as multifactorial

Practice is modifiable and often indispensable to improvement, but it is not an isolated cause. Broad and narrow meta-analyses, replication work, and behavioral-genetic evidence disagree about the magnitude assigned to practice, partly because they use different definitions, samples, outcomes, and reliability corrections [4][5][6][7][8][9][10][11]. The disagreement should not be collapsed into either "practice explains everything" or "practice does not matter." The evidence consistently permits large improvements through training while leaving substantial between-person variation unexplained.

Opportunity determines who can obtain time, equipment, health care, feedback, and qualified instruction. Motivation and persistence affect whether difficult work continues. Cognitive, perceptual, and physical characteristics affect both starting performance and learning rate. Genetic influences are probabilistic and operate through development and environments; heritability is not immutability and does not show that training is futile [10][11]. Practice itself can be selected partly because a person performs well or enjoys the activity. A realistic model therefore asks how training, learner characteristics, and environments interact over time.

## Evidence

**Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Romer (1993).** This theoretical review contained two empirical music studies. Study 1 compared three faculty-selected groups of 10 violin students and collected interviews, retrospective weekly practice estimates, activity ratings, and one-week diaries. Reported accumulated practice-alone means to age 18 were 7,410, 5,301, and 3,420 hours for the best, good, and less accomplished groups. Study 2 compared young professional and amateur pianists and again related reconstructed histories of solitary practice to attained group level. The paper also synthesized laboratory learning research to specify task design, feedback, repetition, effort, and resource constraints [1]. Its strength was a testable account of how routine experience differs from improvement-focused work. Its limits were small selected samples, retrospective self-report, group-level performance classifications, and observational comparisons. It supported a substantial practice-performance association; it did not establish a fixed hour threshold or eliminate alternative contributors.

**Charness et al. (2005).** Two international samples of tournament-rated chess players reported serious study, tournament play, and instruction histories. The samples initially contained 239 and 180 participants; listwise regression samples were smaller. Serious study alone correlated with current rating at 0.54 and 0.48. Six activity variables explained 41% and 31% of current-rating variance in the two samples, and serious study was the strongest measured predictor. In a combined sample of 375, serious study, private lessons, and current study and tournament-play measures contributed to rating prediction [3]. The study extended the practice association beyond music and used an objective tournament rating, but relied on retrospective exposure estimates. Its own authors said the regressions were descriptive and could not establish causal relations.

**Macnamara, Hambrick, and Oswald (2014; corrected 2018).** This random-effects meta-analysis aggregated 88 studies and 157 effect sizes spanning games, music, sports, education, and professions. After the published corrigendum, estimated performance variance associated with broadly defined deliberate practice was 14% overall, 24% in games, 23% in music, 20% in sports, 5% in education, and 1% in professions [4][5]. The estimates were larger when practice and performance were measured less precisely and smaller in more predictable contexts. These percentages are squared meta-analytic correlations, not percentages of any individual's skill caused by practice. The broad inclusion rule -- structured activities created to improve performance -- increased coverage but mixed activities that the strict Ericsson definition would classify as deliberate, purposeful, or merely structured practice [4][9].

**Platz et al. (2014).** A music-specific meta-analysis selected 13 studies with 788 participants, accumulated task-relevant practice, and objectively assessed musical achievement. It reported a correlation corrected for attenuation of 0.61, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.54 to 0.67 [6]. That estimate implies about 37% shared variance before any causal interpretation. The authors' stricter music criteria and reliability correction help explain why their estimate exceeded the 23% music estimate in the corrected broad-domain meta-analysis [4][5][6]. The comparison shows that effect size depends on study eligibility, outcome quality, and correction assumptions; it does not identify one definition-independent percentage.

**Macnamara, Moreau, and Hambrick (2016).** This sports meta-analysis included 34 studies, 52 independent samples, 63 effect sizes, and 2,765 participants. The mean practice-performance correlation was 0.43, corresponding to 18% of observed variance, with substantial heterogeneity. Estimates were 19% in subelite adult samples, 29% in mixed samples, and a nonsignificant 1% in elite samples. Practice alone accounted for 22%, not significantly different from practice that was not specified as solitary. Published studies produced larger estimates than unpublished studies, and higher-skill athletes did not start significantly earlier on average [7]. The study demonstrates both a positive overall association and strong dependence on sampling and measurement. Range restriction among elites and broad definitions limit the interpretation of the 1% result.

**Macnamara and Maitra (2019).** This preregistered, double-blind replication recruited 13 best, 13 good, and 13 less accomplished violin students. It repeated the original practice-alone measure and separately measured teacher-designed practice. At age 18, practice-alone means were 8,224, 9,844, and 4,558 hours. The best group had not practiced significantly more than the good group; the good group had practiced significantly more than the less accomplished group. Practice-alone group differences accounted for 26% of variance and teacher-designed practice for 23%; current weekly estimates exceeded diary records [8]. The replication preserved a substantial association across broad skill levels but did not reproduce complete ordering among the two highest groups. It also found no advantage for teacher-designed hours over practice-alone hours as a predictor.

**Ericsson and Harwell (2019) and Hambrick, Macnamara, and Oswald (2020).** These papers expose the central measurement dispute. Ericsson and Harwell retained 14 effects from the broader meta-analytic dataset that they judged to measure purposeful or deliberate practice and reproducible performance. Their observed mean correlation was 0.54, or approximately 29% variance; after correcting for assumed unreliability, they reported 61% [9]. Hambrick et al. argued that definitions and inclusion standards had shifted and showed that the corrected estimate varied materially with assumed reliabilities. They also noted that, within Ericsson and Harwell's selected effects, correlations for deliberate practice and purposeful practice were 0.56 and 0.51 and did not differ significantly [10]. The observed 29% and reliability-adjusted 61% are therefore not contradictory measurements of one settled quantity; they are estimates produced by different selection and correction decisions.

**Mosing et al. (2024).** A Swedish twin study analyzed 6,471 adults, including 1,302 complete twin pairs. Musical expertise was operationalized as pitch, melody, and rhythm discrimination; lifetime practice came from retrospective weekly categories across age periods. The observed practice-aptitude correlation was 0.36. A classical twin model estimated 50% of variance in auditory-discrimination performance as genetic, 22% as shared environmental, and 28% as residual. As reported practice increased, total variance declined mainly through lower residual variance while the absolute genetic component remained relatively stable, so proportional heritability increased rather than decreased [11]. The study directly challenges a prediction that genetic influence should approach zero with practice, but it measured auditory discrimination rather than complete instrumental performance and cannot be generalized without qualification to every domain.

Across these studies, three conclusions survive methodological disagreement. First, focused training and accumulated improvement-oriented practice are positively associated with performance, and controlled training can produce large within-person gains [1][2][3][9][10]. Second, an hour total is an incomplete measure because definitions, feedback quality, task selection, maintenance, recall, and performance measures vary [8][9][10]. Third, practice does not provide a complete, universal explanation of between-person expertise differences; current evidence favors models that include training, abilities, opportunity, development, and gene-environment interplay [7][8][10][11].

## Implications

### Design a practice system, not an hour target

Start with a representative baseline and retain the artifact. Define one outcome that can be scored consistently. Break the observed failure into components and select the smallest trainable bottleneck. Create a short drill that produces several comparable attempts and immediate evidence. After correction, return to the representative task to test transfer. This sequence operationalizes the task, feedback, repetition, and teacher-evaluation conditions in the framework [1][2][9].

A minimal record can contain six fields:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Target | What exact performance should change? |
| Baseline | What result and error pattern were observed? |
| Drill | Which task isolates the limiting component? |
| Feedback | What trusted comparison reveals the error? |
| Revision | What will change on the next attempt? |
| Transfer | Did the change improve the representative task? |

Add focused minutes only after these fields. Time is useful for workload planning and longitudinal comparison, but it should not replace evidence of what was attempted or learned. Separate acquisition, integration, performance, and maintenance blocks because the same hour count can serve different functions [9].

### Use coaching where diagnosis exceeds self-observation

A qualified coach can compress the search for a tractable weakness, choose a task matched to current skill, and interpret errors that the learner cannot yet perceive. This is the central advantage asserted by the strict deliberate-practice model [9]. Coaching quality should be judged by whether assigned tasks produce measurable corrections and transfer, not by the coach's status alone. When coaching is unavailable, use objective scoring, recordings, worked examples, peer review, or periodic expert audits. Call this purposeful practice if it lacks the strict individualized teacher system; do not inflate the evidence by treating labels as interchangeable [9][10].

### Preserve desirable difficulty without uncontrolled failure

Raise challenge along one dimension at a time: precision, speed, variation, sequence length, interference, or realism. If performance is perfect across repeated trials, increase the demand or test transfer. If errors cannot be classified, reduce complexity or add instruction. There is no evidence here for a universal target such as 80% or 85% success across all skills. The correct difficulty is the level that continues to generate interpretable errors while permitting stable execution of the components not being trained [1].

### Audit feedback quality

Feedback must be accurate, timely enough to affect a later attempt, and specific enough to select a correction. Automated metrics can be precise but invalid if they reward a proxy rather than the target. Human feedback can be rich but inconsistent. Calibrate both against a representative criterion. In high-stakes domains, use multiple cases or raters and track whether corrections generalize. The framework's central claim is not that any feedback works; it is that informative knowledge of results supports focused problem solving and repeated improvement [1][2].

### Track quality, not only duration

For each practice block, record valid attempts, error class, correction, transfer result, concentration failures, and physical or cognitive fatigue. Periodically compare blocks with equal time but different designs. This creates local evidence about which tasks and feedback arrangements work for the learner. It also addresses a major research limitation: retrospective totals cannot recover detailed goals, methods, or attention years later [8][9].

Do not optimize for maximal discomfort. Effort is a constraint and often a sign that the task is not automatic, but strain without diagnostic learning is waste. Stop when attention no longer supports the required discrimination or when physical technique becomes unsafe. The original work treated motivation, resources, and recovery as limits on sustained training, not as proof that suffering itself causes expertise [1].

### Make forecasts probabilistic

Practice history should change expectations, not determine them. A learner who repeatedly improves on valid transfer tests has evidence that the present system is working. A plateau should trigger diagnosis of task design, feedback, prerequisite knowledge, recovery, opportunity, and fit before it is labeled an immutable limit. At the same time, no coach or program can honestly guarantee world-class attainment after a fixed number of hours. Group correlations leave wide individual uncertainty, and observational practice studies do not isolate every causal factor [3][7][8][10][11].

For selection and investment decisions, separate two questions. "Will this person improve with well-designed training?" is a within-person question and is often testable with a learning trial. "Will this person exceed other highly trained performers?" is a between-person forecast requiring evidence about learning rate, current performance, health, opportunity, motivation, and the relevant domain. Confusing the questions is one reason the 10,000-hour slogan misleads.

### Apply the framework beyond established domains with caution

Strict deliberate practice is easiest to identify where a domain has stable rules, reproducible performance, expert teachers, and an accumulated training curriculum, as in classical instrumental music [9]. In newer or less standardized fields, the "best" method may be unknown and performance may be multidimensional or delayed. Use purposeful experimentation: define outcomes, preserve predictions, collect feedback, and update tasks. Do not claim strict deliberate practice when the domain lacks the teacher knowledge or reproducible measure required by the definition.

In knowledge work, representative outcomes may be decisions, forecasts, explanations, code behavior, experimental designs, or written arguments. A researcher can predict a result before reading it, compare the causal model with the evidence, and rehearse the step that failed. A manager can score decisions against predeclared criteria rather than business outcomes alone. A writer can compare claim-evidence structure with an expert edit. These are applications derived from the practice loop; their effectiveness must be tested against the actual work product rather than assumed from analogy.

### Use a multifactorial review when progress stalls

If repeated, well-measured cycles do not improve transfer, inspect four classes of causes:

1. **Training design:** Is the task representative, specific, and paired with valid feedback?
2. **Prerequisites and capacities:** Is a component missing, overloaded, injured, or otherwise constraining execution?
3. **Environment:** Are time, equipment, instruction, safety, and opportunity adequate?
4. **Selection and motivation:** Is the learner choosing practice that protects identity or enjoyment rather than exposing the limiting error?

This review is consistent with evidence that expertise reflects multiple interacting influences [10][11]. It avoids two symmetrical errors: declaring a person incapable after a poor training design, and promising that one training design can erase every difference. The ethical and useful claim is that performance is often modifiable and the modification should be measured.
