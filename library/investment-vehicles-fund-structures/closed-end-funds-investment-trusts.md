# Closed-End Funds and Investment Trusts -- Why a Fixed Share Count Creates Persistent Discounts, Activist Arbitrage, and a Distinct Capital-Permanence Trade-off

Closed-end funds (CEFs) and investment trusts are pooled investment
vehicles that issue a fixed number of shares at inception and then
trade those shares on a secondary exchange, with no continuous
creation or redemption at net asset value (NAV). This structural
feature -- a closed capital base -- produces the field's defining
phenomena: market prices that persistently deviate from NAV (the
discount-premium puzzle), the ability to employ leverage and hold
illiquid assets without redemption risk, and an activist arbitrage
industry that buys discounted shares and forces convergence events.
The closed-end structure is the oldest form of collective investment,
originating in British investment trusts of 1868, and it embodies a
fundamental trade-off between capital permanence and price efficiency
that open-end funds and ETFs were specifically designed to eliminate.

## Background

The closed-end investment vehicle is the oldest surviving form of
collective investment. Its origin traces to the Foreign and Colonial
Government Trust, founded in London in 1868 by Philip Rose, financial
adviser to Benjamin Disraeli. The stated purpose was to bring stock
market investing to those of moderate means, giving the investor of
moderate means the same advantage as the large capitalist. The trust
issued a fixed number of shares and invested the pooled capital in a
diversified portfolio of government bonds, offering small investors
diversification that would have been impractical to assemble
individually.

The concept spread rapidly. In February 1873, Robert Fleming launched
the Scottish American Investment Trust in Dundee, investing in the
railroads of the growing United States. The 1880s saw the founding of
Alliance Trust (1888), the Scottish Investment Trust (1887), and
numerous others. These early investment trusts were public limited
companies listed on the London Stock Exchange, a structure that gave
shareholders governance rights and created the closed-ended character
that defines the vehicle to this day: the fund managers cannot create
or redeem shares without explicit shareholder approval. By the late
nineteenth century, British investment trusts had grown into a
substantial sector, channeling capital from British investors into
global infrastructure, colonial development, and emerging market
securities. The Association of Investment Companies (AIC) records
that the sector has continued for over 150 years, with approximately
335 investment trusts and a collective value of roughly 266 billion
pounds as of the end of 2025.

The closed-end structure crossed the Atlantic in the 1890s, with the
Boston Personal Property Trust and later the wave of investment
trusts that preceded the 1929 crash. These vehicles suffered severely
during the Great Depression, as many employed substantial leverage
and traded at deep discounts, and their collapses contributed to the
regulatory momentum that produced the Investment Company Act of 1940.
The 1940 Act defined four categories of investment companies --
face-amount certificate companies, unit investment trusts (UITs),
open-end management companies (mutual funds), and closed-end
management companies. It imposed fiduciary duties, disclosure
requirements, governance standards, leverage restrictions, and
constraints on affiliated transactions. This framework remains the
governing regulatory architecture for U.S. closed-end funds.

The open-end fund, pioneered by the Massachusetts Investors Trust in
1924, was the structural innovation that came to dominate retail
collective investment. By offering continuous share creation and
redemption at NAV, the open-end fund eliminated the discount risk
that plagued closed-end vehicles. The later ETF, beginning with the
SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY) in 1993, borrowed the secondary-market trading
of closed-end funds but added the creation-redemption arbitrage
mechanism that keeps ETF prices anchored near NAV. Both innovations
were, in part, responses to the closed-end fund's persistent
discount puzzle. Yet the closed-end vehicle did not disappear. Its
structural advantages -- a stable capital base immune to redemption
pressure, the ability to hold illiquid assets, the freedom to employ
leverage, and the capacity to smooth distributions through revenue
reserves -- sustain a distinct niche in the investment-vehicle
landscape.

The empirical study of closed-end fund discounts began with Zweig
(1973), who proposed that discounts reflect the expectations of
individual investors. Malkiel (1977) examined whether discounts could
be explained by agency costs, fees, and the overvaluation of illiquid
or restricted securities. The puzzle acquired its modern form through
Lee, Shleifer, and Thaler (1991), who documented four empirical
regularities that no rational explanation could simultaneously
account for, and proposed that the discount is a measure of investor
sentiment, specifically the sentiment of the individual investors who
disproportionately hold these funds. This research positioned the
closed-end fund discount as one of the most studied anomalies in
behavioral finance, and it remains a live area of debate.

## Core Concepts

### The Closed Capital Base and the Fixed Share Count

The defining structural feature of a closed-end fund is the fixed
share count. At the initial public offering (IPO), the fund issues a
fixed number of shares and raises a fixed pool of capital. After the
IPO, the fund does not issue new shares to incoming investors and
does not redeem shares from departing ones. Investors who wish to buy
or sell must trade existing shares with each other on a secondary
exchange, at whatever price the market sets. The fund's capital base
is closed: the portfolio is managed without the inflows and outflows
that characterize open-end vehicles.

This structural permanence has three direct consequences. First, the
fund manager is insulated from redemption risk. The portfolio cannot
be forced to liquidate holdings to meet shareholder withdrawals,
which permits investment in less liquid securities and the use of
longer-term strategies. Second, the share price is determined by
supply and demand for the fund's shares on the exchange, not by the
underlying portfolio value, so the market price can and typically does
deviate from the net asset value per share. Third, because the share
count is fixed, the fund's expense ratio is applied to a stable asset
base, but the management fee is extracted from the portfolio
regardless of the discount at which the shares trade.

### The Discount-Premium Mechanism

The net asset value (NAV) per share is calculated as the total value
of the fund's investments minus liabilities, divided by shares
outstanding. The market price is whatever the exchange sets through
trading. When the market price exceeds NAV, the fund trades at a
premium. When the market price is below NAV, the fund trades at a
discount. The discount or premium is expressed as (market price minus
NAV) divided by NAV, multiplied by 100.

U.S. closed-end equity funds have historically traded at average
discounts of approximately 5 to 10 percent, with substantial
variation across funds and over time. UK investment trusts have
experienced wider average discounts, with the sector average reaching
approximately 12.5 percent as of early 2026, the longest sustained
period of double-digit discounts on record. Discounts are not
uniform: they vary by sector, by fund quality, and by macroeconomic
regime. Alternative-asset trusts (infrastructure, renewable energy,
private credit, property) saw discounts widen from typical premia of
5 to 15 percent in the 2020-2021 low-rate environment to discounts of
15 to 35 percent by 2023-2024 as interest rates rose. The discount is
the central structural feature that distinguishes closed-end funds
from open-end funds and ETFs, both of which transact at or near NAV.

### The Closed-End Fund Discount Puzzle

The discount puzzle, as formalized by Lee, Shleifer, and Thaler
(1991), comprises four empirical regularities that no single rational
explanation accounts for. First, closed-end fund shares are typically
issued at a premium at IPO, then slide to a discount within months.
Second, seasoned funds trade at persistent discounts, historically 10
to 20 percent. Third, discounts move together across funds --
despite differing managers, holdings, and expense structures -- which
implies a common factor rather than fund-specific explanations. Fourth,
discounts narrow or disappear when funds are open-ended or
liquidated.

Rational explanations have been proposed but fail to account for all
four regularities simultaneously. The agency-cost hypothesis (Malkiel
1977) attributes discounts to management fees, poor performance, and
the overvaluation of illiquid or restricted securities in NAV
calculations. The tax-liability hypothesis notes that NAV does not
reflect the capital gains tax that would be owed if the portfolio were
liquidated, so a discount represents the market pricing of this
embedded liability. The illiquidity hypothesis argues that funds
holding restricted or letter stock have NAVs that overstate the
realizable value of those assets. Each of these is logically coherent
and may explain a portion of observed discounts, but none explains
the comovement of discounts across funds with different holdings, the
IPO premium followed by discount slide, or the convergence at
open-ending.

The investor-sentiment hypothesis (Zweig 1973; Lee, Shleifer, and
Thaler 1991) proposes that discounts reflect the sentiment of
individual investors, who disproportionately hold closed-end funds.
When individual investors are optimistic, they bid up fund shares and
discounts narrow. When they are pessimistic, they sell and discounts
widen. This explains comovement -- all funds are exposed to the same
sentiment factor -- and it explains the IPO timing, as new funds are
launched when seasoned funds trade at premiums or small discounts,
i.e., when sentiment is high. The hypothesis is grounded in the noise
trader model of DeLong, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990), in
which irrational noise traders create risk that rational arbitrageurs
cannot fully eliminate because the sentiment itself is unpredictable
and transaction costs limit arbitrage.

The sentiment hypothesis is contested. Elton, Gruber, and Busse
(1998) and Gemmill and Thomas (2002) find evidence against it, while
Chopra, Lee, Shleifer, and Thaler (1993) defend it. A Warwick
Business School study of 158 UK funds found that noise-trader
sentiment, proxied by retail fund flows, drives short-term discount
fluctuations, but that the long-run discount level is better
explained by arbitrage costs and the freedom of managers to increase
charges when arbitrage is difficult. The debate remains unresolved,
which is itself evidence that no single explanation suffices.

### Leverage and the Regulatory Framework

Closed-end funds can employ leverage, a capability that open-end
mutual funds and most ETFs cannot match. The Investment Company Act
of 1940 imposes asset coverage requirements: for each dollar of debt
issued, the fund must have three dollars of assets immediately after
issuance and at dividend declarations (commonly described as 33
percent leverage). For preferred stock issued, the fund must have two
dollars of assets at issuance and dividend declaration (50 percent
leverage). At year-end 2025, at least 213 traditional funds -- 59
percent of funds -- were using structural leverage, portfolio leverage
(tender option bonds or reverse repurchase agreements), or both.
Preferred share assets were approximately 9 percent, or 24.1 billion
dollars, of traditional CEF total assets.

Leverage amplifies returns in both directions. A fund with 100
million dollars of assets that borrows 20 million gains 12 percent on
shareholder equity if the portfolio rises 10 percent, but loses 12
percent if the portfolio falls 10 percent. The cost of leverage --
typically short-term borrowing rates -- reduces returns when those
rates rise, which is why leveraged CEFs experienced pressure during
the 2022-2024 rate tightening cycle. Leverage is the primary mechanism
through which CEFs generate the high distribution yields (often 8 to
12 percent) that attract income-seeking investors, but it converts
interest-rate risk into share-price volatility.

The 1940 Act also governs other structural dimensions: it requires a
board of directors elected by shareholders, restricts affiliated
transactions, mandates diversification, and (through Rule 18f-4,
adopted 2020, effective 2022) regulates derivatives usage through a
value-at-risk limit and a derivatives risk management program. BDCs,
which are not fully registered under the 1940 Act but elect to be
subject to certain provisions, have a slightly more relaxed 2:1
debt-to-equity leverage limit.

### Distribution Mechanics and Return of Capital

Closed-end funds may distribute to shareholders from three sources:
net investment income (interest and dividends), realized capital
gains, and return of capital (ROC). Some funds follow a managed
distribution policy, which aims to provide predictable cash flow.
Return of capital is not income -- it is the return of the investor's
own principal -- and it reduces the cost basis of the holding rather
than being taxed immediately. The distinction matters: a high stated
yield funded substantially by ROC is not sustainable income but a
partial liquidation. Investors evaluate distribution sustainability
through coverage ratios and undistributed net investment income
(UNII), which measure whether the fund's actual investment income
covers its distributions. UK investment trusts have an additional
structural advantage: revenue reserves, built in good income years,
allow trusts to smooth dividends in poor years, maintaining stable
distributions even when portfolio income fluctuates.

### Activist Arbitrage and Governance

The persistence of discounts creates an arbitrage opportunity. An
investor who buys a fund at a 20 percent discount and then forces the
fund to open-end, liquidate, or conduct a tender offer at NAV captures
the discount as profit. This is activist arbitrage: unlike pure
trading arbitrage, which waits for convergence, activist arbitrageurs
take actions to cause convergence.

The practice became common after the SEC's 1992 proxy reform, which
reduced the costs of communication among shareholders and made it
easier for activists to coordinate. A Columbia Business School study
documented that open-ending attempts reduce the discount of targeted
funds by more than 10 percentage points on average, with discounts of
targeted funds averaging roughly 20 percent of NAV before an attack
and dropping to approximately 5.6 percent three years after. The
study found a dual relationship: activists target deeply discounted
funds, and forward-looking discounts shrink in anticipation of future
attacks, so the observed effect is a lower bound on the true impact.

In the modern market, activism is highly concentrated. According to
the Investment Company Institute, three firms -- Saba Capital
Management, Karpus Investment Management, and Bulldog Investors --
account for the vast majority of CEF activism. Saba Capital, founded
by Boaz Weinstein, has run campaigns at roughly 60 closed-end funds
over a decade and holds 5-percent-or-greater stakes in approximately
28 funds as of early 2026. Its strategy is to accumulate a significant
minority stake, then push for a tender offer near NAV, a merger with
an open-end fund, liquidation, or a board change. The ICI reports
that one in five CEFs have been targeted by these three activists since
2015, and that activists held shares in nearly half of all CEFs as of
mid-2023.

The activism has generated legal battles over defensive mechanisms.
In March 2025, the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of
New York held that a closed-end fund's shareholder rights plan
(poison pill) is legal under the 1940 Act, validating a defensive tool
for boards facing activist campaigns, while limiting the use of
successive identical plans. In June 2026, the Supreme Court ruled in
FS Credit Opportunities Corp. v. Saba Capital Master Fund that a
provision of the Investment Company Act does not create a private
right of action to challenge fund governance measures, removing one
legal tool activists had used. The tension between activist
arbitrageurs (who argue they unlock shareholder value trapped in
discounts) and fund boards (who argue activists extract short-term
profit at the expense of long-term shareholders) is a defining
governance feature of the closed-end vehicle.

## Evidence

### Lee, Shleifer, and Thaler (1991) -- The Four Regularities

The foundational empirical study of the discount puzzle is Lee,
Shleifer, and Thaler (1991), published in the Journal of Finance.
Using data on U.S. closed-end stock funds, the authors documented
four regularities. First, funds are issued at premia at IPO, typically
around 10 percent above NAV, then decline to discounts within roughly
120 days. Second, seasoned funds trade at substantial discounts, with
the value-weighted average discount in their sample approximately 10
to 20 percent. Third, discounts move together: the authors found that
a single sentiment factor (measured by the discount on a portfolio of
closed-end funds) explained a significant fraction of the variance in
individual fund discounts, and that this factor also correlated with
returns on small-capitalization stocks, which are also held
disproportionately by individual investors. Fourth, discounts narrow
or vanish upon open-ending, liquidation, or conversion.

The authors interpreted these findings through the noise trader model
of DeLong, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990), in which
irrational investors create unpredictable sentiment risk that rational
arbitrageurs cannot fully hedge away. Because individual investors
hold closed-end funds disproportionately, the fund's price reflects
their sentiment, and the discount measures that sentiment. The authors
argued that the comovement of discounts, the IPO-timing regularity,
and the convergence at open-ending are all consistent with this
explanation and not with rational explanations based on fees, taxes,
or illiquidity alone.

The study has been both influential and contested. Chen, Kan, and
Miller (1993) and Elton, Gruber, and Busse (1998) argued that the
sentiment factor does not survive more careful controls and that the
small-stock correlation is an artifact. A study using Greek closed-end
funds (a market expected to be more sentiment-prone) found no
evidence supporting the sentiment hypothesis. The Warwick study of 158
UK funds found that sentiment drives short-term fluctuations but that
the long-run discount level is determined by arbitrage costs and
managerial pricing power. The persistence of the debate is itself
evidence that the puzzle is not fully solved by any single framework.

### Brickley and Schallheim (1985) -- Open-Ending and Discount Convergence

Brickley and Schallheim (1985) examined the behavior of discounts
surrounding open-ending events. They found that discounts narrow as
funds approach open-ending or liquidation, which is inconsistent with
the tax-liability hypothesis (which would predict that discounts
widen as the tax realization approaches) and consistent with the view
that the discount reflects a structural illiquidity or sentiment
premium that is eliminated when the closed-end constraint is removed.
This finding supports the fourth Lee-Shleifer-Thaler regularity and
demonstrates that the discount is not purely an accounting artifact --
it responds to the actual removal of the structural feature that
created it.

### Activist Arbitrage -- Columbia Business School Study

A comprehensive study by researchers at Columbia Business School,
documented in the paper Activist Arbitrage: A Study of Open-Ending
Attempts of Closed-End Funds, examined all activist arbitrage
activities in U.S. closed-end funds between 1988 and 2003. The study
found that open-ending attempts reduce the discount of targeted funds
by more than 10 percentage points on average, accounting for both
successful and unsuccessful attempts, and that this effect is above
and beyond mean reversion in discounts. The average discount of
targeted funds was approximately 20 percent of NAV two years before
an attack and dropped to approximately 5.6 percent three years after.
The study estimated that the 1992 proxy reform created approximately
124 million dollars per year in value for CEF shareholders through
facilitated activism, or approximately 32 million dollars per year
after adjusting for mean reversion. The key empirical finding is the
dual relationship: a one percentage point increase in the discount is
associated with a 0.77 percentage point increase in the probability of
an attack in a given year, while forward-looking discounts decrease in
anticipation of future attacks, shrinking before any observable
campaign begins.

### Pershing Square USA -- The Modern Discount Case

Pershing Square USA (PSUS), listed in April 2026 as a closed-end fund
vehicle for William Ackman's concentrated public-equity strategy,
provided a prominent modern illustration of the discount puzzle. The
fund raised approximately 5 billion dollars at IPO and subsequently
traded at a 22 percent discount to NAV, among the widest gaps of any
U.S. closed-end fund invested in public securities. Ackman attributed
the discount to IPO technicals and insufficient marketing to the
closed-end buyer base, and announced an active marketing program
targeting financial advisers. The case illustrates that even a
high-conviction portfolio managed by a prominent investor does not
prevent the closed-end structure from producing a substantial
discount when the secondary-market demand for the specific share class
is insufficient. The discount became the firm's central
capital-formation problem, demonstrating that the closed-end
structure imposes a distribution and demand constraint that is
independent of portfolio quality.

### UK Investment Trust Discounts -- The 2022-2024 Rate Cycle

The UK investment trust sector provided a large-scale natural
experiment in the relationship between interest rates and discounts
during the 2022-2024 tightening cycle. The sector average discount
widened from approximately minus 2 percent at the end of 2021 to
approximately minus 12.5 percent by early 2026, the longest sustained
period of double-digit discounts on record. The widening was
concentrated in alternative-asset trusts: infrastructure, renewable
energy, private credit, and property trusts saw discounts widen from
premia of 5 to 15 percent in the low-rate environment to discounts of
15 to 35 percent. The driver was the rise in discount rates applied
to long-duration cash flows: as UK long-term gilt yields rose to
approximately 4.43 percent by February 2026, the present value of
trusts' underlying assets reset downward, and the market applied an
additional discount for the uncertainty and illiquidity of the
holdings. This case demonstrates that the discount is not a static
anomaly but a dynamic variable that responds to macroeconomic
conditions, with the closed-end structure amplifying the repricing
through the combination of NAV adjustment and sentiment-driven market
price adjustment.

## Implications

### For Investors -- The Discount as Opportunity and Risk

For investors, the closed-end fund discount is a double-edged
structural feature. Buying a fund at a 15 percent discount means
acquiring one dollar of underlying assets for 85 cents, and receiving
income calculated on the full dollar. If the discount narrows, the
investor gains an additional return beyond the portfolio's own
performance. This makes discount-hunting a legitimate strategy,
particularly for income-focused investors who can buy high-yielding
bond or municipal CEFs below NAV and capture the distribution yield
on the full asset value while paying a discounted price.

The risk is symmetric. Discounts can widen further. A fund purchased
at a 20 percent discount may move to a 30 percent discount if the
sector falls out of favor, adding to portfolio losses even if the
underlying NAV is stable. The UK investment trust data showed that
total shareholder return over a holding period depends on both the
NAV return and the discount movement: a trust whose NAV fell 12
percent but whose discount widened produced a share-price return of
minus 20 percent. The investor in closed-end funds must therefore
assess not only the portfolio but the discount trajectory, the
sentiment regime, and the structural likelihood of convergence
(open-ending, tender, or liquidation).

The distribution yield adds another layer. High stated yields funded
substantially by return of capital are not sustainable income. The
investor must decompose the distribution into its sources -- net
investment income, realized gains, and ROC -- and evaluate coverage
ratios and UNII to judge sustainability. A fund with a 10 percent
yield, 60 percent funded by ROC, is effectively returning the
investor's own capital and eroding NAV, not providing genuine income.

### For Fund Design -- The Capital-Permanence Trade-off

The closed-end structure embodies a fundamental trade-off between
capital permanence and price efficiency. The fixed capital base gives
the manager freedom to invest in illiquid assets, employ leverage, and
pursue long-term strategies without redemption pressure. This is why
closed-end vehicles dominate certain asset categories -- municipal
bonds, senior loans, certain emerging market strategies, and UK
alternative-asset trusts -- where the underlying assets are illiquid
and the open-end redemption promise would be structurally
inconsistent with the portfolio.

The cost of this permanence is the discount. Because the share price
is set by secondary-market supply and demand rather than NAV, the
closed-end fund introduces a layer of price inefficiency that the
open-end fund and ETF were designed to eliminate. The ETF's
creation-redemption mechanism keeps its price near NAV by allowing
arbitrageurs to create or redeem shares at NAV; the closed-end fund
has no such mechanism, so the discount persists. Fund designers must
choose: capital permanence with discount risk (closed-end), or price
efficiency with redemption risk (open-end). The choice is dictated by
the liquidity of the underlying assets and the investment horizon of
the intended investor base.

### For Governance and Activism -- The Discount as a Control Contest

The persistent discount creates a governance pressure that does not
exist in open-end vehicles. Because the discount represents trapped
value -- the gap between the market price and the NAV that would be
realized if the structure were removed -- it invites activists who can
profit by forcing convergence. This turns the closed-end fund into a
site of recurring control contests between short-term arbitrageurs and
long-term fund boards.

The ICI argues that activists extract short-term profit at the expense
of long-term shareholders, forcing liquidity events (tender offers,
liquidations, open-endings) that impose tax burdens and destroy the
structural advantages of the closed-end vehicle. Activists argue that
they unlock value that complacent boards and managers have allowed to
remain trapped. The empirical evidence from the Columbia study shows
that activism does reduce discounts substantially and that
forward-looking discounts shrink in anticipation of attacks,
suggesting that the mere threat of activism disciplines fund
management. The legal infrastructure -- the 2025 poison pill ruling,
the 2026 Supreme Court decision limiting private rights of action --
is actively shaping the balance of power between these forces.

### For the Vehicle-Structure Landscape -- Position Relative to Other Pooled Vehicles

The closed-end fund occupies a distinct position in the
investment-vehicle landscape. Relative to open-end mutual funds and
ETFs, it sacrifices price efficiency (trading at discounts rather than
NAV) in exchange for capital permanence, leverage capacity, and the
ability to hold illiquid assets. Relative to private-equity and
venture-capital funds, it offers daily liquidity (through the
secondary market) but without the LP-GP carried-interest structure or
the long lockup periods; the trade-off is that the liquidity is at a
discounted price, not at NAV. Relative to Berkshire Hathaway and other
permanent-capital holding companies, the closed-end fund is a regulated
investment company that must distribute income and is subject to the
1940 Act's diversification and governance requirements, whereas the
holding company can retain earnings and operate with fewer
constraints. Relative to REITs and BDCs, the closed-end fund is a more
general vehicle (any asset class) but without the specialized tax
treatment or the mandatory asset-concentration rules that define those
vehicles. The closed-end structure is the baseline pooled vehicle from
which these more specialized structures deviate, and understanding the
closed-end trade-off clarifies why each specialization exists.

### For Market Structure -- The Discount as a Sentiment Barometer

Because closed-end fund discounts comove and correlate with the
sentiment of individual investors, the aggregate discount level
functions as a market-wide sentiment indicator. Lee, Shleifer, and
Thaler (1991) showed that the closed-end fund discount factor
correlates with returns on small-capitalization stocks, which are
also disproportionately held by individuals. This means the discount
is not merely a property of the funds themselves but a measurable
signal of the risk appetite of the retail investor cohort. When
discounts are narrow, retail sentiment is high; when discounts widen,
retreat is underway. Strategists and macro investors have used the
aggregate CEF discount as one input into sentiment assessment, though
its predictive power for broad market returns remains debated. The
signal is noisy, it is specific to the retail cohort, and it is
confounded by interest-rate effects (as the 2022-2024 cycle showed,
rising rates widen discounts independently of sentiment). But as a
real-time, market-derived measure of retail positioning, the
closed-end fund discount is one of the few observable sentiment
proxies that does not rely on survey data.

### For Capital Formation -- The IPO Discount and the Demand Constraint

The closed-end structure imposes a demand constraint on capital
formation that open-end vehicles do not face. Because the share count
is fixed after IPO, the fund cannot absorb new capital by creating
shares; it can only raise new capital through secondary offerings,
rights issues, or managed distribution reinvestment, all of which
require existing shareholder approval. This means the closed-end fund's
ability to grow assets under management is structurally limited, and
its IPO must be sized to the demand of the specific buyer base for
that vehicle. The Pershing Square USA case demonstrated this
constraint directly: a 5 billion dollar IPO that the closed-end buyer
base did not fully absorb produced a 22 percent discount, and the
solution was not portfolio adjustment but marketing to expand the
buyer base. For fund sponsors, this implies that closed-end issuance
is viable only when the target investor cohort is sufficiently large
and sufficiently informed about the vehicle's specific strategy. The
IPO premium-to-discount slide -- the first Lee-Shleifer-Thaler
regularity -- is partly a reflection of this: the IPO is priced for
the initial buyer cohort, and the subsequent discount reflects the
narrower secondary-market demand. This capital-formation constraint
is the structural reason the closed-end sector has contracted in the
U.S. -- the ICI reports a nearly 35 percent drop in the number of CEFs
since 2007, driven by activism, mergers, and the difficulty of
launching new funds into a discount-prone market.
