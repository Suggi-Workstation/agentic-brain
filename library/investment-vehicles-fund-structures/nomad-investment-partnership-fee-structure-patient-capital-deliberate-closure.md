# The Nomad Investment Partnership -- How Fee Structure, Patient Capital, and Deliberate Closure Made a Vehicle That Compounded Differently Than Any Fund

The Nomad Investment Partnership (2001-2014) was a London-based
investment partnership that compounded investor capital at
approximately 20.8 percent per year net of fees versus 6.5 percent for
the MSCI World Index, a 921 percent total return against 117 percent
for the index. Its structural design -- a cost-reimbursement
management fee capped at 1 percent, a 20 percent performance fee
charged only above a 6 percent annual hurdle and held in a clawback
reserve, a deliberately curated investor base of charities and
educational endowments, extreme portfolio concentration, and a
voluntary closure at peak performance -- represents the most complete
real-world test of aligning fund architecture with patient, long-term
compounding. Nomad is studied not for a novel investment strategy but
for demonstrating that the vehicle's incentive structure, investor
selection, and lifecycle governance can be engineered to serve
compounding rather than to extract fees from it.

## Background

The Nomad Investment Partnership was launched on September 10, 2001,
by Nick Sleep and Qais "Zak" Zakaria, originally as a subsidiary of
Marathon Asset Management in London. Sleep had joined Marathon as an
investment analyst around 1995 after stints at Sun Life of Canada and
a small Scottish fund company. Zakaria, an Iraqi-born Cambridge-trained
mathematician, had been a sell-side analyst at Deutsche Bank
specializing in Asian equities before joining Marathon. The two met
while independently pursuing investment opportunities in Asian markets
in the late 1990s. Jeremy Hosking, a Marathon founding shareholder,
encouraged their contrarian approach, which had proven profitable
during the Asian Financial Crisis when Marathon deployed roughly 500
million dollars into distressed Southeast Asian markets [high].

A pivotal moment in the fund's creation came in May 2000, when Sleep
and Zakaria traveled to Omaha for Berkshire Hathaway's annual
shareholder meeting. The experience inspired Sleep to propose starting
their own fund, modeled on the structural principles of Buffett's
partnership rather than on the hedge fund industry's conventions.
Zakaria embraced the idea, and Sleep persuaded his Marathon bosses to
let them launch Nomad with Zakaria as co-manager. The fund began
investing one day before the September 11 attacks. It was spun out as
an independent partnership in 2006, retaining its original structure
and investor base [high].

The structural lineage Sleep and Zakaria drew upon was the Buffett
Partnership (1957-1969), not the Jones-model hedge fund. Alfred
Winslow Jones had invented the hedged fund in 1949, pairing long
equity positions with short sales and leverage to pursue absolute
returns. The Jones model spawned an industry that by the 2000s managed
over a trillion dollars on a 2-and-20 fee model with quarterly or
annual liquidity. Buffett's partnership operated differently: a
performance fee only above a 6 percent annual hurdle, no management fee
designed to profit the manager, and an investor base of trusted
partners who understood that capital would be deployed patiently over
years. Sleep and Zakaria adopted the Buffett model's core architecture
and added innovations of their own -- the Contingent Performance
Reserve with clawback, the declining percentage management fee, and the
deliberate cultivation of a charitable and educational investor base
[high].

The intellectual foundation for Nomad's structure was a diagnosis of
what was broken in the asset management industry. Sleep identified the
principal-agent conflict as the central structural flaw: fund managers
are paid to manage assets, not to compound them. The standard 2 percent
management fee creates a revenue stream independent of performance,
rewarding asset gathering over capital allocation. Performance fees
without hurdles or clawbacks reward volatility and short-term spikes.
Redemption rights give investors an escape valve that forces managers
to hold cash and avoid concentration, undermining long-term
compounding. Sleep and Zakaria designed Nomad to invert each of these
incentives. The structure was not an afterthought to the investment
process -- it was the process. As Sleep wrote, "it is for this reason
we named Nomad an Investment Partnership and not a fund. The
relationship we seek is quite different" [high].

The fund's lifecycle spanned thirteen years and roughly 218 pages of
partnership letters written by Sleep (with contributions from Zakaria).
The letters documented a genuine intellectual evolution: the early
portfolio held cigar-butt bargains -- deeply discounted, asset-based
businesses in overlooked markets, including four Zimbabwean stocks --
and gradually concentrated into what Sleep called "honestly run
compounding machines." By the end, the portfolio was essentially three
holdings: Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway. The structural
question this evolution raises is central to this topic: what kind of
vehicle allows two managers to hold three stocks for a decade without
investor revolt, regulatory pressure, or the structural incentives that
push most funds toward diversification and activity? [high].

## Core Concepts

### The Cost-Reimbursement Management Fee

Nomad's management fee was not a profit center. It was a
cost-reimbursement model designed to cover the partnership's operating
expenses -- salaries, rent, research, legal costs -- without generating
profit for the managers. The fee was capped at 1 percent of assets
under management, but in absolute terms it remained roughly constant.
As the fund grew, the fee declined as a percentage of assets. Sleep
explained the design: "As the Partnership grows in size the management
fee will decline as a percentage of assets and, that way, all investors
share in the natural scale economics of the operation" [high]. This is
the structural opposite of the standard hedge fund model, where a 2
percent fee on growing assets produces exponentially increasing
management revenue regardless of performance. By capping the fee at
cost and letting the percentage decline, Nomad eliminated the incentive
to gather assets for fee revenue. The managers' only path to
significant compensation was performance -- and even that was
structurally constrained by the hurdle and clawback mechanisms described
below.

The Commoncog analysis estimates that the management fee was
effectively 10 basis points (0.10 percent) at the fund's larger scale,
far below the industry standard of 1-2 percent [medium]. The
massivemoats deep dive confirms the structural intent: the fee was
"constant in absolute terms" but "declined as a percentage as assets
under management (AUM) increased," exemplifying what that analysis
called "the high moral and qualitative standards Sleep and Zakaria
espoused" [medium].

### The Performance Fee: Hurdle, Reserve, and Clawback

Nomad charged a 20 percent performance fee, but only on gains exceeding
a 6 percent annual hurdle rate. The 6 percent threshold was modeled
directly on the Buffett Partnership, where Buffett charged no
performance fee on the first 6 percent of annual returns, treating that
amount as the investor's cost of capital. Below 6 percent, Nomad's
managers earned nothing beyond the cost-reimbursement management fee
[high].

The critical innovation was the Contingent Performance Reserve (CPR).
Performance fees were not paid out immediately when earned. Instead,
they were placed in a reserve account. If subsequent performance fell
below the 6 percent hurdle in future periods, previously earned
performance fees could be clawed back from the reserve and returned to
investors. Sleep described the mechanism as "a desire to be better
system, not a punishment and condemnation system" [high]. The CPR
created a multi-year escrow: managers could not extract performance
fees until the returns were durable. This is structurally distinct
from the hedge fund high-water mark, which merely requires a fund to
recover losses before charging incentive fees again. The high-water
mark is a threshold mechanism; the CPR is a time-escrow mechanism. A
high-water mark prevents double-charging on the same recovery. A
clawback reserve prevents the manager from extracting fees on gains
that later reverse, regardless of whether the fund is above or below
its prior peak [high].

Sleep went further, stating in writing that there would come a day
when the partners could waive the performance fee entirely. He wrote:
"we mention this in writing now so that hopefully you will remind us of
it in years to come and hold us to account" [high]. This commitment --
to potentially eliminate their own performance compensation -- has no
parallel in the institutional fund management industry, where fee
preservation and fee growth are structural imperatives.

### Investor Selection and the Patient Capital Base

Nomad's investor base was not assembled through marketing or
distribution channels. It was curated. The fund's investors were
predominantly charities and educational endowments -- institutions with
perpetual or near-perpetual horizons, no immediate liquidity needs, and
governance structures that tolerated short-term volatility. Sleep and
Zakaria selected for psychological temperament as much as for capital.
They implemented what sources describe as a "no asshole rule" for
partners, refusing capital from investors who would pressure them
during drawdowns [high].

The selection philosophy was explicit. Sleep wrote: "One of Nomad's key
advantages will be the aggregate patience of its investor base. If
Nomad is to have a competitive advantage over our peers this will come
from the capital allocation skills of your manager (if any) and the
patience of our investor base. Only by looking further out than the
short-term crowd can we expect to beat them" [high]. This is a
structural claim: the vehicle's competitive advantage derives not from
information edge or analytical superiority alone but from the time
horizon of its capital. Permanent capital -- capital that cannot be
redeemed on short notice -- allows a manager to hold concentrated
positions through multi-year drawdowns without being forced to sell.
Redeemable capital creates the opposite structural pressure: the threat
of redemption forces cash buffers, diversification, and short-term
performance management, each of which erodes long-term compounding
[high].

The MIT Investment Management Company (MITIMCo) alumni letter from
February 2017 explicitly credits Sleep and Zakaria for the concept of
"net present value positive mistakes" -- mistakes whose learnings
outweigh their cost -- and describes how MITIMCo adopted Nomad's
partnership philosophy in its own manager relationships, including
giving investment managers 12 months' advance notice of redemption
requests rather than calling them on the last possible day [high]. This
documents the institutional influence of Nomad's structural model
beyond the fund itself.

The 2008 financial crisis tested the investor base. When markets
halved and Nomad's portfolio fell 45.3 percent, the partnership
experienced only 2 percent net redemptions. Sleep wrote that this
demonstrated "Nomad truly attracted the partners it deserved" [high].
By contrast, hedge funds industry-wide experienced record redemptions
in 2008-2009, with many forced to liquidate or gate. The structural
difference was not the drawdown -- Nomad's was larger than many hedge
funds -- but the investor base's response. Curated patience converted a
drawdown from a structural threat (forced selling) into a structural
opportunity (buying more of the same businesses at lower prices) [high].

### Concentration as a Structural Choice

Nomad's portfolio concentration was extreme by any industry standard.
The early portfolio held 20 to 30 securities. By 2007, the 10 largest
holdings represented over 80 percent of the portfolio. By 2009,
approximately 75 percent of the portfolio was in growth companies, with
one single company accounting for 30 percent. By the end, the portfolio
was essentially three holdings: Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway
[high].

Concentration at this level is structurally impossible for most fund
vehicles. Mutual funds face regulatory diversification requirements
(typically no more than 5 percent of assets in any single issuer for
diversified fund status). Hedge funds face prime broker margin
constraints and investor concentration limits. PE and VC funds are
structurally diversified across portfolio companies by design. Nomad
operated as an unregulated investment partnership with no external
diversification mandate and an investor base that explicitly permitted
-- and at one point voted to allow -- concentration above the fund's
own stated limits. When Nomad proposed taking Amazon above 20 percent
of the fund, approximately a quarter of investors redeemed rather than
consent. Sleep and Zakaria proceeded anyway, accepting the capital
departures as the cost of structural integrity [high].

The structural logic for concentration was articulated in the letters.
Sleep argued that diversification is a hedge against ignorance: "if
knowledge is a source of value added, and few things can be known for
sure, then it logically follows that owning more stocks does not lower
risk but raises it." The risk that concentration addresses is not
volatility -- a three-stock portfolio is more volatile than a
thirty-stock portfolio -- but the risk of misanalysis, which Sleep
considered the only real investment risk. The vehicle structure enabled
the investment philosophy: without an investor base that would tolerate
concentration, the philosophy could not be implemented [high].

### Liquidity and the Absence of Lockups

Nomad's approach to liquidity differed from both the hedge fund and PE
models. Hedge funds typically impose lockup periods (1-3 years) and
redemption gates to prevent forced selling during crises. PE and VC
funds are closed-end: capital is committed for a decade and cannot be
redeemed. Nomad offered neither formal lockups nor a closed-end
structure. Investors could redeem, and during the Amazon concentration
vote, some did. The fund also periodically opened to new subscriptions
and closed them when the managers could not find investments that
reduced the portfolio's price-to-value ratio [high].

The structural insight is that Nomad relied on investor selection
rather than contractual lockups to achieve capital permanence. This is
a fundamentally different governance model. Lockups are a mechanical
constraint: they prevent redemption regardless of investor preference.
Investor selection is a self-selecting filter: only investors who
genuinely accept the philosophy commit capital, and those who do not
self-select out (or are screened out during the concentration vote).
Sleep and Zakaria preferred the latter because it produced an investor
base that would not exercise redemption rights even under stress --
as the 2 percent net redemption figure in 2008 confirmed. The
mechanical lockup would have retained capital grudgingly; the selection
filter retained capital willingly [high].

### The Deliberate Closure

In 2014, after thirteen years and approximately 2 billion dollars
generated for clients, Sleep and Zakaria closed the Nomad Investment
Partnership. They returned all capital to investors, sold their own
Berkshire Hathaway shares (held for roughly ten years), and shifted to
philanthropic work. The closure was not forced by performance decline,
investor revolt, regulatory action, or succession failure. It was a
voluntary decision made at the peak of the fund's track record [high].

The stated reasons were threefold. First, increasing regulatory burdens
made the cost of continued operation rise relative to the intellectual
satisfaction of managing money. Second, the partners felt a continuous
accountability to evolving stakeholders that eroded the autonomy
required for their approach. Third -- and most structurally
significant -- they believed they had fully maximized their
contribution to the investment process. Sleep wrote: "After all, we had
what we needed, just a few superb businesses and we were unlikely to
sell any of those to fund the purchase of another cigar butt, Philippine
cement company, were we?" [high].

The closure is structurally instructive because it addresses the
end-game problem that all fund vehicles face. Hedge funds that
outperform attract capital, which dilutes the strategy; the manager
either closes to new investment (preserving performance but capping
revenue) or accepts asset growth (increasing revenue but degrading
returns). PE funds face a parallel problem: each fund must be raised
and deployed, creating pressure to invest even when opportunities are
scarce. Permanent-capital vehicles like Berkshire Hathaway solve this
by never returning capital -- the structure has no end date, so there
is no pressure to harvest or redeploy. Sleep and Zakaria chose a fourth
option: dissolve the vehicle entirely and advise investors to hold the
positions personally. They told investors that simply holding Amazon,
Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway for the next decade would likely
outperform most alternatives -- including an actively managed fund
charging fees for the privilege of holding the same three stocks [high].

This decision illuminates a structural truth: the fund vehicle adds
value only when the manager's capital allocation produces results that
investors cannot replicate on their own. Once the portfolio had reached
its "terminal" state -- a handful of businesses whose compounding
engines were self-sustaining -- the vehicle's structural overhead
(fees, reporting, regulation, investor communication) became a net cost
rather than a net benefit. Closing the fund was the logically
consistent extension of the fee structure's original design: minimize
the cost layer between investors and the businesses they own [high].

## Evidence

### Track Record and the Structural Contribution to Returns

Nomad's total return of 921.1 percent over thirteen years (approximately
20.8 percent annualized net of fees) versus 116.9 percent for the MSCI
World Index (approximately 6.5 percent annualized) is documented in the
partnership letters and corroborated by the Financial Times, which
reported the figure as 921.1 percent, or 18.4 percent per annum after
performance fees -- the difference between 20.8 percent and 18.4
percent representing the performance fee drag [high]. In dollar terms,
a 1 million dollar investment with Nomad grew to approximately 10.2
million dollars, versus 2.17 million for the same investment in the
MSCI World Index [high].

A critical structural question is how much of the return is attributable
to the investment selections versus the vehicle architecture. Sleep
himself addressed this directly in the final letter: "As time goes by,
the performance that our clients have received is the capitalisation or
the success of the firms in which we have invested. In other words, the
real work is done by you [addressing investee company managers] and the
good people of Berkshire" [high]. This is not false modesty but a
structural observation: once the portfolio reached its terminal
concentration in three compounding machines, the return was generated by
the businesses, not by the managers' activity. The vehicle's
contribution was enabling the inactivity -- holding through volatility,
concentrating without investor revolt, and charging minimal fees that
preserved the compounding. The same three stocks held in a standard
2-and-20 hedge fund with quarterly liquidity and forced diversification
would have produced a materially different return, not because the
businesses performed differently but because the vehicle would have
traded, diversified, charged more, and potentially been forced to
liquidate during 2008 [high].

### The 2008 Financial Crisis as a Structural Stress Test

The 2008 crisis provides the clearest empirical evidence for how
Nomad's vehicle structure performed under stress. Nomad's portfolio fell
45.3 percent in 2008 -- a deeper drawdown than many diversified hedge
funds. The structural difference manifested in two ways. First,
redemptions: the fund experienced only 2 percent net redemptions, while
the hedge fund industry experienced record outflows. The Financial
Crisis Inquiry Commission and industry data show that hedge fund assets
contracted by approximately 30-40 percent in 2008-2009 through a
combination of performance losses and redemptions, with many funds
imposing gating provisions to prevent liquidation. Nomad required no
gates; its investor base held voluntarily [high].

Second, the crisis response: rather than de-risk or hedge, Sleep and
Zakaria used the drawdown to concentrate further. They added to Amazon,
which had fallen roughly 50 percent from its peak, on the thesis that
the scale-economies-shared model was widening Amazon's competitive lead
over distressed brick-and-mortar rivals. Over the following four years,
the fund returned approximately 404 percent. This decision -- to
concentrate further during a crisis -- is structurally available only
to a vehicle with patient, non-redeemable capital and managers
incentivized by long-term performance rather than short-term fee
preservation. A hedge fund manager facing quarterly redemptions and a
high-water mark would face structural pressure to reduce risk, hold
cash, or hedge, even if the long-term thesis remained intact [high].

### Comparison with the Buffett Partnership

The Buffett Partnership (1957-1969) is the most direct structural
comparison. Buffett charged no management fee and took 25 percent of
profits above a 6 percent hurdle (reduced to 25 percent of profits
above a 4 percent hurdle in later years). He had approximately 100
partners and refused to accept new capital when he could not deploy it
productively. He closed the partnership in 1969, returning capital to
partners and converting the vehicle into Berkshire Hathaway, a
permanent-capital holding company. Sleep and Zakaria explicitly modeled
Nomad's fee structure on the Buffett Partnership: the 6 percent hurdle,
the performance-fee-only-above-hurdle design, and the willingness to
close to new subscriptions. The key structural difference is the
end-game: Buffett transformed the partnership into a permanent-capital
conglomerate, retaining capital under a new vehicle. Sleep and Zakaria
dissolved the vehicle entirely and advised investors to hold the
positions personally -- a more radical structural choice that
prioritized investor autonomy over manager control [high].

The structural parallel raises a question about vehicle evolution:
when a partnership's portfolio reaches a terminal state of a few
compounding machines, what is the optimal next vehicle? Buffett chose
permanent capital (Berkshire). Sleep chose no vehicle. Each choice has
structural consequences: permanent capital allows continued allocation
of new capital flows but imposes governance, regulatory, and succession
costs; dissolution eliminates those costs but leaves investors to manage
their own positions, including the psychological challenge of holding
through future drawdowns without the manager's letters and reassurance
[high].

### Comparison with Hedge Fund and PE Structures

The hedge fund model that Nomad diverged from charges a 2 percent
management fee and 20 percent performance fee, typically with a
high-water mark but no hurdle rate and no clawback. The management fee
on 3 billion dollars at 2 percent would generate 60 million dollars per
year in management revenue alone -- more than Nomad's total management
fee over thirteen years. The incentive to gather assets under the hedge
fund model directly conflicts with the incentive to concentrate and
hold: more assets require more positions, more positions require more
research and trading, and more activity degrades the compounding of the
best ideas [high].

The PE/VC fund model imposes a closed-end, ten-year lifecycle with
called capital and distribution waterfalls. The GP commits to deploy
capital within an investment period (typically 4-5 years) and must
return capital with gains within the fund's life. This creates
structural pressure to invest on a schedule rather than when
opportunities arise, and to exit positions to generate distributions
before fund maturity, regardless of whether the businesses have reached
their full compounding potential. Nomad's open-ended, indefinite-horizon
structure imposed no such pressure. Sleep and Zakaria could hold
Costco for twelve years and Amazon for eight years without any
contractual obligation to harvest or redeploy [high].

The permanent-capital holding company model (Berkshire Hathaway, Markel,
Constellation Software) solves the lifecycle problem by never returning
capital, but it imposes different structural costs: public-company
reporting, shareholder governance, regulatory compliance, and the
expectation of continued capital deployment at increasing scale. As
Berkshire's capital base grew, Buffett repeatedly noted that the
opportunity set narrowed -- large acquisitions of sufficient quality
became rarer. Nomad avoided this scale problem by capping assets: the
fund was deliberately not large (approximately 3 billion dollars at
closure), and the managers chose dissolution over scaling [high].

### The Post-Closure Performance of Recommended Holdings

The structural thesis -- that the vehicle's value was enabling inactivity
rather than active management -- is testable through the post-closure
performance of the three recommended holdings. Sleep advised departing
investors to hold Amazon, Costco, and Berkshire Hathaway for the next
decade. According to multiple analyses, all three continued to compound
significantly after 2014. The Amazon holding alone grew 8 to 10 times
after the fund closed. Zakaria never sold any Amazon shares from his
personal portfolio; Sleep eventually sold half his Amazon stake
(representing roughly 70 percent of his total holdings) around 2018 and
reinvested in ASOS, a decision that proved costly as ASOS subsequently
declined over 90 percent from its peak. The post-closure record supports
the structural claim that the businesses, not the vehicle, generated the
returns -- and that the vehicle's overhead became redundant once the
terminal portfolio was established [high].

## Implications

### For Fund Design and Incentive Alignment

Nomad's structure demonstrates that a fund vehicle can be engineered to
serve compounding rather than to extract fees from it. The specific
design choices -- cost-reimbursement management fee, hurdle rate,
clawback reserve, investor selection for temperament, concentration
tolerance, and willingness to close -- are each individually replicable.
The challenge is replicating them together as a coherent system. A
cost-reimbursement fee without a patient investor base produces a fund
that cannot survive drawdowns. A patient investor base without a hurdle
and clawback produces a fund where the manager can underperform
indefinitely while retaining capital. Concentration without investor
selection produces redemptions at the worst possible moment. The
structural lesson is that each design element reinforces the others, and
removing any one element destabilizes the system [high].

For fund designers, the replicable elements are: (1) separate
management fee from profit -- charge only cost, and let the percentage
decline with scale; (2) impose a hurdle rate calibrated to the cost of
capital, not to zero; (3) escrow performance fees for multiple years
with clawback provisions; (4) select investors for time horizon and
behavioral temperament, not for capital size; (5) permit concentration
up to and beyond conventional limits, with informed consent; (6) treat
closure as a legitimate lifecycle outcome, not as failure. Each of these
choices has a cost: lower management revenue, slower asset growth,
higher operational complexity, and the psychological difficulty of
turning away capital. Nomad's record suggests that the compounding
benefit of these structural costs can exceed their operational cost by
a wide margin [high].

### For Investors Evaluating Fund Vehicles

For limited partners, endowments, and family offices evaluating fund
managers, Nomad provides a structural checklist for assessing
alignment. The standard due diligence framework examines track record,
strategy, team, and risk controls. Nomad's example suggests adding
structural questions: Does the management fee create an incentive to
gather assets independent of performance? Is there a hurdle rate, and
is it calibrated to a real cost of capital or set at zero? Are
performance fees escrowed with clawback, or are they paid out
immediately? What is the investor base -- are partners selected for
patience, or is capital accepted from any source? Does the vehicle
permit concentration, or do diversification mandates and concentration
limits constrain the manager's best ideas? Does the manager have a
stated philosophy about when to close the fund, or is growth the
default endpoint? These questions address the structural architecture
that governs how returns are generated and how fees are extracted
[high].

The MITIMCo alumni letter illustrates how one institutional investor
internalized these lessons. MITIMCo explicitly adopted the principle of
behaving as a long-term partner rather than a transactional capital
provider -- giving 12 months' advance notice on redemptions, extending
transition timelines for departing managers, and recognizing that "the
quality of the partnership with an investment manager" correlates
negatively with "the use of legal documents to define the basis of that
relationship" [high]. This is the institutional application of Nomad's
structural philosophy: the relationship between capital provider and
capital allocator is itself a structural variable that affects returns.

### For the Industry's Structural Convergence Problem

The asset management industry exhibits a structural convergence
problem that Nomad's example throws into relief. Successful fund
vehicles face a consistent pressure: outperformance attracts capital,
capital growth dilutes the strategy, the diluted strategy converges
toward index returns, and the fee structure extracts the same
percentage from a larger but lower-performing asset base. The hedge
fund industry's aggregate return after fees has converged toward or
below broad equity index returns over extended periods, despite the
presence of genuinely skilled managers within the industry. The
structural reason is that the fee-and-redemption architecture rewards
asset gathering and punishes the concentration and inactivity that
produce exceptional returns [high].

Nomad's structural answer to convergence was to refuse growth. The fund
was deliberately not large. The managers closed to new subscriptions
when they could not find investments that reduced the portfolio's
price-to-value ratio. They accepted capital departures rather than
compromise concentration. They ultimately dissolved the vehicle rather
than scale it. Each of these choices has a structural analogue in the
permanent-capital model: Berkshire Hathaway's refusal to pay dividends
(retaining capital for redeployment) and Markel's continued
underwriting discipline (refusing to grow insurance premiums at
inadequate rates) are structural refusals to prioritize revenue over
returns. The difference is that permanent-capital vehicles refuse
growth within a perpetual structure, while Nomad refused growth to the
point of structural dissolution [high].

### For the Permanent-Capital versus Redeemable-Capital Debate

The investment vehicle literature increasingly frames the choice
between permanent and redeemable capital as the central structural
variable in fund architecture. Permanent capital (holding companies,
insurance float, closed-end funds with no redemption) allows managers
to compound without the threat of forced selling. Redeemable capital
(open-end funds, hedge funds with liquidity terms) imposes a structural
cost: the manager must hold cash buffers, maintain diversification, and
manage short-term performance to prevent redemptions. Nomad occupies a
distinct position in this framework. It was technically a redeemable
capital vehicle -- investors could withdraw -- but it achieved
permanent-capital behavior through investor selection rather than
contractual lockup. This is a third structural category: self-selected
permanent capital, where the capital behaves as permanent not because
the contract forbids redemption but because the investors choose not to
redeem [high].

The structural implication is that permanent-capital behavior is not
solely a function of the legal vehicle. It is a function of the
alignment between the vehicle's terms, the manager's philosophy, and
the investor's temperament. A closed-end fund with impatient investors
who lobby for distributions or activist changes can exhibit
redeemable-capital behavior despite the legal structure. An open-ended
partnership with investors who understand and accept the philosophy can
exhibit permanent-capital behavior despite the legal freedom to
withdraw. Nomad's 2 percent net redemption in 2008 is the empirical
evidence for this proposition: the legal structure permitted
redemption, but the investor base did not exercise it [high].
