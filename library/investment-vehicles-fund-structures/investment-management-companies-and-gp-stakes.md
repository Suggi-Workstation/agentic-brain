# Investment Management Companies and GP Stakes -- The Firm Behind the Funds Is a Separate, Durable but People-Dependent Asset

An investment fund owns a defined pool of assets, while the investment management company operates the franchise that raises and manages successive funds. A GP-stakes investor buys an interest in that franchise and its contractual or allocated economics -- management-fee profits, performance-related earnings, and sometimes balance-sheet investments -- rather than a limited-partner interest in one fund. The distinction creates a potentially durable stream of earnings, but its value still depends on investment performance, fundraising, client concentration, talent, costs, governance, and the exact rights attached to a usually illiquid minority position.

## Background

The phrase "the GP" is often used as if it described one entity, but an alternative-asset manager usually contains several legal and economic layers. A private fund holds portfolio investments for its limited partners. A general partner or equivalent entity controls that fund under its governing agreement. An investment adviser or management company employs the team, supplies research and operations, and earns contractual management or advisory fees. Separate carry vehicles can allocate performance economics among founders and employees, while still other entities may hold GP commitments, co-investments, intellectual property, or service businesses. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission distinguishes the private equity fund from the adviser that manages it and notes that the fund's offering documents and agreements govern the investor's fees, expenses, and rights over the fund's life [1]. Form ADV likewise requires an adviser to disclose its business, ownership, clients, affiliations, fees, and conflicts, demonstrating that the adviser is an enterprise with its own ownership and obligations rather than merely another name for the fund [2].

This separation matters because the sources, timing, and risks of cash flow differ across the layers. A limited partner contributes capital to a particular fund and receives that fund's residual distributions after fees, expenses, and carried interest. An owner of the management company participates in the economics generated by providing services across multiple products and vintages, subject to the purchased perimeter and allocation agreements. Public alternative managers describe fee-earning or fee-generating assets under management as the portion of assets on which the manager is entitled to earn management fees or performance revenues, while warning that the definition and fee base vary by vehicle [5][6]. Ownership of the manager is therefore an exposure to a changing operating business, not a static pro rata claim on headline assets under management.

Management companies became valuable because a successful fund sponsor can repeat the fundraising and management cycle. The first fund creates a track record and institutional infrastructure; a successor fund may preserve the same strategy at a larger scale; adjacent products may extend the franchise into new asset classes, geographies, client channels, or vehicle forms. The result can be a portfolio of fee contracts and contingent performance claims rather than one self-liquidating pool. TPG contrasts this "regenerating" exposure with a secondary purchase of an existing fund interest, whose portfolio generally runs off as assets are sold [11]. The author's synthesis is that the management company's central asset is not assets under management by itself. It is the institution's demonstrated ability to convert trust, performance, relationships, and operating capability into profitable mandates without degrading returns or alignment.

Minority ownership in alternative-asset managers existed before it became a dedicated investment strategy, but specialist GP-stakes platforms made the exposure investable at portfolio scale. A GP-stakes transaction typically sells a non-controlling interest in one or more management-company, general-partner, carry, or balance-sheet entities. The sale can be primary, with proceeds entering the firm to finance GP commitments, new products, hiring, technology, or acquisitions; secondary, with proceeds paid to founders or legacy owners for liquidity; or a blend of both [9][10]. Investcorp describes minority stakes as a way to buy out inactive owners, redistribute equity to active leaders, establish a third-party valuation, finance retention, and support succession or business development [12]. Those uses are economically different. Capital that strengthens the franchise may increase future earning power, while an exclusively secondary sale may primarily diversify a seller's personal wealth. Neither use is automatically good or bad, but the buyer must identify which one is occurring.

Empirical evidence challenges a simple adverse-selection story. Gahng and Jackson study sales of private-equity GP cash-flow claims and report that specialist repeat buyers tended to select larger and better-performing firms; seller assets under management, income, investment, and operating scale subsequently increased, while fund performance did not deteriorate in their tests [4]. They also find that contracts selling more performance-insensitive management-fee income relative to performance-sensitive income can mitigate adverse-selection and moral-hazard concerns [4]. This working paper does not prove that every seller is high quality or that every stake structure preserves alignment. It establishes that partial monetization need not mechanically imply deteriorating incentives and that contract design is part of the economic explanation.

The growth of GP stakes also exposes a boundary between the management company and the funds it advises. The SEC warns that private-equity firms can face conflicts when allocating fees, expenses, opportunities, and services among the adviser, multiple funds, affiliates, and portfolio companies [1]. ILPA recommends disclosure of GP ownership and proposed transfers, including the transaction's rationale, effect on long-term cash flows, and changes to fund- and GP-level economics [3]. A stake investor may benefit when the manager raises a larger successor fund, launches another product, or earns more affiliate revenue, while an existing limited partner may bear dilution of attention, new conflicts, or higher organizational complexity. The same event can therefore create value at the firm level and risk at the fund level.

This topic focuses on the ownership and valuation of the management franchise. It does not re-explain every limited-partnership waterfall, fund-liquidity provision, or portfolio-company valuation method. Those are necessary inputs, but the object being valued here is the firm behind the funds: its recurring fee margin, contingent performance economics, balance-sheet exposure, people, governance, and ability to raise and retain capital across cycles.

## Core Concepts

### Separate the fund, GP, adviser, carry vehicle, and owners

A reliable analysis starts with an entity-and-rights map. The fund owns the investment portfolio. The GP or equivalent governing entity exercises authority under the fund agreement. The adviser or management company performs investment and operating services under a management contract. Carry vehicles receive performance allocations and divide them among eligible participants. Employees may own equity in the management company, points in one or more carry pools, or both. The GP commitment may be funded by founders, the management company, a financing vehicle, or an outside investor. Form ADV and fund documents can identify parts of this structure, but private managers may use multiple related entities and privately negotiated allocation arrangements [1][2][10].

The deal perimeter determines what the stake buyer actually owns. A percentage interest in the management company may entitle the buyer to a share of net management-fee earnings but exclude legacy carry, future carry, or balance-sheet investments. Another transaction may convey separate percentages of management-fee earnings, performance-related earnings, and GP commitment returns. Petershill reports that its share of a partner firm's performance and balance-sheet economics can be lower than its share of fee-related earnings, illustrating why a single headline ownership percentage can be misleading [7]. The buyer must reconcile legal ownership with an economic schedule by entity, fund, vintage, product, and revenue stream.

### Management fees become revenue only after identifying the fee base

Management fees are usually the most visible recurring revenue stream, but headline assets under management are not the revenue base. Blackstone defines fee-earning assets under management as the portion of total assets on which it is entitled to earn management fees or performance revenues and states that the fee basis varies by vehicle [5]. Apollo similarly distinguishes total AUM from fee-generating AUM and identifies committed but uninvested capital that may produce future fees [6]. Petershill defines fee-paying AUM as the capital base on which partner firms are entitled to receive management fees and excludes, among other items, co-investments that generally do not pay fees [7].

A valuation model should therefore build fees contract by contract. Closed-end funds may charge on commitments during the investment period and on invested cost, net invested capital, or another base thereafter. Open-ended or perpetual vehicles may charge on net asset value, gross assets, or committed capital. Separate accounts can have negotiated rates and breakpoints. Offsets can reduce the fee retained by the manager when portfolio companies or affiliates pay transaction, monitoring, or advisory charges. Fee waivers, founder classes, co-investments, and seed arrangements can create AUM that carries little or no management fee [3][5][6]. The author's assessment is that "fee-paying AUM x headline rate" is only a first diagnostic; a defensible forecast requires the actual fee base, rate, step-down date, offsets, term, and probability of extension or liquidation for every material mandate.

### Fee-related earnings are an operating margin, not gross fees

Management fees support the organization before they reward owners. Salaries, bonuses, benefits, data, technology, legal and compliance functions, fundraising, offices, insurance, administration, and other operating costs consume revenue. Public managers use fee-related earnings, or FRE, to separate recurring operating economics from more realization-dependent results. Blackstone defines FRE as management and advisory fees net of reductions and offsets, plus certain recurring fee-related performance revenues, less fee-related compensation and other operating expenses [5]. Apollo similarly defines FRE as management fees, capital-solutions and related fees, and specified recurring performance fees, less compensation and non-compensation expenses [6]. Petershill describes partner FRE as management-fee-related earnings and reports a partner FRE margin that links net fee revenue to the associated cost base [7].

These definitions are not uniform accounting standards. Each issuer uses its own adjustments, and privately held firms may present highly adjusted measures without audited management-company statements. PwC recommends reconciling claimed FRE, performance-related earnings, and distributable earnings to historical financials and mapping how management fees and carry flow through the legal entities [10]. A buyer should normalize partner compensation required to retain the team, remove temporary cost deferrals, identify expenses shifted to funds or affiliates, and include the continuing cost of compliance and product support. An apparently high margin can reflect genuine scale, an underbuilt platform, aggressive cost allocation, or founders taking compensation through distributions rather than payroll.

### Performance-related earnings are valuable but less predictable

Carried interest and incentive fees connect the manager's earnings to fund performance. Blackstone describes carried interest as the performance allocation to which a fund's general partner or affiliate is entitled and notes that a clawback can arise when later losses cause cumulative carry paid to exceed the final entitlement [5]. Apollo defines performance fees as allocations, distributions, or contractual fees based on fund or underlying-investment performance and notes that results can depend on hurdles, high-water marks, realization timing, and market conditions [6]. Petershill distinguishes realized performance revenues from fee-related earnings and measures its proportionate share after associated performance-related expenses [7].

The valuation consequence is that accrued carry is not equivalent to cash. It depends on current marks, future exits, fund-level loss netting, hurdles, catch-ups, escrow, clawback, tax, and employee sharing. Future-fund carry is even more remote because it requires a successful fundraise, deployment, investment performance, and realization. A model should separate realized but unpaid carry, accrued carry on existing assets, expected carry from undeployed commitments, and carry from hypothetical future funds. Higher discount rates, explicit scenario probabilities, or both are appropriate for more contingent streams. Petershill's 2024 annual report illustrates this distinction by applying a much higher weighted-average discount rate to private-markets performance-fee-related earnings than to fee-related earnings [7].

### Balance-sheet investments add alignment, capital needs, and volatility

Managers often invest alongside limited partners through GP commitments, seed investments, co-investments, or strategic holdings. A stake buyer may share in the resulting investment income if those assets fall within the perimeter. TPG and Investcorp identify balance-sheet returns as a distinct component of GP-stake economics alongside fee and performance income [11][12]. These positions can align owners with fund investors and provide fee-free exposure to underlying strategies, but they also make the management company more capital intensive and expose distributions to portfolio valuations, capital calls, leverage, and liquidity cycles.

The correct treatment is not to capitalize balance-sheet earnings as if they were recurring fees. The assets should be valued separately, net of associated debt, unfunded commitments, taxes, and any employee participation. The forecast should also reserve capital for future GP commitments. A growing manager can report strong fee income while consuming cash because every larger successor fund requires a larger sponsor commitment. The author's synthesis is that growth creates a financing loop: successful fundraising expands the fee base, but it can also increase working capital, hiring, distribution, regulatory, and GP-commitment requirements before the new economics mature.

### Fundraising converts reputation into duration

Management-fee durability depends on both locked-in existing contracts and the ability to raise successor or adjacent products. Existing closed-end funds can provide several years of contracted fees, but step-downs and expirations eventually reduce the base. New fee-paying assets must replace runoff before they create net growth. Apollo warns that failure to raise sufficient capital can reduce AUM, fees, performance income, and profitability, and that fee reductions without corresponding cost reductions would impair results [6]. Blackstone likewise states that poor fund performance can lower AUM, management fees, investment returns, and performance revenues [5].

A fundraising forecast should distinguish gross capital raised from net fee-base growth. Relevant deductions include expiring fee periods, realizations, redemptions, lost accounts, fee waivers, co-investments, and products that do not begin charging until deployment. It should also distinguish one flagship franchise from repeatable breadth. A manager with several strategies may diversify fee runoff, but product proliferation can stretch the team, introduce cross-fund conflicts, and weaken performance. Client concentration matters at two levels: a few LPs may dominate commitments to individual products, while a few products may dominate the firm's fee profit.

### The franchise is people-dependent even when contracts are long-lived

Alternative managers are asset-light in physical capital but intensive in judgment, trust, and relationships. Investment performance may depend on a founder, a small investment committee, a sourcing network, or a particular portfolio manager. Fundraising may depend on separate client leaders whose departure can weaken the franchise even when portfolio decisions remain stable. Operations, valuation, legal, compliance, and finance personnel can also be critical because institutional clients underwrite the platform as well as the strategy.

A GP-stakes transaction can fund succession and spread ownership to the next generation, but it can also let founders diversify before authority and economics have moved to successors. ILPA treats orderly ownership transition and transparent key-person succession as central to alignment [3]. Morgan Lewis describes transactions that pair partner-equity liquidity rules with vesting, service commitments, transition obligations, restrictive covenants, and broader ownership for next-generation leaders [9]. The owner's task is to determine whether the deal institutionalizes the business or merely monetizes the people on whom it still depends.

### Minority ownership requires negotiated information and protective rights

A GP-stakes buyer usually accepts non-control because founders and active professionals must continue to run the investment organization. Non-control is not the same as no governance. Typical protections can include financial reporting, budgets, inspection rights, consent over fundamental changes, pre-emption, transfer restrictions, tag-along rights, limits on leverage, key-person provisions, and approval of related-party transactions. Morgan Lewis reports that management generally retains operating autonomy while the minority investor receives consent rights over matters that fundamentally change the business, plus negotiated transfer, tag, drag, or put mechanisms [9]. PwC emphasizes governing agreements, allocation mechanics, buy-in and buy-out terms, and notice or consent rights for material events such as restructurings, new products, or key-person departures [10].

The rights must match the cash-flow claim. A holder of fee-related earnings needs visibility into fee contracts, offsets, compensation, budgets, hiring, debt, and allocation of shared costs. A holder of carry needs fund-level performance, valuation, waterfall, escrow, clawback, and realization data. A holder of balance-sheet economics needs asset values, capital calls, debt, and transfer restrictions. Consolidated reporting alone can conceal value migration among entities. The author's assessment is that the central governance risk is perimeter leakage: new economics, expenses, people, or intellectual property can be placed outside the purchased entities unless the agreements govern future products and related-party allocations.

### Valuation is a sum of streams, not one multiple on AUM

A practical valuation separates at least three components. First, normalize fee-related earnings from existing and reasonably forecast products. Second, scenario-weight performance-related earnings from existing and future funds. Third, mark balance-sheet assets and liabilities, including GP commitments and debt. Corporate costs, taxes, minority allocations, required reinvestment, and unfunded obligations must then be deducted. The value of excess cash should be distinguished from cash needed to operate and meet commitments.

Market multiples can inform the valuation, but comparable managers differ in strategy mix, fee duration, perpetual capital, margins, growth, performance income, insurance exposure, leverage, and public liquidity. Petershill states that it values partner firms using earnings multiples, discounted cash flows, and recent comparable transactions; its market approach applies multiples to current operating income, while its income approach discounts estimated future cash flows [7]. It identifies future fundraising, fund performance, exit timing, discount rates, and multiples as significant assumptions [7]. Investcorp likewise describes projected management-fee income, net carry, and investment activity as the primary valuation building blocks [12].

A defensible model should explicitly fade uncertain growth. Existing fee contracts may support a relatively visible forecast, but successor funds should not automatically be larger, equally priced, or raised on schedule. Carry should not receive the same multiple as stable after-compensation fee profit. Terminal value should reflect the chance that the firm fails to replace expiring funds, loses key people, faces fee compression, or requires more owner capital. Sensitivity analysis should vary fund sizes, raise dates, fee rates, margins, carry realizations, discount rates, and terminal assumptions rather than presenting one precise value.

### Leverage can turn durable fees into a fixed claim

Management-fee profit can support debt because existing funds often pay fees under multi-year contracts. Debt may finance the stake purchase, GP commitments, partner liquidity, or distributions. It can improve equity returns when fundraising and realizations proceed as expected. It can also consume the very recurring cash flow that makes the investment attractive. A downside case should combine slower fundraising, fee step-downs, higher compensation, delayed carry, and continuing capital calls. The author's inversion test is simple: if contracted fees fall to the modeled runoff path and carry does not arrive, can the firm still retain its team, service debt, meet GP commitments, and operate without selling strategic assets or raising capital on weak terms?

### Exit is contractual optionality, not an assumed public market

GP stakes are commonly underwritten as long-duration assets. Petershill states that its partner-firm investments are long term and that it has no general exit strategy, expecting recurring revenue instead [7]. Distributions from fee, performance, and investment income may therefore be the primary return mechanism. Morgan Lewis describes negotiated liquidity after an initial lockup through third-party transfers, tag-along participation in partner sales, drag-along provisions in control sales, and in some cases put rights triggered by time or specified events [9]. Investcorp identifies asset sales, portfolio sales or listings, recapitalizations, securitizations, continuation vehicles, and LP- or GP-led secondaries as possible liquidity routes [13].

Each route changes risk rather than eliminating it. A strategic sale may require founder cooperation and regulatory or client consent. A manager buyback needs financing. A portfolio sale can create a discount for heterogeneity. A securitization adds leverage. A continuation vehicle transfers valuation and conflict questions into a new fund. An IPO requires scale, governance, disclosure, and public-market appetite. Exit value should therefore be scenario evidence, not the plug that makes an otherwise weak valuation work.

## Evidence

### Public managers show the operating-company economics

Blackstone's 2025 Form 10-K provides a direct decomposition of a scaled alternative manager. It distinguishes total AUM from fee-earning AUM, defines performance revenues to include carried interest and incentive fees, and defines FRE as net management and advisory fees plus specified recurring performance revenues less compensation and operating expense [5]. It also states that poor investment performance can reduce asset values, management fees, investment returns, and performance revenues, and that carry can be subject to clawback [5]. These disclosures support three propositions: gross AUM is not the fee base, fee revenue is not owner profit, and performance economics remain contingent after accrual.

Apollo's 2025 Form 10-K reaches the same structure through somewhat different definitions. It separates total AUM, fee-generating AUM, future fee potential, and performance-fee-eligible AUM; defines FRE after compensation and operating costs; and warns that fundraising failure, fee compression, cost rigidity, or poor performance can reduce fees and profitability [6]. Apollo also notes that reported AUM can include assets for which it earns service fees rather than management or advisory fees and that its AUM definition differs from regulatory AUM [6]. The evidence therefore cautions against comparing managers by one reported AUM number without reconstructing the underlying economics.

These filings are evidence from large public firms, not a guarantee that a private boutique has the same durability or reporting quality. Scale can create product breadth, distribution, compliance capacity, and operating leverage, but it can also introduce insurance exposure, acquisition accounting, complex compensation, or business lines absent from a smaller GP. The appropriate use of the public firms is to identify economic categories and risk factors, then adjust for the target's actual model.

### Petershill supplies a portfolio-level GP-stakes case

Petershill Partners' 2024 annual report is unusually direct evidence because its stated business was ownership of non-controlling interests in alternative managers. It reported ownership-weighted fee-paying AUM as the share of partner-firm fee-paying AUM represented by its stakes and described fee-related earnings, realized performance revenue, and realized investment income as distinct components of partner distributable earnings [7]. It also reported that management-fee income usually represented a set percentage of the net management fees earned by the underlying firm, while its percentages of performance and balance-sheet economics could differ [7]. This confirms that a GP stake is a negotiated bundle of claims rather than one uniform percentage of every cash flow.

The annual report also explains how such stakes are valued. Petershill used earnings multiples, discounted cash flows, and recent comparable transactions, with forecast earnings, future fundraising, fund performance, exit timing, discount rates, and valuation multiples among the significant inputs [7]. For 2024 it disclosed materially different discount rates for private-markets fee-related earnings and performance-fee-related earnings, reflecting their different uncertainty [7]. The report's audited Level 3 framework does not make valuation objective: it explicitly relies on unobservable forecasts. It does, however, provide a transparent example of why fee profit and carry should be valued separately.

Petershill also demonstrates the distinction between economic cash flow and liquidity. It characterized the investments as long term and generally held without a planned exit, even though it completed selected disposals [7]. The author's interpretation is that the asset can return capital through distributions for years before any sale. A valuation that depends mainly on terminal resale should therefore be tested against a yield-and-runoff case.

### Specialist platforms show both scale and valuation uncertainty

Blue Owl's June 2025 Form 10-Q reported continued fundraising and appreciation in its GP minority-stakes strategy, along with cash distributions from that strategy [8]. It defined realized proceeds as cash distributions to investors and unrealized value as product NAV, while cautioning that unrealized values may not be realized at the amounts indicated [8]. The filing also described fair-value processes using board or valuation-committee judgment, third-party inputs, comparable companies, discounted cash flow, and market factors [8]. These are primary disclosures from an active specialist platform and support the existence of recurring distributions, but they also confirm that a large part of reported value can remain estimate-dependent.

Manager-sponsored papers provide additional transaction detail but require source caution. TPG describes GP stakes as sharing management-fee or fee-related earnings, carry, balance-sheet returns, and eventual equity value, and contrasts their regenerating exposure with self-liquidating fund secondaries [11]. Investcorp identifies management fees, carry on existing and future funds, and balance-sheet returns as separate cash-flow streams, and it emphasizes owner liquidity, succession, team retention, and growth capital as transaction motives [12][13]. These sources are useful descriptions by experienced market participants, but their favorable claims about attractiveness are investment-manager views rather than neutral proof.

### The academic evidence is favorable but bounded

Gahng and Jackson's working paper examines GP-stake sales and reports positive selection by specialist buyers, post-sale growth in seller AUM and income, and no deterioration in fund performance in their tested sample [4]. The paper further argues that selling a greater share of performance-insensitive management fees relative to performance-sensitive income can mitigate agency concerns [4]. Its method is more probative than transaction marketing because it analyzes a sample of firms and outcomes rather than selected case studies.

The evidence remains observational. Firms choose whether and when to sell, buyers screen targets, deal terms are negotiated, and private data limit direct comparison. Better firms may both attract stake buyers and grow afterward without the transaction causing the growth. The paper's result therefore rejects the claim that stake sales necessarily worsen alignment; it does not establish that every deal improves performance or that buyer support causes seller success. A prudent underwriter should treat the research as a prior and still test the particular seller's use of proceeds, retained incentives, governance, and post-deal authority.

### Governance evidence identifies recurring points of conflict

The SEC states that private-equity advisers can face conflicts across the adviser, its funds, affiliates, and portfolio companies and must disclose conflicts and allocate expenses consistently with fiduciary obligations [1]. ILPA goes further for an ownership transfer: the GP should tell limited partners why the transaction is occurring, how it affects distributable and long-term cash flows, and how fund- and GP-level economics change [3]. ILPA also recommends disclosure when an LP advisory committee member owns a minority interest in the management company, because the stake can affect the member's conflict position [3].

Practitioner sources show how contracts address these risks. Morgan Lewis describes minority consent rights over fundamental changes, transfer restrictions, tags, drags, puts, vesting, and service commitments [9]. PwC emphasizes legal-entity mapping, audited or reconciled economics, governance agreements, key-person diligence, and notification or consent for restructurings or personnel departures [10]. These sources do not prove that negotiated rights will be exercised effectively. They establish which failure modes experienced deal counsel and advisers repeatedly address.

### What the evidence does not establish

There is no universal public dataset that reveals every private manager's fee contracts, compensation, carry allocation, side letters, owner rights, or transaction price. Public-manager metrics are not standardized, specialist-platform marks are Level 3 estimates, academic samples are selected from observable transactions, and manager white papers have commercial incentives. Jurisdiction, tax structure, regulatory status, fund strategy, and partnership documents can materially change the economics [2][7][10].

The evidence therefore supports a framework rather than a universal valuation multiple. Management-fee profit can be durable, performance income can create upside, and minority capital can help institutionalize a firm. It also shows that those benefits depend on contract-specific fee duration, cost discipline, fundraising, investment results, people, allocation rules, leverage, and enforceable rights. A GP stake is not automatically a bond-like asset, a private-equity asset, or a public asset-manager stock; it combines features of all three in proportions set by the deal.

## Implications

### For a GP-stakes investor: underwrite the institution before the stream

The first task is to reconstruct historical owner earnings. Start with fund- and account-level fee contracts, then bridge gross management and advisory revenue through offsets, compensation, operating expense, taxes, and required reinvestment to distributable fee profit. Reconcile management's adjusted metrics with audited statements, tax returns, bank records, partner distributions, and legal entities. PwC's warning that private management companies may lack audited standalone financials makes this reconciliation a diligence requirement rather than an accounting refinement [10].

The second task is to build a runoff case. Assume no new funds beyond legally committed or highly visible products, apply contractual step-downs, deduct the cost base needed to service existing vehicles, fund capital calls, and test debt. If the stake is not attractive under a conservative distribution-and-runoff path, the price is mainly a bet on future fundraising or exit. That may still be rational, but it should be labeled as growth underwriting rather than recurring-income protection.

The third task is to underwrite future fundraising from evidence. Examine realized and unrealized performance by vintage, loss ratios, public-market equivalents where appropriate, attribution, dispersion, client re-up rates, concentration, pipeline quality, strategy capacity, and fee changes. Distinguish organic re-ups from capital raised through a new owner or distribution channel. Compare gross raised capital with net fee-paying growth after realizations and expirations. Blackstone, Apollo, and Petershill all distinguish the fee-bearing base from headline AUM, so a target forecast that does not do so is incomplete [5][6][7].

The fourth task is to map every person to decisions, clients, and economics. Test whether the next generation has led investments, losses, exits, fundraising, and difficult client conversations. Review equity, carry, vesting, departure, non-compete where enforceable, and buyback terms. Identify whether seller liquidity is proportional to completed succession or precedes it. The author's assessment is that a stake transaction creates durable alignment only when authority, ownership, and responsibility move together.

### For valuation: separate quality, duration, growth, and optionality

A value-investing approach should avoid a single multiple on current FRE. The same dollar of fee profit can deserve different value depending on contract duration, fund concentration, renewal evidence, client quality, strategy capacity, team depth, cost rigidity, capital needs, and governance. The author's proposed decomposition is:

1. Existing-contract value: after-compensation fee profit from current mandates, modeled through contractual step-down and termination.
2. Renewal value: probability-weighted successor funds from established strategies, net of fundraising cost, larger GP commitments, and capacity limits.
3. Expansion value: new products, geographies, channels, or acquisitions, included only where capabilities and demand are evidenced.
4. Performance value: scenario-weighted carry or incentive fees, net of employee sharing, tax, escrow, and clawback.
5. Balance-sheet value: marked assets less debt, unfunded commitments, and trapped or required operating capital.
6. Control and illiquidity adjustment: the effect of minority rights, transfer limits, information access, leverage, and realistic exit routes.

This is the author's synthesis from the cash-flow and valuation methods disclosed by public managers and specialist holders [5][6][7]. It prevents stable existing fees from being blended with speculative future carry and then capitalized at one optimistic rate. It also exposes where a seller and buyer disagree: fund size, raise timing, margin, carry conversion, terminal value, or governance.

Margin analysis should use normalized compensation. A founder who accepts below-market cash pay while retaining distributions has not created a permanently low cost base. A new owner may need to professionalize finance, compliance, technology, cybersecurity, client reporting, and human resources. Conversely, genuine scale can allow several products to share infrastructure. The buyer should model fixed, semi-variable, and variable costs separately and test whether fee compression or a failed fundraise can be absorbed without losing key people.

### For founders and active partners: choose proceeds that strengthen continuity

A primary transaction can finance GP commitments, seed products, recruit teams, improve systems, or acquire capabilities without selling control [9][10][12]. A secondary component can diversify founders, resolve legacy ownership, and make room for successors. The durable structure links liquidity to an explicit ownership plan: inactive owners sell, active next-generation leaders gain economics, governance roles are clarified, and enough founder exposure remains to preserve alignment.

The worst reversible mistake is accepting capital whose rights or return expectations force short-term growth. A minority investor can pressure the firm indirectly through preferred returns, distribution covenants, leverage, or an expected exit even without formal control. New products can increase AUM while diluting performance and attention. The founder should model how the deal behaves after a weak vintage, missed fundraise, partner departure, or industry downturn, not only after a successful scale-up.

Transparency with fund investors is part of the transaction, not post-closing communication. ILPA recommends explaining the rationale, distributable and long-term cash-flow effects, and changes in fund- and GP-level economics before a transfer [3]. Limited partners should understand whether their fees help service acquisition debt, whether carry allocations or key-person ownership changed, whether the buyer has distribution or co-investment rights, and whether any conflicts affect allocations among products. Clear disclosure protects both the fund relationship and the franchise value the stake investor is buying.

### For limited partners: ownership change can alter the service even when the fund contract survives

An LP interest remains a claim on the fund, not the management company. A GP-stakes investor usually does not become an LP in each fund merely by buying the manager, and an LP does not own the manager merely by committing to its fund. The economic connection is indirect: the LP pays fees and shares profits under fund documents; those amounts become revenue or performance economics for manager-side entities according to the structure [1][5][6].

LP diligence after a stake sale should focus on service continuity and conflicts. Ask who sold, who received primary capital, how partner equity and carry changed, whether debt was added, what rights the buyer obtained, and which products or co-investments it may access. Review key-person, change-of-control, assignment, consent, and disclosure provisions. Examine whether the buyer sits on an LP advisory committee or has other relationships that require conflict management, a concern ILPA identifies directly [3].

A sale can improve alignment if it retires inactive owners, funds GP commitments, builds operating resilience, and gives successors a credible path to ownership. It can weaken alignment if founders monetize heavily, debt captures fee cash flow, new products stretch the team, or economics move outside the entities supporting the fund. Gahng and Jackson's evidence means the LP should not assume harm solely from the sale [4]. It does not remove the need to examine the particular transaction.

### For governance: prevent value from moving outside the purchased perimeter

The minority investor needs reports that match its claim. Monthly or quarterly management-company statements should reconcile fee-paying AUM, gross and net fees, offsets, compensation, operating expense, debt, distributions, and cash. Carry reports should show fund-level performance, unrealized and realized value, hurdles, waterfalls, employee allocations, escrow, and clawback. Balance-sheet reports should show investments, valuation methods, capital calls, debt, and liquidity. Fundraising reports should separate signed commitments, fee-paying activation, re-ups, pipeline, and expirations.

Reserved matters should focus on irreversible changes: issuing senior securities, incurring material debt, moving contracts or personnel to affiliates, changing the economic perimeter, selling control, launching conflicting businesses outside the group, amending distribution policy, or changing key allocation rules. Day-to-day investment decisions should remain with qualified professionals unless the transaction was explicitly designed otherwise. The author's synthesis is that good GP-stakes governance protects the franchise without converting a minority financial investor into a shadow portfolio manager.

Transfer and exit rights require the same precision. A tag right can protect participation in a founder sale, but only if the covered securities and consideration match. A drag can create liquidity but also force a sale at an inconvenient time; form-of-consideration and minimum-return protections matter [9][13]. A put can protect against specified failures but may be worthless if the firm lacks financing. A buyback can return ownership to partners but compete with GP commitments and operating needs. Rights should be stress-tested against the balance sheet that must honor them.

### For financing: preserve the fee stream's shock-absorbing role

The recurring fee margin is the first line of defense when carry and exits slow. Using too much debt against it can reverse that protection. Underwriters should calculate fixed-charge coverage under contractual runoff, not only under a base case with larger successor funds. They should include partner compensation needed for retention, taxes, capex, technology, compliance, and unfunded GP commitments before treating cash as distributable.

A firm with low leverage can use weak markets to hire, seed products, and support funds. A heavily levered firm may need to cut investment capacity, sell a stake, or prioritize creditors during the same period. Fund investors can then experience an indirect conflict between franchise solvency and fund service. The author's worst-case test is whether one disappointing flagship vintage triggers, in sequence, slower fundraising, fee runoff, carry delay, personnel departures, covenant pressure, and a distressed ownership transfer. Capital structure is sound only if the system can interrupt that sequence.

### For exit planning: distributions must carry the thesis

An owner should value cash distributions separately from terminal appreciation. Existing fees may support current yield, while successful future fundraising and carry create upside. If no sale occurs, the cumulative distributions and remaining runoff value should still meet a reasonable return requirement. Petershill's long-hold posture and specialist descriptions of distribution-led returns support this orientation [7][12].

Exit optionality can be preserved through transfer rights, tags, buybacks, strategic-sale provisions, and access to secondary or continuation markets [9][13]. It should not be confused with assured liquidity. The firm's culture, client consents, regulatory status, partner cooperation, and buyer concentration can all narrow the exit set. Public listing is generally a scale outcome, not a default path for a boutique. A valuation that requires multiple expansion at exit has less margin of safety than one supported by distributable earnings and conservative residual value.

### A compact owner-earnings test

The author's synthesis is a seven-question gate for a management-company or GP-stakes investment:

1. What exact legal entities and cash-flow percentages are being purchased?
2. How much after-compensation fee profit is protected by existing contracts, and when does it run off?
3. What evidence supports successor fundraising after netting expirations, realizations, fee changes, and capacity limits?
4. How much value depends on unrealized carry, future funds, or balance-sheet marks?
5. Can the team, ownership, and governance survive founder departure or a weak performance cycle?
6. Can debt, GP commitments, and operating costs be met without optimistic carry or an exit?
7. What enforceable information, consent, transfer, and liquidity rights protect the minority claim?

A strong answer does not make the investment riskless. It makes the risks visible and ties price to the durable part of the franchise. The central conclusion is that the firm behind the funds can be more durable than any one fund, but only if repeat performance, client trust, institutional ownership, and cost discipline convert temporary mandates into renewable earning power.
