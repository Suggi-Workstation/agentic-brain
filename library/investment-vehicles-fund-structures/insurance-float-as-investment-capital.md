# Insurance Float as Investment Capital -- Why the Collect-Now-Pay-Later Model Creates the Lowest-Cost Leverage in Finance

Insurance float is the pool of money an insurer holds between collecting
premiums and paying claims -- money that belongs to policyholders but is
physically in the insurer's hands and available for investment. When
underwriting is disciplined, float functions as interest-free or
negative-cost leverage: capital that costs nothing to hold, has no fixed
maturity, cannot be redeemed, and compounds indefinitely for the insurer's
benefit. Berkshire Hathaway's roughly $176 billion in insurance float at
year-end 2025, generated at a negative cost through consistent underwriting
profits, represents the most successful application of this structural
advantage in financial history. This topic examines the mechanics,
economics, risks, and conditions under which insurance float creates
permanent investment capital superior to debt, equity, or redeemable fund
structures.

## Background

The concept of insurance float as an investment asset is as old as
insurance itself, but its deliberate use as a structural source of
low-cost investment capital is a modern innovation, and its most
systematic application traces to Warren Buffett's acquisition of
National Indemnity Company in 1967. The intellectual lineage, however,
runs deeper. Insurance companies have always collected premiums before
paying claims -- the collect-now, pay-later model is intrinsic to the
contract structure. What changed was the recognition that this timing
gap, far from being a mere accounting artifact, was a source of
investable capital with unique economic properties.

Before Buffett, most insurers invested their float conservatively in
high-grade bonds, matching asset duration to liability duration in
accordance with regulatory convention and actuarial prudence. The
investment portfolio was treated as a passive reservoir, not an active
profit center. The underwriting operation was the business; investment
income was a supplement. Buffett inverted this logic. He saw that if
underwriting could be disciplined enough to produce float at zero or
negative cost, and if the float could be invested in higher-returning
assets -- equities, whole businesses, productive enterprises -- the
insurance operation became not a business that also invested, but an
investment vehicle funded by insurance.

The 1967 acquisition of National Indemnity for $8.6 million illustrates
the founding logic. National Indemnity wrote $12.7 million in premiums
and held $17 million in float, invested conservatively in bonds. For
$8.6 million -- of which only $6.7 million was Buffett's equity, the
remainder covering tangible assets -- Buffett gained control of $17
million in deployable capital. He immediately began redirecting the
float from bonds into higher-returning equities. The acquisition was
not about the insurance business per se; it was about the funding
structure the insurance business provided. The pattern was repeated
with GEICO, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group, and Berkshire
Hathaway Primary Group, each adding float pools with different
characteristics -- short-tail auto claims at GEICO, long-tail
casualty and catastrophe exposure at the reinsurance group, specialty
lines at the primary group.

The intellectual framework for understanding float as leverage was
formalized by Andrea Frazzini, David Kabiller, and Lasse Heje Pedersen
in their study "Buffett's Alpha," first published as an NBER working
paper in 2013 and later in the Financial Analysts Journal (Vol. 74,
No. 4, 2018). They decomposed Berkshire's returns and found that
Buffett applied approximately 1.7-to-1 leverage on average, funded
partly through insurance float at an average cost of about 2.2 percent
-- more than 3 percentage points below the average Treasury bill rate.
They estimated that about 36 percent of Berkshire's liabilities, on
average, consisted of insurance float. The study demonstrated that
float was not merely a side benefit of the insurance business but the
central structural mechanism enabling Berkshire's compounding record.
The Sharpe ratio of 0.79 they calculated for Berkshire was the highest
among all U.S. stocks and mutual funds with histories exceeding 30
years.

Buffett himself articulated the concept repeatedly in his shareholder
letters. In the 1997 letter he wrote: "Float is money we hold but don't
own. In an insurance operation, float arises because premiums are
received before losses are paid, an interval that sometimes extends over
many years." In the 2009 letter he described it as a "collect-now,
pay-later model" that leaves Berkshire holding large sums that will
eventually go to others, while the company gets to invest the float for
its own benefit. He measured the cost of float as the ratio of pre-tax
underwriting earnings (or losses) to average float balances, and he
insisted that the insurance business has value only if its cost of float
over time is less than the cost of obtaining funds otherwise. "But the
business is an albatross if its cost of float is higher than market rates
for money," he warned.

The model has been imitated, with varying degrees of success, by a
class of "float compounders" that includes Fairfax Financial Holdings
under Prem Watsa, Markel Corporation, and a wave of hedge fund
reinsurers that emerged in the 2000s and 2010s. Fairfax, founded in
1985 when Watsa acquired Markel Service of Canada, explicitly modeled
itself on Berkshire: disciplined underwriting to generate float, then
investment of that float in undervalued securities. By 2024 Fairfax
wrote roughly $25 billion in net premiums and produced $1.79 billion in
underwriting profit at a 92.7 percent combined ratio, with an investment
portfolio of approximately $70 billion. The hedge fund reinsurance
variant paired offshore reinsurance operations with aggressive
investment management, using the non-redeemable float to pursue
illiquid, long-horizon strategies impractical with traditional hedge
fund capital subject to redemption windows. These imitations demonstrate
that the float-as-capital model is replicable, but they also illustrate
that the advantage depends on conditions -- underwriting discipline,
regulatory environment, and the manager's investment skill -- that are
difficult to sustain.

## Core Concepts

### The Mechanics of Float Creation

Insurance float arises from a structural timing mismatch embedded in
the insurance contract. When a policyholder pays a premium, the insurer
receives cash immediately. The corresponding obligation -- paying claims
-- materializes later, sometimes much later. For property coverage, the
lag may be months: a windstorm claim is filed, adjusted, and paid within
weeks or months of the premium payment. For liability coverage, the lag
can stretch to years or decades: an asbestos exposure claim from a
policy written in the 1980s may not be resolved until the 2020s. This
timing gap means the insurer holds cash it does not yet owe, and that
cash constitutes the float.

The float is not a single pool but an accounting aggregation of several
liability categories. Berkshire's 2025 10-K filing defines the major
components as: unpaid losses and loss adjustment expenses (including
liabilities under retroactive reinsurance contracts), life and annuity
and health benefit insurance liabilities, unearned premiums (premiums
collected for coverage periods that have not yet elapsed), and certain
other insurance policyholder liabilities. These are reduced by premium
and reinsurance receivables, deferred policy acquisition costs, and
deferred charges on assumed retroactive reinsurance contracts. The net
figure is the investable pool. Discount rate changes on long-duration
insurance contracts, recorded in accumulated other comprehensive income
rather than earnings, are excluded from the float calculation because
they do not represent investable cash.

The float grows through three mechanisms. First, premium growth: more
policies written means more cash collected upfront. Second, claim
payment delay: longer-tail lines of business extend the interval between
collection and payment, keeping money in the pool longer. Third, reserve
development: when loss reserves are set conservatively and claims
ultimately cost less than reserved, the released reserves add to
investable capital. Berkshire's float grew from approximately $88
billion at the end of 2015 to $171 billion at the end of 2024 to $176
billion at the end of 2025 -- a doubling over a decade driven primarily
by premium growth and disciplined reserve management. The pool is
self-renewing: as old claims are paid, new premiums flow in to replace
them, and as the business grows, the pool grows with it. The principal
never comes due all at once; it rolls forward, decade after decade,
available for investment the entire time.

### The Cost of Float

The cost of float is the single most important metric for evaluating
whether an insurance operation creates value as an investment vehicle.
Buffett defined it as the ratio of pre-tax underwriting earnings (or
losses) to the average float balance. If underwriting is profitable --
premiums exceed claims plus expenses -- the float has a negative cost:
the insurer is effectively paid to hold and invest the money. If
underwriting is unprofitable, the float has a positive cost that must be
offset by investment returns to create net value.

The combined ratio captures underwriting performance in a single number.
It is the sum of the loss ratio (claims paid plus reserve changes
divided by earned premiums) and the expense ratio (underwriting expenses
divided by written premiums). A combined ratio below 100 percent
indicates underwriting profit; above 100 percent indicates underwriting
loss. Berkshire's property and casualty operations produced a combined
ratio of 87.1 percent in 2025, compared with a five-year average of 90.7
percent, a ten-year average of 93.0 percent, and a twenty-year average of
92.2 percent. This means Berkshire has consistently generated float at a
negative cost -- policyholders paid Berkshire to hold their money before
it was invested. In 2025 the insurance operations generated $7.258
billion in pre-tax underwriting gains, meaning the $176 billion float
pool was not merely free but positively remunerative.

The Frazzini, Kabiller, and Pedersen study quantified the structural
advantage with academic rigor. They estimated that Berkshire's insurance
float carried an average annual cost of approximately 2.2 percent, which
was more than 3 percentage points below the average Treasury bill rate
over the sample period. This is the key insight: even in years where
underwriting produced a loss, the float was cheaper than risk-free
borrowing. When underwriting produced a profit, the float was cheaper
than free -- it was negatively priced. No conventional funding source
offers this combination. Bank deposits cost 3-5 percent in interest.
Private equity funds require 8-15 percent returns from limited partners.
Corporate bonds carry coupon payments and maturity dates. Equity
dilutes ownership. Float, when underwriting is disciplined, carries none
of these costs and none of these constraints.

### Float as Permanent Capital

The permanence of float is what distinguishes it from every other form
of leverage. Bank deposits can be withdrawn, causing bank runs. Bonds
mature and must be refinanced. Hedge fund capital is subject to
redemption windows that force managers to sell positions at inopportune
times. Mutual fund shares are redeemable on demand. Private equity fund
capital is locked for a contractual period but must be returned at
maturity. Insurance float, by contrast, cannot be redeemed by
policyholders. It stays as long as the policies remain in force and the
insurer remains solvent. The insurer cannot be forced to liquidate
investments to meet float withdrawals because float is not a withdrawal
facility -- it is a liability that dissipates gradually as claims are
paid, while new premiums simultaneously replenish the pool.

This permanence creates a structural advantage that compounds over time.
Because the float does not have to be repaid on a fixed schedule, the
insurer can invest it in long-duration, illiquid, or volatile assets
without facing the forced-sale risk that destroys leveraged strategies
during drawdowns. A hedge fund using 1.7-to-1 leverage through
borrowed money would face margin calls during market downturns,
potentially forcing liquidation at the worst possible time. Berkshire,
funded by permanent float, could hold through drawdowns without any
redemption or margin pressure. The Frazzini study found that applying
1.7-to-1 leverage to the market index would have produced approximately
12.7 percent average annual returns, still 5.9 percentage points short
of Berkshire's 18.6 percent. The gap between mechanical leverage and
Berkshire's actual returns is explained by the combination of float
permanence, low cost, and Buffett's stock selection -- the three
reinforcing components of the architecture.

The distinction between permanent and redeemable capital is the
structural axis along which investment vehicles should be compared.
Redeemable structures -- mutual funds, hedge funds, closed-end funds at
maturity -- create a principal-agent conflict: the manager's optimal
time horizon may exceed the investor's redemption horizon, forcing
suboptimal liquidation. Permanent structures -- insurance float, retained
earnings in a holding company, permanent-capital vehicles -- eliminate
this conflict by removing the redemption option. The capital stays until
the liability is settled or the business is wound down, not when the
capital provider chooses to exit.

### Underwriting Discipline as the Gatekeeper

The entire float-as-capital architecture depends on one condition:
underwriting discipline. If the insurer systematically underprices risk,
the float is not free -- it carries a positive cost that can exceed
market borrowing rates. Buffett's warning was explicit: an insurance
business is valuable only if its cost of float over time is less than
the cost of obtaining funds otherwise; it is "an albatross" if the cost
exceeds market rates. The discipline required is not occasional or
average-case; it is persistent, multi-decade, and tested by cycles.

The underwriting cycle creates the central tension. In soft markets,
excess capital compresses premiums below actuarially adequate levels as
participants compete for float volume. The incentive to grow float --
more premiums means more investable capital -- can lead insurers to
underprice risk, eroding the very advantage the float is supposed to
provide. Eventually, accumulated losses or a catastrophic event destroys
enough capital to trigger a hard market: premiums rise, terms tighten,
and capacity contracts. The cycle repeats because the incentive to grow
float is always present and the feedback loop between underpricing and
loss realization operates on a delay, not in real time. Berkshire's
approach has been to resist this cycle -- to walk away from business when
pricing is inadequate rather than write policies at a loss to maintain
float volume. The 2025 annual report notes that the company expects to
write less property and casualty business for a period as the market
softens, accepting reduced float growth rather than compromising
underwriting standards.

The combined ratio is the summary metric of discipline, but it is only
as reliable as the reserve estimates behind it. Because the true cost of
claims is not known at the time premiums are collected -- especially for
long-tail liability lines -- reserve adequacy is the hidden variable.
Reserves set too low produce artificially favorable combined ratios in
the short term, followed by adverse development that restates years of
apparent profitability. Reserves set conservatively produce apparent
underperformance that reverses favorably when claims cost less than
reserved. Berkshire's consistent reserve discipline, with conservative
estimation and favorable development over decades, is a core component of
the float's negative cost. Rating agencies like Fitch evaluate reserve
adequacy as a distinct rating driver, examining reserve leverage relative
to capital, development trends, and the gap between carried reserves and
actuarial best estimates. Consistent favorable development is viewed
positively; adverse development or reserve strengthening is viewed
negatively.

### Catastrophe Risk and Float Volatility

The structural weakness of float as capital is catastrophe exposure.
Insurance liabilities are not uniform -- property catastrophe
reinsurance, for example, involves low-frequency, high-severity events
that can produce correlated losses across an entire portfolio in a single
event. A major hurricane making landfall in a densely insured region can
generate claims that deplete reserves and force the insurer to liquidate
investments at a loss to meet obligations. This is the float model's
Achilles heel: the same timing mismatch that creates the investable pool
also creates the risk that claims arrive in clusters that overwhelm the
pool.

Catastrophe risk imposes two constraints on float as investment capital.
First, it requires a liquidity buffer. Berkshire held approximately $369
billion in cash and Treasury bills at year-end 2025 -- more than double
its $176 billion float -- precisely to meet extraordinary claim
obligations without forced liquidation of long-duration investments. This
liquidity buffer is a cost: capital held in low-yielding Treasury bills
is capital not deployed in higher-returning assets. The buffer's size is
a function of the catastrophe exposure profile: an insurer with heavy
property catastrophe concentration needs a larger buffer than one
focused on attritional, uncorrelated lines. Second, catastrophe risk
introduces float volatility. In a catastrophe year, claims can exceed
premiums and reserves, producing an underwriting loss that raises the
cost of float and can shrink the pool if claims draw down the liability
balance faster than new premiums replenish it. The 2025 annual report
acknowledges that the 87.1 percent combined ratio was aided by an
unusually benign Atlantic hurricane season -- no hurricane made U.S.
landfall for the first time in a decade -- and that wildfire-related
losses in Los Angeles at the start of the year demonstrated the
randomness of catastrophe exposure. "Nature controls the winds," the
report states, a reminder that underwriting discipline cannot eliminate
catastrophe risk, only price for it and reserve against it.

Research on U.S. insurance industry catastrophe capacity provides
context. A study by Dionne and Desjardins (updated 2024) found that U.S.
insurers could pay 98 percent of a $200 billion loss in 2020, compared
to 81 percent in 1997, reflecting significant capacity growth. However,
climate risk is increasing catastrophe exposure: average home premiums
rose 22 percent from 2020 to 2023, and the coefficient of variation for
industry losses showed increased relative volatility in recent years.
The implication for float as capital is that catastrophe risk is not
static -- it evolves with climate patterns, construction density, and
insurance penetration, requiring continuous recalibration of reserves
and liquidity buffers.

### Comparative Vehicle Architecture

The float-as-capital model should be understood comparatively, against
the alternative vehicle structures available for pooling and compounding
investment capital. Each structure has a distinct funding profile,
incentive structure, and set of constraints.

Closed-end limited partnerships -- the private equity and venture capital
model -- lock capital for a decade or more, giving the general partner a
stable pool. But the capital must be returned at maturity, the general
partner charges management fees and carried interest (typically 2 and 20),
and the closed-end structure creates a J-curve return pattern driven by
the timing mismatch between costs and harvest. The capital is temporary,
not permanent.

Open-end mutual funds and ETFs pool retail and institutional capital
with daily or intraday liquidity. The redeemability of shares forces
managers to hold liquid reserves against redemption risk, constraining
the ability to invest in illiquid or long-duration assets. The capital
is permanent only in the aggregate -- individual investors can exit at
any time -- and the manager's time horizon is subordinated to the
investor's liquidity preference.

Hedge funds charge performance-based fees and may impose lockup periods
and gates, but they remain subject to redemption windows. The hedge fund
reinsurance model attempts to solve this by embedding the investment
strategy inside an insurance entity, where the float cannot be redeemed.
The structure works, but it introduces insurance regulation, underwriting
risk, and the obligation to maintain reserves and capital adequacy --
costs and constraints absent from a pure investment partnership.

Insurance float within a permanent-capital holding company -- the
Berkshire model -- combines non-redeemable funding, negative cost
through underwriting profit, long-duration investment discretion, and
the ability to move capital across businesses and securities without
redemption pressure. The trade-offs are catastrophe risk, regulatory
capital requirements, the obligation to maintain liquidity buffers, and
the dependence on underwriting discipline to keep the cost of float
below market rates. No structure is universally superior; each is
optimized for a different set of constraints. The float model's
distinctive advantage is the combination of low cost and permanence,
which allows compounding over decades without the forced-sale risk that
constrains redeemable structures.

## Evidence

### Frazzini, Kabiller, and Pedersen: "Buffett's Alpha" (2018)

The most rigorous academic decomposition of Berkshire's returns was
published by Andrea Frazzini (AQR Capital Management), David Kabiller
(AQR), and Lasse Heje Pedersen (NYU Stern) in the Financial Analysts
Journal, Vol. 74, No. 4, 2018, originally released as an NBER working
paper in 2013. The study compiled Berkshire's balance sheets and stock
price from 1976 through 2017 and decomposed returns into leverage,
factor exposures, and stock selection.

The key findings: Berkshire achieved a Sharpe ratio of 0.79, the highest
among all U.S. stocks and mutual funds with histories exceeding 30 years.
One dollar invested in Berkshire in 1976 would have been worth $3,685 in
2017. The authors estimated average leverage of 1.7-to-1, explaining why
Berkshire's stock volatility (24.9 percent) exceeded the market's (15.8
percent) despite holdings in relatively stable businesses. Applying the
same 1.7-to-1 leverage to the market index would have produced
approximately 12.7 percent average annual returns, still 5.9 percentage
points below Berkshire's 18.6 percent -- meaning leverage alone did not
explain the performance.

The leverage composition was critical. Approximately 36 percent of
Berkshire's liabilities, on average, consisted of insurance float. The
float carried an average annual cost of about 2.2 percent, more than 3
percentage points below the average Treasury bill rate. Berkshire also
benefited from a AAA credit rating from 1989 to 2009, giving cheaper
access to debt than competitors, and from deferred tax liabilities --
another form of low-cost, non-interest-bearing funding. The authors
characterized Buffett's investment style as a systematic preference for
cheap, safe, high-quality stocks (captured by the Betting Against Beta
and Quality Minus Junk factors), magnified by consistent, low-cost
leverage applied over six decades. The float's permanence was central:
it provided the stable leverage that allowed the style to compound
without the forced liquidation risk that destroys leveraged strategies
during drawdowns. The study's method was factor regression: Berkshire's
stock returns were regressed on market, size, value, momentum, betting-
against-beta, and quality-minus-junk factors, with leverage estimated
from balance sheet data. The alpha was reduced but not eliminated when
these factors were controlled for, indicating that Buffett's edge was
partly style-based and partly idiosyncratic stock selection, amplified by
the float-funded leverage structure.

### Berkshire Hathaway 2025 Annual Report and 10-K

The 2025 annual report provides primary-source data on the mechanics and
scale of Berkshire's float operation. At year-end 2025, insurance float
stood at approximately $176 billion, up from $171 billion at year-end
2024, $169 billion at year-end 2023, and $138 billion at year-end 2020.
The float has grown from approximately $88 billion at the end of 2015 --
a doubling over a decade. The 10-K defines float as the approximate net
policyholder funds generated through underwriting activities that are
held for investment, comprising unpaid losses and loss adjustment
expenses, life and annuity and health benefit liabilities, unearned
premiums, and other insurance policyholder liabilities, net of premium
and reinsurance receivables, deferred policy acquisition costs, and
deferred charges on retroactive reinsurance.

The combined ratio for property and casualty operations was 87.1 percent
in 2025, compared with a five-year average of 90.7 percent, ten-year
average of 93.0 percent, and twenty-year average of 92.2 percent. The
insurance operations generated $7.258 billion in pre-tax underwriting
gains in 2025, and the average cost of float was negative in each of the
three years ending December 31, 2025. Insurance-investment income for
2025 was $12.513 billion. The insurance businesses returned $29 billion
to Berkshire in 2025 through ordinary dividends, with regulatory
permission for up to $31 billion without prior approval. This
dividend flow is the mechanism by which float-generated investment
returns and underwriting profits move from the insurance subsidiaries
to the holding company for redeployment.

The report acknowledges the catastrophe risk dimension: the year began
with significant wildfire-related losses in Los Angeles, but the Atlantic
hurricane season was unusually benign -- no hurricane made U.S. landfall
for the first time in a decade. Management explicitly noted that the
87.1 percent combined ratio reflects a favorable catastrophe year and
that the reinsurance industry is entering a pricing downcycle, meaning
premium growth will face headwinds. This candor is itself a signal of
the underwriting discipline that sustains the model: the company does
not extrapolate favorable conditions into the future.

### Fairfax Financial Holdings: The Replication Case

Fairfax Financial Holdings, founded by Prem Watsa in 1985 through the
acquisition of Markel Service of Canada, provides evidence that the
float-as-capital model is replicable beyond Berkshire. Fairfax explicitly
adopted the Berkshire blueprint: disciplined property and casualty
underwriting to generate float, then investment of that float in
undervalued securities with a long-term compounding orientation.

By 2024, Fairfax wrote approximately $25 billion in net premiums and
produced $1.79 billion in underwriting profit at a 92.7 percent combined
ratio. The investment portfolio stood at approximately $70 billion.
Fairfax's decentralized model -- over 20 autonomous subsidiaries making
local underwriting and pricing decisions -- mirrors Berkshire's
delegation philosophy. The company's contrarian moves during the 2008
financial crisis, including credit default swap positions that produced
$2.1 billion in gains, demonstrated the advantage of permanent float:
Fairfax could hold contrarian, illiquid positions through the crisis
without redemption pressure forcing liquidation.

The Fairfax case also illustrates the model's dependence on the
individual manager's judgment. Watsa, now 75, is transferring operational
control to a new generation of subsidiary managers and investment
professionals. The open question -- whether the discipline that produced
underwriting profits and contrarian investment returns can survive the
founder's departure -- is structurally identical to the succession
question Berkshire faced with the transition from Warren Buffett to Greg
Abel. The model's durability depends on institutionalizing the
underwriting discipline and investment temperament that the founder
embodied, not merely on the structural advantage of the float itself.

### U.S. Insurance Industry Catastrophe Capacity Research

Research by Dionne and Desjardins, updated through 2024 and published as
a CIRRELT working paper, provides empirical evidence on the industry's
capacity to absorb catastrophe losses -- the tail risk that threatens
float stability. Building on the theoretical framework of Cummins et al.
(2002), which showed that catastrophe capacity is maximized when
insurers maintain underwriting portfolios correlated with aggregate
industry losses, the study estimated the U.S. property-liability
insurance sector's ability to pay catastrophe losses.

Key findings: U.S. insurers could pay 98 percent of a $200 billion loss
in 2020, compared to 81 percent in 1997 -- a significant improvement in
catastrophe capacity. However, total net losses grew from $461 billion
in 2020 to $633 billion in 2023, while total industry capital rose from
$1.109 trillion to $1.231 trillion. The ratio of total net losses to
total capital -- a leverage indicator -- was 51.4 percent in 2023, down
from a peak of 59.9 percent in 2005 but indicating that the industry
remains significantly leveraged to catastrophe exposure. The coefficient
of variation for losses showed a sharp peak in 2005 (linked to
large-scale catastrophes including Hurricanes Katrina, Rita, and Wilma),
with subsequent decline through 2020 and a renewed increase by 2023.
Climate risk is identified as a growing factor: average home premiums
increased 22 percent from 2020 to 2023, and the study notes that climate
risk may affect both reserve adequacy (through more volatile reserve
experience) and access to reinsurance protection.

The implication for float as investment capital is that catastrophe
risk is not a static parameter but an evolving exposure that requires
continuous recalibration of reserves, capital, and liquidity buffers.
The industry's capacity to absorb large losses has improved, but the
losses themselves are growing, and the volatility of those losses is
increasing relative to the capital base. For a float compounder, this
means the liquidity buffer must be sized not for average catastrophe
years but for tail events, and the investment portfolio must be
structured to withstand forced liquidation in scenarios where
catastrophe claims exceed reserves.

## Implications

### For Capital Allocation and Vehicle Design

The primary implication of the float-as-capital model is that the
funding structure of an investment vehicle is not a secondary
consideration -- it is a primary determinant of long-term returns. The
cost, permanence, and redemption characteristics of capital shape what
the vehicle can invest in, how long it can hold, and how it behaves
under stress. A vehicle funded by permanent, low-cost float can pursue
strategies that redeemable structures cannot: long-duration holdings,
concentrated positions, contrarian bets during market dislocations, and
investments in illiquid assets that require years to realize value.

For capital allocators evaluating investment vehicles, the funding
structure is a first-order variable. A mutual fund and a float-funded
insurance holding company may hold similar assets, but their behavior
during drawdowns will differ fundamentally: the mutual fund faces
redemption-driven forced selling, while the float-funded vehicle can
hold or buy more. This structural difference is invisible in a static
portfolio snapshot but decisive over a full market cycle. Allocators
should evaluate not only what a vehicle holds but how its capital is
funded, what redemption rights exist, and what liquidity buffers are
maintained against funding-source stress.

For vehicle designers, the float model suggests that combining an
insurance operation with an investment management function creates
structural advantages unavailable to either business alone. The
insurance operation provides low-cost, permanent funding; the investment
function deploys that funding at higher returns than the insurance
liabilities cost. The design challenge is maintaining underwriting
discipline: the incentive to grow float (more premiums, more investable
capital) can lead to underpricing, which raises the cost of float and
can reverse the advantage. Vehicle designers must build governance
mechanisms -- conservative reserving, willingness to walk away from
inadequately priced business, independent actuarial review -- that
constrain the growth incentive in favor of the discipline incentive.

### For Value Investing and the Buffett-Munger Framework

The float-as-capital model is central to understanding Berkshire
Hathaway's investment record, and therefore central to the Buffett-Munger
value investing framework. The Frazzini study demonstrated that
approximately 70 percent of Buffett's market-beating performance is
attributable to leverage, and that the leverage was predominantly
low-cost insurance float. This does not diminish Buffett's stock
selection skill -- the remaining alpha after controlling for leverage and
factor exposures is still positive -- but it locates the structural
foundation of the record in the funding architecture, not solely in
security selection.

For value investors studying the Buffett-Munger approach, the
implication is that replicating the investment philosophy without
replicating the funding structure will produce different results. An
investor applying Buffett's stock selection criteria without float-funded
leverage would have generated strong but far less spectacular returns.
The compounding record depends on the combination: disciplined stock
selection plus low-cost, permanent leverage plus a holding period
unconstrained by redemption pressure. Investors who attempt to clone
Buffett's portfolio without understanding the float engine are, in the
words of one analyst, "looking at the output while ignoring the machine."

This insight connects to the broader principle that investment returns
are a function of both asset selection and capital structure. The
Modigliani-Miller theorem holds that capital structure is irrelevant in
a frictionless world, but the real world has frictions: taxes,
information asymmetry, agency costs, and -- critically -- the
redemption option. Float eliminates the redemption friction, and its
negative cost exploits the tax and agency-cost frictions. The result is
a capital structure that is theoretically irrelevant but practically
decisive.

### For Risk Management and Regulatory Oversight

The float-as-capital model creates a distinctive risk profile that
requires specialized risk management and regulatory oversight. The
structure combines an insurance operation (subject to underwriting,
reserve, catastrophe, and regulatory capital risks) with an investment
operation (subject to market, credit, liquidity, and concentration
risks), and the interaction between the two creates correlated risks
that neither business would face in isolation.

The primary correlated risk is asset-liability mismatch. If the
investment portfolio is concentrated in equities or long-duration
assets while the insurance liabilities are short-tail, a catastrophe
event that triggers large claims during a market downturn forces the
insurer to liquidate equity positions at depressed prices. Berkshire
mitigates this by holding a large cash and Treasury buffer -- $369
billion at year-end 2025, more than double the float -- but this
buffer has an opportunity cost. The regulatory framework for insurers
imposes capital requirements and investment restrictions precisely to
limit this mismatch, but the restrictions also limit the investment
return that makes the float model attractive. The tension between
regulatory prudence and investment flexibility is a structural feature
of the model.

For regulators, the float model raises the question of whether
insurance entities with large, concentrated, or illiquid investment
portfolios pose systemic risks beyond the insurance sector. The hedge
fund reinsurance variant -- where an offshore reinsurer invests float
in aggressive, illiquid strategies -- illustrates the concern: the
insurance entity becomes a conduit for capital that would otherwise be
subject to hedge fund regulation, using the insurance wrapper to access
permanent funding. Regulatory frameworks like Solvency II in Europe and
risk-based capital requirements in the U.S. attempt to address this by
imposing capital charges for asset risk, but the framework must
continuously adapt as investment strategies evolve.

### For the Broader Question of Permanent vs Redeemable Capital

The float model is one instance of a broader architectural choice
between permanent and redeemable capital that applies across the entire
spectrum of investment vehicles. The choice has consequences for
investment strategy, manager incentives, and investor protection.
Permanent capital -- whether insurance float, retained earnings in a
holding company, or a permanent-capital vehicle structure -- allows
longer holding periods, more concentrated positions, and greater
tolerance for short-term volatility. Redeemable capital -- whether
mutual fund shares, hedge fund interests, or private equity fund
commitments -- provides investor liquidity and discipline through the
threat of exit, but constrains the manager's time horizon and forces
liquidity buffers.

The implication for investors evaluating where to allocate capital is
that the permanence or redeemability of the vehicle's funding is a
characteristic to be assessed alongside return history, fee structure,
and strategy. An investor in a float-funded holding company accepts
the absence of redemption rights in exchange for the structural
advantages of permanent capital: potentially higher long-term returns
from the ability to hold through drawdowns, but also exposure to the
insurance and catastrophe risks that the float structure carries. An
investor in a redeemable fund accepts liquidity constraints and the
cost of liquidity buffers in exchange for the right to exit. Neither is
universally superior; the optimal choice depends on the investor's own
time horizon, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance, and on whether the
vehicle's investment strategy is best served by permanent or redeemable
funding.
