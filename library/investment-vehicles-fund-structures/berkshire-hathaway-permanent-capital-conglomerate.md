# Berkshire Hathaway as a Permanent-Capital Conglomerate -- Why a Public Holding Company Compounds Differently Than Any Fund

Berkshire Hathaway is a publicly traded holding company that owns decentralized operating businesses and a concentrated equity portfolio; its chief executive controls major capital-allocation decisions, while shareholders normally exit by trading their shares rather than redeeming capital from Berkshire [1]. Insurance float, retained earnings, operating cash flow, and permanent corporate equity give the parent a patient financing base, but each has constraints: float is a liability, retention must create value, and greater scale narrows the opportunity set [1][4][6]. The author's assessment is that Berkshire's structural advantage comes from combining these elements, not from permanent capital alone.

## Background

Berkshire's present structure emerged from a failed textile business rather than from a purpose-built investment fund. Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing, both with nineteenth-century roots, merged in 1955; by fiscal 1964, textile losses and share repurchases had reduced the combined company's net worth from $55 million at the merger to $22 million, all of which remained tied to the textile operation [3]. Buffett Partnership Ltd. owned about 7 percent of Berkshire in May 1964, and Warren Buffett obtained control in 1965 after a dispute over the price of a tender offer [3]. Berkshire continued operating textiles until 1985, when Buffett closed the remaining operation after concluding that years of capital commitments had not produced an acceptable business [3].

The decisive change was Berkshire's March 1967 purchase of National Indemnity Company and a sister insurer for $8.6 million. Berkshire's 2006 annual report stated that the two insurers had approximately $17 million of float when purchased, defined as policyholder funds held before claims and related obligations are paid [5]. That acquisition placed an insurance balance sheet inside the corporate group and created a funding source that could grow through underwriting rather than through periodic capital calls. It also changed Berkshire from a textile company holding investments into a parent that could own insurers, operating subsidiaries, and marketable securities within one corporate group [1][5].

Berkshire's early owner principles made the governing objective explicit. Its 1983 shareholder letter said the company would measure progress by the average annual gain in intrinsic business value per share, not by corporate size, and would prefer whole businesses with favorable economics while also buying marketable securities when price and availability made them more attractive [4]. The same letter stated that Berkshire would test retained earnings over a rolling five-year period by asking whether each dollar retained produced at least one dollar of market value [4]. These principles connect corporate form to capital allocation: the parent can compare reinvestment in a subsidiary, an acquisition, a security, a repurchase, debt reduction, and cash without a fund mandate forcing one asset class [1][4].

Permanent capital must be defined carefully. A shareholder in an open-end mutual fund can redeem shares from the fund, so the fund must manage portfolio liquidity to meet redemption obligations; the SEC identifies daily redemption as the defining feature of open-end funds and warns that large redemptions can require asset sales [14]. A retail ETF investor normally trades with another market participant, while authorized participants create or redeem large blocks directly with the ETF, typically in exchange for a basket of securities or its cash equivalent [13]. A private-equity partnership usually has a contractual term rather than daily redemption: the ILPA model limited partnership agreement uses a ten-year term with two possible one-year extensions subject to specified approvals [15]. Berkshire is different from all three. Its public shareholders can sell on the New York Stock Exchange, but that secondary-market sale does not itself require Berkshire to redeem the seller or dispose of an operating subsidiary [1].

The corporate shell nevertheless does not make every dollar permanent in an economic sense. Berkshire can choose to repurchase shares, pay a dividend, acquire assets, repay obligations, or reinvest in subsidiaries, and insurance claims and debt maturities still require cash [1]. Insurance regulators also restrict dividends that insurance subsidiaries may send to the parent; Berkshire reported that its principal insurance subsidiaries could declare up to approximately $33 billion of ordinary dividends during 2026 without prior regulatory approval [1]. The relevant distinction is therefore control over timing: ordinary shareholders do not possess a contractual right to withdraw corporate capital at net asset value, while management remains responsible for obligations and discretionary distributions [1][14].

By year-end 2025, Berkshire reported approximately 387,800 employees, more than $370 billion of cash and U.S. Treasury holdings, and $176 billion of insurance float [1]. Greg Abel became chief executive effective January 1, 2026 and assumed ultimate responsibility for major capital allocation and investment decisions [1]. On September 18, 2026, Warren Buffett became chairman emeritus while remaining a director, and Howard Buffett became chairman; Berkshire stated that Greg Abel runs the company and Howard Buffett's board role is to guard its culture and values [2]. The succession therefore separates executive allocation authority from board chairmanship, an important governance test of whether the vehicle can preserve its operating system beyond its builder [1][2].

## Core Concepts

### The Permanent Corporate Shell

Berkshire's equity is permanent relative to redeemable fund capital because a common shareholder cannot tender shares to the company at net asset value as a matter of ordinary course. Berkshire's Class A and Class B shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange, so a selling owner normally receives cash from a buyer in the secondary market rather than from Berkshire [1]. The company may repurchase shares, but its 2025 annual report describes repurchases as a discretionary capital-allocation action undertaken when the chief executive believes the price is below conservatively determined intrinsic value [1]. This distinction moves the liquidity decision from the investor's contract to the corporation's board and management.

That arrangement protects the asset side from a specific feedback loop. An open-end fund must prepare for daily redemptions, and the SEC notes that funds with less-liquid portfolios can face greater difficulty meeting heavy withdrawals without disadvantaging remaining shareholders [14]. Berkshire does not have to sell BNSF, an insurance subsidiary, or a public equity position because an owner sells Berkshire stock. The market price may fall sharply and financing conditions may deteriorate, but shareholder trading alone does not shrink the corporate asset base. This is a narrower and more defensible claim than saying Berkshire has no liquidity risk: claims, debt, taxes, collateral requirements, capital expenditures, acquisitions, and voluntary repurchases all consume cash [1].

ETF comparisons require a second distinction. Retail ETF investors also trade on an exchange, and authorized participants commonly redeem creation units in kind for baskets of portfolio assets [13]. A retail ETF sale therefore does not automatically force the ETF manager to sell securities. However, an ETF remains a pooled vehicle governed by a stated investment objective and creation-redemption architecture, whereas Berkshire is an operating holding company whose parent can allocate among wholly owned businesses, securities, cash, and acquisitions [1][13]. The structural advantage is not that only Berkshire has exchange-traded shares; it is the breadth of permissible internal destinations for capital combined with no routine shareholder redemption at net asset value.

Private-equity capital is committed for years, but it is not normally indefinite. The ILPA model agreement illustrates a ten-year term with consent-based extensions [15]. Berkshire has no contractual fund termination date. It can own a subsidiary indefinitely, yet it can also sell one if economics or regulation require it. Permanent capital therefore creates an option to wait; it does not make waiting automatically correct.

### Centralized Capital Allocation and Decentralized Operations

Berkshire separates operating authority from capital-allocation authority. Its 2025 Form 10-K says operating subsidiaries are managed on an unusually decentralized basis, with few centralized or integrated business functions, while the chief executive is ultimately responsible for significant capital-allocation and investment decisions and for evaluating operating performance [1]. Greg Abel's 2025 shareholder letter similarly describes autonomy grounded in deserved trust, minimal bureaucracy, and accountability at the operating-company level [1].

This division is not equivalent to stripping every subsidiary of investment authority. Operating managers decide how to serve customers, hire people, price products, maintain assets, and pursue approved opportunities within their businesses; the parent decides how much incremental capital each operation should receive relative to competing uses [1]. Regulated subsidiaries also retain capital needed to satisfy policyholder, utility, railroad, and other obligations. The model is centralized at the level of portfolio choice and decentralized at the level of operating knowledge.

The small parent office is evidence of this design, not proof that bureaucracy is always destructive. Berkshire's 2025 operating-company appendix listed 28 employees at corporate headquarters against 387,815 across the group [1]. A lean center reduces duplication and leaves detailed decisions with managers closer to their operations, but it also concentrates judgment and increases dependence on accurate reporting, internal controls, and succession. The author's assessment is that decentralization works here because autonomy is paired with explicit accountability and the parent preserves the right to reallocate capital; either element without the other would weaken the model.

### Insurance Float as Conditional, Regulated Funding

Float is the approximate net policyholder funding generated by underwriting activities and held for investment before related claims and obligations are settled. Berkshire's 2025 annual report identifies unpaid losses, loss-adjustment expenses, life and health liabilities, unearned premiums, and other policyholder liabilities, net of specified receivables and deferred items, as major components [1]. Consolidated float rose from about $138 billion at year-end 2020 to about $176 billion at year-end 2025 [1].

Float is not equity and is not automatically free. Berkshire measures its cost through pre-tax underwriting earnings or losses relative to average float; its combined insurance operations recorded underwriting gains and therefore a negative average float cost in each of 2023, 2024, and 2025 [1]. In an adverse period, catastrophe losses, reserve strengthening, or poor pricing can make float costly. Claims are not margin calls triggered by Berkshire's stock price, but they are real obligations whose timing can accelerate and whose ultimate amount is uncertain [1]. Regulatory capital rules and restrictions on subsidiary dividends further limit how freely insurance resources can move to the parent [1].

The peer-reviewed study "Buffett's Alpha" estimated Berkshire's average leverage at about 1.7-to-1 and found that roughly 35 percent of liabilities consisted of insurance float with an average annual cost of 1.72 percent, about 3 percentage points below the U.S. Treasury-bill rate over the study period [6]. The study's interpretation was not that leverage alone created the record, but that relatively stable, low-cost financing amplified a portfolio tilted toward cheap, safe, high-quality companies [6]. The structural lesson is conditional: float adds value when underwriting discipline keeps its cost attractive and liquidity is sufficient for claims. It can destroy value when premiums are inadequate or liabilities are underestimated.

### Retained Earnings and Distribution Discipline

Retained earnings are the second compounding engine. Berkshire's current policy is not to pay cash dividends while management reasonably expects each retained dollar to create more than one dollar of market value for shareholders, and the board reviews that policy annually [1]. This is the modern statement of the 1983 rolling five-year test [4]. Retention is therefore a hurdle-rate decision, not a claim that dividends are inherently inferior.

Retention preserves management's ability to compare uses of capital inside the group. Cash can fund a subsidiary's attractive project, acquire a whole company, purchase a marketable security, repurchase Berkshire shares below intrinsic value, reduce debt, or remain in Treasury bills until prospective returns improve [1][4]. The policy also exposes shareholders to agency risk: if management retains funds without earning adequate incremental returns, permanence traps rather than compounds capital. The one-dollar test is retrospective and market value is noisy, so it should be supplemented by forward estimates of incremental returns and by evidence that per-share value, not corporate size, remains the objective [1][4].

The mechanics are also more complex than a simple dividend-versus-no-dividend comparison. Berkshire pays corporate taxes where applicable, its regulated subsidiaries may be unable to distribute all cash immediately, and capital-intensive businesses such as BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy require continuing reinvestment [1]. A retained dollar is valuable only if its risk-adjusted use inside Berkshire exceeds what the shareholder could obtain after receiving it. The author's assessment is that the corporate form supplies the option to retain; disciplined allocation determines whether that option has value.

### Stock as Acquisition Currency

Publicly traded shares can finance acquisitions without an external fund-raising round, but issuing them transfers a portion of all future Berkshire economics to the seller. The 1998 General Re agreement offered each General Re share a fixed exchange ratio in Berkshire Class A or Class B stock; the announced consideration was approximately $22 billion and the transaction was expected to be tax-free to General Re shareholders [10]. The transaction brought a large investment portfolio and reinsurance franchise into Berkshire, but Berkshire's 2008 annual report later described serious underwriting, reserving, expense, and cultural problems that had required years of repair [12]. Permanent capital did not prevent an acquisition mistake, and stock consideration did not make the price free.

The 2009 BNSF agreement used a different mix. Berkshire agreed to acquire the 77.4 percent it did not already own for $100 per share in cash and stock; the announcement valued the whole transaction at approximately $44 billion including $10 billion of BNSF debt, while describing Berkshire's investment as $34 billion [9]. Consideration was targeted at roughly 60 percent cash and 40 percent stock, and Berkshire approved a 50-for-1 split of its Class B shares to accommodate smaller holders electing a share exchange [9]. The case demonstrates both flexibility and dilution discipline: a public parent can combine cash and shares, but the economic cost of issued stock depends on Berkshire's own value at the exchange date.

### Investment-Company Law and the Operating-Company Boundary

The Investment Company Act of 1940 defines an investment company through several tests. Section 3(a)(1)(A) covers an issuer primarily engaged or holding itself out as primarily engaged in investing, reinvesting, or trading in securities. Section 3(a)(1)(C) includes an issuer whose investment securities exceed 40 percent of total assets, excluding government securities and cash items, on an unconsolidated basis [7]. The statute's definition of investment securities excludes securities of qualifying majority-owned subsidiaries, which matters for an operating holding company [7].

Rule 3a-1 supplies a nonexclusive safe harbor from the Section 3(a)(1)(C) test. It requires both an asset test and an income test: no more than 45 percent of specified assets and no more than 45 percent of after-tax net income for the last four fiscal quarters may consist of or derive from securities outside listed categories, including certain controlled operating companies [8]. It also requires that the issuer not fall within the primary-business definitions in Sections 3(a)(1)(A) or 3(a)(1)(B) [8].

Berkshire's Form 10-K describes a holding company owning insurers, a railroad, utilities and energy businesses, manufacturers, service companies, and retailers, and it reports the parent as an operating conglomerate rather than a registered investment company [1]. The author's legal-structural interpretation is that wholly owned operating subsidiaries and their earnings cannot be analyzed as though they were merely a fund's portfolio securities; the statutory treatment of majority-owned operating subsidiaries is central to the boundary [1][7][8]. This is not a claim that corporate form alone creates an exemption. The Act applies factual primary-business, asset, income, and subsidiary tests, and those tests must be assessed from current facts.

### Succession Is Part of the Vehicle

A permanent vehicle must transfer decision rights without confusing board stewardship, executive authority, and investment responsibility. Berkshire's 2025 annual report assigns major capital allocation and investment decisions to Greg Abel as chief executive [1]. The September 2026 transition made Howard Buffett chairman, Warren Buffett chairman emeritus and a continuing director, and Greg Abel the executive running the company [2]. This separates the protection of culture and governance from day-to-day allocation.

The structure can preserve capital after a leader leaves, but it cannot preserve judgment automatically. Concentrated authority reduces committee delay and helps Berkshire act quickly, yet the same concentration can magnify a poor decision. A durable Berkshire therefore requires clear authority, board oversight, capable subsidiary managers, conservative liquidity, and an allocation process that can reject opportunities. Permanent capital increases the duration over which good or bad judgment compounds.

## Evidence

### Academic Decomposition of Berkshire's Returns

Frazzini, Kabiller, and Pedersen analyzed Berkshire's stock returns, public-equity holdings reported through Form 13F, balance-sheet leverage, and factor exposures. The peer-reviewed 2018 article reports a Sharpe ratio of 0.79 for 1976-2017, higher than that of every U.S. mutual fund in its comparison with a history exceeding forty years, and an average excess return above the Treasury-bill rate of 18.6 percent versus 7.5 percent for the broad stock market [6]. Traditional market, size, value, and momentum factors left significant alpha, while adding betting-against-beta and quality-minus-junk exposures made the estimated alpha statistically insignificant [6].

The authors also decomposed Berkshire into public stocks and wholly owned private companies. Both contributed, but the public-stock component performed better in their estimates, leading them to attribute more of the measured record to stock selection than to operating-management effects [6]. They estimated average leverage of about 1.7-to-1 and identified insurance float as an unusually cheap part of the financing structure [6]. The result supports a combined explanation: exposure to cheap, safe, quality companies plus financing that could be sustained at attractive cost. It does not establish that future float will remain cheap or that the same factor portfolio can reproduce Berkshire's operating businesses, taxes, acquisition access, and governance.

### The Long-Run Record and Its Limits

Berkshire's 2025 annual report compares annual changes in per-share market value with the S&P 500 including dividends. From 1965 through 2025, the table reports a 19.7 percent compounded annual gain for Berkshire and 10.5 percent for the S&P 500; the cumulative gains were 6,099,294 percent and 46,061 percent, respectively [1]. In 2025 alone, Berkshire gained 10.9 percent while the index gained 17.9 percent [1]. The method is transparent and relevant to shareholders because it is per-share, but it is an observational record, not a causal experiment. It cannot by itself separate allocation skill, leverage, valuation changes, operating performance, taxes, and luck.

The scale evidence also cuts both ways. At year-end 2025 Berkshire had more than $370 billion in cash and U.S. Treasury holdings, $176 billion of float, and nearly 400,000 employees [1]. Those resources expand the set of crises and acquisitions Berkshire can address, but the company itself states that compounding becomes harder at its size and expects per-share progress to occur in smaller increments [1]. The record supports structural resilience; it does not support extrapolating historical returns unchanged.

### Crisis Liquidity in 2008

Berkshire's third-quarter 2008 Form 10-Q records the purchase of newly issued perpetual preferred stock in Goldman Sachs and General Electric with an aggregate liquidation value and cost of $8 billion. The preferred shares paid 10 percent annual dividends and came with five-year warrants to buy common shares at stated prices [11]. Berkshire's 2008 annual report said three large insurance-portfolio investments made on terms unavailable in normal markets were expected to add about $1.5 billion of annual pre-tax earnings and also offered potential capital gains [12].

This is evidence of option value created by liquidity and credibility. Berkshire could commit capital while markets were impaired and negotiate securities combining senior income with equity upside [11][12]. It is not proof that any permanent-capital company would receive the same terms, nor that no fund could participate. Berkshire's reputation, balance sheet, speed, and counterparty confidence were part of the bargaining position. The structural conclusion is narrower: a vehicle that preserves substantial liquid assets and does not face routine shareholder redemption has greater freedom to provide capital during stress.

### General Re and BNSF Show Both Sides of Acquisition Currency

General Re is the adverse case. The 1998 announcement valued the all-stock transaction at approximately $22 billion and expected tax-free treatment for General Re shareholders [10]. Berkshire's 2008 annual report later stated that General Re had serious problems Berkshire failed to detect at purchase, including deterioration in underwriting, reserving, and expenses, and that subsequent management had to restore discipline [12]. The case shows that permanent ownership permits a long repair period, but it also allows the cost of an error to remain inside the group. Share issuance transferred permanent Berkshire ownership to the seller, making overpayment or weak diligence especially consequential.

BNSF is the capital-absorption case. The 2009 agreement valued the transaction at about $44 billion including $10 billion of debt and used both cash and stock to acquire the remaining 77.4 percent [9]. Berkshire's 2025 annual report describes the completed acquisition as a $34.5 billion equity investment and reports that BNSF generated $8.1 billion of net operating cash flow and paid $4.4 billion of dividends to Berkshire in 2025 [1]. BNSF also requires continuing infrastructure investment. The case demonstrates how a large permanent-capital parent can own a regulated, capital-intensive asset indefinitely and recycle part of its cash flow while funding the asset's own needs.

### Evidence Against a Pure 13F Explanation

Form 13F reveals only a defined reporting slice. The SEC states that institutional managers report positions in securities on the official Section 13(f) list, permits confidential-treatment requests in specified circumstances, and instructs managers not to report written put or call options [16][17]. A 13F therefore does not present Berkshire's wholly owned subsidiaries, insurance liabilities, float cost, debt, cash and Treasury holdings as a financing system, or every security exposure [1][16][17].

The academic study used 13F data to estimate the public-stock component and then separately modeled private businesses and leverage [6]. That method itself demonstrates why copying disclosed common stocks is not equivalent to copying Berkshire. The disclosed assets are only one component; the liability structure, tax position, operating cash flows, and ability to hold through stress affect the return and risk borne by Berkshire shareholders.

## Implications

### For Fund Architects

The first design question is not whether permanent capital is universally superior. It is which party controls the timing of liquidity and who bears the cost when cash is demanded. In an open-end fund, investors possess redemption rights and the manager must maintain enough liquidity to honor them; heavy withdrawals can impose transaction costs or force sales that affect remaining owners [14]. In a conventional ETF, retail investors trade with each other and authorized participants usually exchange creation units for securities baskets or cash, so a retail sale should not be described as automatically forcing the manager to liquidate portfolio assets [13]. In a private-equity fund, investor capital is locked for a contractual term, but consent-based extensions can alter the exit schedule [15]. Berkshire places ordinary exit liquidity in its stock market while leaving asset-sale timing with corporate management [1].

Interpretation: vehicle design should be evaluated across at least six linked variables -- investor liquidity rights, asset liquidity, liability duration, leverage and collateral terms, fee and distribution rules, and governance. Changing only one variable does not remove risk. Permanent equity can stop a redemption run but cannot eliminate insurance claims, debt maturities, regulatory restrictions, poor underwriting, or bad acquisitions [1]. Long-duration capital is most useful when obligations are also durable and decision rights remain accountable.

Berkshire also shows that a broad mandate has option value. The parent can allocate among internal projects, acquisitions, public equities, repurchases, debt reduction, and Treasury bills [1][4]. A specialist fund may possess deeper expertise in one asset class but cannot necessarily move outside its mandate. Interpretation: the broader vehicle earns an advantage only when the allocator can compare unlike opportunities on a common intrinsic-value basis. Without that discipline, flexibility becomes style drift and conglomerate sprawl.

Fees are another structural difference, but they should not be simplified into "fund fees bad, corporate costs absent." Funds disclose management and other expenses; Berkshire instead bears corporate overhead, compensation, acquisition costs, taxes, operating expenses, and the opportunity cost of retained capital [1]. Berkshire's parent office is small, but its subsidiaries employ hundreds of thousands of people and incur their own costs [1]. The relevant comparison is total cost and incentive alignment per unit of value created, not whether a charge is labeled a management fee.

### For Investors Evaluating Holding Companies

A holding-company analysis should begin with sources and uses of capital. The sources include shareholder equity, operating cash flow, float, debt, deferred obligations, and proceeds from asset sales. The uses include subsidiary reinvestment, claims, capital expenditures, acquisitions, securities, repurchases, taxes, and liquidity reserves [1]. Each source has a different cost and maturity. Calling all of them permanent capital obscures the central risk: float and debt are claims, while common equity absorbs residual outcomes.

For float, investors should examine underwriting results, reserve development, claim duration, catastrophe exposure, regulatory capital, and the proportion of parent liquidity that cannot be extracted from insurers [1]. A growing float balance is not sufficient. Berkshire's negative float cost in 2023-2025 is valuable because underwriting was profitable, not merely because the liability grew [1]. The "Buffett's Alpha" finding that float cost less than Treasury bills over its study period is historical evidence, not a contractual guarantee [6].

For retained earnings, the correct test is incremental rather than average return. A business may have an excellent historical return on equity yet earn much less on the next dollar because scale has exhausted its best opportunities. Berkshire explicitly evaluates retention through expected per-share value creation and acknowledges that size makes compounding harder [1][4]. Interpretation: investors should compare the expected return on the next retained dollar with dividends, repurchases, debt reduction, and outside opportunities. A no-dividend record is not evidence of value creation by itself.

For acquisition currency, investors should treat issued shares as a sale of a proportional claim on every existing asset and future opportunity. General Re shows the cost of combining stock consideration with operating problems that were not fully understood at acquisition [10][12]. BNSF shows how cash and stock can secure a large asset that remains useful at Berkshire's scale [1][9]. Interpretation: the relevant question is not whether stock conserves cash, but whether the intrinsic value received per share issued exceeds the intrinsic value surrendered.

A sum-of-the-parts framework must also avoid double counting. The public-equity portfolio, cash, and Treasury bills are visible assets, but insurance float, debt, deferred taxes, policyholder obligations, and required subsidiary capital are not free financing. Wholly owned operating businesses contribute cash flows and consume capital; they should be valued on their economics, not at book value by default [1]. The author's assessment is that Berkshire's reported balance sheet is a map of claims and resources, while intrinsic value requires estimates of future distributable cash flow and the cost of maintaining each funding source.

### For Succession and Governance

The September 2026 organization assigns distinct roles: Greg Abel is chief executive and runs the company, Howard Buffett chairs the board, and Warren Buffett remains a director as chairman emeritus [2]. The 2025 Form 10-K places significant capital-allocation and investment responsibility with the chief executive [1]. This clarity matters because a decentralized conglomerate can fail if subsidiary autonomy, parent allocation, and board oversight overlap without explicit authority.

Interpretation: succession should be evaluated as a system rather than as a search for a replica of Warren Buffett. The system needs managers who can operate subsidiaries, a chief executive who can compare capital uses, a board that can replace leadership and protect culture, and liquidity rules that prevent one error from threatening the group [1][2]. The permanent shell may outlive an individual, but decision quality is not embedded in the legal charter. Concentrated authority can accelerate good decisions and bad ones.

Governance should therefore track outcomes that reveal allocation quality: per-share intrinsic-value progress, incremental returns on retained capital, disciplined issuance and repurchase of shares, underwriting profitability, reserve adequacy, and the willingness to leave cash idle when prospective returns are insufficient [1][4]. None is a perfect single-period metric. Together they test whether management is preserving the reason permanent capital is valuable rather than using permanence to avoid accountability.

### For Understanding the Limits of 13F Cloning

A 13F-based clone sees selected reportable securities, not Berkshire's complete economic architecture. The SEC's rules and guidance limit the form to Section 13(f) securities, allow specified confidential treatment, and exclude written options from the public holdings table [16][17]. Berkshire's 13F also cannot convey the cash generated by operating subsidiaries, the timing and cost of insurance liabilities, regulated dividend capacity, acquisition agreements, debt, or internal capital transfers [1].

Interpretation: copying the reported equity names can approximate only one asset sleeve. It does not reproduce Berkshire's leverage, liquidity, tax position, operating cash flow, governance, transaction access, or capacity to hold an asset without investor redemption. The Frazzini, Kabiller, and Pedersen decomposition is useful precisely because it separates public stocks, private businesses, and leverage rather than treating the 13F as the whole company [6].

### For Value Investors

Berkshire demonstrates a value-investing principle at the vehicle level: price discipline must apply not only to securities but also to retained earnings, acquisitions, repurchases, and financing. The 2025 annual report states that Berkshire evaluates operating investment, acquisitions, public equities, and repurchases by their ability to increase intrinsic value per share over a long horizon [1]. The 1983 principles likewise put per-share intrinsic value ahead of size [4].

The durable lesson is conditional rather than heroic. Permanent capital increases the time available for an investment thesis to work and can prevent investor redemptions from dictating asset sales. Low-cost float can expand earning assets. Decentralization can preserve operating knowledge while a central allocator compares opportunities. Yet permanence also lengthens the life of mistakes, float can become expensive, decentralization can weaken control, and scale can depress future returns [1][6][12]. The vehicle creates a favorable opportunity set; disciplined judgment converts that opportunity into value.
