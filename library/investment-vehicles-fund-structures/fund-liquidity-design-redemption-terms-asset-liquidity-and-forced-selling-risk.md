# Fund Liquidity Design Determines Whether Investor Exits Become Forced Asset Sales

Fund liquidity design connects an investor's contractual exit right to the time and cost of converting portfolio assets into cash. If redemptions can arrive faster than assets can be sold without material loss, a fund may transfer exit costs to remaining investors, sell attractive holdings under pressure, or restrict withdrawals; matching terms, reserves, financing, and pricing tools changes who bears those costs rather than making illiquidity disappear ([1] [2] [6]).

## Background

An investment fund pools claims on assets but does not necessarily give its investors the same access to cash that the assets themselves provide. In an open-end mutual fund, investors ordinarily redeem fund shares at a calculated net asset value (NAV); the manager must arrange settlement, often by using cash or selling holdings. A closed-end fund's shareholders normally sell their shares to another investor instead of redeeming them from the portfolio. A private partnership may prohibit withdrawal while its general partner calls capital and distributes proceeds over a multiyear life. These are different liability designs applied to assets that can themselves range from cash and listed equities to corporate bonds, real estate, and private loans ([1] [12] [14] [15]).

The central problem predates any particular liquidity tool: the value assigned to a fund interest and the cash obtainable by selling a meaningful position today are not always the same. A quoted bond price may not indicate how much the fund could realize at scale during a common sell-off. Trading costs include spreads, commissions, taxes, and market impact; forced liquidation can leave those expenses with investors who remain after others receive an unadjusted NAV. This is a problem of vehicle promises and cost allocation, not a prediction that every redemption will trigger a crisis. The FSB defines structural mismatch by comparing redemption terms with the time needed to liquidate holdings in an orderly way in normal and stressed markets ([1] [2] [4]).

Fund architecture offers several ways to allocate this risk. Holding cash and liquid securities provides an immediate buffer, but their presence can change strategy implementation and does not establish that a long sequence of outflows can be met cheaply. Requiring advance notice buys time for orderly sales. A lockup prevents withdrawal for an initial period, while reduced dealing frequency batches requests into fewer windows. A gate caps the quantity redeemed in a dealing period; a suspension temporarily stops redemptions. Swing pricing and anti-dilution levies instead change what transacting investors pay or receive, seeking to place liquidity costs where they originate. Each choice trades some combination of investor access, remaining-holder fairness, implementation expense, and manager discretion ([1] [2] [3]).

The 2008 crisis made private-fund restrictions and financing constraints visible, but it would be misleading to attribute all liquidity pressure to one form of vehicle. In the 2020 COVID-19 market shock, fixed-income open-end funds faced both investor outflows and deteriorating market liquidity. An IMF analysis reports global fixed-income-fund outflows of $481 billion in March 2020 based on its Morningstar data and describes affected funds initially drawing on cash and selling liquid securities. This pattern can preserve payments today while making the residual fund less liquid tomorrow. Subsequent BIS work found that some bond-fund managers also sold beyond amounts needed for withdrawals in order to replenish cash. Observed sales therefore cannot be inferred from redemption totals alone ([10] [11]).

Policy responses increasingly begin at fund inception rather than waiting for emergency intervention. The FSB's revised 2023 recommendations ask authorities to align redemption terms with asset liquidity and encourage anti-dilution tools. IOSCO's 2025 recommendations and companion guidance connect design, day-to-day liquidity assessment, stress testing, governance, disclosure, and both price-based and quantity-based tools. They are international recommendations to authorities and fund operators, not a claim that every jurisdiction applies identical binding rules. U.S. Rule 22e-4 addresses registered open-end funds through program requirements and an illiquid-investment purchase limit, with specified exclusions and ETF exceptions ([1] [2] [3] [4]).

The vehicle-specific distinction matters because the same word, liquidity, can describe different things. Secondary-market liquidity means an investor can sell a share to someone else at a market price. Redemption liquidity means the vehicle itself must supply proceeds according to its contract. Portfolio liquidity means the underlying assets can be converted into cash at tolerable cost and speed. Funding liquidity means cash or eligible collateral remains available to meet obligations such as margin. The author's synthesis is that a claim of daily share tradability proves neither cheap redemption from the vehicle nor assured liquidity of its assets. Comparisons of fund types must state which claim is being offered and who must fund it ([1] [3] [12] [14]).

This topic treats redemption design as a structural decision within the investment vehicle. The related brain topic on mutual funds and ETFs explains their broader ownership and creation-redemption machinery; the hedge-fund topic discusses incentives, leverage, lockups, and side pockets inside that one category; the closed-end and private-equity topics describe their broader capital structures. Here the question is narrower and comparative: when cash is requested, what resources and constraints does each wrapper supply, and who absorbs the gap between promised exit price and realizable asset value?

## Core Concepts

### The liquidity bargain has four clocks

The first clock is the dealing clock: when may an investor give an effective redemption instruction? The second is the notice and settlement clock: how much warning does the manager receive and when is payment due? The third is the asset clock: how long would liquidation of the required position size take at an acceptable price, including settlement of the sale? The fourth is the financing clock: when can a lender demand repayment, collateral, or margin? The author's framework separates these clocks because a fund can have quarterly dealing yet a very short margin deadline, or daily dealing yet ample cash to fund ordinary withdrawals. Their alignment must be checked under stress, when asset-sale times lengthen and requests may become correlated ([1] [3] [5] [14]).

An illustrative bond fund with next-day dealing and positions requiring several days to sell in size has a more demanding contract than one holding only assets readily converted into cash. This is not a numerical liquidity classification: the feasible size, bid depth, settlement arrangements, counterparty availability, and willingness to accept price impact must be tested. IOSCO distinguishes liquid, less liquid, and illiquid assets by considering conditions in both ordinary and stressed markets rather than assuming a security is permanently liquid because it traded yesterday. The SEC's covered-fund regime similarly classifies holdings using expected conversion time and material effect on market value. Neither framework treats a screen quotation alone as reliable sale proceeds ([2] [3] [4]).

### Cost allocation can create a first-mover incentive

When redemptions are paid at an unswung NAV, the fund may pay to sell assets after a departing investor has already received proceeds. The remaining investor then bears the transaction cost and often retains a less liquid portfolio. Investors who expect substantial future withdrawals can prefer to leave early, even when they have no private information about the asset's fundamental value. Research on mutual funds describes this interaction as payoff complementarity: my incentive to redeem grows when I expect others to redeem. The mechanism depends on actual liquidation costs, pricing, investor behavior, and fund terms; it is not equivalent to claiming that every open-end fund behaves like an uninsured bank ([1] [6] [7]).

A simple hypothetical illustrates the direction without claiming a universal parameter. If a fund pays departing owners before estimating the cost of liquidating positions, the unrecovered cost reduces value left for continuing owners. A tool that charges an appropriate exit cost to the transacting owners can reduce this transfer, but an overestimate can unfairly penalize them. Moreover, liquidating only highly marketable holdings can avoid immediate market impact while degrading the portfolio's remaining liquidity. The relevant fairness test compares the fund after withdrawals with the fund before them: who received cash, which assets were sold, and how much realizable value is left for each owner ([1] [3] [8]).

### Price-based tools change the transaction price

Swing pricing changes the dealing NAV when net flows imply trading costs. With net outflows, a downward swing can lower proceeds to redeemers so continuing owners do not alone bear spreads and estimated market impact; with net inflows, an upward swing can charge subscribers for deployment costs. The same swung price may apply to all investors transacting at that valuation point, so the design must consider offsetting flows and treatment of investors with different liquidity needs. An anti-dilution levy instead adds or subtracts an explicit amount for the fund's benefit, and subscription or redemption fees can perform a similar function if credited to the vehicle. A quoted NAV is therefore not necessarily the final cash price under these mechanisms ([2] [3]).

Calibration is difficult: stressed sale costs cannot be known precisely before executing transactions, and a fixed swing factor may undercharge when market depth collapses. The fund also should not pretend it must sell a full proportional slice of every position if the realistic liquidation policy differs. IOSCO explicitly discusses realistic portfolio trading assumptions in its guidance. The BIS's theoretical work finds a trade-off between passing on trading costs and preserving liquidity for genuinely cash-constrained investors; its empirical work finds less flow sensitivity to weak performance among Luxembourg funds permitted to use swing pricing than among comparison U.S. funds in ordinary conditions, but only limited protection in stress. Price tools redistribute and may reduce risk; they cannot manufacture counterparties ([3] [8] [9]).

### Quantity-based tools change when, or how much, exits occur

A notice period gives the manager advance warning and time to plan sales, while dealing frequency determines how often requests can be executed. A lockup prevents withdrawal for a stated initial horizon; after it expires, the fund still needs a viable dealing and settlement policy. A gate limits executed withdrawals within a window, either at fund level or for an individual holder, and defers or prorates the excess as governing terms specify. A suspension is a more severe interruption of redemption. Side pockets isolate hard-to-value or hard-to-sell holdings and allocate their eventual realization to the relevant investors; they require valuation, consent, and conflict controls because they can also be abused to conceal losses or favor certain holders ([2] [3] [14]).

Quantity restrictions protect the vehicle from selling every asset immediately, but they transfer waiting risk to investors. If investors expect a gate or suspension and fear being trapped after others leave, they may submit orders earlier; IOSCO explicitly warns of such anticipatory behavior. Disclosed restrictions are therefore not automatically stabilizing. Design should specify priority and proration, whether unfilled requests roll into later windows, the treatment of new subscriptions, how conditions for activation are documented, and who authorizes decisions. The author's assessment is that precommitted terms with independently reviewable activation criteria are more intelligible to investors than improvised restrictions imposed only after a funding shortfall emerges ([2] [3]).

### Buffers, liquidation sequencing, and financing are joint choices

A cash buffer is immediately available but reduces the assets deployed in the intended strategy. A ladder of marketable holdings adds capacity yet creates a sequencing question. Selling the most liquid assets first may satisfy withdrawals at modest immediate cost but leaves the remaining investors with a less liquid pool. Selling all holdings pro rata can preserve portfolio composition yet require unacceptable discounts on illiquid positions. A credit line can bridge settlement timing but adds interest, covenants, collateral requirements, and lender withdrawal risk. It is funding, not new asset liquidity. If the line is uncommitted or callable while asset markets freeze, relying on it can conceal rather than eliminate the mismatch ([3] [5] [10] [11]).

The manager must therefore map both asset proceeds and every claim on them. Investor withdrawals, derivative margin, borrowing maturities, fund expenses, capital commitments, and operating reserves can compete for the same cash. A manager who borrows to pay redeeming investors while locking less liquid assets into collateral can leave the remaining owners exposed to both leverage and illiquidity. IOSCO asks that portfolio-level liquidity thresholds account for potential margin calls, while the FSB's work on private credit identifies financing rollover as a separate vulnerability even in vehicles whose investor capital is locked. A redemption gate does not stop a financing counterparty from making its own contractual demand ([2] [14]).

### Vehicle architectures place the claim at different levels

An open-end mutual fund ordinarily settles direct investor redemptions from the vehicle, so its asset and cash policies must support its published terms. An ETF gives ordinary holders an exchange-traded share, while authorized participants can create or redeem large blocks, often in kind. An in-kind redemption transfers specified securities rather than requiring the fund to sell every holding for cash, but an ETF shareholder can still face a widened trading spread or a price away from NAV when intermediation is strained. It is inaccurate to equate ETF intraday trading with guaranteed cash exits from the fund at NAV ([4] [12]).

A hedge-fund partnership can set longer notice, lockups, gates, and side pockets to support less liquid strategies, but leverage and broker financing can still impose faster clocks. A conventional private-equity partnership avoids routine investor redemption and calls capital as deals arise, then distributes after realization. The limited partner's need for earlier cash may be addressed by selling its partnership interest to a secondary buyer, subject to governing documents and consent; this sale is not a redemption from fund assets and its price need not equal reported NAV. Periodic-liquidity private-credit vehicles depart from that traditional model and must make their withdrawal caps credible relative to loan-sale capacity ([14] [15]).

A closed-end listed fund or investment trust normally has no direct ongoing redemption claim: owners sell shares to other owners, and the price can trade below reported NAV. A permanent-capital company can likewise retain assets without routine shareholder redemption, but share-price risk and any separate debt maturities remain with investors and the company. These architectures move liquidity pressure from asset liquidation to investor-level pricing; they do not make illiquid assets liquid. The author's synthesis is that the investor chooses between a more certain right to fund cash subject to available tools and a more flexible ability to sell a claim at a potentially uncertain market price ([1] [12] [14]).

### Liquidity stress tests must be contractual

A useful vehicle test combines an outflow path, asset-sale capacity, changing valuation, and financing demands on one calendar. It asks how many units can be redeemed at each dealing point, what cash is available without sales, how much can be sold by each settlement deadline, which costs must be borne, and which residual holdings remain. Tests should change both sides at once: larger, concentrated withdrawals and narrower markets, not just a historic outflow imposed on normal-market liquidity. ESMA's guidance includes time to liquidation, liquidation cost, investor concentration, historical and hypothetical scenarios, and the combined asset-liability impact ([3] [5]).

Reverse testing begins with failure: at what redemption rate and haircut does a gate become necessary, or at what financing call does the vehicle have to liquidate a long-term asset? The answer should change the vehicle before a crisis: less generous terms, more reliable cash, different investment limits, smaller concentration, revised price tools, or less leverage. The stress test is not a forecast of a particular run; it is a check that the contract, operations, and portfolio can work together under plausible adverse conditions. This is the vehicle-level counterpart to portfolio stress testing, whose different focus is the whole portfolio's exposure to price and risk-factor shocks ([1] [3] [5]).

## Evidence

### Open-end fund outflows vary with the liquidity of holdings

Chen, Goldstein, and Jiang studied open-end mutual funds using differences in the liquidity of fund holdings. Their model predicts that withdrawals by one investor are more damaging to remaining investors when assets are harder to sell, giving other investors stronger incentives to leave after bad performance. In the data, outflows of illiquid funds were more sensitive to bad performance than those of liquid funds. The pattern was weaker in funds with shareholder bases dominated by large investors, a useful qualification to any claim that all holders respond identically. The design compares cross-fund behavior rather than measuring an actual sale price for each fund on each redemption day ([6]).

Goldstein, Jiang, and Ng then examined corporate-bond mutual funds, whose daily investor redemptions coexist with underlying instruments that can be costly to sell. Using fund flow and performance data, they documented a concave flow-performance relation: weak returns were associated with especially strong outflows, unlike the more familiar convex relation in some other fund categories. They also compared liquid Treasury-bond and less liquid municipal-bond fund categories, finding patterns consistent with the asset-liquidity mechanism. These are associations plus mechanism tests, not proof that every outflow was irrational or caused by fear of a gate; credit news and investor cash needs can independently affect fund flows ([7]).

### Swing pricing changes incentives, but stress results are qualified

Lewrick and Schanz compared funds in Luxembourg, where swing pricing was available, with U.S. comparison funds that lacked the same option in their sample period. They found lower outflow sensitivity following weak performance among the Luxembourg funds in ordinary conditions, alongside evidence consistent with supported fund returns, more volatile accounting share prices, and lower cash buffers. Their study specifically reported limited effect during stress episodes, including the taper-tantrum setting. A jurisdiction comparison can reflect other differences in fund clientele, portfolio, or legal arrangements despite controls; it should not be read as a randomized test or as evidence that swing pricing always stops redemptions ([8]).

Their companion model analyzes why simply passing all conceivable sale costs to redeeming owners is not a free solution. Under trading frictions and heterogeneity in investors' need for cash, the model balances the benefit of reducing run incentives against the harm to investors who genuinely need liquidity. Its result supports adjusting redemption prices as costs rise while maintaining a cash buffer, with outcomes conditional on assumed preferences and market structure. The theoretical prediction and empirical finding have different evidentiary status; together they explain why pricing is a useful design tool, not why any particular swing factor is optimal for every fund ([9] [8]).

### Crisis evidence separates withdrawals from managers' own sales

The IMF examined global fixed-income funds during the March 2020 COVID-19 market disruption and reported $481 billion of monthly sector outflows in its dataset. Its holdings analysis found that stressed funds first used cash, cash equivalents, and liquid securities, while the residual share of less liquid assets rose. The finding identifies a deterioration in the liquidity of what remained, not a claim that every fund immediately dumped its least liquid holdings. The IMF also described sharp increases in transaction-cost indicators as underlying market liquidity deteriorated. A fund surviving the first wave by using a buffer must still plan for a subsequent wave ([11]).

A BIS Bulletin on bond funds during the same event used holdings and flow information to identify sales beyond investor redemptions. It found that funds holding less liquid assets often raised end-period cash despite facing withdrawals; those entering stress with higher buffers tended to need smaller precautionary cash increases. This complements the IMF's observation of initial liquid-asset use: different funds, time points, and management responses can produce depletion, replenishment, or both in sequence. It would be false to infer a single invariant liquidation rule from either study. BIS work on emerging-market bond funds similarly found manager-initiated sales reinforcing redemption-driven sales, underscoring that precautionary behavior can amplify aggregate selling even when it protects one fund's next settlement ([10] [11] [13]).

### Institutional designs are evidence of constraints, not proof of success

The FSB's 2023 recommendations distinguish funds mainly holding liquid assets from those with less liquid or materially illiquid assets and link those categories to expectations for dealing terms and anti-dilution tools. IOSCO's 2025 recommendations operationalize this into design, fund-specific monitoring, tool selection, scenario analysis, governance, and disclosure. These publications reflect international supervisory assessments; they are not empirical estimates that any one prescribed tool will prevent the next stress episode. Their significance is that fund liquidity is treated as an ex-ante promise whose costs and failure modes must be disclosed and managed, not as a back-office calculation alone ([1] [2] [3]).

The SEC's Rule 22e-4 illustrates a jurisdiction-specific implementation. Covered U.S. open-end funds must maintain liquidity risk programs with portfolio classification, applicable highly liquid investment minimums, board oversight, and restrictions on purchasing additional illiquid assets when such assets exceed 15 percent of net assets. Money market funds are excluded from this rule; qualifying in-kind ETFs have exceptions to some classification requirements. Neither the 15 percent limit nor classification ensures that liquid holdings will find bids at ordinary spreads in a systemic shock; the rule creates controls and information for managers and boards, not a guarantee of cash conversion at NAV ([4]).

The SEC's ETF investor bulletin describes a different allocation of liquidity work: ordinary holders trade with other market participants, while authorized participants can redeem creation units for securities or cash. In-kind redemptions can reduce the need for the fund to sell securities itself. The distinction helps explain how ETF shares can trade continuously while investors bear secondary-market execution and premium-discount risk. This mechanism differs from both a mutual fund's individual redemption and a closed-end fund's absence of an ordinary creation-redemption arbitrage channel; it should not be collapsed into a binary label of liquid or illiquid ([12]).

For illiquid private assets, the FSB's 2026 report on private credit notes that traditional closed-end structures limit investor redemption mismatch, whereas newer semi-liquid designs with periodic withdrawals need closer attention to loan liquidity and financing. It identifies leverage at several levels and the risk of rolling over fund financing, separate from investor withdrawal rights. A professional review of private-fund secondaries describes a typical closed-end LP interest as transferable subject to manager consent rather than redeemable on investor demand. Neither source establishes that secondary sales will always clear quickly or near manager-stated NAV; they show how illiquidity can be shifted to the LP's sale price and execution process ([14] [15]).

## Implications

### Fund sponsors should design from liabilities backward

The author's synthesis is to begin product design with the strongest realistic investor claim, not the desired marketing label. Specify who may demand payment, the dealing dates, notice and settlement lags, request concentration, distributions, and the circumstances in which a tool can change the ordinary rule. Then list the assets that can provide proceeds at those dates in a severe but plausible market, net of transaction costs and settlement delays. Add margin, debt maturity, and collateral needs to the same schedule. If the proceeds cannot support the promise without distress sales, change the asset mandate, extend the claim's timeline, or change the funding structure before accepting subscriptions ([1] [2] [3] [5]).

A fund that advertises daily liquidity while investing substantially in assets that require long negotiations to sell faces a basic constraint that labels cannot cure. Holding some cash can cover routine withdrawals, but a large cash sleeve may dilute the intended strategy and become exhausted just as other owners decide to leave. A line of credit bridges timing only if it is reliable in the same scenario and the fund can ultimately repay it without harming the remaining investors. Clear notice periods, less frequent dealing, or a genuinely closed-end structure may be more honest contracts for assets whose realization cannot be accelerated cheaply. That judgment depends on actual portfolio composition and investor behavior, not a universal asset label ([1] [3] [5] [14]).

Price tools should be specified and operationally tested before a run. The board or responsible entity needs a defensible estimate of spreads, market impact, and other direct costs; controls over who may alter thresholds and factors; records of application; and a way to explain the resulting cash price to investors. Undercharging permits dilution and retains first-mover incentives; overcharging impedes legitimate withdrawals and can transfer value in the other direction. Governance also should challenge incentives to set the factor for marketing appeal or to protect reported returns rather than investor fairness. The BIS evidence that swing pricing had limited effects in stress is a reminder to keep complementary tools, not a reason to discard pricing altogether ([2] [3] [8] [9]).

Quantity tools require an equally precise investor bargain. A prospectus or partnership agreement should make clear whether the gate is at fund or investor level, how excess requests are allocated, when requests expire or carry forward, whether side-pocket holders can redeem the remainder, and how valuations are controlled. A gate that reallocates execution rights without a transparent rule can create conflict among departing, waiting, and remaining investors. A side pocket can make it possible to redeem liquid assets without assigning the illiquid position to a new investor, but it concentrates valuation discretion and delays realization for existing holders. These devices buy time at the cost of a less certain investor exit date ([2] [3] [14]).

### Allocators should assess the exit route, not just a stated frequency

An allocator should distinguish the legal right to request cash from the practical ability to get it at the expected price when many others do the same. For an open-end fund, examine distribution of asset-sale horizons, redemption concentration, swing or levy rules, borrowing, and prior use of restrictions. For an ETF, examine the distinction between selling shares to another investor and authorized-participant creation-redemption, including spread and premium-discount exposure. For a listed closed-end fund, the portfolio may avoid forced redemptions while an individual investor sells at a wide discount to NAV. For a private partnership, a negotiated secondary sale, potential transfer consent, and unfunded commitments can be more relevant than the last reported NAV ([3] [12] [14] [15]).

Two investors in the same vehicle may have different liquidity needs. A long-horizon owner might prefer fewer NAV-based redemptions if that prevents distressed liquidation, while an owner funding fixed near-term commitments may need a reliable exit even at a price concession. Neither preference makes one design universally superior. The useful question is which risk the investor has agreed to bear: fund-level sales and dilution, restrictions and uncertain payment timing, or secondary-market price variation. When a fund promises near-term redemption and uses illiquid assets to seek higher returns, the apparent return enhancement must be evaluated net of the liquidity service, cash drag, stressed sale cost, and possible lock-in ([1] [3] [12]).

Investor due diligence should include the exact order of cash claims. A fund can hold liquid bonds yet have the same assets pledged as collateral; a committed LP can hold liquid securities in another account yet owe near-term capital calls. Ask what resources remain unencumbered after foreseeable collateral, expenses, and settlement demands. Ask whether a redemption facility is legally committed, how its covenants change after NAV declines, and whether multiple vehicles rely on the same lenders or markets. These are tests of the vehicle's capital and financing architecture, not forecasts of individual security prices ([2] [5] [14]).

### Boards and regulators should evaluate distribution of losses

A board should not judge success solely by whether all redemption requests were paid. If the fund met withdrawals by liquidating its best holdings, the liquidity of remaining owners may have deteriorated; if it borrowed for cash, the remaining owners may bear interest and refinancing risk; if it activated a gate, deferred investors bear waiting risk. Reports should therefore include post-redemption asset composition, transaction costs, price adjustments, drawdown of buffers, financing exposures, and the treatment of queued withdrawals. ESMA's combined asset-liability stress method and IOSCO's disclosure guidance support looking at both immediate payment and consequences for those who stay ([3] [5]).

International recommendations establish design principles, but local law controls which tools a given fund may use and how. The SEC's 22e-4 framework is for specified U.S. registered open-end funds and is not the operating contract for every hedge fund, private fund, or money market fund. A private fund's gate and side-pocket powers depend on its constitutive documents and applicable jurisdiction; they are not automatic management entitlements. The practical implication is to inspect the actual documents and authority chain rather than infer rights from a generic label such as hedge fund or evergreen fund. This is a structural review, not a substitute for legal advice ([1] [2] [3] [4]).

At system level, many managers selling to build buffers can increase market stress beyond the cash actually withdrawn by investors. The BIS 2015 and 2021 analyses demonstrate this possibility in emerging-market and 2020 bond-fund samples; the IMF documents how buffers and liquidation order can change residual holdings. Macroprudential discussion must therefore separate beneficial preparedness of one vehicle from the consequences of many vehicles preparing in the same way at the same time. The author's synthesis is that credible tools need both ex-ante cost allocation and stress assumptions about common selling, rather than treating a fund as the only seller in the market ([10] [11] [13]).

### A decision test joins the architecture to the asset mandate

The author's proposed decision test has five stages. First, state precisely the investor claim: fund redemption at a specified price, transfer of an interest, exchange sale, or a manager-discretionary request. Second, map the payment calendar alongside the minimum orderly realization time of assets under normal and stressed conditions. Third, inventory reliable, unencumbered cash and contingent resources and identify every competing claim on them. Fourth, calculate how sale costs and deferred exits are allocated among transacting and remaining owners under the actual documents. Fifth, reverse-test the conditions that force a gate, suspension, or below-NAV disposal and decide whether the contract should change before those conditions occur. This is a synthesis of the FSB, IOSCO, ESMA, and SEC approaches rather than a prescribed regulatory formula ([1] [2] [3] [4] [5]).

The endpoint is not maximum liquidity at any cost. Less frequent redemptions can make an illiquid strategy feasible but impose a burden on investors who need cash; daily redemptions can provide a valuable service but require reserves, realistic pricing, or a different asset mandate; permanent capital can shield the portfolio while leaving the shareholder with discount and trading risk. A robust fund makes this exchange explicit and allocates stress costs consistently with the promise it sold. When the only way to honor the ordinary promise is to assume calm markets, unlimited dealer capacity, or inexhaustible borrowing, the promise itself is the risk ([1] [3] [8] [12] [14]).
