# Pension Funds and Endowments Turn Long Horizons Into Advantage Only When Claims, Spending, and Governance Are Explicit

Pension funds and endowments both pool capital for obligations that can extend across generations, but they do not owe the same thing: pension assets finance retirement claims, while endowment assets support an institutional mission under donor restrictions and board-approved spending rules [1][3][7][8]. Their long horizons create investment capacity only when funding, distributions, liquidity, delegation, fees, valuation, and decision rights remain consistent with those claims; permanence without those controls can amplify rather than absorb risk [1][4][5][13].

## Background

Pension funds and endowments are institutional ownership vehicles rather than investment styles. A pension fund is a legally separated pool of assets financed by contributions and held for pension benefits; plan members have a legal, beneficial, or contractual claim against the arrangement [1]. An endowment is normally a collection of gifts or institutional funds designated for stated purposes, often pooled for investment even though each component retains its own legal restrictions. Yale, for example, describes its endowment as thousands of funds managed in one pool and reports that approximately 75 percent is restricted to specified uses [8]. The shared activity is capital pooling. The decisive difference is the nature of the claim on that pool.

Retirement assets are economically large and heterogeneous. The OECD reported USD 63.1 trillion of assets earmarked for retirement in OECD countries at the end of 2023, up 10 percent in nominal terms during that year; the total had more than tripled from USD 20.8 trillion at the end of 2003 [2]. Those assets sit in multiple arrangements rather than one universal pension form. Defined-benefit, or DB, plans specify a benefit formula and leave the vehicle and its sponsor to finance the resulting obligation. Funded defined-contribution, or DC, plans accumulate contributions in individual accounts invested in financial assets, so the retirement outcome depends more directly on contributions, net returns, fees, and conversion of the account into retirement income [3]. Public reserve funds, hybrid plans, and collective arrangements add further variations in risk sharing [2][3].

Endowments solve a different financing problem. A charitable or educational institution wants to support programs today without exhausting resources intended to serve the same mission later. The Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act, or UPMIFA, governs donated institutional funds in adopting U.S. jurisdictions through modern investment and expenditure principles, including total-return investing rather than a rigid separation between income and appreciation [6][7]. The governing institution must still honor the gift instrument and decide what appropriation is prudent in light of the fund's duration, purpose, economic conditions, inflation or deflation, expected total return, other resources, and investment policy [7]. An endowment distribution is therefore discretionary within legal and donor constraints, not an actuarially promised pension benefit.

The two vehicles nevertheless meet at a common balance-sheet question: what must the capital accomplish, when must cash leave, and who bears a shortfall? A DB plan must compare assets with projected benefit payments and the assumptions used to value them. A DC plan does not normally promise a fixed benefit, but it must still administer contributions, investment menus or defaults, fees, withdrawals, and retirement conversion in the members' interests [1][3]. An endowment must translate a perpetual or long-lived mission into annual budget support while preserving donor purposes and purchasing power [7][8]. In all three cases, a high expected return does not by itself establish that the vehicle can meet its actual claim.

Institutional investment practice moved beyond portfolios dominated by conventional public equities and bonds as these vehicles grew. Lerner, Schoar, and Wang studied about 1,300 educational endowments from 1992 through 2005 and documented a large increase in alternative-asset allocations, especially among large endowments; they associated superior results at elite institutions with scale, access, and investment expertise rather than with asset labels alone [9]. The 2025 NACUBO-Commonfund Study of Endowments, covering 657 U.S. institutions and affiliated foundations, reported dollar-weighted allocations led by private equity, marketable alternatives, U.S. equities, venture capital, and fixed income [12]. Pension funds also use private equity, real estate, infrastructure, hedge funds, private credit, and derivatives, creating the same requirements for commitment pacing, liquidity, valuation, fee measurement, and oversight [1][5][14].

Delegation became a defining structural feature. Boards usually set purpose, risk tolerance, asset allocation, and oversight standards, while internal staff, consultants, custodians, actuaries, external managers, and private-fund general partners execute specialized functions [1][4][5]. Delegation can add expertise and reach, but it lengthens the principal-agent chain. The OECD identifies board competence, balanced representation, conflicts controls, and clear responsibility as central pension-governance issues [4]. UPMIFA permits delegation of endowment investment functions subject to prudence in selecting, defining, and monitoring the agent [7]. The authority to outsource work does not outsource the governing body's accountability.

Long-horizon capital is often described as patient because beneficiaries or programs do not normally redeem at daily net asset value. That description is incomplete. A mature pension can pay benefits faster than contributions arrive; a leveraged hedge can demand collateral immediately; a private-market program can face capital calls when liquid assets fall; and an endowment can be asked to support operations during a recession that also depresses its portfolio [1][8][13]. Synthesis: the practical horizon is the shortest binding clock among benefit payments, spending distributions, financing calls, contractual commitments, and governance response time. A perpetual charter cannot make capital patient if one of those clocks forces a sale.

The comparison also clarifies what belongs outside this topic. Detailed pension accounting assumptions belong to accounting analysis; security selection and valuation belong to valuation; strategic asset allocation mathematics belongs to portfolio risk management; and personal retirement planning belongs elsewhere. Here the unit of analysis is the vehicle: how claims, funding sources, spending or benefit rules, governance, delegation, liquidity, fees, valuation, and public or donor accountability shape the ability to compound capital over long periods.

## Core Concepts

### The claim determines the vehicle's economic benchmark

A DB pension begins with promised benefits. The obligation is a schedule of future payments whose present value depends on benefit formulas, demographic assumptions, inflation linkage, and the discount basis. OECD/IOPS good practices require plans offering defined benefits or guarantees to maintain appropriate assets against their commitments and to adopt funding and solvency policies that specify monitoring, target funding, recovery from underfunding, and treatment of surpluses [1]. The economic benchmark is therefore funded status and benefit security, not simply whether the asset portfolio outperformed a market index.

A funded DC plan changes the claim rather than eliminating responsibility. Contributions accumulate in individual accounts, and the eventual pension depends on the account balance and the way it is converted into income [3]. Investment and longevity risk shift toward the member, but governing institutions still choose or supervise defaults, menus, disclosure, fees, administration, and sometimes drawdown or annuity options. The OECD notes that governance gaps can be especially important in DC arrangements because no traditional DB sponsor may be clearly responsible for the final benefit [4]. A DC vehicle should be judged by member outcomes and the quality of its default architecture, not by the absence of a sponsor-guaranteed liability.

An endowment has no class of beneficiaries entitled to a fixed actuarial payment. Its claims come from donor restrictions, the institution's mission, board appropriations, and operating dependence on recurring distributions [6][7][8]. Funds restricted to scholarships cannot ordinarily be redirected to unrelated facilities merely because the institution has a cash need. The institution can pool assets operationally while retaining separate legal purposes [8]. Its benchmark is thus a combination of mission support, compliance with restrictions, stability of distributions, preservation of real purchasing power, and investment performance net of all costs.

Synthesis: these three architectures allocate residual risk differently. In DB, the sponsor, guaranty arrangement, participants, or some combination ultimately absorbs underfunding through contributions, benefit changes where lawful, or loss. In DC, the member's account absorbs market outcomes and fees more directly. In an endowment, current programs, future programs, donors' purposes, and the institution's wider balance sheet absorb the consequences of excessive or insufficient spending. An investment committee cannot define risk until it identifies which party loses, by how much, and through which legal mechanism [1][3][7].

### Funding rules create or erode investment capacity

Pension funding connects benefit design to cash contributions and assets. A DB plan can appear long-lived while remaining economically fragile if contributions are discretionary, discount assumptions reduce reported liabilities without changing promised cash flows, or recovery periods postpone funding gaps [1]. A strong policy states contribution sources, target funding, tolerance for temporary deficits, triggers for corrective action, and the sponsor's capacity to contribute in adverse conditions. The OECD/IOPS framework treats sponsor strength and responsibility for guarantees as part of funding risk, not as external facts unrelated to the investment portfolio [1].

DC funding is simpler in form but not in effect. The contribution rate, contribution regularity, employer match, vesting, default enrollment, investment expenses, and time in the system jointly determine the amount that compounds [3]. A vehicle with no actuarial deficit can still produce inadequate retirement income if contributions are too low, participation is interrupted, fees are excessive, or the investment default is unsuitable. Synthesis: DB funding risk is visible as a gap between assets and promised liabilities; DC adequacy risk is dispersed across many accounts and may become visible only when members retire.

Endowment funding comes from gifts, retained investment returns, and sometimes transfers of institutional reserves. Each gift can impose purpose and duration restrictions. UPMIFA's total-return framework permits prudent expenditure from both income and appreciation rather than treating dividends and interest as uniquely spendable, but the board must evaluate preservation, purpose, inflation, expected return, other resources, and investment policy [6][7]. The economic constraint is straightforward even when the legal test is qualitative: over long periods, spending plus fees cannot persistently exceed the portfolio's net real return without reducing real purchasing power.

A spending rule converts that constraint into annual cash. Common designs apply a fixed percentage to a trailing average market value, blend the prior year's distribution with a target percentage of current value, or impose floors and caps on annual change. Yale states that it aims to spend 5.25 percent of endowment value each year and uses a policy designed to smooth short-term market fluctuations because abrupt reductions would disrupt its budget [8]. Synthesis: smoothing reallocates volatility across time; it does not eliminate it. A rule that stabilizes today's distribution after a market decline can temporarily raise the effective payout rate and transfer more adjustment to future budgets.

### Asset-liability management is broader than duration matching

For a DB plan, asset-liability management maps benefit cash flows, interest-rate and inflation sensitivity, sponsor contributions, and funding objectives against the asset portfolio [1][13]. Liability-driven investing can use physical bonds or derivatives to reduce mismatch, but leverage adds collateral and operational clocks. The Pensions Regulator states that many DB schemes use LDI to match assets and liabilities and requires robust processes to withstand market shocks; its monitoring framework includes funding levels, stress tests, hedge ratios, and the behavior of growth and matching assets [13]. A long-duration hedge is useful only if the vehicle can finance it through the path of market movements.

An endowment has no actuarial liability curve, but it has a spending liability in an economic sense. Annual appropriations support payroll, scholarships, research, facilities, and other programs, while private-fund commitments create additional future cash calls [8][12]. The endowment should therefore model operating distributions, donor-restricted uses, capital calls, collateral, debt service where relevant, and emergency support together. Synthesis: an endowment's liability is partly endogenous because the board can change future spending, but dependence of the operating budget makes rapid reductions costly even when legally possible.

DC plans face another form of asset-liability relationship. The relevant horizon and risk capacity vary across members, so lifecycle or target-date defaults commonly reduce risk as retirement approaches. The vehicle also must manage contribution and withdrawal flows, transfers, benefit conversion, and the behavior of members under stress. Synthesis: the aggregate plan can look permanent while each member's claim has a finite horizon. Treating the pool's continuity as permission to expose near-retirement members to the same illiquidity or volatility as young contributors confuses institutional permanence with individual risk capacity [3][4].

Liquidity analysis should therefore use multiple clocks. Benefit payments and endowment distributions are scheduled cash needs; private-market commitments are contingent calls; derivatives and borrowing can impose daily or intraday demands; and portfolio assets have different sale and settlement horizons [1][13]. The governing body should distinguish cash on hand, unencumbered liquid securities, contractual but uncertain credit, and assets that require negotiation or discounts to sell. Counting one asset simultaneously as a return generator, spending reserve, capital-call reserve, and collateral source overstates resilience.

### Governance separates purpose, policy, and execution

A durable structure assigns different decisions to the owner or sponsor, governing board, investment staff, and external agents. The sponsor or institution defines the mission and bears specified residual risks. The board approves funding or spending policy, strategic risk, delegation, and oversight. Staff implement the policy, select investments or managers within authority, manage operations, and report. Independent control functions test valuation, compliance, custody, risk, and performance [1][4][5]. Separation prevents both board micromanagement of individual investments and staff expansion of the mandate without accountable approval.

Board composition matters because these vehicles administer capital for people who do not control day-to-day decisions. OECD research identifies expertise, stakeholder representation, training, independent trustees, conflicts policies, and scale as recurring pension-governance issues [4]. The World Bank's study of major Canadian public pension organizations describes a model built around arm's-length governance, professional internal management, scale, and diversification, supported by clear legislation and persistent institutional development [5]. These are complements: independence without competence can isolate poor decisions, while competence without accountable authority can create an unreviewable technocracy.

Endowment governance must join investment oversight with institutional finance and donor compliance. An investment committee may approve asset allocation and managers, while a finance committee or full board approves spending and the operating budget. Yale describes its investment committee as providing strategic guidance, reviewing allocation and investment decisions, and overseeing stewardship [8]. UPMIFA makes prudence a process that considers the whole fund and the institution's circumstances; it does not equate prudence with choosing the most conservative asset or achieving a favorable result after the fact [6][7].

Synthesis: governance quality should be tested at the seams. Who may change the discount rate, expected return, spending rate, private-market pacing, leverage limit, benchmark, or valuation policy? Who can approve an exception? Which body sees the consolidated liquidity position? Who reports a breach, and can that person bypass the executive responsible for performance? A policy is incomplete when authority is clear inside each silo but ambiguous where funding, portfolio, and operations interact [1][4][5].

### Delegated management adds a second portfolio of relationships

Institutional pools rarely select every security internally. They hire external managers, invest through limited partnerships, retain consultants, and use administrators, actuaries, custodians, and valuation agents [1][5]. The contractual portfolio of mandates can be as consequential as the asset portfolio. Lockups, notice periods, side letters, key-person provisions, fee waterfalls, reporting rights, valuation methods, custody, benchmark definitions, and termination rights determine what the owner can know and do.

Manager selection should begin with a required function rather than a recent return. Goyal and Wahal examined 3,400 plan sponsors from 1994 through 2003 and found that sponsors hired managers after strong excess performance, but the return-chasing did not produce positive excess returns after hiring. In round-trip decisions, replacing a fired manager with a newly hired manager did not improve excess performance relative to retaining the fired manager [11]. The evidence does not prove that managers should never be terminated. It shows that past performance alone is a weak substitute for a documented thesis about skill, process, capacity, fees, organization, and portfolio role.

Private assets intensify the delegation problem. Reported values rely on appraisal and manager estimates between transactions, fees can be distributed across management companies, funds, portfolio companies, and carried interest, and capital cannot be withdrawn on demand [1][14]. The OECD/IOPS framework calls for attention to lockups, unlisted-asset valuation, liquidity, leverage, and sufficient manager reporting [1]. Pew reports that incomplete private-equity fee disclosure can omit carried interest and other costs material to evaluating net performance [14]. A long horizon permits illiquidity; it does not make opaque costs or stale values harmless.

### Scale creates options but also organizational risk

Scale can lower unit costs, support internal teams, improve bargaining power, and provide access to specialized markets. The OECD's pension-governance work argues that consolidation may help small funds obtain economies of scale and devote more resources to governance [4]. The World Bank's Canadian cases connect scale with professional in-house management and global diversification [5]. Lerner, Schoar, and Wang likewise find that large elite endowments benefited from investment-management scale and access during their sample period [9].

Scale does not guarantee skill. A larger institution can accumulate bureaucracy, political attention, concentration by manager, and pressure to deploy large amounts. Internal management replaces an external fee with compensation, systems, governance, and operational obligations; external management buys specialization but introduces agency costs and opacity [5][14]. Synthesis: the relevant question is not whether internal or external management is categorically cheaper. It is whether the all-in operating model produces a replicable net return or risk-reduction benefit after staff, systems, external fees, carried interest, transaction costs, valuation uncertainty, and oversight.

### Intergenerational equity is a decision rule, not a slogan

Endowments explicitly balance current and future mission support. Yale describes pooled management as a means of providing future generations with resources comparable to those available today, and its spending policy is designed to reduce short-term budget disruption [8]. UPMIFA requires boards to consider both the duration and preservation of the fund and the purposes for which it exists [7]. Spending too much can impoverish future programs; spending too little can withhold resources from the mission and beneficiaries for whom donors intended support.

Pension systems also distribute risk across generations and cohorts. DB contribution holidays, optimistic assumptions, delayed amortization, or benefit changes can move costs among workers, retirees, sponsors, taxpayers, and future entrants. DC systems make accumulation more individually visible but can still distribute costs through default design, fees, subsidies, and retirement-conversion rules [1][3]. Synthesis: intergenerational fairness cannot be inferred from a stable headline contribution or spending rate. It requires showing how adverse investment, inflation, longevity, and funding outcomes change each cohort's contributions, benefits, or program support.

A practical fairness test uses real, not merely nominal, capacity. For an endowment, the board compares distributions and costs with returns after inflation while respecting the mission. For a DB plan, the board compares assets and expected contributions with benefit cash flows under realistic assumptions. For DC, the plan evaluates whether contribution and default arrangements plausibly support adequate retirement income for members with different ages and work histories [1][3][7]. The author's assessment is that fairness improves when adjustment rules are specified before a shortfall, because after-the-fact discretion invites the strongest present constituency to transfer losses to less represented future claimants.

## Evidence

The global pension evidence establishes both scale and structural change. OECD data show USD 63.1 trillion of retirement assets in OECD countries at the end of 2023, with assets more than tripling in current prices over two decades [2]. The same report found that the long-term shift away from DB toward DC arrangements continued even as funding ratios for DB plans improved in several jurisdictions during 2023 [2]. The data are aggregated across legal systems and do not identify one optimal vehicle. They show that decisions about claim design and governance affect a stock of capital large enough to influence households, public finance, and markets.

The OECD/IOPS good-practices document supplies institutional evidence rather than a performance experiment. It defines a pension fund as a legally separated pool for pension benefits, assigns the governing board responsibility for the risk-management system, requires funding and solvency controls for guarantees, and lists the components of a comprehensive investment policy: objectives, allocation, diversification, liquidity, currency exposure, valuation, derivatives, asset-liability targets, benchmarks, controls, alternatives, and leverage [1]. The framework's strength is coverage of interacting risks. Its limit is normative status: adoption of a policy does not prove that a board understands or enforces it.

The World Bank's Canadian-model study used structured interviews with 25 current and former pension leaders and advisers, combined with institutional case studies [5]. It identifies independent governance, professional in-house management, scale, and broad diversification as recurring characteristics of major Canadian public pension organizations [5]. The method provides process detail and comparative lessons, not random assignment. Successful institutions may possess unmeasured political, labor, or administrative conditions that are difficult to transfer. The evidence supports sequencing governance and organizational capability before complex investment expansion; it does not justify copying a private-asset allocation without the supporting institution.

Andonov, Hochberg, and Rauh provide more direct evidence that board architecture can correlate with investment outcomes. They matched board-composition data for public pension funds with thousands of private-market investments and found that higher representation by state-appointed or ex officio officials was associated with weaker net private-equity performance, different category allocations, and greater selection of local or otherwise disadvantaged investments [10]. Their estimates are observational, and the sample concerns private-market investments rather than the entire pension portfolio. Statutory board composition may correlate with other state characteristics. The bounded conclusion is that political representation and financial expertise can affect selection and oversight; a formal fiduciary label does not neutralize governance incentives.

Manager-hiring evidence tests another recurrent governance behavior. Goyal and Wahal's sample of 3,400 plan sponsors showed that recently strong manager performance influenced hiring, but subsequent excess returns did not validate the chase [11]. Terminations had multiple causes, and fired managers' later excess returns were generally indistinguishable from zero or sometimes positive; in paired firing-and-hiring decisions, replacement did not create a clear advantage [11]. The study covers a particular 1994-2003 period and mainly conventional institutional mandates, so it cannot settle every private-market or organizational decision. It does demonstrate why boards should preserve the ex ante selection thesis and evaluate process, fees, capacity, and portfolio function rather than rewrite the rationale around recent returns.

The endowment evidence shows both potential and inequality of capability. Lerner, Schoar, and Wang analyzed roughly 1,300 educational endowments between 1992 and 2005. Median alternative allocations rose substantially, and elite endowments earned higher returns associated with alternative assets, scale, and access to skilled managers [9]. The authors did not find that every institution could obtain the same result merely by matching allocations. Access, selection skill, and organizational resources differentiated the leaders [9]. This limits the popular inference that the endowment model is a portable asset-mix recipe.

Recent survey evidence confirms that alternatives and spending remain central. The 2025 NACUBO-Commonfund study covered 657 U.S. colleges, universities, and affiliated foundations. Participants reported a 10-year average annual net return of 7.7 percent and a one-year FY2025 net return of 10.9 percent; dollar-weighted allocations were led by private equity at 16.8 percent, marketable alternatives at 15.4 percent, U.S. equities at 13.7 percent, venture capital at 12.2 percent, and fixed income at 10.7 percent [12]. Endowments funded an average 15.2 percent of participating institutions' operating budgets, up from 14.0 percent in FY2024 [12]. The study is based on voluntary participants and aggregate categories, so it cannot establish causal superiority of any allocation. It does show that portfolio liquidity and institutional operations are economically linked.

Yale supplies a transparent case of that link. Yale reports that its endowment supports roughly one-third of the university's annual operating budget, aims to spend 5.25 percent of value each year, and uses a smoothing policy to limit disruptive budget changes when markets fall [8]. It describes the Yale Model as long-term, equity-oriented, diversified, and implemented through partnerships with selected managers [8]. This is primary evidence of Yale's policy and governance, not proof that another institution can replicate its returns. The case instead illustrates a vehicle whose spending dependence, restricted funds, manager relationships, and investment horizon must be managed together.

UPMIFA provides legal-design evidence for endowments. The Uniform Law Commission states that the act modernizes management of donated institutional funds by incorporating portfolio theory and permitting total-return investing [6]. NACUBO's summary explains that prudent appropriation replaces the older historic-dollar-value constraint and requires consideration of preservation, purpose, economic conditions, inflation, expected total return, other resources, and investment policy [7]. The standard grants flexibility while making board process more important. It does not create a universal safe spending rate, guarantee that an underwater fund should be spent, or override a donor's controlling restriction [7].

The 2022 UK gilt episode demonstrates that a nominally long horizon can fail through short financing clocks. The Pensions Regulator reports that sharp and rapid gilt-yield increases led to widespread gilt sales by pension LDI arrangements and required Bank of England intervention to restore market functioning [13]. Its response emphasizes collateral resilience, stress testing, governance, monitoring, and operational capacity [13]. The case concerns leveraged LDI in a specific market, not all pension funds or all liability matching. Its general lesson is narrower and stronger: an improvement in long-term asset-liability alignment can still create immediate liquidity risk if the financing path is not funded.

Fee evidence reveals another place where reported horizon and economic outcome diverge. Pew's review of large U.S. state plans notes expanded use of alternative investments and continuing variation in cost disclosure. It reports that undisclosed private-equity fees, including carried interest, can equal 1.5 percent or more of annual assets in the relevant estimate and describes reforms that report management fees, fund expenses, and performance fees more completely [14]. Pew is a policy research organization rather than an official accounting standard setter, so the estimate should not be generalized to every plan. It supports a control principle: gross or partially net returns are not comparable until the owner identifies all fee layers and the valuation basis.

Taken together, the evidence rejects two simple stories. First, a long horizon is not by itself an investment edge: governance, cash-flow design, access, skill, and liquidity determine whether the horizon can be used [1][5][9][13]. Second, complex assets are neither automatically superior nor automatically imprudent: large endowments and Canadian pension organizations show potential benefits, while manager-selection, board, valuation, fee, and collateral evidence identifies conditions under which complexity destroys value [5][9][10][11][14]. The author's synthesis is that vehicle architecture is the enabling technology. It cannot generate returns, but it determines whether a sound investment strategy survives long enough and transparently enough to realize them.

## Implications

### For pension sponsors and trustees

Begin with the benefit or account claim, not the desired return. A DB board should maintain a cash-flow and sensitivity map of benefits, contributions, inflation linkage, demographic uncertainty, and the valuation bases used for funding, accounting, and transactions [1][13]. It should state who contributes after an adverse outcome, over what period, and under which triggers. An expected-return assumption should not be used to erase the distinction between a valuable risky portfolio and a promised benefit; returns can finance a liability, but changing the forecast does not change the cash owed.

A DC governing body should define success in member terms. Contribution adequacy, default design, diversification, fees, portability, communication, and retirement conversion all influence outcomes even though the sponsor does not promise a fixed pension [3][4]. Reports should show net results for representative cohorts rather than only aggregate fund returns. The author's synthesis is that a DC plan's central governance failure is not formal underfunding but silent inadequacy: the system can balance perfectly while members accumulate too little or bear risks they did not understand.

Funding and investment policies should be tested jointly. A DB plan that expects sponsor contributions after losses should evaluate whether the sponsor will be financially weakest in the same scenario. A public plan should show how statutory contribution practices and political budget pressure affect recovery. A corporate plan should evaluate sponsor credit, covenant, and operating exposure. The board should distinguish policy choices from actuarial estimates and report how each change affects contributions, funded status, and benefit security [1][4].

Leverage should be sized backward from liquidity. For any swap, repo, derivative overlay, or borrowing, identify the legal entity that receives the call, eligible collateral, transfer time, authority to act, and the market move that exhausts the buffer [1][13]. The worst preventable outcome is a solvent long-horizon pool forced to unwind a sensible hedge or sell growth assets because cash and authority were located elsewhere. A leverage limit based only on expected volatility omits the operating mechanism that causes failure.

### For endowment boards and institutional leaders

Treat spending policy as part of investment policy. The board should publish the target rate, valuation base, averaging or smoothing formula, treatment of underwater funds, floors or caps, fees charged to funds, and authority for exceptions [7][8]. It should also show the real-return arithmetic: expected nominal return less inflation, investment costs, administrative costs, and spending. A payout can be legally prudent under the facts of one year yet economically unsustainable if repeated under weaker returns.

Budget dependence should set liquidity limits. An institution receiving a large share of operating revenue from its endowment has less freedom to suspend distributions after a loss than a peer with diversified revenues [8][12]. Private-market commitments, donor restrictions, debt service, collateral, and emergency operating support should be placed on one cash-flow calendar. Restricted assets should not be counted as freely available simply because they are invested in the same pool. Synthesis: the effective liquidity reserve is the portion that is both financially liquid and legally usable for the need being modeled.

Intergenerational equity requires two-sided discipline. Overspending transfers resources from future students, patients, researchers, or beneficiaries to the present; underspending can deny current beneficiaries the support for which funds were donated [7][8]. The board should therefore report both preservation and mission delivery: real value by restriction class, distributions by purpose, operating-budget dependence, and stress outcomes under alternative return and inflation sequences. A single endowment growth rate cannot show whether the institution fulfilled donor purposes or shared resources fairly across time.

Do not copy the asset mix of a large endowment without copying its capabilities and constraints. The Lerner, Schoar, and Wang evidence ties elite outcomes to scale, access, and selection expertise, not alternatives alone [9]. A smaller institution should compare the net expected benefit of each complex mandate with the cost of staff, consultant dependence, manager fees, illiquidity, valuation lag, and governance time. Public-market assets can be a superior structural choice when the organization cannot reliably select, monitor, and finance private assets.

### For both types of governing bodies

Use an integrated mandate with seven linked components. First, define the claim and who bears residual loss. Second, specify funding inflows and benefit or spending outflows. Third, set the strategic risk budget against that claim. Fourth, reserve liquidity for distributions, collateral, and commitments. Fifth, define delegation and escalation rights. Sixth, measure net performance, total fees, and valuation uncertainty. Seventh, report outcomes to beneficiaries, donors, sponsors, regulators, and the public in forms tied to their actual interests. This is the author's synthesis of the OECD/IOPS, World Bank, UPMIFA, and empirical governance evidence [1][5][7][10][11].

Separate policy return from implementation return. The board owns the strategic benchmark, funding or spending rule, and material constraints. Staff and managers own implementation within delegated authority. Contributions, gifts, and withdrawals change asset values but are not investment performance. Private-asset appraisal changes should not be treated as equivalent to realized cash without explanation. Attribution should identify market exposure, active decisions, leverage, currency, fees, and policy cash flows so that each decision maker is evaluated for the choices actually controlled [1][5][14].

Preserve the ex ante record. Before hiring a manager or entering a private fund, document the portfolio role, expected source of return, benchmark, capacity, fees, liquidity, key-person dependence, valuation, information rights, and conditions for termination. Goyal and Wahal's evidence shows why recent performance should not become the primary selection rule [11]. At review, compare the original thesis with observed process and results; do not retroactively redefine success around whichever metric looks favorable.

Make fee reporting comprehensive and decision-useful. Include internal costs, consulting, custody, management fees, performance fees or carried interest, partnership expenses, portfolio-company fees where borne economically by investors, transaction costs, and financing costs [1][14]. Report gross and net results against a benchmark that can actually be implemented. The question is not whether a fee is high in isolation but whether the net, risk-adjusted, liquidity-adjusted result compensates the vehicle for complexity and lost optionality.

Governance reviews should examine competence and incentives as well as formal independence. Boards need financial, actuarial, legal, operational, and mission knowledge; no single constituency supplies all of it [4][5]. Appointment methods, term lengths, training, conflicts, political or donor influence, staff compensation, and access to independent advice should be explicit. Andonov, Hochberg, and Rauh's findings do not prescribe one universal board composition, but they justify testing whether representation and incentives distort manager selection, local investment, or risk taking [10].

Stress tests should join markets, funding, operations, and governance. A pension scenario might combine falling growth assets, changing discount rates, higher collateral calls, weak sponsor finances, and rising benefit outflows. An endowment scenario might combine a market decline, reduced gifts, higher inflation, private-fund calls, debt constraints, and greater operating demand. The test should identify the first binding limit, decision owner, and corrective action. If the assumed response requires selling an asset that cannot settle in time or using a restricted fund for an ineligible purpose, the scenario has not been solved [1][7][13].

Transparency should match the claim. Pension members need funded status or account outcomes, contribution policy, net returns, fees, and benefit risks. Donors and institutional beneficiaries need distributions by purpose, real preservation, restrictions, costs, and governance. Public plans also owe taxpayers and elected bodies enough information to distinguish policy choices from investment execution [4][14]. Transparency need not disclose every position in real time; it must reveal the mandate, authority, costs, valuation basis, and consequences well enough to assign responsibility.

For allocators, beneficiaries, and analysts, evaluate the vehicle before praising its horizon. Ask whether capital is truly durable, which payments are mandatory, what spending can change, how assets are valued, what is encumbered, who can demand cash, and whether governance can act before a forced sale. A pension fund can have permanent institutional existence but a mature negative cash flow. An endowment can be legally perpetual but heavily dependent on annual distributions. The author's assessment is that patient capital is not a legal category. It is a demonstrated ability to refuse bad sales and bad investments while continuing to meet legitimate claims.

The durable conclusion is conditional. Pension funds and endowments can convert scale and time into diversification, internal capability, access, countercyclical action, and compounding. They do so only when the vehicle makes obligations or purposes explicit, funds them honestly, controls liquidity and leverage, prices delegation and fees, values assets consistently, and assigns authority before stress [1][5][7][9][13]. Without those conditions, the long horizon becomes a narrative that hides current underfunding, excessive spending, stale valuation, political direction, or illiquidity from the people whose capital and future support are at risk.
