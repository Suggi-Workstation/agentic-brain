# Hedge Fund Structures -- How Fee Arrangements, Lockups, and Leverage Shape Manager Incentives and Systemic Risk

Hedge funds are privately organized investment partnerships that use performance-based fees, restricted liquidity, and leveraged capital to pursue absolute returns uncorrelated with market direction. Invented by Alfred Winslow Jones in 1949, the hedge fund structure has grown into a global industry managing over $5 trillion across thousands of funds. The defining features of the vehicle -- the 2-and-20 fee model, high-water marks, lockup periods, redemption gates, side pockets, and leverage obtained through prime brokerage -- are not incidental details but structural choices that govern manager incentives, risk-taking behavior, and the systemic fragility of the financial system.

## Background

The first hedge fund was created in 1949 by Alfred Winslow Jones, a
sociologist, former Fortune journalist, and onetime U.S. Vice Consul
in Berlin who formed an investment partnership with $40,000 of his
own money and $60,000 from friends. Jones had been researching
technical market analysis methods for a Fortune article titled
"Fashions in Forecasting" when he began formulating ideas for a fund
that could keep its capital fully invested while reducing exposure to
market swings. His key insight was that a manager could combine two
speculative techniques -- buying stocks with leverage and selling
short other stocks -- to create what he considered a conservative
portfolio. He described the approach as "hedging," a term used on
Wall Street to describe risk management, and he called his fund a
"hedged fund" [high].

Jones structured the partnership as a limited partnership with no
more than 99 investors, deliberately avoiding registration under the
Investment Company Act of 1940. He chose to take 20 percent of
profits as compensation, invoking the Phoenician sea captains who
kept a fifth of the profits from successful voyages. Initially he
charged no fee unless he made a profit. He later added a 2 percent
management fee on all assets regardless of performance. These three
elements -- a partnership structure with a percentage of profits paid
to the general partner, a small number of limited partner investors,
and a combination of long and short positions -- became the template
for every hedge fund that followed [high].

The term "hedge fund" was coined by Carol Loomis in a 1966 Fortune
article, "The Jones Nobody Keeps Up With," which revealed that Jones
had gained 670 percent over ten years compared to 358 percent for the
best-performing mutual fund. The article catalyzed interest in the
structure, and by 1968 New York magazine called Jones the "big
daddy" of the hedge fund business. Despite this attention, the
industry remained small for decades. Jones himself maintained a low
profile, preferring to remain almost invisible. "Hedging," he once
said, "is a speculative tool used to conservative ends" [medium].

The industry grew slowly through the 1970s and 1980s, then
accelerated dramatically in the 1990s. By the turn of the millennium,
hedge funds managed approximately $200 billion. This grew to over $1
trillion before the 2008 financial crisis, contracted sharply during
the crisis as investors redeemed approximately 16 percent of industry
assets over a twelve-month period, and then resumed growth. By Q1
2026, total global hedge fund industry capital reached a record
$5.22 trillion, its 14th consecutive quarterly gain, as reported by
Hedge Fund Research (HFR). An estimated 562 hedge funds launched in
2025, the most since 2021, against just 287 liquidations [medium].

The post-2008 period transformed the industry in several structural
ways. Before 2009, many hedge funds marketed their strategies as
generating absolute returns with a benchmark of T-bills plus 400 basis
points, giving investors the impression they would generate positive
returns regardless of market direction. The average hedge fund lost
approximately 18 percent in 2008, shattering that expectation. Since
then, managers have done a better job of educating investors about
their investment process and expected behavior in different market
environments. Operational due diligence, largely nonexistent before
2009, became standard practice after the Bernie Madoff fraud exposed
weaknesses in the selection process. The alignment of liquidity terms
with underlying investments improved substantially: before 2008,
many funds offered monthly liquidity with 30-day notice to attract
fund-of-funds assets regardless of the liquidity of the underlying
portfolio, and this mismatch contributed to the wave of gates and
redemption suspensions during the crisis [medium].

The industry also became more concentrated. By 2026, billion-dollar-
plus firms held approximately 86 percent of all industry assets,
roughly $3.6 trillion. The top 5 percent of funds attracted 80 to 90
percent of net asset flows. This concentration reflected a
preference among institutional investors -- pension funds, endowments,
sovereign wealth funds -- for established managers with robust
infrastructure. The investor base shifted from fund-of-funds and
high-net-worth individuals toward institutional allocators who are
longer-term oriented and more stable [medium].

## Core Concepts

### The 2-and-20 Fee Model

The defining economic feature of the hedge fund vehicle is its
two-part fee structure. The "2-and-20" model consists of a 2 percent
annual management fee charged on assets under management (AUM)
regardless of performance, plus a 20 percent incentive fee (also
called a performance fee or carried interest) charged on profits. The
management fee compensates the fund for operating costs -- salaries,
rent, data, compliance -- and is collected whether the fund gains,
loses, or stays flat. The incentive fee aligns the manager's
interests with investors by rewarding profitable performance [medium].

The arithmetic of this model reveals its economic significance.
Consider a $500 million fund at 2-and-20 that returns 12 percent
gross in a strong year. The management fee is $10 million, the
incentive fee is $12 million (20 percent of the $60 million gross
profit), and total fees are $22 million, leaving the investor with a
net return of 7.6 percent. In a flat year, the investor loses 2
percent to management fees alone. In a loss year with an 8 percent
gross decline, the management fee compounds the drawdown, turning an
8 percent gross loss into a 9.6 percent net loss. The management fee
is asymmetric: it is paid in all scenarios, while the incentive fee
is paid only in profitable ones [medium].

Fee compression has reduced average fees significantly. By recent
years, the average management fee had declined to approximately 1.34
percent and the average performance fee to approximately 15.8
percent, according to HFR data. New fund launches in 2023 averaged
1.2 percent management fees. Despite this compression, the structure
remains the template across hedge funds, private equity, and venture
capital. The Hedgeweek-AIMA H1 2026 Allocator Survey found that
preferred management fee bands shifted decisively downward, with the
1.5-1.99 percent range jumping from 21 percent to 35 percent of
preferences, suggesting it is becoming the new industry standard
[medium].

At the extreme end, multi-strategy platforms have actually increased
effective fees through pass-through expense models. When technology
costs, data subscriptions, and operational overhead are passed
through to investors, effective fees at firms like Citadel and
Millennium can reach 5 to 10 percent or more of AUM. Renaissance
Technologies' Medallion Fund famously charges a 44 percent
performance fee plus a 5 percent management fee, and investors
eagerly pay it because net returns still far exceed alternatives
[medium].

### The High-Water Mark

The high-water mark (HWM) is the single most important investor
protection in the hedge fund fee structure. It represents the
highest previous net asset value (NAV) that the fund has achieved. The
manager can only charge an incentive fee on gains above this mark. If
the fund loses money, it must first climb back above its prior peak
before any new performance fee is charged. Without it, an investor
could pay incentive fees on the same dollars twice -- once on the
way up, and again after a loss and recovery [medium].

The mechanics work as follows. In Year 1, a fund starts at $500
million, returns 15 percent gross, reaching $575 million before fees.
The management fee is $10 million and the incentive fee is $15
million (20 percent of $75 million), leaving NAV at $550 million. The
high-water mark is set at $550 million. In Year 2, the fund falls 10
percent to $495 million. No incentive fee is charged because NAV is
below the HWM. In Year 3, the fund rises 20 percent to $594 million
before fees, but since this exceeds the prior HWM of $550 million,
the incentive fee applies only to the $44 million gain above the
mark, not to the full $99 million recovery from the Year 2 trough.
The HWM ensures the manager earns performance fees only for creating
new wealth, not for recovering losses the investor already suffered
[medium].

A limitation of the HWM is that it does not require the manager to
return fees already collected in prior profitable years. If a fund
earns a large incentive fee in Year 1 and then loses substantially in
Year 2, the manager retains the Year 1 fee. The investor bears the
full loss while the manager has already captured the upside. This
asymmetry is a structural feature of the vehicle, not a bug in its
documentation [medium].

### Hurdle Rates

A hurdle rate sets a minimum return threshold that the fund must
exceed before any incentive fee applies. Hurdle rates are less common
than high-water marks but provide complementary investor protection
by ensuring investors receive a baseline return before sharing
profits with the manager. The benchmark is often a cash rate --
historically something like the 3-month Treasury bill yield, an
interbank rate, or a fixed number such as 5 percent or 8 percent. The
logic is that an investor should not pay an active manager 20 percent
of returns that merely match what risk-free cash would have delivered
[medium].

There are two types of hurdle, and the distinction materially affects
the fee. A hard hurdle means the performance fee applies only to
returns above the hurdle. If the hurdle is 5 percent and the fund
returns 12 percent, the fee is charged on the 7 percent excess
return, not the full 12 percent. A soft hurdle means that once the
fund clears the threshold, the performance fee applies to the entire
return. If the hurdle is 5 percent and the fund returns 12 percent,
having cleared 5 percent the manager collects on the full 12 percent.
Hard hurdles are more favorable to investors; soft hurdles are more
favorable to managers. A fund with both a high-water mark and a
hurdle rate must satisfy both conditions before charging an incentive
fee [medium].

### Lockup Periods

A lockup period is a contractual provision that prevents investors
from withdrawing their capital for a predetermined timeframe,
typically ranging from one to three years. Lockups serve a critical
function: they protect the fund from forced liquidation of illiquid
positions at unfavorable prices during periods when the manager needs
time for the investment thesis to play out. Without lockups, investor
redemptions during market stress can force a fund to sell its best
liquid positions first, leaving remaining investors with a
concentrated, illiquid, and lower-quality portfolio [medium].

Lockups come in two varieties. A hard lockup is an absolute bar on
redemptions during the lockup period. A soft lockup is a period
during which redemption is permitted but subject to an early
redemption charge that is paid to the fund, often used as a
behavioral anchor rather than an absolute restriction. The choice
depends on the liquidity of the underlying portfolio. A fund holding
large-cap equities can sustainably offer shorter lockups. A credit
strategy with material exposure to private credit, distressed
positions, or structured paper typically requires longer lockups and
a side-pocket framework [medium].

### Redemption Gates

Gates limit the total amount of redemptions in any single period,
typically 10 to 25 percent of fund NAV per quarter. A fund with $500
million in assets and a 20 percent quarterly gate can honor no more
than $100 million in redemption requests per quarter, regardless of
total demand. When aggregate requests exceed the gate, redemptions
are typically applied pro rata across requesting investors. Gates are
a contractually disclosed risk management mechanism -- their
activation signals genuine liquidity stress, but it is not the same
as a default or insolvency event [medium].

Gates can operate at two levels. Investor-level gates limit the
proportion of any single investor's holding that may be redeemed in
a single dealing day, spreading a large institutional investor's exit
over a defined period. Fund-level gates limit the aggregate
proportion of NAV that may be redeemed in a single dealing day,
preventing a single dealing day from forcing liquidation of a
disproportionate share of the portfolio. During Q4 2008, more than
100 hedge funds activated gate provisions as simultaneous redemption
requests exceeded their ability to liquidate positions at reasonable
prices [high].

### Side Pockets

Side pockets are segregated accounts within a hedge fund used to
isolate illiquid or hard-to-value positions from the main portfolio.
When an asset becomes illiquid -- due to a credit event, trading halt,
or market dislocation -- the manager transfers it to a side pocket.
Only investors who were in the fund at the time of the transfer
participate in the side pocket's gains or losses. New subscriptions
do not share in the side pocket, and existing investors cannot redeem
from it until the underlying position is sold or matures [medium].

The purpose of side pockets is fairness, not performance management.
They address the problem of how to treat an illiquid asset fairly
across investors entering, holding, and exiting the fund at different
points in time. Without side pockets, a fund might be forced to sell
illiquid positions at fire-sale prices to meet redemption requests,
or new investors might receive exposure to legacy illiquid positions
they did not underwrite. However, the governance of side pockets is
critical: the offering memorandum must describe the circumstances in
which a side pocket may be created, the valuation methodology, the
basis for realization, and the board's role in approval. Side pockets
created without proper governance are a source of investor disputes
and regulatory attention [medium].

### Leverage and Prime Brokerage

Leverage plays a central role in hedge fund management. Many hedge
funds rely on leverage to enhance returns on assets which on an
unlevered basis would not be sufficiently high to attract funding.
Leverage amplifies or dampens market risk and allows funds to obtain
notional exposure greater than their capital base. It is often
employed to target a level of return volatility desired by investors
[high].

Hedge funds obtain leverage primarily through prime brokerage
relationships. A prime broker provides bundled services including
financing, securities lending, clearing, and settlement. The
financing takes the form of margin loans and repurchase agreements,
collateralized by the fund's assets. Under standard U.S. prime
brokerage agreements, brokers may rehypothecate client assets worth
up to 140 percent of the client's net debit balance per SEC Rule
15c3-3. In the U.K., there is historically no statutory cap, making
London prime brokerage desks a key node in global collateral chains
[medium].

Rehypothecation means the prime broker takes the hedge fund's
collateral and pledges it to another bank to obtain its own
financing, creating a chain of collateral reuse. When the prime
broker defaults -- as Lehman Brothers did in 2008 -- the hedge fund
may be left with an unsecured claim against the bankrupt estate
rather than its assets. This creates a structural exposure that is
invisible in normal markets but catastrophic in a counterparty
failure [high].

### The Liquidity Mismatch

The liquidity mismatch is one of the oldest and most consequential
failure modes in the hedge fund industry. It occurs when the
redemption terms a fund offers to its investors do not match the time
required to exit the underlying portfolio at fair value. The mismatch
is usually invisible when markets are calm and net flows are positive,
and acutely visible the moment they are not [medium].

The spiral works as follows. During market stress, multiple investors
submit redemption requests simultaneously. The fund manager must sell
illiquid holdings at distressed prices to meet those requests. Forced
sales push NAV lower, which triggers more redemption requests from
remaining investors, which forces more distressed sales. This
self-reinforcing cycle converts theoretical paper losses into
permanent realized losses. The critical insight is that leverage and
liquidity risk compound each other. A fund can survive high leverage
if it can exit positions gradually. A fund can survive illiquidity if
it has no leverage creating margin calls. The specific failure mode
occurs when a highly leveraged fund holds illiquid positions --
leverage creates the scale of losses, and illiquidity prevents the
orderly exit that would allow positions to recover [medium].

## Evidence

### The 2008 Gate Wave and Discretionary Liquidity Restrictions

During Q4 2008, more than 100 hedge funds activated gate provisions
as simultaneous redemption requests exceeded their ability to
liquidate positions at reasonable prices. Funds using Lehman Brothers
as a prime broker faced an additional crisis: Lehman's bankruptcy
froze client assets held in the prime brokerage, creating involuntary
illiquidity even for funds that held liquid securities. Investors
expecting quarterly redemptions received gate notices limiting them
to 20 to 25 percent of their requested amounts [high].

Aiken, Clifford, and Ellis (2012) provided the first large-scale
empirical study of discretionary liquidity restrictions (DLRs) --
gates and side pockets imposed ex-post at the manager's discretion,
in addition to the ordinary lockup and notice period restrictions
present ex-ante in partnership agreements. Using a hand-collected
dataset of hedge fund holdings from institutional investors covering
2006 through 2011, they found that more than 30 percent of hedge fund
managers used their discretion to restrict investor liquidity during
the crisis. In 2006, before the crisis, only 4 percent of funds in
their sample had enacted a gate or side pocket. Funds enacted DLRs
following poor performance and when their portfolio assets were more
illiquid. Despite managers' claims that DLRs protected investor
interests by preventing fire-sales, funds that enacted DLRs continued
to underperform comparable funds afterward. Consistent with DLRs
reflecting agency problems, restricting investor liquidity during the
crisis had a negative impact on fund reputation that spilled over
across the hedge fund family. DLR funds and their family affiliates
had more difficulty raising capital and were more likely to cut fees
in the post-crisis era [high].

### Arbitrage Crashes and the Speed of Capital

Mitchell and Pulvino (2012), published in the Journal of Financial
Economics, measured the relative pricing errors that occurred during
the 2008 financial crisis when arbitrage hedge funds experienced a
sudden loss of debt capital. The imminent failure of prime brokers
caused a sudden decrease in the leverage afforded hedge funds,
resulting from the asymmetrical payoff to rehypothecation lenders --
the ultimate financiers of hedge funds through prime brokers.
Seemingly long-term debt capital became short-term capital, creating
a duration mismatch between arbitrage positions on the asset side and
short-term liabilities on the funding side [high].

According to Hedge Fund Research, hedge funds lost 19 percent in 2008.
The researchers focused on arbitrage strategies including convertible
debenture arbitrage, credit default swap (CDS)-corporate debenture
arbitrage, closed-end fund arbitrage, merger arbitrage, and Special
Purpose Acquisition Company (SPAC) arbitrage. When prime brokerage
financing was revoked, arbitrage funds were placed into immediate
financial distress and responded by selling assets at significant
discounts to fundamental values. Because their peers were also
distressed, the assets were sold to non-arbitrageurs at fire-sale
prices, causing substantially similar assets to trade at wildly
different prices. This demonstrated that the hedge fund sector's role
as the marginal provider of price efficiency depends on continuous
access to short-term financing -- a dependency that becomes a systemic
vulnerability when that financing is withdrawn [high].

### Hedge Fund Leverage: Empirical Evidence

Ang, Gorovyy, and van Inwegen (2011), published in the Journal of
Financial Economics, were the first to formally investigate hedge
fund leverage using actual leverage ratios from a unique dataset from
a fund-of-hedge-funds. They tracked hedge fund leverage in time series
from December 2004 to October 2009, a period including the worst of
the 2008 to early 2009 financial crisis. They characterized the
cross-section of leverage, examined the dispersion of leverage across
funds, and investigated the macro and fund-specific determinants of
future leverage changes [high].

Their findings established that hedge funds began to decrease leverage
even before the financial crisis started in mid-2007. The research
connected to broader theoretical work: in Fostel and Geanakoplos
(2008), economy-wide equilibrium leverage rises in times of low
volatility and falls in periods of high uncertainty. Stein (2009)
showed that leverage can be chosen optimally by individual hedge
funds, but this can create a fire-sale externality causing systemic
risk when hedge funds simultaneously unwind positions and reduce
leverage. The empirical evidence confirmed this dynamic: during the
crisis, hedge funds reduced equity holdings by 6 percent each quarter
during the Quant Meltdown of 2007 and by 15 percent on average (29
percent with compounding) during Lehman Brothers' bankruptcy in 2008.
Ben-David et al. (2012) reported that a quarter of all hedge funds
sold over 40 percent of their equity holdings during 2008 Q3-Q4. Fund
withdrawals explained half of the decline in hedge fund equity
holdings [high].

### The LTCM Precedent

Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM), though predating the 2008 crisis
by a decade, established the canonical case study of how leverage and
liquidity compound in a hedge fund structure. At the beginning of
1998, before losses began, LTCM had a balance sheet leverage of more
than 25-to-1, implying that a 4 percent adverse move in asset values
would wipe out all equity. The Federal Reserve's post-crisis report
noted that at year-end 1998, the five largest investment banks had an
average leverage ratio of 27-to-1. LTCM's convergence trades --
theoretically correct relative-value positions -- diverged instead of
converging because forced liquidation under illiquidity amplified
losses rather than allowing positions to recover [high].

### Fee Economics: What Investors Actually Keep

A 22-year NBER study of 5,917 hedge funds found that investors paid
$1.76 in fees for every $1 of net return received, with managers
capturing 64 percent of gross excess returns versus just 36 percent
for investors. The effective incentive fee rate was approximately 50
percent, not the headline 20 percent, because performance fees apply
to gross gains with no provision to recover subsequent losses. Around
60 percent of the gains on which fees were levied were eventually
wiped out by declines that investors carried entirely on their own.
LCH Investments data shows fees have consumed just over 50 percent of
hedge fund gross gains over the past two decades, up from around 30
percent in the earlier decades of the industry, meaning the fee
problem is worsening rather than self-correcting [medium].

### Performance Erosion: End of an Era

The academic literature documents a structural decline in aggregate
hedge fund performance after 2008. Ennis and Hill (2021), published in
the Financial Analysts Journal, studied whether prediction models
could select subsets of individual funds that buck the decline. Over
the full 1997 to 2016 sample, two predictors generated a significant
increase in the multi-asset-class portfolio's Sharpe ratio relative to
a stock/bond portfolio, achieved by substantial volatility reduction.
But when the analysis was split into sub-periods, the improvement in
Sharpe ratio was present only in the 1997 to 2007 period. From 2008
onwards, the hedge fund allocation generally reduced volatility but
lowered average return as well, leaving the Sharpe ratio unchanged
[high].

Dichev and Yu (2011), published in the Journal of Financial Economics,
found that annualized dollar-weighted returns were 3 to 7 percent
lower than corresponding buy-and-hold fund returns, and that the real
alpha of hedge fund investors was close to zero. In absolute terms,
dollar-weighted returns were reliably lower than the return on the
S&P 500 index and only marginally higher than the risk-free rate as
of the end of 2008. The combined impression was that the return
experience of hedge fund investors was much worse than previously
thought [high].

By 2025, the average hedge fund returned approximately 12.5 percent
(HFRI Fund Weighted Composite), the best year since 2009, yet still
trailed the S&P 500's approximately 17.9 percent. The percentage of
funds with positive and significant alpha dropped from about 20
percent in the earlier period to 10 percent, while the percentage
with significantly negative alpha increased from 5 percent to
approximately 20 percent [high].

## Implications

### For Investors: Structural Due Diligence

The hedge fund vehicle's structural features demand a due diligence
framework that goes beyond return analysis. An investor evaluating a
hedge fund must assess not only the strategy and track record but the
structural terms that govern the relationship: fee calculation
methodology, hurdle type (hard versus soft), high-water mark
provisions, lockup duration and type, gate thresholds (investor-level
versus fund-level), side-pocket governance, and prime brokerage
arrangements. Each of these is a structural choice that shapes what
the investor actually receives. A fund with a soft hurdle and no
high-water mark charges materially more in fees over a full cycle than
one with a hard hurdle and HWM, even if the headline "2-and-20"
description is identical [medium].

The post-2008 evolution of the industry offers lessons. Investors
who understood that many hedge fund strategies are positively
correlated to capital markets were less shocked by 2008 losses and
less likely to redeem at the bottom. Those who did due diligence on
custody arrangements were less exposed to the Lehman prime brokerage
freeze. Those who read offering documents carefully understood the
gate and side-pocket provisions before they were activated, rather
than discovering them during a crisis. The allocators who object to
liquidity tools are those who experience them as introduced
reactively, applied inconsistently, or used in ways not contemplated
in the offering documents [medium].

### For Managers: The Incentive Architecture

The fee structure creates incentive effects that managers must
understand about their own vehicle. The 2-and-20 model produces a
call-option-like payoff for the manager: the manager participates in
upside through the incentive fee but does not share downside beyond
the loss of the management fee. This asymmetry can encourage
risk-taking that benefits the manager more than the investor,
particularly when the fund is below its high-water mark and the
manager faces a "go for broke" incentive to recover the mark quickly.
The HWM mitigates this by preventing fee collection on recovered
losses, but it also creates a long dry period during which the
manager earns only the management fee -- a period that can last years
for deeply underwater funds [medium].

The pass-through expense model adopted by multi-strategy platforms
illustrates how the fee structure is evolving. By passing technology,
data, and operational costs directly to investors, these platforms
extract effective fees of 5 to 10 percent or more of AUM, far above
the headline management fee. Whether this represents fair
compensation for institutional-grade infrastructure or a structural
extraction depends on whether the costs genuinely enhance returns or
simply build organizational scale. The concentration of 86 percent of
industry assets in billion-dollar-plus firms suggests that
institutional investors have voted with their capital in favor of the
platform model, at least for now [medium].

### For Regulators: Systemic Risk Transmission

The hedge fund vehicle's use of leverage creates systemic risk
transmission channels that regulators must monitor. The 2008 crisis
demonstrated that the failure of a single prime broker can freeze
assets across hundreds of funds simultaneously, and that the
simultaneous deleveraging of leveraged funds can force fire sales
that destabilize asset prices across markets. The Bank of England's
Prudential Regulation Authority has launched probes into prime
brokerage exposure to hedge fund leverage in Asian equities,
specifically targeting synthetic equity derivatives including Total
Return Swaps and Contracts for Difference that allow multi-billion-
dollar leveraged positions without purchasing physical shares [medium].

The regulatory question is whether the structural tools -- gates,
side pockets, lockups -- adequately protect the financial system or
merely shift risk from the fund to its investors. The Aiken, Clifford,
and Ellis evidence that DLR funds underperformed and suffered
reputational damage suggests that the market imposes discipline on
managers who use liquidity restrictions opportunistically. But the
concentration of the industry among a small number of mega-funds and
a small number of prime brokers means that the failure of any single
large node can cascade through the system regardless of the
discipline applied to individual funds [high].

### For Value Investors: The Structural Lens

Value investors evaluating whether to allocate to hedge funds -- or
evaluating hedge fund managers as potential stewards of capital --
should apply the same structural lens they apply to any business. The
fee structure is the economics of the vehicle: what does the
investor keep after fees, and does the incentive arrangement align
the manager with long-term value creation or with short-term option-
like payoffs? The liquidity terms are the capital permanence of the
vehicle: can the manager compound without forced selling, and does
the investor's redemption right create a conflict with the
underlying portfolio's liquidity? The leverage is the balance-sheet
risk of the vehicle: what is the true equity, and what happens to it
in a stressed scenario [medium].

The Buffett and Munger school's skepticism of hedge fund fees is
grounded in arithmetic. On a 10 percent gross return in a 5 percent
rate environment, a 2-and-20 fund delivers 6.4 percent net, capturing
3.6 percent in fees -- 72 percent of the excess return over the
risk-free rate. The investor assumes equity-like illiquidity,
complexity, and manager risk to earn 140 basis points over Treasury
bills. The NBER finding that managers captured 64 percent of gross
excess returns over 22 years confirms that the fee structure
systematically transfers the majority of alpha from investor to
manager. This does not mean hedge funds cannot add value -- the
diversification benefit documented in the academic literature is real
-- but it means the structural terms determine who captures that value
[medium].
