# Structural Engineering -- Collapse Safety Depends on Continuous, Redundant, Ductile Load Paths, Not on Member Strength Alone

Structural engineering turns uncertain demands -- gravity, wind, earthquake, flood, fire, impact, and occupancy -- into a continuous chain of members and connections that carries every force to the ground with adequate strength, stiffness, stability, and ductility [3][9]. The major collapse investigations of the last six decades, from Ronan Point (1968) and the Hyatt Regency walkways (1981) to the I-35W bridge (2007) and Champlain Towers South (2021), traced the decisive weakness to an interrupted or under-designed link in that chain, usually a connection, and to the absence of an alternative path once that link failed [1][4][5][8]. Robustness, the insensitivity of a structure to local damage, has therefore become a design objective in its own right, now written into a dedicated United States national standard (ASCE/SEI 76-23), while load standards are being extended to future climate conditions [2][10][12].

## Background

Systematic study of how local damage spreads through a building began with Lord Baker's analysis of bomb-damaged buildings in London during the Second World War, as recounted by Adam and colleagues in their 2018 review of the field [2]. The event that turned the question into a regulatory concern came on 16 May 1968, when a gas explosion in an 18th-floor apartment of Ronan Point, a 22-story precast concrete panel apartment block in London, blew out an exterior load-bearing wall panel [1]. The loss of that single panel removed support for the floors above, which collapsed; their falling debris then triggered the collapse of the floors below, and the corner of the building came down to ground level [1]. The investigators attributed the collapse to a lack of structural integrity: the precast panels were slotted and bolted together in a way that left no alternate path for redistributing load, so that NIST's later summary describes the structure as "like a 'house of cards'" [1].

The regulatory response set the pattern that most codes still follow. The United Kingdom responded with the Fifth Amendment to its Building Regulations, and the resulting rules required that buildings be tied together, tolerate the notional removal of members, or have key elements designed for an accidental pressure of 34 kPa (5 psi) [1][2]. That pressure was an estimate of the explosion pressure on the flank wall at Ronan Point rather than a derived design value, and NIST's 2007 best-practice report on progressive collapse describes such specified abnormal loads as arbitrary [1][2]. In the United States the first progressive collapse requirement appeared in ANSI A58.1-1972, the first edition of the national load standard (the predecessor of ASCE 7) after Ronan Point, and New York City adopted rules similar to the UK rules in 1973; during the 1980s the requirement evolved into "general structural integrity" provisions emphasizing continuity, redundancy, and ductility [1][2].

A second lineage came from earthquake engineering. Modern seismic codes do not ask buildings to remain elastic in a design earthquake; they permit design for a fraction of the elastic force on the condition that the structure can deform inelastically without losing strength [9]. That bargain made ductility, continuity, and redundancy explicit design properties: FEMA's introduction to the NEHRP seismic provisions lists stable foundations, continuous load paths, adequate stiffness and strength, regularity, redundancy, ductility and toughness, and ruggedness as the features that let a structure survive strong shaking [3]. The 1994 Northridge earthquake then showed that welded steel moment frames, long regarded as among the most ductile systems in the building code, could suffer brittle fractures of their beam-to-column connections at low levels of plastic demand [9].

Several failures showed that connections are the critical links. In 1981 two suspended walkways in the Kansas City Hyatt Regency hotel fell, killing 113 people and injuring 186; the National Bureau of Standards found that the box beam-hanger rod connections had insufficient capacity and that a change of rod arrangement during construction had roughly doubled the load on the critical connections [5]. In 2007 the I-35W bridge in Minneapolis collapsed because gusset plates at its U10 nodes had been under-designed in the 1960s, and its deck truss had no alternative load path [4].

Interest in robustness intensified after the 1995 bombing of the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City and the 2001 collapses at the World Trade Center. United States federal agencies issued progressive collapse guidelines for government buildings (GSA 2003, DoD 2005), research output grew sharply after 2001, and from about 2009 China became the leading producer of research in the field [1][2]. NIST's investigation of World Trade Center Building 7, which collapsed after about seven hours of uncontrolled fire, concluded that the structure had not been designed to prevent fire-induced progressive collapse and recommended methods to prevent it [6]. A decade of committee work informed by NIST research then produced ASCE/SEI 76-23, described by NIST as the first national standard of its kind for mitigating disproportionate collapse [10].

Two current lines of work frame the field in 2026. In June 2026 NIST presented its technical findings on the 2021 partial collapse of Champlain Towers South in Surfside, Florida, which killed 98 people; the final report with recommendations was still pending at that time [7][8]. In parallel, according to a January 2025 NOAA report, the committee preparing ASCE 7-28, the next edition of the United States load standard, was developing a future conditions chapter so that climate-sensitive loads would no longer be defined only by historical records [12]. Code calibration, load and resistance factors, and conformity assessment are covered in the companion topic on standards and safety margins; this topic concentrates on how structures carry load, how they fail, and how designers make local failure stay local.

## Core Concepts

### The Load Path Is a Chain of Members and Connections

A load path is the sequence of elements through which a force travels from where it arises to the foundation. FEMA's guide states the requirement directly: all parts of a building, including nonstructural components, must be tied together to provide a continuous path that transfers inertial forces from their point of origin to the ground; otherwise individual pieces move independently, pull apart, and allow partial or total collapse [3]. Its examples are unambiguous. In the 1971 San Fernando earthquake a concrete tilt-up building collapsed because its exterior walls were not adequately connected to the wood roof they supported, and in the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake houses in Watsonville that were not connected to their foundations fell off them [3]. The same logic extends below grade: individual footings are tied together with grade beams or replaced by a continuous mat so that differential ground movement does not tear the structure apart [3].

Engineers organize load paths into recognizable structural systems. The NEHRP provisions distinguish bearing wall systems, in which walls carry both weight and lateral load; building frame systems, in which beams and columns carry weight while braces or shear walls resist lateral load; moment-resisting frames, which rely on rigid beam-column connections and leave open floor plans; dual systems that combine frames with walls or braces; cantilever column systems; and structures not specifically detailed for seismic resistance [3]. Each system concentrates demand at different places. In a moment frame, for example, the beam-column connection is where bending moment, shear, and the code's assumed ductility all meet [9].

The author's synthesis from the cases in this topic is that a load path behaves like a chain in series: its capacity is set by its weakest link, and the links most often found deficient are connections rather than members. The NTSB found that bridge owners and rating software commonly assumed gusset plates to be stronger than the members they joined, so the plates were never checked in later load ratings of the I-35W bridge [4]. The Hyatt Regency box beam-hanger rod connection was inadequate even as originally designed, and the fabricator's shop drawings then changed the hanger arrangement in a way that essentially doubled the load on the fourth-floor connections, although those drawings carried the review stamps of the contractor, structural engineer, and architect [5].

### Limit States Separate Strength, Stability, Serviceability, and Collapse

Design codes express performance as limit states: conditions beyond which a structure no longer meets a requirement. Ultimate limit states concern strength and stability; serviceability limit states concern deflection, vibration, and other conditions that impair use without causing collapse. Probability-based limit state design targets small failure probabilities for members under ordinary loads: NIST's report states that members currently designed for gravity loads, failing in a ductile fashion, have failure probabilities on the order of 1e-5 per year, and that system failure is roughly an order of magnitude less likely, depending on redundancy and continuity [1]. Adam and colleagues give the corresponding target reliability index for the ultimate limit state of new residential structures as about 4.7 for a one-year reference period, or 3.8 over 50 years [2].

Collapse after local damage is treated as a different, conditional limit state. Because the annual rates of most extreme triggering events are on the order of 1e-6 to 1e-5, keeping the overall collapse risk near a de minimis level of 1e-7 per year requires only that the conditional probability of collapse given local damage be limited to about 0.01 to 0.10, equivalent to a reliability index near 1.5 [1][2]. This explains why codes allow reduced load combinations for the damaged state: NIST suggested, for alternate path checks, a combination of 0.9 or 1.2 times dead load plus 0.5 times live load plus a small wind or snow component, and EN 1990 likewise recommends 0.5 times live load in accidental combinations [1][2].

Serviceability is not a minor category. The London Millennium Footbridge was closed two days after opening in June 2000 because it swayed sideways under crowds, while its vertical movements stayed within the limits of normal good practice, as the design had predicted [11]. Champlain Towers South illustrates the other end of the scale: NIST defined a structure's margin against failure as the extra capacity beyond the loads expected to be placed on it, and found that at the failed pool deck connections that margin had reached zero [8].

### Idealization Creates Its Own Hazards

Every structural calculation rests on an idealized model: members become lines, connections become pins or rigid joints, loads become uniform pressures, and dynamic events become static forces with factors. The models are indispensable, and the investigations show that their simplifications can hide the governing failure mode. Design models for pre-1994 welded steel moment connections assumed that the beam flanges carried nearly all bending stress and the web all the shear; research after Northridge, summarized in FEMA-350, found that the flanges in fact carried a significant share of the shear, which intensified stresses at the root of the flange welds where defects were common [9]. Load rating programs used for United States bridges, including the widely used BARS and Virtis, did not include connection strength at the time of the I-35W collapse [4].

Analytical simplifications for collapse analysis carry similar risks. NIST's report notes that linear elastic static procedures for column-removal scenarios, though often conservative, can "mask hazardous dynamic effects" and cannot represent load redistribution, P-Delta instability, or catenary and membrane action; it calls the approach "a fairly subjective method" that may be no better than prescriptive detailing [1]. Adam and colleagues add that alternate load path analysis involves so many modelling choices -- dynamic treatment, material nonlinearity, strain-rate effects, connection deformation capacity -- that different combinations of assumptions can lead to design solutions with different levels of robustness, and that the shell-element models commonly used for slabs do not adequately capture the transition from flexural to membrane behavior that slabs use to bridge a lost column [2].

### Ductility and Redundancy Let a Structure Fail Gracefully

FEMA-350 defines ductile behavior as the capacity to withstand large inelastic deformations without significant degradation in strength and without instability and collapse [9]; FEMA's seismic guide describes ductility and toughness together as the properties that let an element sustain damage when overloaded while continuing to carry load [3]. Steel yields and stretches a great deal while still carrying load; reinforcing bars yield in tension and protect the surrounding concrete; and connections made stronger than the members they join keep yielding in the members, so that, in FEMA's words, "some members can yield to protect the rest of the structure" [3]. Brittle modes behave differently: concrete and masonry crush in compression and crack in tension or shear, and steel buckles in compression, so an element overloaded in these modes may fail abruptly and lose its load-carrying capacity [3]. NIST's guidance accordingly asks designers to avoid slender compression elements and brittle details and to give members more shear than flexural strength [1]. Seismic codes exploit ductility explicitly: systems judged more ductile are designed for lower forces, and the codes classify detailing as ordinary, intermediate, or special according to how much ductility it secures [3][9].

Redundancy is the number of independent paths available. FEMA's guide describes it as "not putting all of your earthquake-resistant eggs in one basket": if strength is concentrated in a few elements, damage to them leaves no residual capacity [3]. FEMA-350 records how economic pressure eroded redundancy in steel moment frames. Early frames moment-connected nearly every beam to every column, but as labor costs rose the industry moment-connected only a few larger members, which raised the strain demand on each remaining connection and made it more susceptible to brittle behavior [9]. In bridges the NTSB uses the term non-load-path-redundant for structures in which the fracture of a single element can cause collapse; in 2007 about 19,273 of the roughly 600,000 bridges in the National Bridge Inventory were so classified [4]. Redundancy is necessary but not sufficient for robustness: Adam and colleagues note that measures based only on connectivity capture redundancy but not the strength and ductility needed to use it [2].

### Dynamic Response Changes the Demand

When a column is suddenly removed, the structure above does not settle gently onto a new equilibrium. NIST describes the response as impulsive, then oscillatory, then quasi-static, with a failure front that can propagate faster vertically than horizontally because columns are much stiffer axially than beams are in bending [1]. Static design procedures therefore multiply gravity loads by a dynamic load factor, typically 2 in the linear static methods of the GSA and DoD guidance, while nonlinear dynamic analysis models the motion directly [1][2]. Large displacements also change the resisting mechanism: as a beam or slab sags over a lost support it can carry load through Vierendeel frame action, arching, and finally catenary or membrane tension, which Adam and colleagues describe as a building's last line of defense; NIST notes that catenary forces can be significantly larger than the vertical loads they carry and that their horizontal components must be resisted by the surrounding structure [1][2].

Dynamic response also governs ordinary use. The Millennium Bridge revealed synchronous lateral excitation: when a crowd walks on a deck swaying sideways at less than about 1.3 Hz, pedestrians fall into step with the motion, and their average lateral force becomes proportional to the deck's velocity [11]. The crowd then acts as negative damping: below a critical number of people there is no evidence of the effect, and once the number is exceeded the motion quickly increases [11]. Because the effect is nonlinear, the designers concluded that "satisfactory past performance of a bridge under less than critical loading is not a guide to its future performance" [11].

### Robustness and Disproportionate Collapse

Progressive collapse is the spread of local damage from element to element until an entire structure, or a disproportionately large part of it, collapses [1][6]. Adam and colleagues separate two ideas that are often merged: "progressive" describes the propagation mechanism, while "disproportionate" describes the ratio between the size of the initiating event and the final collapse [2]. A progressive collapse can be proportionate if it is arrested quickly, and a statically determinate structure can collapse disproportionately after losing a single member without any chain of propagation [2]. Robustness is the property that prevents disproportion; Eurocode EN 1991-1-7 defines it as the ability to withstand events such as fire, explosion, impact, or human error "without being damaged to an extent disproportionate to the original cause" [2].

NIST's report frames robustness probabilistically. The annual probability of collapse equals the product of the hazard rate, the probability of local damage given the hazard, and the probability of collapse given local damage, and each factor maps to a strategy: event control reduces the hazard, specific local resistance reduces the chance of local damage, and alternate load paths reduce the chance that local damage becomes collapse [1]. Codes implement these through four families of methods: prescriptive tie forces that tie floors horizontally and columns vertically; alternate path analysis in which a column or wall is notionally removed; key element design, in which critical members are made strong enough to resist the hazard; and risk-based assessment for high-consequence structures [1][2]. Segmentation, which deliberately isolates parts of a structure so that collapse cannot cross the boundary, is a fifth strategy discussed by researchers but rarely codified [2]. Acceptance criteria usually limit collapse to the bay of initial damage and the levels immediately above and below, or to a floor area such as 100 m2 or 15 percent of the floor in EN 1991-1-7 [1][2].

ASCE/SEI 76-23 adopts a performance-based approach. NIST reports that it sets required performance and the extent of initial damage to be tolerated according to a building's size, occupancy, and use, and leaves the design solution to the engineer; in the committee chair's words, it "points towards solutions but doesn't prescribe them" [10]. Adam and colleagues add a principle worth keeping: a robust structure is not over-designed, but is able to activate latent resisting mechanisms that are not used under normal loads [2].

## Evidence

### Ronan Point, London (1968): Missing Ties in Precast Construction

NIST's 2007 report reviews the Ronan Point investigation [1]. The building was a 64 m, 22-story tower of 110 apartments whose precast concrete walls and floors were connected by bolts and dry-packed mortar in slots [1]. The British investigating team judged the explosion to be small, with a pressure below 10 psi (about 70 kPa), while tests showed that the as-built exterior wall would fail at about 20.7 kPa (3 psi); losing one 18th-floor bearing wall brought down floors 19 to 22, and the impact of their debris then failed the floors below, so that the southeast corner collapsed to ground level [1]. The investigation attributed the collapse to the lack of structural integrity and of any alternate load path, found that strong winds or fire could also have caused progressive collapse, and judged that poor workmanship -- only half the specified mortar in the connections and bolts not tightened as specified -- had little effect on the event [1]. The method was a forensic reconstruction of an initiating event and its propagation; the finding was that a small explosion became a disproportionate collapse because no alternate load path could redistribute the load [1].

### Hyatt Regency Walkways, Kansas City (1981): A Connection Detail and a Construction Change

The National Bureau of Standards combined debris inspection, weighing of walkway spans, analysis of a television videotape to estimate occupancy, full-scale laboratory tests of replica connections, and structural analysis [5]. It found that the walkways failed under loads well below the design loads of the Kansas City Building Code: the maximum load on a fourth-floor box beam-hanger rod connection was estimated at 21.4 kips, 53 percent of the 40.7 kips required for design [5]. The connections as built had a mean ultimate capacity of about 18.6 kips, whereas a connection designed to the AISC specification would have been expected to reach at least 68 kips [5]. The original detail, a continuous rod through both walkways, was itself inadequate under the code, but the fabricator's shop drawings replaced it with two sets of rods, so that the fourth-floor connections carried both walkways; the change, which essentially doubled the connection load, was reviewed by the contractor, structural engineer, and architect [5]. The Bureau concluded that because the connections had so little reserve capacity and failure of one would redistribute load to the others, "progressive failure of the walkway system was assured" once any connection failed; workmanship and materials were not significant factors [5].

### Murrah Federal Building, Oklahoma City (1995): Transfer Girders Without Redistribution

The 1995 truck bomb, about 1.8 Mg TNT equivalent, detonated about 4.9 m from a column of the nine-story reinforced concrete frame [1]. It destroyed three columns that supported a third-floor transfer girder, and roughly half of the building's usable space collapsed [1]. NIST's summary of the FEMA building performance assessment reports that the building had been designed and detailed adequately under the codes of its time as an ordinary moment frame, but that it could not redistribute the lost support; the assessment estimated that special moment frame detailing would have reduced the collapsed area by at least 50 percent and at most 80 percent [1]. The author's reading of the case is that compliance with codes written for ordinary loads did not provide robustness; NIST's guidance separately treats transfer girders as contrary to redundancy and asks designers to avoid them [1].

### Northridge Earthquake (1994): Brittle Connections in a "Ductile" System

The SAC Joint Venture program behind FEMA-350 combined post-earthquake damage surveys, statistical evaluation, analytical studies, finite element and fracture mechanics investigations, and more than 120 full-scale connection tests [9]. Steel moment-frame buildings from 1 to 26 stories, including some under construction, were found to have brittle fractures of beam-to-column connections, often originating at the complete joint penetration weld of the beam bottom flange and sometimes in buildings that had remained essentially elastic [9]. The buildings did not collapse and so met the basic intent of the code, but they did not behave as assumed; damage was hidden behind fireproofing and finishes, repairs were costly, and at least one building was demolished [9]. The program traced the vulnerability to combined causes: stress concentration at the connection, downhand field welds interrupted at the beam web, backing bars that hid weld-root defects from inspection, low-toughness weld metal, weak panel zones, larger and fewer moment connections, and beam steel stronger than specified, which left the welds under-matched [9]. Its outcome was a set of prequalified connections and the instruction that users exercise independent engineering judgment before applying them [9].

### World Trade Center Building 7 (2001): Fire-Induced Progressive Collapse

NIST reconstructed the collapse from photographs, video, documents, interviews, and validated fire and structural simulations [6]. Fires ignited by debris from the north tower burned uncontrolled for almost seven hours because the sprinklers on the lower floors had no water supply [6]. Thermal expansion of long-span floor beams, at temperatures at or below about 400 C, pushed a girder off its seat at Column 79; floor failures cascaded down to the fifth floor; the column, unbraced over nine stories, buckled; and failure spread vertically to the roof and then horizontally across the core until the building above moved down as a unit [6]. NIST called this "a fire-induced progressive collapse," the first known total collapse of a tall building primarily due to fire [6]. The design had been generally consistent with the 1968 New York City Building Code, yet the connections had been designed for gravity loads, not thermally induced lateral loads, and the system had not been designed to prevent fire-induced progressive collapse [6].

### I-35W Bridge, Minneapolis (2007): Design Error, Added Load, and Rating Practice

The NTSB combined a reconstruction of the collapse sequence, finite element analysis of the gusset plates, an FHWA demand-to-capacity evaluation, and a review of design, modification, and load rating records [4]. The collapse began with the failure of gusset plates at the U10 nodes of the deck truss; because the truss was non-load-path-redundant, total collapse was likely once those plates failed, and about 1,000 ft of the deck truss fell, killing 13 people [4]. The plates were half an inch thick where about one inch was needed, and analysis showed parts of them yielding under the original dead load alone; the designer had sized the plates from chord splice forces without the shear calculations the specification required, and neither the firm's checking process nor state and federal reviewers detected the omission [4]. Over four decades, a 2-inch concrete overlay in 1977 and new median barriers and railings in 1998 increased the dead load by 13.4 and 6.1 percent, and on the day of the collapse construction equipment and aggregates were staged toward the south end of the center section of the deck truss; in the most highly loaded member at the U10 node, a compression diagonal, the load at the time of collapse was about 5 percent above its design load, yet the finite element models showed the stresses in all truss members remaining within acceptable ranges while the gusset plates around that diagonal yielded and finally allowed it to shift sideways [4]. Load ratings in 1979 and 1998 did not evaluate gusset plates, and the Board concluded that inspections would not have been expected to detect the design error [4].

### Champlain Towers South, Surfside (2021): Low Margins, Degradation, and an Unarrested Collapse

NIST's investigation spanned nearly five years and combined interviews, testing of recovered material, eight full-scale replicas of pool deck slab-column connections, two full-scale replicas of the tower's critical slab-beam-column joints, and computational collapse modeling [8]. It found that punching shear failures at two pool deck slab-column connections, about three weeks and one week before the collapse, initiated the failure [8]. Margins had been low from the start because of severe and widespread deviations of the original design from the codes of the day, with some locations providing less than half the code-required strength, and because of construction deviations: top reinforcement sat at about 2 inches of cover rather than the 3/4 inch shown on the drawings, and typically less than the required share of column-strip reinforcement passed over the columns [8]. Heavier planters than designed, pavers and sand added in a 1996-1997 rehabilitation, and corrosion that had been evident more than 25 years earlier eroded the remaining margin [8]. When the pool deck fell, it damaged two joints at the base of the tower that lacked column ties and had weaker floor concrete than the columns; the joints crushed, and collapse spread north through the middle part of the tower by successive punching shear failures until it was arrested at the west part by a concrete wall and by slabs that failed before the next columns could punch through [8]. The east part then fell when the westward pull and release of the collapsing middle part set it oscillating and its 12th floor drifted about 21 inches, beyond the capacity of the lower-story columns [8]. NIST stated that "insufficient strength and reinforcement detailing at the connection between the pool deck and the tower" caused the deadly partial collapse of the tower, and noted that details may change before the final report [8].

### Full-Scale Tests Show What Latent Capacity Exists

Adam and colleagues review the experimental record, which ranges from beam-column sub-assemblages to whole buildings [2]. A one-third scale, four-bay, three-story concrete frame tested quasi-statically showed resistance shifting from beam action to arch action and then catenary action as the central column was removed [2]. Tests on buildings scheduled for demolition show that frame action above a lost column and nonstructural walls can contribute substantially to alternative load paths: two steel-framed buildings tolerated the sudden removal of four perimeter columns with insignificant damage, tested with their external walls and partitions still in place, and the six-story Hotel San Diego and the eleven-story Crowne Plaza Hotel in Houston redistributed the loss of multiple columns through Vierendeel action of the frames above, infill walls, and deep beams [2]. The same review notes that masonry and timber structures have been practically ignored in testing, that no frame or whole-building tests existed for precast concrete, that research on prestressed structures was insufficient, and that flat slab punching failures, which propagate both vertically and horizontally, had received very few studies [2].

## Implications

### For Structural Designers

The evidence shifts attention from members to systems and their links. A designer who checks every member against its factored load has not yet shown that the structure is robust; the remaining questions are what happens if any one element is lost, and whether the connections can carry the forces of the redistributed path [1][2]. NIST's best-practice report recommends continuous top and bottom slab reinforcement anchored into columns, ties in three directions, moment-resisting rather than simple connections where they add redistribution capacity, shear capacity that exceeds flexural capacity, avoidance of transfer girders and other discontinuities, and particular care at exterior bays, which cannot redistribute load in two directions, and at corners, where catenary action is ineffective [1]. Connections should be stronger than the members they join, so that yielding occurs in the members and the structure does not pull apart at the joints [3]. Dynamic serviceability needs explicit checks for slender or lively structures; the Millennium Bridge required more than 20 percent of critical damping in its lowest lateral mode, supplied by 37 viscous dampers and tuned mass dampers, because stiffening it out of the critical frequency range would have required more than a tenfold increase in lateral stiffness [11].

Constructability belongs to the same problem. FEMA-350 traced the pre-1994 connection fractures partly to how the joints were built: bottom flange welds made downhand by a welder sitting on the beam, interrupted at the web, with backing bars left in place that hid root defects from visual and ultrasonic inspection [9]. At Champlain Towers South the top slab reinforcement was placed with roughly 2 inches of cover instead of the 3/4 inch drawn, a deviation that NIST said, "while seemingly minor, significantly diminishes the strength of the slabs and the slab-column connections" [8]. The author's interpretation is that a detail which cannot be reliably built, placed, and inspected as drawn is a structural weakness, and that designers carry responsibility for choosing details that the construction process can actually deliver.

### For Design Checkers, Peer Reviewers, and Building Officials

In three failures in this topic, serious errors survived whatever checking existed. At I-35W the design firm's checking procedures had no explicit step to confirm that all necessary calculations had been made, and the state and federal agencies relied on a professional engineer's stamp; in a survey of 14 state transportation departments, 10 reported approving bridge designs later found deficient [4]. At the Hyatt Regency the change in hanger rod arrangement passed through shop-drawing review by the contractor, engineer, and architect [5]. At Champlain Towers South a design with severe and widespread deviations from the codes of the day was built with further deviations from its drawings, and NIST reported that records of whether the reinforcement changes were approved by the design engineer or observed by inspectors are not available [8]. The author's interpretation is that review should verify the load path itself -- each transfer from slab to beam to connection to column to foundation -- and should treat changes proposed during fabrication or construction as redesigns of that path [4][5]. Mn/DOT's post-collapse requirement for independent review of all major consultant-designed bridges is one institutional response [4].

### For Owners, Asset Managers, and Inspectors

Structures change after they are built. Added dead load from overlays, barriers, landscaping, and pavers consumed margin at both I-35W and Champlain Towers South, and in both cases the loads accumulated over decades [4][8]. Load ratings and condition assessments therefore need to include connections and to be redone when permanent load is added; the NTSB recommended that the FHWA require owners of non-load-path-redundant steel truss bridges to calculate stresses in all elements, including gusset plates, whenever planned modifications or operational changes may significantly increase them [4]. Corrosion and waterproofing failures are structural issues, not cosmetic ones: at Champlain Towers South corrosion was the most likely mechanism that reduced an already thin margin to zero [8]. After an earthquake, damage to connections may be hidden behind finishes and fireproofing, so the absence of visible distress is weak evidence of integrity [9]. These points connect this topic to structural health monitoring, corrosion control, and lifecycle asset management, which treat the inspection-feedback loop in detail.

### For Standards Writers and Climate Adaptation

Standards are minimums written for typical cases, and several investigations show what lies outside them. Murrah met the codes of its time; WTC 7 was generally consistent with its building code; the Northridge buildings met the basic intent of the seismic provisions; and the lateral crowd force that moved the Millennium Bridge had been described in earlier literature but never quantified, so there was no quantitative way to design against it [1][6][9][11]. Robustness standards close part of this gap. ASCE/SEI 76-23 was voluntary at release, and NIST anticipated that all or part of it may later be incorporated into the model building code [10]. Future climate loads raise a parallel problem. The NOAA workshop report states that the basis of the ASCE 7-28 future conditions chapter is a 3 C rise in global mean temperature above pre-industrial levels by 2080-2100, that the time from initial research to adoption by local jurisdictions can easily take ten years, and that, with the exception of seismic hazards, long-term federal funding for the underlying research is rare, the NEHRP earthquake program being the model of coordinated research and standards cycles [12]. The report also identifies an inherent bias in standards that rely on data from periods whose greenhouse gas concentrations differ from those expected during a structure's design life [12]; the resilience and adaptation topic treats this non-stationarity as a design input.

### The Boundary Between Code Compliance and Engineering Judgment

The author's synthesis is that codes encode lessons from past failures and cannot anticipate every new configuration, hazard, or construction practice. FEMA-350 told users to "exercise their own independent engineering judgment" even when applying its research-based criteria [9], ASCE/SEI 76-23 leaves the means of achieving robustness to the engineer [10], and NIST's 2007 report states that prescriptive (indirect) requirements may be used for the vast majority of structures while direct analytical methods may be used for special structures, iconic structures, or critical facilities [1]. Judgment in this sense is not permission to deviate from codes; it is the discipline of asking, for each structure, what the code did not ask: where the load path is discontinuous, which connection is non-redundant, what happens dynamically when a member is lost, and how the structure will degrade over its life [1][2][8].

## Limits of Current Knowledge

The gain in robustness provided by prescriptive tie-force rules could not be quantified as of the 2018 review, although tying is generally accepted as beneficial, and research has shown that the connection rotations needed to develop full catenary action are unachievable in some forms of construction, which led the UFC guidance to add rotation limits and, in 2009, to rely on floor systems rather than beams for internal ties [2]. Probabilistic approaches to robustness were relatively recent and had not been implemented in codes as of the 2018 review, and that review found experimental data thin for precast, prestressed, masonry, and timber structures and for flat slab punching propagation [2]. NIST's Champlain Towers South findings were presented as technical findings in 2026 with the final report and recommendations still to come, and NIST cautioned that details may change [7][8]. ASCE 7-28 was still in development at the time of the NOAA workshop, so the final form of its future conditions provisions is not established by the sources used here [12].
