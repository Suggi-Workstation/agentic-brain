# Construction Methods and Project Management -- Why the Delivery Method Determines Whether Infrastructure Delivers on Its Design Promise

Construction project management coordinates design, procurement, construction, and controls so that a physical asset can meet its specified purpose. Delivery method changes contractual responsibility, the timing of builder involvement, and opportunities to overlap work; it does not by itself guarantee a cheaper or better project ([1] [2] [9]). A credible owner combines an appropriate delivery arrangement with explicit scope, a risk-adjusted estimate, an executable schedule, and verification of work actually completed ([3] [6] [7]).

## Background

Design-bid-build (DBB) separates the owner's designer contract from its construction contract and normally finishes the design before bidding construction. This sequencing lets an owner compare bids against common documents, but it also postpones the builder's formal participation and much of the construction work. The owner remains the link between contracts when a design issue requires a field change. These are features of the contractual structure, not proof that DBB inevitably causes disputes or costs more than its alternatives. Public agencies may face rules governing which delivery methods and award procedures they can use; NIGP instructs agencies to establish legal availability before evaluating project characteristics ([1] [2] [3]).

Design-build (DB) places design and construction under one owner's contract and can overlap final design with construction. Construction management at risk (CMAR, sometimes CM/GC) retains a separate designer while bringing a construction manager into preconstruction and later obtaining construction services through that entity, often with a negotiated guaranteed maximum price (GMP). Agency construction management is different: a manager acting as the owner's adviser does not itself become the at-risk constructor. Integrated project delivery (IPD) uses early participation and, in its multiparty contractual form, shared decision making and agreed risk and reward. The architectural and construction associations' primer explicitly declines to designate any one method as universally best ([1] [2] [11]).

The historical motivation for alternative delivery was partly the wish to use construction expertise before the design became difficult to change, and partly the possibility of shortening the sequence between design and build. FHWA describes both mechanisms for highway projects and notes that public owners need procurement and oversight skills to execute DB. A shorter planned sequence is not equivalent to a shorter realized project: unresolved utilities, permits, third-party interfaces, or deficient owner requirements can delay any method. An owner also has to distinguish a proposed construction price from its entire capital budget, including design, land, utilities, management, contingency, and operating interfaces as applicable ([2] [4] [9]).

Scheduling and estimating developed as separate but interdependent disciplines. A network schedule links activities and computes which path currently governs completion; a work breakdown structure decomposes scope for cost analysis. The US Government Accountability Office (GAO) treats a credible schedule as necessary to a credible cost estimate because a change in project duration can change resource and financing needs. Its schedule guide calls for validating logic, float, and the critical path, conducting schedule risk analysis, and updating actual progress. Its cost guide calls for clear scope, documented assumptions, data, independent comparison, risk analysis, and updates with actual costs ([6] [7]).

Construction estimates are not promises of identical precision at every design stage. An AACE recommended practice for *process-industry* projects classifies estimates using the maturity of defining deliverables; it explicitly excludes commercial buildings and transportation infrastructure from that sector-specific matrix. Its Class 5 concept-screening estimate has a much wider possible outcome range than a mature Class 1 bid estimate, even after appropriate contingency. The guide cautions that project-specific risks, not the class label alone, determine the appropriate accuracy range. Translating those process-industry percentages into a universal allowance for every building, road, or bridge would be a category error ([5]).

The need for independent forecasting became clear in Flyvbjerg's transport-infrastructure research. His 2005 World Bank working paper reports that roughly nine out of ten projects in its transport sample had construction cost overruns relative to estimates at the decision to build. Its table separates rail, bridges and tunnels, and roads: comparing a new bridge to the aggregate average can conceal different project-type distributions. The paper's evidence supports a warning about early forecasts, not the proposition that nine out of ten *all* construction jobs will overrun, or that every overrun has one cause ([13]).

Later comparisons added useful but qualified evidence about contractual arrangements. A US building-project research program compared 351 projects using DBB, CMAR, and DB; a 2018 follow-up examined 212 more contemporary projects, and an FHWA highway study examined a narrower design-build program. Their project populations and comparison methods are not interchangeable. The building results favored DB on several cost and schedule measures, whereas the highway study found shorter duration in a comparable subset but mixed cost outcomes. An evidence-based delivery choice thus begins with project type, risk, legal setting, owner capability, and the quality of available data rather than the blanket claim that a single contract form determines success ([9] [10]).

## Core Concepts

### Project Delivery Methods

A delivery method allocates responsibility for design and construction and determines the interfaces among the owner, designer, constructor, and trade contractors. A procurement or award method determines how a team is selected; a payment basis determines how it is compensated. They interact but are not synonyms. A DB job may use a low-bid or best-value selection; a GMP is a contractual price mechanism rather than a guarantee that the owner's entire project budget cannot change. Scope changes, exclusions, differing site conditions, and owner-retained obligations require explicit treatment in each contract. The owner's first task is to describe the required performance and identify who can actually control each major risk ([1] [2] [3] [9]).

In DBB the designer develops bid documents under the owner's contract, and competing constructors price the construction work. This can support comparable bids and detailed owner design control. It also means that the constructor usually cannot test buildability during the design stage unless the owner obtains separate advice. A change found in construction must be resolved across contractual boundaries. Calling DBB inherently adversarial mistakes a potential coordination problem for a universal outcome; a competent owner can invest in design reviews, construction-management advice, and clear change procedures while retaining DBB. Equally, an early low bid should not be mistaken for a complete, risk-adjusted final project cost ([1] [2] [3]).

In DB the owner contracts with a design-builder for both disciplines. Early constructor participation can help phase work, obtain long-lead items, and revise a design before a conflict reaches the field. The owner's required performance must be defined before much design detail exists; that moves important design and risk choices into procurement. The owner still manages third-party permissions and retained responsibilities and must check what the contractor's offer actually includes. FHWA's 2005 highway evaluation reported shorter durations for a subset of comparable DB projects, but its cost findings were mixed, so DB should not be sold as an automatic cost reduction. FHWA's 2026 alternative contracting guidance also describes progressive DB, in which the final price is negotiated after further design development ([4] [9]).

CMAR brings a constructor into preconstruction while the owner separately contracts for design. During design, the constructor can advise on sequence, materials, cost, and buildability. Later, a negotiated construction price, often a GMP, creates a different commercial relationship from competitive DBB bidding. The owner must examine allowances, assumptions, trade-bid coverage, contingency ownership, and what happens if the parties cannot agree on a price. DelDOT's selection matrix identifies both the prospect of managing cost risk using CM/GC input and the risk of reduced price competition when the owner negotiates with one firm. CMAR therefore does not eliminate the need for independent estimating or strong owner project management ([2] [3] [14]).

Multiparty IPD agreements bring the owner, designer, and constructor into a common arrangement intended to align incentives around a shared target and collaborative problem solving. Contracts differ, so it is inaccurate to assume that every participant shares every cost overrun in a fixed proportion. Shared risk may be limited to agreed profit pools, while other costs or liabilities remain allocated by contract. Lean planning methods, such as pull planning and the Last Planner System, can support coordination but are operating practices rather than a mandatory legal definition of IPD. The ten-project IPDA/LCI investigation is instructive about how teams worked together; because it deliberately examined successful integrated projects, it cannot establish that IPD causes better results than a comparable non-IPD portfolio ([2] [11]).

### Scheduling: The Critical Path Method

A critical path method (CPM) schedule maps activities, durations, and logical relationships into an estimate of project completion. A forward pass calculates early dates, and a backward pass calculates late dates; total float is the amount an activity can slip without moving the modeled completion date under the specified logic and constraints. Critical and near-critical work merits scrutiny, but a simplistic rule that every critical activity always has exactly zero float fails when calendars, constraints, or schedule conventions differ. GAO distinguishes the longest path from a low-float path and warns that imposed dates, missing logic, and level-of-effort activities can produce misleading results ([6]).

The engineering value of a network lies in its interfaces. For a bridge, design approvals precede fabrication, utility relocation may have to precede excavation, and inspection may be required before covering structural work. A plausible bar chart can conceal an omitted dependency between these activities. The schedule must capture the entire authorized scope at useful detail, align with contracts and the cost baseline, and update progress and logic as conditions change. If one path has little remaining float, an unrelated activity's acceleration need not change completion. When the critical path changes, the response should change with it ([6]).

Fast-tracking overlaps work formerly done in sequence, potentially reducing elapsed time while increasing rework exposure if upstream decisions change. Crashing spends additional resources to shorten an activity whose duration constrains completion, but added crews are not automatically productive where workspace or approvals bind. GAO recommends a schedule risk analysis using duration uncertainty and risk drivers; the result is a distribution of possible completion dates rather than an unsupported single date. A schedule needs updating and documented progress for either a forecast or a delay analysis to be credible. Schedule and cost analysts should examine their common assumptions, not produce independent numbers that cannot both be achieved ([6] [7]).

### Cost Estimation Methods

An estimate first defines what is being estimated: scope, quantities, performance, date, location, base-year prices, exclusions, and owner versus contractor costs. Analogy uses outcomes from comparable projects, adjusted for differences; parametric estimating models cost as a function of measurable drivers; engineering build-up estimates quantities and unit prices for defined work. GAO describes all three, along with sensitivity analysis, data normalization, and independent estimates. No method is categorically the most accurate across every design maturity and data set: a meticulous quantity takeoff still misses a missing scope item, and an analogy loses credibility when its comparison cases are not comparable ([7]).

Estimate class is not a confidence guarantee. AACE's process-industry matrix classifies a Class 5 estimate as concept screening at roughly 0-2 percent definition of specified deliverables. Its typical low/high bounds vary from -20/+30 percent to -50/+100 percent, depending on context and appropriate contingency, not a symmetric plus-or-minus 30-50 percent promise. A Class 1 estimate is intended for detailed checking or bidding at a far more mature stage; even that class has a range rather than a universal 3-5 percent error. The AACE document does not authorize fixed contingency percentages for all project types. For buildings or highways use their relevant recommended practice and project-specific evidence rather than copy the process-plant matrix ([5]).

A bottom-up cost model and an outside-view forecast answer different questions. The model measures the known design and identified work. The outside view asks how similar *complete projects* actually differed from their decision-stage forecasts. Flyvbjerg's reference class approach requires a comparable class, an empirical distribution of actual outcomes, and a transparent choice of risk tolerance for the new project. These views can be reconciled: investigate why the class uplift and the bottom-up allowance differ rather than automatically add both and count the same uncertainty twice. Different scope baselines, inflation conventions, and budget dates must be made comparable before a historical overrun distribution is transferred ([7] [13]).

Contingency funds uncertainty in the defined project; it is not a substitute for excluding known scope. A risk analysis should model the likely cost and schedule effects of threats, the correlations between them, and the funding confidence sought by the owner. The required reserve varies with uncertainty, project phase, and who bears the risk. An owner should distinguish the project contingency from a constructor's allowances or contractual management reserve, and changes in scope from the realization of risks within approved scope. GAO explicitly criticizes estimates that ignore risk and produce unsupported confidence levels. Re-estimation with new information is a control action, not an admission that early estimates were inherently useless ([7]).

### Earned Value Management

Earned value management (EVM) compares the budget for scheduled work (PV), the budget for *completed* work (EV), and actual cost (AC). Cost variance is EV - AC; schedule variance in budget units is EV - PV. CPI = EV / AC and SPI = EV / PV when denominators are nonzero. A ratio below one signals adverse performance relative to the approved baseline, but monetary schedule variance is not a count of delayed days. The US Department of Energy's EVMS card includes EAC = BAC / cumulative CPI as one general estimate-at-completion formula and alternatives that treat remaining work differently. Whether past cost efficiency predicts remaining work is a project judgment, not a mathematical certainty ([8] [7]).

Suppose a $10 million work baseline has PV of $4 million, EV of $3 million, and AC of $5 million at a reporting date. SPI is 0.75 and CPI is 0.60; the simple constant-CPI formula yields EAC of approximately $16.67 million if the remainder performs like past work. These numbers are an illustrative calculation, not study findings. They do not show when the job will finish; only an updated logic-based schedule and schedule-risk analysis can evaluate the time consequences. Reconciliations must also account for approved scope changes, measurement of physical completion, and any costs outside the performance baseline ([6] [7] [8]).

### Risk Management and Megaproject Forecasts

A useful construction risk register records the uncertain event, cause, affected objective, owner, response, status, and assumptions behind probability and impact. A rough qualitative screen may identify urgent exposures before numeric data are available; quantitative analysis can then represent ranges, dependencies, and schedule effects. Requiring an exact percentage probability for every uncertain event would invent precision where evidence is poor. Assign control to the party able to manage an exposure, retain a decision path for unresolved interfaces, and revisit responses when design, procurement, or site information changes ([4] [6] [7]).

Early project definition matters because design, price, and schedule are linked. A geotechnical unknown is not just a line in a register: it can change foundations, equipment, durations, and permitting. A late design decision can consume float and contingency simultaneously. The owner should specify trigger thresholds and actions: investigate a soil anomaly, approve a revised method, quantify time and cost effects, then update the baseline according to agreed change controls. If the same risk is carried independently as a cost percentage, schedule reserve, and reference-class uplift, the resulting budget may count it repeatedly. Conversely, omitting owner-retained approvals from all three models leaves a gap ([6] [7] [13]).

Flyvbjerg's transport research defines an overrun using real construction outturn cost minus the forecast at the *decision to build*, divided by that forecast. This denominator differs from a contractor's post-design bid or latest approved budget. In his transport sample, rail, bridge/tunnel, and road averages were respectively 44.7, 33.8, and 20.4 percent; they do not imply that each new project will match its class mean. He discusses technical error, optimism, and political-economic incentives as different possible sources of inaccuracy. A comparison based on an early political promise cannot be silently equated with a change from a later contractual GMP ([13]).

## Evidence

### Building Delivery Comparisons

Konchar's comparison assembled project-specific information for 351 US buildings delivered through DBB, CMAR, and DB. Its reported sample composition was 33 percent DBB, 23 percent CMAR, and 44 percent DB. The research compared cost, schedule, and quality attributes using observed projects and multivariate methods, not random assignment of otherwise identical buildings to contracts. Its scope was design and construction cost rather than land, extensive site work, process equipment, or all owner costs. That denominator is necessary when reading its performance comparisons: an advantage in building delivery cost is not necessarily an advantage in the entire asset's lifecycle economics ([15]).

The later CII/Pankow-sponsored follow-up collected 212 verified contemporary building projects and reported adjusted performance comparisons. DB projects had, on average in that study, 3.8 percent less cost growth and 1.7 percent less schedule growth than DBB, and the reported delivery-speed comparison favored DB. The report also attributes differences to team chemistry, contracting, size, and early builder involvement. Its cost-growth regression table reports R-squared of 22 percent, indicating that most observed variation in this performance measure remains outside that model. A comparative association is evidence for asking what an early constructor changes; it is not an experiment proving that replacing a DBB contract alone would reproduce the reported differences on an arbitrary project ([10]).

### Highway Design-Build Evaluation

FHWA's design-build effectiveness study surveyed completed federal-aid highway DB projects in the SEP-14 program and compared a limited subset of similar DBB projects. In that subset, the DB projects had a 9 percent shorter total project duration and a 13 percent shorter construction phase. Project-manager perceptions, separately reported in the study, estimated a 14 percent duration reduction and a 3 percent cost reduction; these are perceptions, not independently measured causal effects. Importantly, the comparison of cost records for a subset of similar projects favored DBB, while the overall cost findings varied by project type, size, and complexity. This is a direct counterexample to the unqualified assertion that DB always lowers costs ([9]).

The FHWA findings also highlight sequencing. Overlap between final design and construction can shorten elapsed time, but preparing a design-build procurement may take longer than a familiar DBB bid. A decision using only the construction period misses the owner's earlier design and selection work. Highway results should not be generalized without checking differences in site conditions, third-party approvals, project definition, and choice of the starting and ending clock. The study itself identifies owner experience as a challenge and recommends investment in design-build training ([9]).

### Forecasting and Controls

Flyvbjerg's 2005 World Bank paper presents a table of 258 transport projects: 58 rail, 33 bridges and tunnels, and 167 roads. Their respective mean real construction cost overruns measured against decision-to-build estimates are 44.7, 33.8, and 20.4 percent. He reports overrun in nine out of ten projects in the sample and no reduction in average cost error over the covered historical period. Transport-project results are a reason to use reference classes and explicit uncertainty, not a verified rate for *all* building projects or a demonstration that procurement method alone caused the discrepancy. The cost baseline, inflation adjustment, and omission of financing and operations matter ([13]).

The table also gives standard deviations of 38.4 percent for rail, 62.4 percent for bridges and tunnels, and 29.9 percent for roads. The dispersion matters as much as the averages: even two projects of the same broad transport type need not have similar outcomes. These figures measure errors in *construction cost* forecasts at a particular decision point; they do not include subsequent maintenance expenditure or the value of benefits actually realized. Nor can the distribution for a road be applied to a building merely because both use concrete. Before calculating a project-specific uplift, analysts must define a comparable type, cost boundary, price basis, and forecast date; otherwise the apparent empirical precision belongs to the wrong question ([13]).

GAO's Schedule Assessment Guide is a methodological synthesis rather than a project-delivery trial. It sets out ten schedule best practices including complete activities, logically sequenced work, a valid critical path, reasonable total float, schedule risk analysis, and updates based on progress. GAO's Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide links scope definition, a work breakdown structure, independent comparison, sensitivity, uncertainty, risk-adjusted contingency, and EVM. Read together, they give a falsifiable control test: when a supplier delay appears, can the team identify its dependent work, modeled cost, available float, and owner of the response? A schedule that has not been updated cannot supply the answer merely by having once passed a baseline review ([6] [7]).

### Integrated Teams and Early Warning Signs

The IPDA/Lean Construction Institute report studied ten *successful* building projects using integrated agreements in the US and Canada. Interviews, surveys, and document reviews describe team selection, trust, early coordination, pull planning, and collaborative decision making. Seven in ten teams used the full Last Planner System, according to the report's tools section. Since the selection condition was success and there was no matched conventional control group, these observations support an account of practices used by those teams, not an estimated average treatment effect of IPD or lean delivery ([11]).

A 2026 Scientific Reports study by Alsulami, Al-Shaery, and Kassem used a Saudi construction-professional questionnaire and a four-expert nominal-group exercise. The final survey instrument had 41 warning-sign items and a reported Cronbach alpha of 0.86. Its authors identify weak setup, inadequate response to problems, inaccurate costs, leadership weaknesses, and limited project experience as salient warning signs. These were perceived importance ratings and expert consensus, not proof that detection or any specific mitigation *caused* a reduction in project failure. The authors themselves describe the result as context-specific and stop short of claiming full statistical validation; this limits application to other jurisdictions and delivery types ([12]).

## Implications

### For Infrastructure Owners and Public Agencies

The owner should begin with a decision record, not a preferred acronym. Define the physical outcome, operations requirements, time constraints, available market, legal procurement options, and the owner team's ability to review designs and make timely decisions. Then compare how DBB, DB, CMAR, and, where feasible, integrated arrangements would allocate the project's material risks. NIGP and CMAA describe selection as a project-specific exercise. FHWA's current guide identifies progressive DB and best-value DBB alongside more familiar forms, demonstrating that contract type and award criteria need not be treated as a single rigid choice ([2] [3] [4]).

If the priority is design control and a common completed specification for bidders, DBB can be sensible, provided the owner budgets adequate design review and checks interfaces. If an early start is important, DB may allow overlapping work, but the owner needs a reliable performance brief and a credible plan for retained permits and utilities. If the physical conditions are uncertain and constructor advice during design is valuable, CMAR may help, provided its GMP assumptions and trade-price competition are made explicit. IPD requires an owner prepared for sustained participation and a contract that actually specifies decisions, risks, and incentives. These are conditional applications of the cited guidance, not predictions of project savings ([1] [2] [3] [14]).

A public agency should also specify what counts as success before award: construction cost, total owner cost, opening date, whole-life performance, and safety or service requirements. FHWA's highway evidence and the building comparisons measure different populations and different metrics. Comparing one contractor's bid to another project's all-in budget would create an apparent advantage with no physical basis. The owner should make scope and time endpoints comparable and publish significant retained risks, so that a procurement decision can later be evaluated against its stated objectives ([7] [9] [10]).

### For Cost Estimators and Project Controllers

Estimate maturity must match its decision. An early screening number should carry explicit assumptions, a date, exclusions, and an uncertainty distribution. A later bid estimate should use more developed quantities and current unit prices, but both can be wrong if scope is missing. The AACE process-industry classification is an example of why ranges are conditional; it must not be transposed mechanically to a highway or commercial building. GAO's cost guide recommends an independent cross-check and continued updates with actual costs. When an owner wants one affordable funding number, the estimator should show the selected confidence level and the implications of choosing a leaner one ([5] [7]).

A controller should connect the approved work breakdown, cost baseline, and live schedule. EV, PV, and AC can reveal whether completed work is costing more than planned and whether less work has been earned than scheduled. CPI is not itself a diagnosis: a negative cost trend can arise from low productivity, scope omissions, incorrect measurement, or cost posting before corresponding progress credit. An EAC forecast should disclose the assumption about future efficiency and be tested against supplier quotes, remaining quantities, and expected changes. SPI measured in currency cannot substitute for a time forecast from a current critical-path network ([6] [7] [8]).

For a simple check, consider the illustrative baseline of $10 million with $3 million earned and $5 million spent. The difference shows $2 million more spent than budgeted for accomplished work, but it does not say which task caused it. Investigate the underlying work package before extrapolating CPI = 0.60 to all remaining work. If the poor performance came from a one-off excavation issue that is now resolved, a constant-CPI projection could overstate cost; if underestimated production rates persist, it may be informative. The calculation has value only alongside engineering evidence of remaining work and risk ([7] [8]).

### For Megaproject Governance

Governance should keep the original decision-stage forecast, the later contract price, and the latest authorized budget as separate baselines. Flyvbjerg's transport sample measures the first comparison; many routine construction dashboards use the second or third. Confusing them obscures both early forecast bias and changes during execution. Ask an independent team to examine whether the reference class matches the technology and scope, what its cost and schedule distributions contain, and why the proposed project is believed to differ. Use this evidence to select contingency and decision thresholds without pretending it eliminates every site-specific risk ([7] [13]).

An outside view also needs governance of its own. If a proponent can choose whichever historical projects yield the smallest uplift, the baseline will reproduce the same optimism in a new format. Record inclusion criteria, comparable cost definitions, actual completion dates, inflation treatment, and excluded cases before selecting a percentile. Investigate the difference between the class-based result and a technical bottom-up estimate; neither justifies silently replacing the other. Flyvbjerg describes both psychological optimism and incentives for strategic misrepresentation, which require different controls. An independent challenge of assumptions can address the former; auditability and approval accountability are needed for the latter ([13]).

### For Construction and Engineering Teams

Early builder participation is valuable when it changes an engineering decision in time: for example, when an erection sequence reveals inaccessible joints, a proposed pipe route clashes with a structure, or an equipment lead time threatens a dependent activity. The drawing, quantity, procurement plan, risk register, and CPM logic should then be revised together. A meeting without a design change or a documented decision has not by itself created buildability. IPDA's ten integrated cases illustrate collaboration practices, and CII/Pankow's results associate early builder involvement with better performance, while neither source licenses a universal saving estimate from adding meetings ([10] [11]).

For the contractor, risk transfer on paper cannot make a party control an owner approval or hidden utility. Pricing an unmanageable risk may create contingencies, disputes, or an incentive to narrow the scope. A clearer interface specifies who gathers information, who authorizes a change, what records are required, and how schedule and cost consequences are evaluated. For the owner, the parallel obligation is to make decisions on time and preserve an auditable baseline. This engineering interpretation follows FHWA's risk-allocation guidance and GAO's controls guidance; it is not a statement that all DBB jobs are litigious or all collaborative contracts succeed ([4] [6] [7]).

### For Risk Management Practitioners

Convert warnings into response rules. A rising rate of design changes may prompt a coordinated interface review; repeated missed weekly commitments may require a near-critical-path update; cost-to-complete growth may require independent quantity checking. The responsible party, decision authority, and next review date should be explicit. Saudi practitioners ranked poor setup and inaccurate costs highly in one mixed-methods study, but local ratings are not a calibrated probability of project failure. Repeated measures and completed-project outcomes would be needed before claiming a predictive threshold ([6] [7] [12]).

A combined cost-and-schedule risk review should ask which event can affect several work packages at once, which reserve belongs to the owner, and what remedy remains feasible after work begins. If a utility conflict delays foundations, a nominal material-price contingency cannot restore the schedule. If a design change adds quantities, calling the cost difference a forecast error without examining scope change is equally misleading. Project controls matter because they make these distinctions visible while an intervention is still possible. The general conclusion is narrower than the original claim that contract selection explains most overruns: delivery structure influences coordination and incentives, while the documented studies also identify scope, project type, front-end definition, team practice, and uncertainty as separate determinants ([6] [7] [9] [10] [12]).
