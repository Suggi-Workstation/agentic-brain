# Structural Health Monitoring and Condition-Based Maintenance -- Evidence Must Trigger Action Before Assets Fail

Structural health monitoring (SHM) observes changes in the response or condition of an engineered asset; condition-based maintenance (CBM) uses credible condition evidence to choose the timing and scope of intervention. Sensors and nondestructive evaluation can reveal deterioration missed by occasional visual inspection, but neither a warning signal nor an algorithm by itself proves remaining capacity or prevents failure. Their engineering value arises when measurements are checked against physical mechanisms, uncertainty and acceptance criteria, then translated into inspection, repair, operating restrictions, or renewal ([1] [3] [5] [8]).

## Background

Engineers have long inspected bridges, pipelines, rail track, rotating machinery, and other assets because the conditions under which they operate change after construction. Loads, moisture, temperature, movement, corrosion, wear, installation defects, and repair history make actual behavior differ from design assumptions. An intermittent inspection can document observable defects, but it may miss hidden damage or the progression between visits. Conversely, instrumentation records responses under operating loads but may not directly reveal which component has deteriorated. FHWA's bridge research therefore combines visual inspection, nondestructive evaluation (NDE), field monitoring, and models rather than treating one method as a replacement for the others ([1] [3] [10]).

The intellectual shift was from inspecting a structure as a static object to learning from its behavior over time. Farrar, Sohn, and Park described SHM as a statistical pattern-recognition problem: establish what damage matters, acquire measurements, extract features, and distinguish meaningful changes from normal variation. Their operational evaluation is prior to buying sensors; it asks whether a measurable feature will identify a particular damage mechanism at the decision time required. Environmental and operational changes such as temperature and load can move a feature even when the asset remains sound. A detection system therefore needs a baseline covering relevant operating regimes and a way to test both false alarms and missed damage ([2]).

The extension to maintenance is older and broader than continuous electronic sensing. DOE's operations and maintenance guide contrasts reactive repair after failure, calendar- or run-time-based preventive work, and predictive or condition-based work scheduled when observed machine condition warrants it. The condition may come from a periodic measurement rather than a live data stream: vibration, temperature, oil analysis, pressure, and performance trending are examples for equipment. The object is not to minimize the number of maintenance visits at any cost; it is to intervene before unacceptable degradation while avoiding unnecessary replacement of serviceable parts. DOE also stresses that skilled analysis, work control, and time to act determine whether monitoring yields practical benefit ([8]).

Physical infrastructure complicates that logic. A bridge deck is not a pump bearing; local defects may be concealed by a wearing surface, movement may depend strongly on traffic and temperature, and a global vibration feature can be insensitive to a small critical defect. FHWA distinguishes NDE methods for finding and characterizing local material defects from SHM methods that capture repeated or continuous structural response. Its bridge load-rating research attempts to combine the two: measured load effects inform demand, while observed deterioration updates the dimensions or properties used to estimate capacity. Neither a normal response trace nor the absence of a visible crack is equivalent to a capacity demonstration ([3] [5]).

Infrastructure agencies also face a portfolio problem. Bridges and rail routes cannot all receive permanent instruments; buried water mains may be inaccessible and may differ in material, soil, pressure, and failure consequence. EPA's water-pipe assessment review separates historical failures, inspection technologies, physical and empirical deterioration models, condition rating, and renewal decisions. It notes that limited failure history can make model validation difficult. FHWA's Long-Term Bridge Performance effort likewise links representative inspections, periodic monitoring, NDE, and longitudinal data to better deterioration models, rather than assuming that one instrumented bridge represents a whole network ([7] [10]).

High-consequence assets make the decision boundary explicit. The IAEA's ageing-management framework for nuclear plant components covers ageing of active and passive structures, including the need for monitoring and corrective actions over the plant lifecycle. Such programmes use condition indicators and acceptance criteria tied to an intended safety function, not a generic sensor score. That architecture is instructive for other physical assets, but nuclear-specific limits and processes must not be copied to a highway, water network, or industrial plant without an asset-specific engineering basis ([9]).

The topic is consequently the measurement-to-action chain for physical systems. Materials degradation explains mechanisms such as corrosion; reliability engineering explains failure probabilities and post-failure investigation; transport and water topics explain sector-specific plant. Here the question is how to choose observations, interpret changes, and make timely maintenance decisions across assets before loss of intended function. This is applied engineering and lifecycle asset management, not a survey of digital-product architecture, general corporate strategy, industrial-sector economics, or forensic reconstruction after a collapse ([1] [3] [7] [8]).

## Core Concepts

### Start With a Failure Mode and a Decision

Monitoring begins with an asset register, function, credible deterioration mechanism, consequence, and a decision that new information could change. A cable's corrosion, a bridge deck's delamination, a track segment's gauge deviation, a buried main's wall loss, and a motor bearing's wear require different observables and intervention lead times. A baseline should identify the as-built configuration, known repairs, operating envelope, inspection access, and condition at installation. The author's synthesis is that a measurement without a defined action is data collection, not a maintenance programme; the measurement plan should specify who decides, what evidence they review, and how quickly a safe action can be taken ([2] [6] [7] [8]).

Criticality is distinct from condition. A modest defect in a nonredundant element with severe failure consequences may merit earlier confirmation than a larger defect in a replaceable, isolated component. EPA's water-main review describes screening critical pipes for closer inspection and combining condition and failure mode with service consequences. DOE's reliability-centered maintenance discussion similarly treats scarce money and staff as reasons to select different maintenance regimes for different equipment. A simple age rank alone cannot encode detectability, time to deterioration, access, or loss of service if the asset fails ([7] [8]).

### Select a Method for the Defect, Scale, and Time Horizon

Visual inspection documents accessible cracks, displacement, leakage, and distress. NDE can interrogate material without destroying the asset: ground-penetrating radar, electrical resistivity, half-cell potential, impact echo, ultrasonic methods, infrared thermography, acoustic methods, and electromagnetic methods answer different questions in a bridge deck or pipe. Some methods indicate a possible pre-damage corrosion environment; others locate existing separation or geometry. A signal correlated with corrosion likelihood is not a direct measurement of remaining steel area. FHWA's preservation brief recommends combining measures where a decision needs information about both present damage and likely imminent deterioration ([4] [5] [7]).

SHM instead collects repeated measurements of structural response or exposure: strain, displacement, tilt, acceleration, temperature, crack width, load, or chloride ingress. Sensor placement must be tied to the suspected location and the expected spatial scale of damage. Low-frequency readings may suit a slow process such as chloride ingress, whereas dynamic load events require an adequate sampling rate and synchronized measurements. FHWA's substructure report describes wired and wireless acquisition, power and transmission trade-offs, and instruments that failed during installation or service. More sensors can increase coverage but also create more channels requiring calibration, maintenance, communication, and quality control ([1] [11]).

A method can be inappropriate even when technically impressive. FHWA's bridge-deck preservation study found structural monitoring of limited applicability for assessing decks as individual components and did not carry it into the proposed deck decision framework; it emphasized NDE instead. That finding qualifies any universal claim that continuous SHM should replace targeted inspection. Conversely, a bridge's global structural response or a hard-to-access foundation may justify monitoring that cannot be replicated efficiently through periodic deck surveys. Selection must follow the decision and defect, not the popularity of the instrument ([1] [5]).

### Convert Signals Into Defensible Condition Evidence

An installed sensor produces a reading, not a diagnosis. Baseline loads, ambient temperature, humidity, seasonal cycles, changing operating speed, altered support conditions, repair, and sensor drift can change a response. Farrar and colleagues prescribe normalization and data cleansing so the classification model distinguishes damage-related features from environmental and operational variability. A missing or implausible reading should trigger instrument checks; an anomaly detected in data should trigger independent confirmation if the consequence warrants it. Otherwise false alarms can waste shutdowns and missed damage can give false reassurance ([1] [2]).

Independent observations reduce ambiguity but do not eliminate it. A bridge deck survey could combine a surface image, an electrical indicator of corrosion conditions, and a method sensitive to delamination. FHWA's 2025 accelerated deck investigation collected several NDE modalities over time and compared condition and defect indexes with observed deck condition and modeled deterioration. A method's performance depends on ground-truth labels, environmental range, specimen design, and the target defect; a laboratory receiver-operating characteristic is not a universal field detection probability. The author's assessment is that validation should record the inspection method's resolution and known blind spots as part of every condition estimate ([4] [5]).

### Model Deterioration, Demand, and Capacity Separately

Deterioration models connect exposure, material, loads, and time to potential damage. Mechanistic models represent a process such as chloride ingress or wall loss; empirical models learn associations from break history, condition ratings, or inspection trends. EPA documents both approaches for water pipes and cautions that available observations may not suffice to validate a predicted failure curve. FHWA's bridge research similarly develops models from longitudinal observations. Models should show their inputs, applicable asset class, calibration period, and uncertainty; a precise-looking remaining-life date may overstate what sparse measurements establish ([4] [7] [10]).

Condition, capacity, and demand are separate quantities. A localized indication may reduce available section or change a component's limit state, while live loads and their distribution determine demand. FHWA-HRT-21-011 investigated how NDE information revises estimates of member capacity and how field SHM or load-test data revise demand. Its finite-element study reported less than 3 percent change in member demand for the local deterioration scenarios analyzed, not a general rule that deterioration is harmless. The report warns that an in-service bridge can behave differently from an assumed demand model. Therefore an alarm threshold tied only to vibration amplitude cannot substitute for an engineer's evaluation of the relevant load path and remaining margin ([3]).

### Set Thresholds With Verification and a Safe Action Window

A threshold is an operational rule, not a natural constant. The relevant criterion may be measured section loss, crack growth under a particular load, pressure loss, track geometry outside tolerance, a modeled capacity margin, or a rising trend whose projected crossing occurs before the next inspection. Define both a measurement-quality alert and a condition alert: a disconnected probe should not silently report health, and a small but genuine change should not automatically cause a shutdown without interpretation. IAEA ageing-management practice links condition indicators and acceptance criteria to corrective action before loss of intended function; DOE describes condition-based repairs when equipment trends reach predetermined unacceptable levels ([8] [9]).

Thresholds should have response tiers. An unusual reading can demand data validation and targeted inspection; a confirmed deterioration rate can shorten the next inspection interval or schedule protection and repair; a confirmed loss of adequate margin can require restriction, isolation, or replacement. Time to discover, confirm, procure, mobilize, and repair must be shorter than the credible time to unsafe condition. This response logic is the author's synthesis of FHWA's preservation decision framework, EPA's pipe renewal pathway, and IAEA's acceptance-and-corrective-action architecture; the numeric trigger for any asset must be established by competent local engineering rather than copied from a case study ([5] [7] [9]).

### Compare Maintenance Policies by Whole-Life Consequence

Run-to-failure can be rational for a cheap, noncritical and safely isolated item, but not for a structural member whose failure endangers users. Calendar-based maintenance can be sensible where degradation is poorly observable, inspection is costly, or a prescribed test interval has a safety basis. CBM is most useful where a meaningful precursor is detectable early enough and an intervention is feasible. The cost comparison includes sensors, surveys, access, false alarms, delayed repairs, downtime, replacement, and consequences of missed defects; it is not a calculation of instrument purchase price alone. DOE's guide explicitly distinguishes condition-based and scheduled strategies, while FHWA assesses NDE for bridge preservation treatment choice and timing ([5] [8]).

## Evidence

### Bridge Substructures: Long Follow-Up Reveals Measurement Failure

FHWA-HRT-09-040 reviewed bridge substructure monitoring methods and field experience with embedded gauges, including a West Seattle bridge foundation whose measurements extended over approximately two decades. The report records individual instruments damaged during installation and strain gauges that failed during service, alongside surviving observations. This is observational engineering evidence, not a randomized comparison of maintenance strategies. Its finding is that a long record can inform structural behavior only when instrument health and environmental exposure are tracked too; the report discusses possible groundwater-related gauge failures rather than asserting a single verified failure cause. It also distinguishes slow threshold measurements, such as chloride ingress, from higher-frequency acquisition required for dynamic bridge loading ([1]).

That case bears directly on decision reliability. If a channel fails quietly, an apparent flat condition trend can be an instrumentation artifact; if gauges are placed where their readings do not represent the suspect load path, excellent telemetry still cannot resolve the maintenance question. Physical access, baseline measurement and occasional independent inspection remain necessary. FHWA's 2017 wireless-sensor fact sheet reports field research on acceleration, strain, displacement, and tilt for bridge assessment, but describes developing systems and prospective early warning rather than a validated claim that wireless coverage by itself prevents collapse ([1] [11]).

### Bridge Load Rating: Distinguish Field Response From Material Loss

FHWA-HRT-21-011 combined analytical studies and field-collected data from bridge case studies, including Paint Creek Bridge, Haymarket Bridge, and an accelerated-test specimen. Its method integrated SHM or load-testing measurements into estimates of load effects and NDE observations into estimates of capacity. The report found that experimental distribution factors estimated field demands more reliably than the comparison methods drawn from bridge-evaluation practice for the cases studied; analytical simulations of certain localized defects changed member demands by less than 3 percent. The result is not that local defects need no repair. It shows why estimating the actual capacity separately from the actual load effect matters: a defect can affect a limit state or section strength even when it barely changes the measured distribution of force ([3]).

An owner considering a load restriction should not infer a bridge's remaining reserve from an isolated vibration feature or one photograph. The reported integration approach can improve a rating if deterioration measurements and field responses are credible, but it retains the limitations of the assumed model, representative load tests, sampling locations, and condition characterization. The study itself cautions that in-service response may depart from predicted demand. The author's synthesis is that a monitoring programme should preserve the chain from measurement to the particular capacity and demand calculation it changes, and keep uncertainty visible in any decision memo ([3]).

### Accelerated Deck Testing: Useful Correlations Have Transfer Limits

FHWA-HRT-25-029 followed a bridge-deck specimen under accelerated deterioration, collecting repeated NDE observations including impact echo, ultrasonic surface waves, electrical resistivity, ground-penetrating radar, half-cell potential, and thermal imaging. The researchers compared NDE condition and defect indexes, receiver-operating-characteristic measures of detection, observed condition, and service-life or deterioration-model predictions. The study describes corrosion-sensitive indicators useful before visible delamination and damage-sensitive indicators for later deterioration; some modeled trends were compared against half-cell observations. This is a detailed experimental and modeling case, not a measured national effect of SHM on bridge failure rates ([4]).

The report also notes limitations in visual-condition observations and explicitly qualifies some derived index thresholds as based on limited specimen data. Accelerated loading and exposure produce useful controlled contrasts but do not duplicate every field environment, deck detail, or inspection history. FHWA's separate 2024 preservation study built a decision framework from literature, agency interviews, deterioration modes, NDE methods, and example economic cases; notably it excluded structural monitoring from its proposed deck-specific framework because it judged applicability limited. Together these studies support selective NDE and model calibration, not a sensor-first promise of automatic maintenance optimization ([4] [5]).

### Rail Geometry: Automation Still Needs Field Verification

The Federal Railroad Administration's 2021 Report to Congress described approved automatic track-geometry measurement system test programmes with combinations of automated and visual inspections. Automated equipment measures gauge, crosslevel, warp, alignment, and profile and can identify defects or trends that call for remedial action. FRA compared reported measurements with baseline inspections and its own inspection vehicles; inspectors field-verified reported defect locations and checked that railroads took appropriate action. This is a documented field evaluation of inspection practices, not proof that all visual inspections can be eliminated or that automation detects every rail failure mode ([6]).

Rail is a distinct test of the cross-asset principle because a geometry defect is a measurable departure from defined track condition, but the railway still needs people who confirm locations, investigate underlying causes, and repair them. FRA's programme made inspection results, defect records, and corrective action auditable across participating networks. Its scope also limits inference: geometry measurements do not themselves detect every internal rail flaw, ballast condition, bridge defect, or operational hazard. The transferable finding is an accountable measurement-confirmation-repair loop, not a general permission to replace human judgment with a defect counter ([6]).

### Water Pipes and Machinery: Evidence Has Different Strengths

EPA/600/R-12/017 synthesized water-distribution condition technologies, pipe distress indicators, mechanistic and empirical deterioration models, and decision-support methods. It distinguished network screening from more costly detailed examination of critical pipes, and reviewed methods such as acoustic, electromagnetic, ultrasonic, and environmental assessment with material-specific applicability. The report observes that scarce failure events and incomplete condition data can prevent validation of predicted failure curves. This is a technology and practice synthesis, not a controlled trial demonstrating a particular percentage reduction in breaks. For a utility, it supports using condition evidence and consequence together while retaining uncertainty about uninspected buried segments ([7]).

DOE's operations and maintenance guide provides a different kind of evidence for industrial machinery. Its reported example of a manufacturer switching oil changes for hundreds of machines from a fixed timetable to in-house condition-based oil analysis illustrates a feasible change in work practice and reports annual savings, but is a single case with a site-specific baseline. The guide also describes vibration, thermography, oil analysis, motor analysis, and performance trending as diagnostic methods. It does not establish that the same economic result applies to a bridge deck, a municipal pipe, or a high-consequence process unit. The defensible conclusion is that CBM can avoid some unnecessary work when the condition measure is relevant, repeatable, and actually governs a maintenance decision ([8]).

## Implications

### For Owners and Infrastructure Agencies

Start with a decision register rather than a sensor catalogue. For each critical asset, record its intended function, failure mode, observable precursor, detection limit, likely consequence, current evidence, action threshold, owner of the decision, and realistic time to intervene. If no plausible measurement would change inspection or repair timing, continuous monitoring may not be justified. Conversely, an inaccessible member with a credible progressive mechanism and severe consequence may warrant higher-quality measurement and a conservative response plan even if the instrument is expensive. This is the author's proposed application of the FHWA, EPA, DOE, and IAEA frameworks, not a numerical standard for every asset ([1] [5] [7] [8] [9]).

Match the budget to the full chain. Equipment acquisition without installation quality, protected power, communications, calibration, data storage, qualified analysts, confirmatory inspection, work orders, and money for intervention will not deliver the intended risk reduction. FHWA's decades-long gauge record illustrates that sensors fail; FRA's field programme illustrates that a measured defect must be located and remedied. Record both successful alerts and failures of observation, then revise placements, intervals, and thresholds. The most important feedback is whether a completed repair or operating change actually stabilized the condition the programme was designed to control ([1] [6]).

For network prioritization, use inexpensive screening where appropriate and reserve detailed methods for segments whose condition or consequence justifies them. A water utility can combine break history, soil and operating exposures, service criticality, and targeted pipe assessment; a bridge owner can use routine inspection to choose where NDE or load testing may change a preservation or restriction decision. A poor condition score and a high consequence score are not interchangeable, and neither is a substitute for verifying the specific failure mechanism. The EPA synthesis and FHWA preservation framework support this staged, decision-led approach ([5] [7]).

### For Inspectors and Engineers

State exactly what each instrument measures, its spatial and temporal resolution, and what it cannot see. A strain gauge measures response at its location, not the entire bridge's health. Half-cell potential concerns corrosion likelihood under applicable conditions, not the amount of steel remaining. Track geometry equipment measures geometry, not every internal defect. A thermographic anomaly requires a physical interpretation and often confirmation. Interpretation must consider calibration, repairs, operating regime, noise, temperature, and baseline drift before an engineer calls a change damage. These distinctions are supported by the method descriptions and limitations in FHWA, FRA, EPA, and the Los Alamos SHM paper ([2] [4] [6] [7]).

Do not treat a predictive model as an independent fact about an individual asset. A bridge-deck index derived under accelerated exposure may not transfer to a different material and climate. A pipe break model built from a short, incomplete record may lack observations in its wear-out regime. A field load test can correct demand assumptions but does not automatically measure concealed capacity loss. The validation task is to compare predictions with subsequent inspections, repairs, removed material, and operating history; record missed detections as well as true positives. This follows the explicit limits reported in the FHWA experiments and EPA review ([3] [4] [7]).

An intervention threshold should include a plan for uncertainty and time. If an anomaly may indicate a safety-critical loss of margin, first establish safe interim operating conditions while confirming the signal; do not wait for a model to claim an exact remaining life. Conversely, do not automatically condemn an asset from one unverified outlier. Define a reversible next observation where feasible, with escalation to an independent capacity or integrity evaluation where the potential consequence is severe. The author's synthesis is an engineering response rule informed by FHWA's capacity-demand separation and the IAEA acceptance-criteria model, not an asset-specific regulatory requirement ([3] [9]).

### For Operators and Maintainers

CBM should change work packages, not merely dashboards. A confirmed bearing trend can prompt lubrication review, alignment checks, or planned replacement; a bridge-deck survey can direct preservation or targeted repair before widespread damage; a water-main assessment can alter leak investigation, isolation preparedness, or renewal sequence. Work completion should include as-found condition, material removed, remedy, follow-up measurement, and any change to the detection model. Without those records, a programme can appear successful because it issues alerts while repeatedly treating symptoms or silently missing defects ([5] [7] [8]).

Mix policies instead of making CBM universal. Scheduled inspection can be necessary even with live instruments because sensors are local and can fail. A hard-to-observe, rapidly progressing defect might justify preventive replacement or conservative operating limits rather than waiting for a noisy predictor. Run-to-failure may remain acceptable for a safely redundant component with readily available replacement. Criticality, observability, and repair lead time determine the policy. The author's assessment follows the differing method limitations documented by FHWA and the maintenance-program distinctions in DOE's guide ([1] [5] [8]).

### For Lifecycle Decisions and Risk Governance

Compare alternatives against an explicit counterfactual: what maintenance and failure exposure would occur without this monitoring plan? Include sensor and analyst cost, shutdowns, repeat inspections, preserved service, avoided emergency repair, and the uncertainty of rare high-consequence events. FHWA's bridge-deck study considered example economic cases, while DOE's oil-analysis case reports a site-specific saving; neither supplies a universal savings multiplier. Pilot monitoring should be judged by whether it improves a validated decision or reduces consequential uncertainty, not by volume of collected data or number of alarms ([5] [8]).

The worst failure mode is false confidence: a green display for an asset whose damage lies outside sensor coverage, whose channel drifted, or whose deterioration model was never validated. Prevent it by mapping observables to failure modes, retaining independent inspection, making uncertain and missing data visible, and assigning authority to act before the safety margin disappears. The contrasting evidence is important: FHWA's substructure report documents failed instruments, the deck brief rejects indiscriminate monitoring for one component, and the load-rating study separates observed behavior from residual capacity. More data can be valuable, but only when engineers know which decisions it can and cannot support ([1] [3] [5]).

This topic complements, rather than repeats, the existing engineering library. Corrosion and materials degradation explains physical causes and protection; reliability and failure analysis supplies system risk and post-failure learning; transport and water topics supply the network-specific assets. The author's synthesis is a common prospective discipline: select credible precursors, validate observations, maintain an uncertainty-aware deterioration model, and fund the action triggered by evidence. If a monitoring programme cannot show where that chain ends in an intervention, it has not yet demonstrated condition-based maintenance ([2] [5] [7] [8]).
