# Ecosystems Function Through Coupled Energy, Matter, and Interaction Networks

An ecosystem is not merely a list of species in a place; it is a functional system in which organisms and the physical environment exchange energy and matter through interacting biological, chemical, and physical processes. Ecosystem science matters because disturbances to one process, such as primary production, predation, decomposition, or nutrient supply, can propagate through food webs and biogeochemical cycles, changing the stability and services of the whole system. [2][8][12][13]

## Background

Ecology began by asking how organisms are distributed and how they respond to their surroundings, but ecosystem science required a further conceptual move: organisms and the nonliving environment had to be analyzed as one system. In 1935, A. G. Tansley introduced the term "ecosystem" in a paper on vegetational concepts and terms, at a time when Clements's view of the plant community as a superorganism progressing to a climax was influential. Tansley accepted that mature, well-integrated plant communities had enough of the characters of organisms to be considered quasi-organisms, but he argued that organisms cannot be separated from the habitat factors with which they form one physical system, and he described such ecosystems as the basic units of nature, of the most various kinds and sizes. The word itself had been suggested to him by A. R. Clapham. [1] The National Research Council later summarized the same research program as the study of flows of energy and materials among groups of organisms and between biological communities and the abiotic environment. [14]

That systems view acquired a quantitative foundation through Raymond Lindeman's 1942 analysis of trophic dynamics. Lindeman organized organisms into trophic levels according to how they obtain energy and treated production, consumption, respiration, decomposition, and loss as measurable transfers. His work shifted food-chain description toward energy accounting: the important question was not only who eats whom, but how much energy enters a level, how much becomes biomass, and how much is dissipated before reaching the next level. Lindeman defined the ecosystem as the system composed of physical-chemical-biological processes active within a space-time unit of any magnitude. [2] Howard T. Odum's later measurements at Silver Springs, Florida (published in 1957), made that approach concrete by estimating energy content and transfer among producers and successive consumer levels in a whole spring ecosystem. [15]

Eugene Odum extended this functional approach to ecosystem development. His 1969 synthesis defined succession as an orderly, reasonably directional and predictable process of community development, controlled by the community's modification of its physical environment, that culminates in a stabilized ecosystem. It tabulated 24 attributes expected to differ between developmental and mature stages; for example, the ratio of gross production to community respiration approaches 1, the biomass supported per unit of energy flow rises, and mineral cycles become closed. [3] Connell and Slatyer later criticized this model: they argued that most of the proposed characteristics of mature communities are consequences of the passage of time rather than of internal control, and that the view of the mature community as a homeostatic, organism-like system rests on analogy rather than evidence. They instead distinguished facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition as alternative testable mechanisms by which early colonists affect later species, and they emphasized that recurrent disturbance interrupts succession and starts it over. [5] Succession is therefore best treated as a contingent reorganization of organisms and processes, not a universal ladder with one predetermined endpoint (interpretation).

Food-web research added a second challenge to simple succession stories. Robert Paine's rocky-intertidal removal experiment at Mukkaw Bay, Washington, kept a stretch of shoreline free of the sea star Pisaster ochraceus from June 1963; barnacles and then mussels came to occupy the cleared space, and the number of species there fell from 15 to 8, although the standing crop increased. Paine concluded that predation interrupts the successional process and, locally, increases diversity. [4] The author's interpretation is that this case is the experimental basis usually cited for the keystone-species concept, and that it shows ecosystem function cannot be inferred from biomass alone. Hooper and colleagues likewise conclude that relative abundance is not always a good predictor of a species' ecosystem-level importance, because even relatively rare species, such as a keystone predator, can strongly influence pathways of energy and material flow. [8]

Ecologists also debated whether complexity makes ecosystems stable. Elton and MacArthur had argued that stability is roughly proportional to the number of links between species in a trophic web, because alternative links maintain the same flows of energy and nutrients when a species becomes rare. [6] Robert May's 1972 analysis showed that randomly assembled complex systems are in general less stable, and never more stable, than less complex ones. [6][11] For a large random community matrix with S species, connectance C, and interaction strengths with standard deviation sigma, May's criterion makes local stability improbable when sigma times the square root of S times C exceeds 1. [11] C. S. Holling (1973) then separated stability, the ability of a system to return to an equilibrium after a temporary disturbance, from resilience, the persistence of relationships within a system and its ability to absorb changes in state variables, driving variables, and parameters and still persist. A system can be very resilient and still fluctuate greatly. [6] These results did not prove that diversity destabilizes nature. Holling noted that May's definition of stability was the equilibrium-centered one and that ecological systems may have evolved to a small subset of all possible interaction sets. [6] The results showed that "stability" has several meanings and that the arrangement and strength of interactions matter as much as a species count.

From the 1990s onward, controlled biodiversity experiments and cross-study syntheses clarified the debate. Research distinguished taxonomic richness from functional traits, functional diversity, and response diversity. A species can influence productivity, decomposition, nutrient retention, or disturbance response because of what it does and how it responds, not merely because it adds one unit to a richness total. Loreau and colleagues grouped the mechanisms behind biodiversity effects into complementarity and selection (dominance of species with particular traits, with a sampling component), and described an insurance hypothesis in which species respond differently to environmental fluctuations, so that aggregate properties become more predictable. [7] Hooper and colleagues found broad consensus that species' functional characteristics strongly influence ecosystem properties and that changes in composition can alter goods and services. [8]

Long-term experiments and theory then tested the diversity-stability hypothesis directly. Loreau and colleagues reported that the variability of aggregate ecosystem processes decreases as diversity increases, even though the variability of individual populations may increase because of strong species interactions, and they noted that interpretation was complicated by factors that covary with richness, such as soil nitrogen. [7] By 2012, Cardinale and colleagues concluded that total resource capture and biomass production are generally more stable in more diverse communities, through over-yielding, statistical averaging, and compensatory dynamics, while stressing that there is no theoretical reason to expect biodiversity to enhance all forms of stability. [10] Syntheses also linked biodiversity loss to changes in productivity, resource use, decomposition, and the reliability of ecosystem services. [9][10] Chapin and colleagues found no universal relationship between species richness and ecosystem functioning in experimental communities, and Hooper and colleagues noted that responses to varying the diversity of consumers are much more complex than responses to varying only primary producers. [8][9]

Ecosystem science now connects these classical ideas to Earth-system change. The IPBES Global Assessment evaluates biodiversity, ecosystem functions, and nature's contributions to people together rather than as separate subjects. [12] The IPCC assesses climate impacts on ecosystem structure, functioning, resilience, terrestrial carbon stocks, and ecosystem services, and reports that non-climatic hazards such as land-use change, habitat fragmentation, pollution, and invasive species have been the primary drivers of change in terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems in the past, while the relative impact of climate change is increasing, with greater interactive effects of non-climate and climate hazards now occurring. [13] The resulting discipline is neither only organismal biology nor only geophysics. It is the study of how biological interactions transform flows of energy and matter within a changing physical planet.

## Core Concepts

### Ecosystem boundaries are analytical, not absolute

An ecosystem includes a biotic community, its physical environment, and the exchanges connecting them. The boundary may be a pond shoreline, a watershed divide, a forest plot, an estuary, or the entire biosphere. The correct boundary depends on the process and timescale being studied. Lindeman's definition allows a space-time unit of any magnitude. [2] A lake can be treated as a unit for measuring primary production, but Odum argued that for problems such as eutrophication, which results when nutrients are imported into a lake from its watershed, the entire drainage or catchment basin must be considered the ecosystem unit. [3] The watershed-ecosystem approach developed at Hubbard Brook in the 1960s defined a bounded system in which inputs and outputs of materials and energy could be measured and on which experiments could be imposed. [14]

This boundary choice determines the accounting. A stock is the amount of material or energy stored in a compartment, such as plant biomass, soil carbon, dissolved phosphorus, or detritus. A flux is a rate of movement or transformation, such as photosynthesis, herbivory, respiration, leaching, or nitrogen fixation. A large stock need not imply a large flux, and a small rapidly turning-over pool can control an important process. Historically, ecosystem studies proceeded from simple descriptions of the amounts of energy or nutrients in an area, through measurement of rates of flow, to analysis of the regulation of those flows. [14] The author's synthesis is that ecosystem descriptions should always state the boundary, major compartments, fluxes, timescale, and unmeasured exchanges; otherwise, apparent balances may be artifacts of what was left outside the frame.

### Energy flows while matter cycles

Most ecosystems are powered by solar energy captured by photosynthetic organisms, although chemosynthetic communities use chemical energy. Primary producers convert external energy into organic matter. Gross primary production is the total rate of carbon fixation; net primary production is what remains after producers' respiration and is available for growth, storage, detritus, and consumers. Heterotrophs obtain chemical energy by consuming living or dead organic matter, and respiration at every trophic level releases energy as heat. Because usable energy is dissipated during each transformation, energy moves directionally through ecosystems and requires continuing input. [2][15] Energy is dissipated in its transfer through biological systems, whereas the elements that make up organisms are conserved, and the dissipation of energy drives the circulation of elements. [14]

Trophic levels simplify this flow into producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and higher consumers. That hierarchy is useful, but real feeding relations form networks rather than single chains. Omnivores feed at more than one level, organisms change diets across life stages, and detrital pathways connect waste and dead biomass to decomposers and consumers. In Paine's Gulf of California subweb, two gastropod species grow too large to be eaten by the top-ranking starfish and so join it as top predators, which complicates assigning species to fixed trophic levels (interpretation). [4] Odum noted that in a mature forest less than 10 percent of annual net production is grazed in the living state, most being used as dead matter through detritus pathways. [3] Energy pyramids usually narrow toward higher trophic levels because energy is lost as heat in each transfer. [15] Transfer efficiency is not a universal fixed percentage. Lindeman's progressive efficiencies for Cedar Bog Lake rose from about 0.10 percent for producers (relative to incident radiation) to 13.3 percent for primary consumers and 22.3 percent for secondary consumers. [2] The Silver Springs productivities of 20,819, 3,368, 383, and 21 kcal per square meter per year [15] correspond to ratios of about 16 percent, 11 percent, and 5.5 percent between successive levels (calculated here from the cited values).

Matter behaves differently. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and water are repeatedly transformed and moved among atmosphere, hydrosphere, rocks, soils, organisms, and detritus. Photosynthesis moves carbon into organic matter; respiration and decomposition return it to inorganic forms; burial can store it for long periods; combustion and volcanism can return stored carbon to the atmosphere. Nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen into biologically available compounds, while microbial transformations and denitrification move nitrogen among chemical forms and back to the atmosphere. Phosphorus is mobilized mainly from rocks and sediments, passes through food webs, and can be retained, exported, or buried. [15]

These cycles are coupled. The NRC summary states that biological processes tend toward defined ratios of the elements they use, so that alterations in the availability of one element have consequences for ecosystem functioning. [14] Primary production and decomposition are often limited by the supply of nitrogen, and phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in aquatic ecosystems. [15] Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer runoff and sewage cause excessive growth of microorganisms and depletion of dissolved oxygen, which kills fauna in dead zones. [15] Odum described nutrient input from the watershed as pushing a lake back to a younger, bloom-type state. [3] Water movement carries nitrogen and phosphate into rivers, lakes, and oceans, and the ocean is a major carbon reservoir. [15] The author's interpretation is that soils, wetlands, and organisms therefore act not as passive containers but as processors that regulate the form, residence time, and destination of elements. Related library topics are `library/earth-climate/carbon-cycle-greenhouse-effect.md` and `library/earth-climate/oceanography-marine-systems.md`.

### Food webs transmit bottom-up and top-down effects

Bottom-up control begins with resource supply. Light, water, temperature, and nutrients constrain primary producers; changes in production can then affect herbivores and predators. Top-down control begins with consumers. Predators can suppress prey, release lower trophic levels from consumption, and alter habitat structure through trophic cascades. Chapin and colleagues describe cases in which removing top predators released prey: removal of sea otters allowed sea urchins to overgraze kelp, and removal of bass from phosphorus-fertilized lakes increased minnows, reduced phytoplankton grazers, and produced algal blooms. [9] These directions are not mutually exclusive. Loreau and colleagues note that top-down control is often thought to be more common in freshwater than in terrestrial ecosystems. [7] Their relative importance varies across ecosystems and disturbances (author's synthesis).

Interaction strength is central. Hooper and colleagues list several contexts in which species' functional characteristics operate: effects of dominant species, keystone species, and ecological engineers, and interactions among species such as competition, facilitation, mutualism, disease, and predation. They add that relative abundance alone is not always a good predictor of the ecosystem-level importance of a species, as even relatively rare species, such as a keystone predator, can strongly influence pathways of energy and material flows. [8] Paine's sea star, which kept space open by feeding on barnacles and mussels, is the experimental case. [4] These categories describe different mechanisms and should not be treated as synonyms (author's note).

Indirect effects make prediction difficult. In Paine's removal area, the sponge-nudibranch food chain was displaced and the anemone population declined, although Pisaster eats neither the sponge nor those carnivores, which indicated to Paine that the number of food chains initiated on the limited space was influenced by the starfish through an indirect process. [4] Chapin and colleagues report that the algal blooms that followed bass removal turned a lake from a net source to a net sink of carbon dioxide, so that biotic change and altered nutrient cycles can interact to influence whole-system carbon balance. [9] Mutualisms are essential for many processes such as nitrogen inputs and mycorrhizal nutrient uptake, and because many mutualistic interactions are highly specific, the loss of a given species can have cascading effects on the rest of the system. [9] The impact of losing a species thus depends on its partners, interaction strengths, and functional role, not only on its rarity.

### Succession is process change after disturbance

Succession describes changes in community composition and ecosystem processes after a new substrate forms or a disturbance removes organisms. Primary succession begins where biological legacies are sparse, such as newly exposed rock. Secondary succession begins where soil, seeds, roots, microorganisms, or other legacies remain. The distinction matters because the starting soil differs: Connell and Slatyer predicted that facilitation would commonly apply to primary successions, in which newly exposed soils, for example behind retreating glaciers, have extreme properties that make invasion difficult, and to heterotrophic successions in logs and carcasses, whereas tolerance and inhibition would apply to most secondary successions, in which the soils have already supported plants. [5]

Connell and Slatyer's three mechanisms explain why sequences differ. Under facilitation, early species modify conditions in ways that help later species. Under tolerance, later species can invade regardless of early occupants and eventually prevail because they tolerate lower resource levels or other conditions. Under inhibition, established occupants suppress later colonists until death, damage, or another disturbance releases space. In all three models certain species appear first because they have colonizing characteristics, such as many well-dispersed propagules and rapid growth, and are poorly adapted to establishing in occupied sites, so that their offspring seldom survive in the presence of adults; the models differ in the mechanisms that determine how later species appear. [5] Different mechanisms may apply in different systems and stages (author's synthesis).

Succession changes function as well as composition. Biomass, production, respiration, decomposition, nutrient retention, vertical structure, and food-web organization may all change. Odum noted that very few of the generally accepted hypotheses about ecosystem development had been tested experimentally and that one may question whether all the trends are characteristic of all types of ecosystems. [3] Connell and Slatyer argued that many of the proposed trends follow from the passage of time, for example because trees take time to grow and biomass therefore accumulates, rather than from internal control. [5] A forest, grassland, reef, lake, or post-fire landscape may follow several trajectories, including long transients or alternative regimes, as Holling's domains of attraction illustrate. [6] The author's synthesis is that restoration should specify the desired processes and tolerable trajectories instead of assuming that time alone will recreate a historical endpoint.

### Stability contains several different properties

Holling distinguished two properties. Stability is the ability of a system to return to an equilibrium state after a temporary disturbance: the more rapidly it returns, and the less it fluctuates, the more stable it is. Resilience is the persistence of relationships within a system, measured by its ability to absorb changes of state variables, driving variables, and parameters and still persist. [6] A system can be very resilient and still fluctuate greatly; Holling's example is the spruce budworm and its forest community, whose large fluctuations are bound up with the persistence of the system. [6] Loreau and colleagues note that the general concept of "stability" covers a wide array of properties, and that the relationship between these properties and diversity may change across levels of organization, so that large variability at the population level need not imply large variability of ecosystem processes. [7] Cardinale and colleagues likewise note that numerous forms of stability have been described and that there is no theoretical reason to expect biodiversity to enhance all of them. [10] These properties can disagree: a system can maintain aggregate production while its species composition changes, or fluctuate widely while persisting.

Feedbacks help explain regime behavior. Holling described the behavior of predator-prey models as an interplay between stabilizing negative feedback, such as density-dependent responses to resources and predation, and destabilizing effects, such as predator satiation, which produces inverse density dependence (positive feedback). Realistic fecundity and mortality curves can yield several equilibria separated by extinction and escape thresholds, so that a system has distinct domains of attraction rather than a single global equilibrium. [6] Field examples fit this pattern: Great Lakes stocks of lake sturgeon, lake herring, and lake whitefish that were intensively fished dropped precipitously and did not return to previous abundance when fishing pressure was relaxed, and in arid grazing lands of the western United States, once shrubs and trees had grown large and dense enough to use the moisture supply, eliminating grazing did not re-establish grass; return required explicit reduction of the woody plants. [6] Reversing the original pressure therefore may not restore the former state, which Holling's distinction between resilience and stability was designed to capture. [6]

Threshold language must be used carefully. A nonlinear response or rapid transition does not by itself prove that a universal tipping point exists. Demonstrating a regime shift requires evidence about state variables, feedbacks, alternative dynamics, and the scale of observation. The author's assessment is that ecosystem management should treat suspected thresholds as risk ranges rather than precise numbers unless repeated observations or experiments establish a defensible boundary. Holling noted that his sample of examples was inevitably biased, that few cases were well documented over a long period, and that some greatly disturbed systems had fully recovered, mostly open systems in which reinvasion was the key ingredient. [6]

### Diversity affects function through traits, complementarity, and response variation

Species richness counts species, but ecosystem function depends more directly on their traits, abundances, and interactions. Functional effect traits influence processes such as production, decomposition, pollination, predation, or nutrient acquisition. Functional response traits determine how organisms react to drought, warming, disturbance, disease, or other pressures. Species can share an effect trait yet differ in response traits, providing response diversity that helps preserve a function when conditions change. [7][8][9]

Complementarity occurs when species use resources, places, or times differently, raising collective resource capture or process rates. Selection occurs when a more diverse assemblage is more likely to contain a species with a large functional effect. Statistical averaging or portfolio effects arise when species fluctuate asynchronously, reducing variability in their aggregate function. None of these mechanisms implies that every added species contributes equally or that richness always improves every function. Composition, trophic structure, environmental constraints, and the function measured can change both the magnitude and direction of an effect. [7][8][10]

This resolves part of the diversity-stability debate (interpretation). May's random-network result concerns local stability, the tendency of a system to return to equilibrium after small perturbations, in abstract interaction matrices. [11] The grassland data concern the temporal variability of aggregate plant biomass. [7] Both can be correct because they test different systems and definitions. Network theory further shows that predator-prey, competitive, and mutualistic structures have different stability criteria, so complexity cannot be reduced to species count or connectance alone. [11]

## Evidence

### Whole-system energy accounting

Lindeman's trophic-dynamic framework treated Cedar Bog Lake, Minnesota, as a system of energy transfers among trophic levels. He treated succession as the process of ecosystem development brought about primarily by the effects of the organisms on the environment and on each other, with productivity rising rapidly in the early phases and much more slowly in later phases. The method combined observations and available measurements into an energy-budget representation rather than treating species occurrences as sufficient evidence of function. Its enduring finding was methodological: production, consumption, respiration, and decomposition can be compared in common energetic units, revealing limits and losses that a food-species list cannot show. His progressive efficiencies for Cedar Bog Lake, about 0.10 percent for producers relative to incident radiation, 13.3 percent for primary consumers, and 22.3 percent for secondary consumers, illustrate such a comparison. [2]

Howard T. Odum's Silver Springs study provided a field-scale demonstration. Measurements and estimates were used to compare the energy embodied in producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and tertiary consumers, at about 20,819, 3,368, 383, and 21 kcal per square meter per year. The energy content declined sharply at successive consumer levels, supporting the use of production and transfer efficiency to explain why upper trophic levels contain less available energy than the producer base. [15] The case does not establish one universal efficiency for every ecosystem, but it validates the broader prediction that trophic structure is constrained by energetic transformation and dissipation.

These studies also established a measurement discipline. A credible energy budget must define the system boundary, time interval, production units, unconsumed material, detrital flow, and respiration. If detritus or microbial processing is omitted, much of the ecosystem's functional activity disappears from the model. The author's synthesis is that the most important legacy of trophic energetics is not a textbook pyramid but a requirement to balance inputs, transfers, storage, and losses explicitly. [2][14][15]

### Predator removal and trophic control

Paine's 1966 rocky-intertidal experiment tested top-down control through manipulation rather than correlation. Since June 1963 he had kept a typical piece of shoreline at Mukkaw Bay, about 8 m long and 2 m in vertical extent, free of the sea star Pisaster, while an adjacent control area was allowed to follow its natural course. Line transects measured the number and density of resident macroinvertebrate and benthic algal species in both areas. In the predator's absence, barnacles occupied 60 to 80 percent of the available space within months, were then crowded out by mussels and goose-necked barnacles, benthic algae largely disappeared, and species richness fell from 15 to 8. [4] The experiment linked one changed interaction to a sequence of competitive and community consequences, supporting the idea that a top predator can keep a food web diverse by preventing space monopolies.

The importance of this case lies in its causal design. Observational association between predators and diversity could reflect habitat differences, productivity, or other confounders. Removal changed one focal component and followed the response through time. The design has limits that Paine acknowledged: it consisted of one removal area and one adjacent control, and the events had not reached an equilibrium. [4] Carnivorous snails (Thais) increased 10- to 20-fold in the removal area with no apparent effect on diversity, because snails do not clear large patches of space as the starfish does. [4] The result does not mean that every predator is a keystone or that predator removal always lowers diversity. It is consistent with the conclusion of Hooper and colleagues that relative abundance alone is not always a good predictor of a species' ecosystem-level importance. [8]

### Succession mechanisms and interrupted trajectories

Odum's synthesis compared ecosystem properties across developmental stages and proposed that succession reorganizes production, respiration, nutrient cycling, and community structure. [3] Connell and Slatyer reviewed natural succession studies and translated competing explanations into three testable interaction models: facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition. They emphasized field manipulations of early and late species as the appropriate way to distinguish mechanisms and noted that disturbance often restarts succession before a late sequence is completed. They judged that controlled field experiments would test the models much better than the observational evidence usually offered, and they reported that trenching experiments indicated that later species grow better when early species are absent in many forests in the intermediate stages of succession, a result that rejects the facilitation and tolerance models for those cases. [5]

The models explain why similar disturbances can produce different outcomes. If early colonists improve soil or microclimate, removing them can slow later establishment; if they inhibit later species, removal can accelerate it; if later species are tolerant, the effect may be weak. A chronological series by itself cannot identify which mechanism operated because age, environment, dispersal, and species interactions change together. The evidentiary lesson is that succession is best inferred through repeated observations, experiments, and process measurements rather than by substituting space for time without testing assumptions. [3][5]

### Controlled biodiversity experiments

Controlled biodiversity experiments set species number by planting rather than inferring its effect from natural gradients. The Cedar Creek Biodiversity II experiment (E120) in Minnesota was planted in 1994 and manipulates the number of perennial grassland plant species in 168 plots of 9 m by 9 m, with 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16 species drawn at random from a pool of 16 species in four functional groups (C4 grasses, C3 grasses, legumes, and other forbs), with 29 to 39 replicate plots at each level. [16] For temporal stability, Loreau and colleagues reported Minnesota data from experimental and natural grasslands in which the adjusted coefficient of temporal variation of annual total plant biomass over 11 years decreased as plant species richness increased, but they cautioned that soil nitrogen covaried with richness in those plots; the diversity effect nevertheless remained significant after controlling for potentially confounding variables. [7] They called for experiments in which both diversity and environmental fluctuations are controlled to test the insurance hypothesis rigorously. [7] Cardinale and colleagues' synthesis identifies over-yielding, statistical averaging, and compensatory dynamics as the mechanisms by which diversity confers temporal stability on community properties such as total biomass. [10]

The method matters because in natural gradients diversity is correlated with soil fertility, climate, disturbance regime, and other factors that also influence productivity, which is why the planted designs were developed. [7] The stability pattern described by Loreau and colleagues and by Cardinale and colleagues supports the hypothesis that asynchronous responses and higher aggregate production can buffer an ecosystem-level function. [7][10] It is not proof that richness stabilizes all functions or trophic levels. The evidence concerns plant production in grassland plots, and extrapolation to decomposition, predation, aquatic food webs, or whole landscapes requires additional evidence. Hooper and colleagues explicitly identified multitrophic diversity as an area needing further work and noted that theoretical work on stability has outpaced experimental work, especially field research, so that long-term experiments are needed. [8]

Broader syntheses supply that context. Loreau and colleagues reviewed experiments and theory and concluded that biodiversity can affect productivity, nutrient use, and temporal variability through multiple mechanisms, while calling for stronger integration of biodiversity dynamics, ecosystem processes, and abiotic factors. [7] Hooper and colleagues reached consensus that functional characteristics, composition, and abundance strongly influence ecosystem properties, and that species responding differently to perturbations can stabilize process rates. [8] Chapin and colleagues connected biodiversity change to ecosystem processes and resilience, while Cardinale and colleagues reviewed two decades of evidence on how biodiversity loss changes ecosystem functioning and services. [9][10]

### Network theory and the complexity-stability problem

May analyzed mathematical systems in which species interactions were assigned at random. In the community-matrix formulation, with self-regulation set to -1 on the diagonal and off-diagonal coefficients drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation sigma with probability C, he showed that for large S the probability of local stability is close to zero whenever sigma times the square root of S times C exceeds 1. [11] The result clashed with the prevailing belief of ecologists that large, highly complex ecosystems are more stable than simpler ones. [11] Holling reported May's conclusion in the same terms: randomly assembled complex systems are in general less stable, and never more stable, than less complex ones. [6] The result also clarified the need to compare like definitions: local stability measures the tendency to return to equilibrium after small perturbations, which is not identical to persistence, resistance, recovery, or low variability.

Allesina and Tang revisited the problem by separating interaction types. They found that predator-prey interactions increase stability whereas competitive and mutualistic interactions are destabilizing, and derived analytic criteria for each case: stable predator-prey systems can be much larger and more complex than random ones (the criterion becomes sigma times the square root of S times C below about 2.75 rather than below 1), while a mixture of competition and mutualism is far less stable (below about 0.61). [11] Contrary to expectation, imposing realistic food-web structure (cascade and niche models), a large preponderance of weak interactions, or nestedness in mutualistic networks decreased stability in their comparisons; they concluded that realistic structure alone does not contribute to stability, and that stability depends on the interplay between network structure and interaction strengths. [11] These results explain part of why natural systems can persist despite May's random-network warning: real interactions are not random, and their signs, strengths, and architecture matter. They also prevent an opposite overstatement. Observed diversity-function benefits do not license the claim that every additional link always stabilizes every ecosystem property.

### Global assessments of ecosystem change

IPBES assembled a global assessment of biodiversity, ecosystem functions, and nature's contributions to people in a report and its summary for policymakers. [12] The summary reports that the average abundance of native species in most major terrestrial biomes has fallen by at least 20 percent, potentially affecting ecosystem processes and hence nature's contributions to people, and ranks the direct drivers with the largest global impact, in descending order, as changes in land and sea use, direct exploitation of organisms, climate change, pollution, and invasion of alien species. [12] The assessment expands the unit of concern from isolated species losses to changes in ecological relationships and functional capacity (interpretation).

The IPCC Working Group II assessment applies a similar systems approach to climate risk. Chapter 2 provides a global assessment of observed impacts and projected risks of climate change to terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems, including their component species and the services they provide to people. It states that species are the fundamental unit of ecosystems: the increasing risk of local species loss increases the risks of reduced ecosystem integrity, functioning, and resilience with increasing warming, as species become rare their role in ecosystem functioning diminishes, and local species loss reduces the ability of an ecosystem to provide services and lowers its resilience to climate change (each statement carries high confidence). [13] It reports that non-climatic hazards, including land-use change, habitat fragmentation, pollution, and invasive species, have been the primary drivers of change in terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems in the past, and that climate change, although not the predominant influence to date, has an increasing relative impact, with greater interactive effects of non-climate and climate hazards now occurring. [13] The chapter also emphasizes an attribution limit: it is difficult to establish full end-to-end attribution from climatic changes to changes in a given ecosystem service, and it found limited research on observed impacts of climate change for 14 of 18 ecosystem services. [13] That caution is evidence discipline, not evidence absence.

Together, the experimental, theoretical, and assessment literatures support a bounded conclusion. Energy flow, material cycling, interactions, composition, and disturbance jointly determine ecosystem function; no single metric captures all of them. Diversity often improves the magnitude or reliability of functions through identifiable mechanisms, but outcomes depend on traits, network structure, environmental context, scale, and the definition of stability. [7][8][9][10][11][12][13]

## Implications

### Ecosystem diagnosis should follow mechanisms, not labels

For researchers, the first implication is methodological. Calling a place a forest, wetland, reef, grassland, or degraded system does not identify how it functions. A useful diagnosis specifies stocks and rates: primary production, respiration, decomposition, nutrient retention, hydrologic exchange, biomass, species interactions, and variability through time. It also identifies the physical drivers and system boundary. [2][13][14][15] Two sites with similar species counts can differ in functional traits and process rates; two sites with different species composition can maintain a similar aggregate function for a period. Measurement must therefore match the question.

A second implication is that food-web position and interaction strength deserve explicit attention. Monitoring only abundant species can miss keystone predators, pollinators, decomposers, pathogens, or engineers whose effects propagate indirectly. Paine's experiment demonstrates why causal interventions or natural experiments are valuable: they reveal whether an association represents a regulating interaction. [4][8] Where manipulation is unsafe or impossible, convergent evidence from time series, spatial comparisons, stable isotopes, diet data, and process models can test the same mechanism.

A third implication concerns scale. Nutrient retention in a plot, carbon storage in a soil profile, productivity in a lake, and resilience across a landscape operate at different spatial and temporal scales. A short experiment can identify a mechanism without capturing slow succession, rare disturbance, dispersal, or long-lived organisms. Conversely, a global assessment can establish broad patterns while obscuring local pathways. The author's assessment is that robust ecosystem inference should combine process experiments with long-term and cross-scale observation rather than demand that one method answer every question. [7][12][13]

### Conservation should protect interactions and response diversity

For conservation practitioners, preventing extinction remains necessary but is not sufficient to preserve ecosystem function. Functional composition, interaction networks, and response diversity determine whether production, decomposition, pollination, predation, carbon storage, or water regulation persist under change. [8][9][10] Protecting several species that perform similar functions but respond differently to drought, heat, disease, or disturbance can preserve options when conditions shift. That apparent redundancy is insurance, not waste.

This does not imply that species are interchangeable. A dominant producer, foundation species, ecosystem engineer, or keystone consumer can have a unique effect. Removing it may produce a response that other species do not replace. [4][8] Prioritization should therefore consider functional uniqueness, interaction strength, vulnerability, and the consequences of loss, while avoiding unsupported claims that every rare species has a known irreplaceable function. Uncertainty itself has value implications: irreversible losses reduce future ecological options before all functions are understood. Hooper and colleagues make this precaution explicit in their synthesis. [8]

Connectivity also follows from the systems view. Organisms, water, nutrients, sediment, and disturbances move across local boundaries. Isolated protected patches may retain species yet lose migration routes, hydrologic processes, fire regimes, or upstream water quality. The IPCC chapter reports that protection and restoration of ecosystems builds resilience of service provision, and notes that globally over two-thirds of river reaches by length lack protected areas in their upstream catchments. [13] The author's synthesis is that conservation units should be designed around functional landscapes and seascapes, not only around static inventories inside administrative lines.

### Restoration should rebuild processes and feedbacks

For restoration managers, succession theory argues against passive assumptions. Time since disturbance is not a mechanism. Early colonists may facilitate, tolerate, or inhibit later species, and surviving soils, seed banks, mutualists, herbivores, and hydrologic structure can determine the trajectory. [3][5] A restoration plan should state which processes are missing, which feedbacks maintain the degraded state, and which intervention could change them.

Examples of process targets include restoring water flow before planting wetland vegetation, rebuilding decomposer and soil communities before expecting nutrient retention, controlling a competitively dominant invader before adding late-successional species, or re-establishing a consumer that releases vegetation from excessive herbivory. These are generalized applications of succession and food-web mechanisms rather than universal prescriptions. Site evidence is required to identify which feedback is active. [4][5][8]

Success criteria should likewise be multidimensional. Survival of planted species is not proof that an ecosystem has recovered production, trophic structure, nutrient cycling, resistance, or resilience. A restored site may resemble a reference site in composition while exporting nutrients or failing to regenerate. Conversely, composition may differ under a changed climate while key functions persist. The appropriate comparison depends on the objective, but reporting both composition and process reduces the risk of declaring success from one visible metric. [6][13]

### Climate analysis must include ecological feedbacks

For Earth-system scientists, ecosystems are active components of climate and biogeochemical cycles. Vegetation and soils exchange carbon, water, and energy with the atmosphere; aquatic systems transform and transport nutrients; the ocean is a major carbon reservoir; disturbance can change a sink into a source. [9][13][15] The IPCC chapter states that land cover changes affect regional and global climate through changes to albedo, evapotranspiration, and roughness. [13] Climate affects ecosystems, and ecosystem reorganization can alter climate-relevant fluxes. Related library topics are `library/earth-climate/carbon-cycle-greenhouse-effect.md` and `library/earth-climate/oceanography-marine-systems.md`.

This coupling means that climate exposure alone does not determine ecological outcome. Warming, drought, altered fire, ocean chemistry, nutrient supply, land use, and exploitation interact with species traits and food webs. The same temperature anomaly can produce different functional effects where soils, water, species composition, or disturbance history differ. [9][13] The IPCC chapter accordingly states that it is difficult to establish full end-to-end attribution from climatic changes to changes in a given ecosystem service. [13]

Biodiversity can influence this response through complementarity and response variation, but it is not a substitute for reducing environmental pressure. More species cannot guarantee stability if disturbance exceeds physiological limits, eliminates habitat, or reorganizes key feedbacks. Conversely, simplified systems may maintain one optimized output while becoming more vulnerable to a different stress. [8][9][10] The author's assessment is that climate-risk models should test multiple functions and disturbances instead of treating present productivity as a complete proxy for resilience.

### Resource and organizational decisions should recognize ecosystem dependence

For organizations that depend on land, water, fisheries, forests, or biological production, ecosystem function is an operational dependency rather than an external label. Water purification, soil formation, nutrient retention, pollination, pest regulation, coastal protection, carbon storage, and production emerge from ecological processes and interactions. IPBES assesses these as nature's contributions to people, and the IPCC cautions that end-to-end attribution from climate change to a given ecosystem service is often difficult. [12][13]

A defensible dependency assessment traces a pathway: the activity depends on a function; the function depends on organisms, physical conditions, and interactions; identified pressures alter those components; and monitoring can detect change before failure. This is more informative than assigning one monetary value to "nature" without a causal model. The author's synthesis is that scenario analysis should ask which process can fail, what substitutes exist, how long recovery takes, and whether a threshold or irreversible loss is plausible.

The worst analytical error is to equate stable output with a healthy system. Fertilizer, irrigation, stocking, or other inputs can temporarily maintain a product while soils, food webs, water quality, or response diversity decline. A narrow output metric may therefore conceal transferred costs or reduced resilience. Odum noted that the high fertility of young, open-cycle systems cannot be maintained without compensating inputs of new nutrients, such as the fertilizer imported into intensive agriculture. [3] Holling noted that the goal of a maximum sustained yield may result in a more stable system of reduced resilience, and the IPBES summary states that prioritizing one contribution of nature, such as food production, can reduce others. [6][12] The preventive rule is to pair production indicators with measures of supporting stocks, fluxes, diversity, and disturbance response.

### Claims about stability require explicit definitions

Finally, ecosystem communication should name the stability property being claimed. "Stable" may mean low variance, resistance to a shock, rapid recovery, persistence of species, persistence of function, or remaining within a regime. These are not interchangeable. [6][7][10][11] Stating the variable, disturbance, reference condition, spatial scale, and time horizon converts a vague claim into a testable one.

The same discipline applies to diversity claims. Evidence supports many positive effects of biodiversity on ecosystem functions and their reliability, but it does not support a context-free slogan that more species always make every system stable. [7][8][9][10][11] Functional traits, response differences, trophic position, interaction signs and strengths, resource conditions, and the selected endpoint all matter. The scientifically useful conclusion is conditional: biodiversity can support function and resilience through identifiable mechanisms, and those mechanisms must be measured where decisions depend on them.

The author's synthesis is that ecosystem science is most powerful as an accounting and causal framework. It links energy input, material cycles, biological interactions, disturbance, and scale; it then asks which observations could falsify the proposed mechanism. This approach connects the carbon cycle, biodiversity loss, ocean change, and resource depletion without collapsing them into one undifferentiated environmental problem.
