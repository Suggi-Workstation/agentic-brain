# Ecosystems Function Through Coupled Energy, Matter, and Interaction Networks

An ecosystem is not merely a list of species in a place; it is a functional system in which organisms and the physical environment exchange energy and matter through interacting biological, chemical, and physical processes. Ecosystem science matters because disturbances to one process, such as primary production, predation, decomposition, or nutrient supply, can propagate through food webs and biogeochemical cycles, changing the stability and services of the whole system. [2, 9, 14, 15]

## Background

Ecology began by asking how organisms are distributed and how they respond to their surroundings, but ecosystem science required a further conceptual move: organisms and the nonliving environment had to be analyzed as one system. In 1935, A. G. Tansley introduced the term "ecosystem" while criticizing concepts that treated a biological community as if it developed independently of soils, climate, and physical forces. His formulation made the organism-environment complex, rather than the community alone, the fundamental unit of analysis. [1] The National Research Council later summarized the same research program as the study of flows of energy and materials among groups of organisms and between biological communities and the abiotic environment. [16]

That systems view acquired a quantitative foundation through Raymond Lindeman's 1942 analysis of trophic dynamics. Lindeman organized organisms into trophic levels according to how they obtain energy and treated production, consumption, respiration, decomposition, and loss as measurable transfers. His work shifted food-chain description toward energy accounting: the important question was not only who eats whom, but how much energy enters a level, how much becomes biomass, and how much is dissipated before reaching the next level. [2] Howard T. Odum's later measurements at Silver Springs, Florida, made that approach concrete by estimating energy content and transfer among producers and successive consumer levels in a whole spring ecosystem. [17]

Eugene Odum extended this functional approach to ecosystem development. His 1969 synthesis described succession as an organized sequence of changes in production, respiration, nutrient retention, food-web structure, and other system properties after a new surface appears or a disturbance resets a community. [3] Later theory rejected the idea that all ecosystems follow one fixed march toward a single climax. Connell and Slatyer instead distinguished facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition as alternative mechanisms by which early colonists affect later species. They also emphasized that recurrent disturbance can interrupt or redirect succession. [5] Succession is therefore a contingent reorganization of organisms and processes, not a universal ladder with one predetermined endpoint.

Food-web research added a second challenge to simple succession stories. Robert Paine's rocky-intertidal removal experiment showed that a predator can have an effect on community diversity disproportionate to its abundance. Removing the sea star Pisaster allowed a competitively dominant mussel to monopolize space and reduced local diversity, establishing the experimental basis for the keystone-species concept. [4] The case demonstrated that ecosystem function cannot be inferred from biomass alone. A relatively uncommon consumer may regulate competition, habitat occupancy, and indirect effects across several trophic levels.

Ecologists also debated whether complexity makes ecosystems stable. Early arguments proposed that more species and more alternative energy pathways should buffer fluctuations. Robert May showed that randomly assembled interaction networks become less likely to be locally stable as species number, connectance, and average interaction strength increase. [6] C. S. Holling then separated engineering stability, meaning rapid return toward an equilibrium, from ecological resilience, meaning persistence of relationships and processes despite disturbance and possible movement among regimes. [7] These results did not prove that diversity destabilizes nature. They showed that "stability" has several meanings and that the arrangement and strength of interactions matter as much as a species count.

From the 1990s onward, controlled biodiversity experiments and cross-study syntheses clarified the debate. Research distinguished taxonomic richness from functional traits, functional diversity, and response diversity. A species can influence productivity, decomposition, nutrient retention, or disturbance response because of what it does and how it responds, not merely because it adds one unit to a richness total. Loreau and colleagues concluded that biodiversity effects arise through mechanisms such as complementarity, selection, and temporal insurance, while Hooper and colleagues found broad consensus that species' functional characteristics strongly influence ecosystem properties and that changes in composition can alter goods and services. [8, 9]

Long-term experiments then supplied direct evidence that diversity can stabilize aggregate ecosystem function even when individual populations fluctuate. Tilman, Reich, and Knops followed experimental grassland plots for a decade and found that plots with more plant species had more stable annual aboveground production. The stabilizing mechanisms included statistical averaging and greater production, not universal stability of each constituent species. [10] Later syntheses linked biodiversity loss to changes in productivity, resource use, decomposition, and the reliability of ecosystem services, while also warning that effects vary among functions, trophic levels, and environmental contexts. [11, 12]

Ecosystem science now connects these classical ideas to Earth-system change. The IPBES Global Assessment evaluates biodiversity, ecosystem functions, and nature's contributions to people together rather than as separate subjects. [14] The IPCC assesses climate impacts on ecosystem structure, functioning, resilience, carbon storage, freshwater regulation, and other services, and reports that climate change increasingly interacts with land use, exploitation, pollution, and invasive species. [15] The resulting discipline is neither only organismal biology nor only geophysics. It is the study of how biological interactions transform flows of energy and matter within a changing physical planet.

## Core Concepts

### Ecosystem boundaries are analytical, not absolute

An ecosystem includes a biotic community, its physical environment, and the exchanges connecting them. The boundary may be a pond shoreline, a watershed divide, a forest plot, an estuary, or the entire biosphere. The correct boundary depends on the process and timescale being studied. A lake can be treated as a unit for measuring primary production, but migratory animals, groundwater, atmospheric deposition, and inflowing rivers cross its perimeter. A forest plot can reveal local decomposition while missing nutrients imported by animals or lost downstream. [16, 17]

This boundary choice determines the accounting. A stock is the amount of material or energy stored in a compartment, such as plant biomass, soil carbon, dissolved phosphorus, or detritus. A flux is a rate of movement or transformation, such as photosynthesis, herbivory, respiration, leaching, or nitrogen fixation. A large stock need not imply a large flux, and a small rapidly turning-over pool can control an important process. The author's synthesis is that ecosystem descriptions should always state the boundary, major compartments, fluxes, timescale, and unmeasured exchanges; otherwise, apparent balances may be artifacts of what was left outside the frame. [2, 15, 17]

### Energy flows while matter cycles

Most ecosystems are powered by solar energy captured by photosynthetic organisms, although chemosynthetic communities use chemical energy. Primary producers convert external energy into organic matter. Gross primary production is the total rate of carbon fixation; net primary production is what remains after producers' respiration and is available for growth, storage, detritus, and consumers. Heterotrophs obtain chemical energy by consuming living or dead organic matter, and respiration at every trophic level releases energy as heat. Because usable energy is dissipated during each transformation, energy moves directionally through ecosystems and requires continuing input. [2, 17]

Trophic levels simplify this flow into producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and higher consumers. That hierarchy is useful, but real feeding relations form networks rather than single chains. Omnivores feed at more than one level, organisms change diets across life stages, and detrital pathways connect waste and dead biomass to decomposers and consumers. Energy pyramids usually narrow toward higher trophic levels because only part of consumed energy is assimilated and only part of assimilated energy becomes new biomass. Transfer efficiency is not a universal fixed percentage; it varies with food quality, consumer physiology, temperature, and ecosystem type. [2, 17]

Matter behaves differently. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and water are repeatedly transformed and moved among atmosphere, hydrosphere, rocks, soils, organisms, and detritus. Photosynthesis moves carbon into organic matter; respiration and decomposition return it to inorganic forms; burial can store it for long periods; combustion and volcanism can return stored carbon to the atmosphere. Nitrogen fixation converts atmospheric nitrogen into biologically available compounds, while microbial transformations and denitrification move nitrogen among chemical forms and back to the atmosphere. Phosphorus is mobilized mainly from rocks and sediments, passes through food webs, and can be retained, exported, or buried. [15, 17]

These cycles are coupled. Decomposition requires organic substrates and is affected by temperature, moisture, oxygen, and nutrient ratios. Plant growth can be limited by nitrogen or phosphorus even when light and carbon dioxide are abundant. Nutrient enrichment can increase primary production but also reorganize competition, oxygen demand, and food-web structure. Ocean circulation, soils, wetlands, and organisms therefore act not as passive containers but as processors that regulate the form, residence time, and destination of elements. [15, 17; library/earth-climate/carbon-cycle-greenhouse-effect.md; library/earth-climate/oceanography-marine-systems.md]

### Food webs transmit bottom-up and top-down effects

Bottom-up control begins with resource supply. Light, water, temperature, and nutrients constrain primary producers; changes in production can then affect herbivores and predators. Top-down control begins with consumers. Predators can suppress prey, release lower trophic levels from consumption, and alter habitat structure through trophic cascades. These directions are not mutually exclusive. Their relative importance varies across ecosystems, seasons, and disturbances. [4, 9, 17]

Interaction strength is central. A dominant species affects function through abundance or biomass. A foundation species creates or structures habitat, as trees, corals, or kelps do. An ecosystem engineer changes physical conditions, as beavers alter hydrology. A keystone species has an effect disproportionate to its abundance, as Paine's predator did by preventing monopolization of limited space. These categories describe different mechanisms and should not be treated as synonyms. [4, 9]

Indirect effects make prediction difficult. Removing a predator can increase herbivores, reduce vegetation, alter sediment or stream conditions, and change nutrient retention without the predator ever interacting directly with plants or soil. Mutualists and pathogens can likewise reorganize networks. The impact of losing a species thus depends on its partners, interaction strengths, and functional role, not only on its rarity. Hooper and colleagues identify dominant species, keystone species, ecological engineers, competition, facilitation, mutualism, disease, and predation as mechanisms through which functional characteristics affect ecosystem properties. [9]

### Succession is process change after disturbance

Succession describes directional changes in community composition and ecosystem processes after a new substrate forms or a disturbance removes organisms. Primary succession begins where biological legacies are sparse, such as newly exposed rock. Secondary succession begins where soil, seeds, roots, microorganisms, or other legacies remain. The distinction matters because retained structure and propagules can accelerate recovery and constrain which trajectories are possible. [3, 5, 17]

Connell and Slatyer's three mechanisms explain why sequences differ. Under facilitation, early species modify conditions in ways that help later species. Under tolerance, later species can invade regardless of early occupants and eventually prevail because they tolerate lower resource levels or other conditions. Under inhibition, established occupants suppress later colonists until death, damage, or another disturbance releases space. Multiple mechanisms can operate in one ecosystem, and animals, microbes, dispersal, climate, and disturbance history can alter their relative strength. [5]

Succession changes function as well as composition. Biomass, production, respiration, decomposition, nutrient retention, vertical structure, and food-web organization may all change. Odum proposed recurring patterns, but modern interpretation treats them as hypotheses rather than universal laws. [3, 5] A forest, grassland, reef, lake, or post-fire landscape may follow several trajectories, including long transients or alternative regimes. The author's synthesis is that restoration should specify the desired processes and tolerable trajectories instead of assuming that time alone will recreate a historical endpoint.

### Stability contains several different properties

Resistance is the degree to which a variable changes during disturbance. Recovery or engineering resilience concerns the speed or extent of return toward a prior state. Ecological resilience concerns how much disturbance a system can absorb before its organizing relationships and feedbacks shift into another regime. Persistence asks whether populations, interactions, or processes continue. Variability measures fluctuation through time, while robustness asks whether conclusions or functions survive component loss or uncertain conditions. These properties can disagree: a system can resist small shocks but recover poorly from a large one, or maintain aggregate production while its species composition changes. [7, 10, 13]

Feedbacks help explain regime behavior. Negative feedback counteracts change, as density dependence can slow population growth when resources become limiting. Positive feedback amplifies change, as vegetation loss can increase erosion, reduce water retention, and make revegetation harder. If feedbacks reorganize, reversing the original pressure may not immediately restore the former state. Holling's distinction between resilience and stability was designed to capture this possibility. [7]

Threshold language must be used carefully. A nonlinear response or rapid transition does not by itself prove that a universal tipping point exists. Demonstrating a regime shift requires evidence about state variables, feedbacks, alternative dynamics, and the scale of observation. The author's assessment is that ecosystem management should treat suspected thresholds as risk ranges rather than precise numbers unless repeated observations or experiments establish a defensible boundary. [7, 15]

### Diversity affects function through traits, complementarity, and response variation

Species richness counts species, but ecosystem function depends more directly on their traits, abundances, and interactions. Functional effect traits influence processes such as production, decomposition, pollination, predation, or nutrient acquisition. Functional response traits determine how organisms react to drought, warming, disturbance, disease, or other pressures. Species can share an effect trait yet differ in response traits, providing response diversity that helps preserve a function when conditions change. [8, 9, 11]

Complementarity occurs when species use resources, places, or times differently, raising collective resource capture or process rates. Selection occurs when a more diverse assemblage is more likely to contain a species with a large functional effect. Statistical averaging or portfolio effects arise when species fluctuate asynchronously, reducing variability in their aggregate function. None of these mechanisms implies that every added species contributes equally or that richness always improves every function. Composition, trophic structure, environmental constraints, and the function measured can change both the magnitude and direction of an effect. [8, 9, 10, 12]

This resolves part of the diversity-stability debate. May's random-network result concerns local stability in abstract interaction matrices; the Cedar Creek experiment concerns temporal stability of aggregate plant production in structured grassland communities. Both can be correct because they test different systems and definitions. Later network theory confirms that predator-prey, competitive, and mutualistic structures have different stability criteria, so complexity cannot be reduced to species count or connectance alone. [6, 10, 13]

## Evidence

### Whole-system energy accounting

Lindeman's trophic-dynamic framework treated Cedar Bog Lake as a system of energy transfers among trophic levels and linked ecological succession to changes in production and efficiency. The method combined observations and available measurements into an energy-budget representation rather than treating species occurrences as sufficient evidence of function. Its enduring finding was methodological: production, consumption, respiration, and decomposition can be compared in common energetic units, revealing limits and losses that a food-species list cannot show. [2]

Howard T. Odum's Silver Springs study provided a field-scale demonstration. Measurements and estimates were used to compare the energy embodied in producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, and tertiary consumers. The energy content declined sharply at successive consumer levels, supporting the use of production and transfer efficiency to explain why upper trophic levels contain less available energy than the producer base. [17] The case does not establish one universal efficiency for every ecosystem, but it validates the broader prediction that trophic structure is constrained by energetic transformation and dissipation.

These studies also established a measurement discipline. A credible energy budget must define the system boundary, time interval, production units, unconsumed material, detrital flow, and respiration. If detritus or microbial processing is omitted, much of the ecosystem's functional activity disappears from the model. The author's synthesis is that the most important legacy of trophic energetics is not a textbook pyramid but a requirement to balance inputs, transfers, storage, and losses explicitly. [2, 16, 17]

### Predator removal and trophic control

Paine's 1966 rocky-intertidal experiment tested top-down control through manipulation rather than correlation. He removed a predatory sea star from selected shoreline plots and compared the resulting community with areas where the predator remained. In the predator's absence, a mussel expanded and monopolized limiting space, and local species diversity fell. [4] The experiment linked one changed interaction to a sequence of competitive and community consequences, supporting the idea that a low-abundance predator can organize a food web.

The importance of this case lies in its causal design. Observational association between predators and diversity could reflect habitat differences, productivity, or other confounders. Removal changed one focal component and followed the response through time. The result does not mean that every predator is a keystone or that predator removal always lowers diversity. It establishes that interaction strength can dominate abundance as a predictor of ecosystem effect and that trophic cascades require direct experimental or mechanistic evidence. [4, 9]

### Succession mechanisms and interrupted trajectories

Odum's synthesis compared ecosystem properties across developmental stages and proposed that succession reorganizes production, respiration, nutrient cycling, and community structure. [3] Connell and Slatyer reviewed natural succession studies and translated competing explanations into three testable interaction models: facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition. They emphasized field manipulations of early and late species as the appropriate way to distinguish mechanisms and noted that disturbance often restarts succession before a late sequence is completed. [5]

The models explain why similar disturbances can produce different outcomes. If early colonists improve soil or microclimate, removing them can slow later establishment; if they inhibit later species, removal can accelerate it; if later species are tolerant, the effect may be weak. A chronological series by itself cannot identify which mechanism operated because age, environment, dispersal, and species interactions change together. The evidentiary lesson is that succession is best inferred through repeated observations, experiments, and process measurements rather than by substituting space for time without testing assumptions. [3, 5]

### Controlled biodiversity experiments

Tilman, Reich, and Knops conducted a decade-long field experiment in which plots were established with different numbers of perennial prairie species. Climate varied across years, allowing the researchers to examine whether diversity changed the temporal stability of annual aboveground plant production. Greater plant richness increased aggregate temporal stability, even though the stability of individual species did not increase. Statistical averaging and overyielding contributed to the result. [10]

The method matters because species number was directly controlled and production was measured over ten years. The finding supports the hypothesis that asynchronous responses and higher aggregate production can buffer an ecosystem-level function. It is not proof that richness stabilizes all functions or trophic levels. The experiment concerns plant production in grassland plots, and extrapolation to decomposition, predation, aquatic food webs, or whole landscapes requires additional evidence. Hooper and colleagues explicitly identified multitrophic diversity as an area needing further work. [9, 10]

Broader syntheses supply that context. Loreau and colleagues reviewed experiments and theory and concluded that biodiversity can affect productivity, nutrient use, and temporal variability through multiple mechanisms, while calling for stronger integration of biodiversity dynamics, ecosystem processes, and abiotic factors. [8] Hooper and colleagues reached consensus that functional characteristics, composition, and abundance strongly influence ecosystem properties, and that species responding differently to perturbations can stabilize process rates. [9] Chapin and colleagues connected biodiversity change to ecosystem processes and resilience, while Cardinale and colleagues reviewed two decades of evidence on how biodiversity loss changes ecosystem functioning and services. [11, 12]

### Network theory and the complexity-stability problem

May analyzed mathematical systems in which species interactions were assigned at random. He showed that increasing the number of components, the fraction connected, or average interaction strength can cross a stability boundary in such models. [6] The result contradicted the simple proposition that more complex networks are automatically more stable. It also clarified the need to compare like definitions: local asymptotic stability around an equilibrium is not identical to persistence, resistance, recovery, or low variability.

Allesina and Tang revisited the problem by separating interaction types. Their analytic models found different stability criteria for predator-prey, competitive, and mutualistic networks and showed that network structure and interaction strengths cannot be collapsed into one complexity measure. [13] These results explain why natural systems can be diverse and persistent despite May's random-network warning: real interactions are not random, and their signs, strengths, and architecture matter. They also prevent an opposite overstatement. Observed diversity-function benefits do not license the claim that every additional link always stabilizes every ecosystem property.

### Global assessments of ecosystem change

IPBES assembled a global assessment of biodiversity, ecosystem functions, and nature's contributions to people using six chapters and a government-approved summary for policymakers. Its evidence base links changes in biological communities to material, regulating, and nonmaterial contributions, and evaluates direct drivers such as land-use change, exploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species. [14] The assessment expands the unit of concern from isolated species losses to changes in ecological relationships and functional capacity.

The IPCC Working Group II assessment applies a similar systems approach to climate risk. Chapter 2 evaluates observed impacts and projected risks to terrestrial and freshwater species, communities, ecosystem structure, functioning, resilience, carbon stores, freshwater regulation, and other services. It reports that local species loss reduces ecosystem integrity and service capacity and that climate change interacts with land-use and resource pressures. [15] The assessment also emphasizes an attribution limit: complete chains from climate forcing through ecological change to a particular service are often difficult to establish. That caution is evidence discipline, not evidence absence.

Together, the experimental, theoretical, and assessment literatures support a bounded conclusion. Energy flow, material cycling, interactions, composition, and disturbance jointly determine ecosystem function; no single metric captures all of them. Diversity often improves the magnitude or reliability of functions through identifiable mechanisms, but outcomes depend on traits, network structure, environmental context, scale, and the definition of stability. [8-15]

## Implications

### Ecosystem diagnosis should follow mechanisms, not labels

For researchers, the first implication is methodological. Calling a place a forest, wetland, reef, grassland, or degraded system does not identify how it functions. A useful diagnosis specifies stocks and rates: primary production, respiration, decomposition, nutrient retention, hydrologic exchange, biomass, species interactions, and variability through time. It also identifies the physical drivers and system boundary. [2, 15-17] Two sites with similar species counts can differ in functional traits and process rates; two sites with different species composition can maintain a similar aggregate function for a period. Measurement must therefore match the question.

A second implication is that food-web position and interaction strength deserve explicit attention. Monitoring only abundant species can miss keystone predators, pollinators, decomposers, pathogens, or engineers whose effects propagate indirectly. Paine's experiment demonstrates why causal interventions or natural experiments are valuable: they reveal whether an association represents a regulating interaction. [4, 9] Where manipulation is unsafe or impossible, convergent evidence from time series, spatial comparisons, stable isotopes, diet data, and process models can test the same mechanism.

A third implication concerns scale. Nutrient retention in a plot, carbon storage in a soil profile, productivity in a lake, and resilience across a landscape operate at different spatial and temporal scales. A short experiment can identify a mechanism without capturing slow succession, rare disturbance, dispersal, or long-lived organisms. Conversely, a global assessment can establish broad patterns while obscuring local pathways. The author's assessment is that robust ecosystem inference should combine process experiments with long-term and cross-scale observation rather than demand that one method answer every question. [10, 14, 15]

### Conservation should protect interactions and response diversity

For conservation practitioners, preventing extinction remains necessary but is not sufficient to preserve ecosystem function. Functional composition, interaction networks, and response diversity determine whether production, decomposition, pollination, predation, carbon storage, or water regulation persist under change. [9, 11, 12] Protecting several species that perform similar functions but respond differently to drought, heat, disease, or disturbance can preserve options when conditions shift. That apparent redundancy is insurance, not waste.

This does not imply that species are interchangeable. A dominant producer, foundation species, ecosystem engineer, or keystone consumer can have a unique effect. Removing it may produce a response that other species do not replace. [4, 9] Prioritization should therefore consider functional uniqueness, interaction strength, vulnerability, and the consequences of loss, while avoiding unsupported claims that every rare species has a known irreplaceable function. Uncertainty itself has value implications: irreversible losses reduce future ecological options before all functions are understood. Hooper and colleagues make this precaution explicit in their synthesis. [9]

Connectivity also follows from the systems view. Organisms, water, nutrients, sediment, and disturbances move across local boundaries. Isolated protected patches may retain species yet lose migration routes, hydrologic processes, fire regimes, or upstream water quality. IPBES and IPCC assessments link ecosystem integrity to interacting pressures and emphasize protection and restoration at scales that sustain processes. [14, 15] The author's synthesis is that conservation units should be designed around functional landscapes and seascapes, not only around static inventories inside administrative lines.

### Restoration should rebuild processes and feedbacks

For restoration managers, succession theory argues against passive assumptions. Time since disturbance is not a mechanism. Early colonists may facilitate, tolerate, or inhibit later species, and surviving soils, seed banks, mutualists, herbivores, and hydrologic structure can determine the trajectory. [3, 5] A restoration plan should state which processes are missing, which feedbacks maintain the degraded state, and which intervention could change them.

Examples of process targets include restoring water flow before planting wetland vegetation, rebuilding decomposer and soil communities before expecting nutrient retention, controlling a competitively dominant invader before adding late-successional species, or re-establishing a consumer that releases vegetation from excessive herbivory. These are generalized applications of succession and food-web mechanisms rather than universal prescriptions. Site evidence is required to identify which feedback is active. [4, 5, 9]

Success criteria should likewise be multidimensional. Survival of planted species is not proof that an ecosystem has recovered production, trophic structure, nutrient cycling, resistance, or resilience. A restored site may resemble a reference site in composition while exporting nutrients or failing to regenerate. Conversely, composition may differ under a changed climate while key functions persist. The appropriate comparison depends on the objective, but reporting both composition and process reduces the risk of declaring success from one visible metric. [7, 15]

### Climate analysis must include ecological feedbacks

For Earth-system scientists, ecosystems are active components of climate and biogeochemical cycles. Vegetation and soils exchange carbon, water, and energy with the atmosphere; wetlands and aquatic systems transform and transport nutrients; marine organisms and circulation influence carbon export; disturbance can change a sink into a source. [15, 17; library/earth-climate/carbon-cycle-greenhouse-effect.md; library/earth-climate/oceanography-marine-systems.md] Climate affects ecosystems, and ecosystem reorganization can alter climate-relevant fluxes.

This coupling means that climate exposure alone does not determine ecological outcome. Warming, drought, altered fire, ocean chemistry, nutrient supply, land use, and exploitation interact with species traits and food webs. The same temperature anomaly can produce different functional effects where soils, water, species composition, or disturbance history differ. IPCC attribution therefore distinguishes confidence in observed physical change from confidence in the full pathway to a specific ecosystem service. [15]

Biodiversity can influence this response through complementarity and response variation, but it is not a substitute for reducing environmental pressure. More species cannot guarantee stability if disturbance exceeds physiological limits, eliminates habitat, or reorganizes key feedbacks. Conversely, simplified systems may maintain one optimized output while becoming more vulnerable to a different stress. [9-12] The author's assessment is that climate-risk models should test multiple functions and disturbances instead of treating present productivity as a complete proxy for resilience.

### Resource and organizational decisions should recognize ecosystem dependence

For organizations that depend on land, water, fisheries, forests, or biological production, ecosystem function is an operational dependency rather than an external label. Water purification, soil formation, nutrient retention, pollination, pest regulation, coastal protection, carbon storage, and production emerge from ecological processes and interactions. IPBES and IPCC assess these as contributions and services while cautioning that complete quantitative attribution is often difficult. [14, 15]

A defensible dependency assessment traces a pathway: the activity depends on a function; the function depends on organisms, physical conditions, and interactions; identified pressures alter those components; and monitoring can detect change before failure. This is more informative than assigning one monetary value to "nature" without a causal model. The author's synthesis is that scenario analysis should ask which process can fail, what substitutes exist, how long recovery takes, and whether a threshold or irreversible loss is plausible.

The worst analytical error is to equate stable output with a healthy system. Fertilizer, irrigation, stocking, or other inputs can temporarily maintain a product while soils, food webs, water quality, or response diversity decline. A narrow output metric may therefore conceal transferred costs or reduced resilience. [9, 14, 15] The preventive rule is to pair production indicators with measures of supporting stocks, fluxes, diversity, and disturbance response.

### Claims about stability require explicit definitions

Finally, ecosystem communication should name the stability property being claimed. "Stable" may mean low variance, resistance to a shock, rapid recovery, persistence of species, persistence of function, or remaining within a regime. These are not interchangeable. [6, 7, 10, 13] Stating the variable, disturbance, reference condition, spatial scale, and time horizon converts a vague claim into a testable one.

The same discipline applies to diversity claims. Evidence supports many positive effects of biodiversity on ecosystem functions and their reliability, but it does not support a context-free slogan that more species always make every system stable. [8-13] Functional traits, response differences, trophic position, interaction signs and strengths, resource conditions, and the selected endpoint all matter. The scientifically useful conclusion is conditional: biodiversity can support function and resilience through identifiable mechanisms, and those mechanisms must be measured where decisions depend on them.

The author's synthesis is that ecosystem science is most powerful as an accounting and causal framework. It links energy input, material cycles, biological interactions, disturbance, and scale; it then asks which observations could falsify the proposed mechanism. This approach connects the carbon cycle, biodiversity loss, ocean change, and resource depletion without collapsing them into one undifferentiated environmental problem. [14, 15; library/earth-climate/biodiversity-sixth-extinction.md; library/earth-climate/ocean-acidification.md; library/earth-climate/resource-depletion-sustainability-science.md]
