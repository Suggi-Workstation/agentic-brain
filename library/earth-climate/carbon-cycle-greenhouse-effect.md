# The Carbon Cycle and the Greenhouse Effect -- How Earth's Thermostat Works and Why It Is Breaking

The carbon cycle moves carbon among the atmosphere, ocean, land, life, and rocks, while the greenhouse effect governs how strongly atmospheric gases impede the loss of thermal infrared energy to space. Human extraction and combustion of fossil carbon, together with land-use change and cement production, transfer carbon into the active cycle faster than land and ocean sinks remove it, so atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) and radiative forcing rise ([1] [2] [4]). The natural greenhouse effect is indispensable to a habitable Earth, but the same physics means that increasing long-lived greenhouse gases changes the planetary energy balance ([8] [15]).

## Background

The modern account began as an energy-budget problem rather than a pollution problem. Joseph Fourier argued in 1824 that Earth would be much colder without an atmosphere. John Tyndall then demonstrated in 1859 that gases including water vapor and CO2 absorb infrared radiation, and Svante Arrhenius published the first calculation of warming from human CO2 emissions in 1896 ([10]). These contributions were incomplete by present standards, but they established three durable questions: why the surface is warmer than a simple radiative calculation predicts, which gases interact with outgoing thermal radiation, and how changing their abundance changes climate.

Eunice Foote belongs in that history. In 1856 she exposed gas-filled cylinders to sunlight and found that carbon dioxide and water vapor produced greater heating than ordinary air. She inferred that an atmosphere richer in CO2 would be warmer. Her apparatus did not isolate the longwave infrared mechanism that Tyndall later measured, so the two experiments are related but not identical. Foote nevertheless made and published an early experimental connection between atmospheric composition and temperature three years before Tyndall's infrared work ([11]). Omitting her is therefore a substantive historical gap, while describing her experiment as a complete measurement of the terrestrial greenhouse mechanism would overstate what it established.

Carbon-cycle science developed along a separate path before the two subjects were joined. Carbon is exchanged continually among reservoirs, so scientists needed to distinguish rapid exchange of individual molecules from removal of a human-caused atmospheric perturbation. Revelle and Suess used carbon isotopes and ocean carbonate chemistry in 1957 to estimate atmosphere-ocean exchange and to show that seawater chemistry constrains how much added CO2 the surface ocean can accommodate. Their paper described industrial emissions as a large-scale geophysical experiment and helped displace the assumption that the full ocean volume would promptly absorb fossil-fuel CO2 ([12]). The resulting buffer concept, now expressed through the Revelle factor, remains central to estimates of ocean uptake ([1] [12]).

Direct atmospheric monitoring supplied the missing time series. Charles David Keeling began continuous CO2 observations at Mauna Loa in March 1958. The record separates a seasonal cycle, driven mainly by Northern Hemisphere land biology, from a persistent long-term rise; NOAA later added parallel measurements and a global marine-surface network ([5]). The record is not simply a graph drawn through isolated samples. Instruments are calibrated, water vapor is removed before the dry-air mole fraction is reported, local contamination is screened, and observations from many stations test whether Mauna Loa represents a broader atmospheric change ([5]).

Paleoclimate measurements extended the baseline far beyond the instrumental period. Air enclosed in Antarctic ice preserves samples of past atmospheres. Luthi and colleagues used dry extraction followed by laser absorption spectroscopy and gas chromatography on the EPICA Dome C core to extend a composite CO2 record to 800,000 years. Their measurements placed late-Quaternary CO2 within about 172 to 300 parts per million (ppm) and found a strong correlation with Antarctic temperature across eight glacial cycles ([7]). The correlation alone does not specify one-way causation: orbital changes initiated deglaciations, while CO2 changes amplified and propagated the climate response through the greenhouse effect ([1] [7]).

The mature framework joins radiation, chemistry, biology, geology, and fluid circulation. Carbon-cycle observations explain how emissions are partitioned among atmosphere, land, and ocean. Radiative-transfer calculations explain how the resulting concentrations alter outgoing energy. Climate feedbacks then change water vapor, clouds, snow and ice, ecosystems, soils, ocean circulation, and natural carbon fluxes ([1] [2]). No single diagram is the evidence by itself; the explanation is constrained by laboratory spectroscopy, atmospheric and ocean observations, isotope ratios, ice cores, energy-budget calculations, and Earth-system models.

Two distinctions prevent common errors. First, a natural greenhouse effect is not evidence against an enhanced greenhouse effect. With a fixed planetary reflectivity, the difference between Earth's observed mean surface temperature and a no-atmospheric-absorption thought experiment is about 33 degrees Celsius; adding greenhouse gases changes that existing radiative system rather than creating it from nothing ([15]). Second, gross natural carbon fluxes can exceed annual human emissions while remaining close to balance with opposing natural fluxes. A comparatively smaller one-way human input can still accumulate because the relevant quantity is the net budget, not the largest gross arrow in a cycle diagram ([1] [4] [6]).

## Core Concepts

### Reservoirs, stocks, fluxes, and units

A reservoir is a place where carbon is stored; a flux is a rate of transfer between reservoirs. Stocks are commonly reported in petagrams or gigatonnes of carbon (PgC or GtC), which are numerically equal, while emissions inventories may instead report gigatonnes of carbon dioxide (GtCO2). Confusing carbon mass with CO2 mass introduces a factor of 44/12 because a CO2 molecule includes two oxygen atoms. Concentrations are reported as dry-air mole fractions in ppm, not as a mass of atmospheric carbon ([1] [4] [5]). These units describe related quantities, but they are not interchangeable.

Most of Earth's carbon is in rocks and sediments. NASA estimates roughly 65.5 million GtC in this slow geological reservoir, while the ocean contains about 38,000 GtC and therefore about 50 times as much carbon as the atmosphere. Land vegetation, soils, and permafrost contain smaller but climatically active stocks ([1] [6] [13]). Stock size alone does not determine climate importance. Atmospheric CO2 is a small fraction of planetary carbon, yet it directly affects infrared radiation, while transfer from rock carbon into the atmosphere alters a reservoir that exchanges rapidly with land and the surface ocean.

The atmosphere, land biosphere, and ocean exchange large quantities in both directions. Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air; respiration, decomposition, and fire return it. Surface seawater takes up or releases CO2 according to the air-sea partial-pressure difference, temperature, circulation, and carbonate chemistry. The annual net change in atmospheric carbon equals anthropogenic emissions plus any net natural sources, minus net land and ocean uptake, subject to measurement and model uncertainty ([1] [4]). This mass-balance identity is the organizing principle of the contemporary carbon budget.

### Fast exchange is not permanent removal

The fast carbon cycle operates from days to centuries through photosynthesis, food webs, soils, rivers, surface-ocean exchange, and transport into the ocean interior. The slow cycle transfers carbon through weathering, carbonate formation, burial, metamorphism, subduction, and volcanic degassing over thousands to hundreds of millions of years ([6]). Fossil-fuel combustion short-circuits this separation by moving geological carbon into the atmosphere-ocean-land system on an industrial timescale.

An individual CO2 molecule can leave the atmosphere in years through photosynthesis or ocean exchange, but another molecule can return at the same time. The adjustment time of an added atmospheric CO2 pulse is therefore much longer than the residence time of a tagged molecule. Land and upper-ocean processes remove part of a pulse over decades to centuries; mixing, carbonate compensation, and rock weathering act over centuries to many millennia. NASA's synthesis notes that a fraction of added carbon can remain atmospheric for thousands of years ([1] [6]). A single atmospheric lifetime is consequently a poor description of CO2 persistence.

The slow carbon cycle does provide a negative feedback. Carbonic acid formed in rainwater reacts with silicate minerals; dissolved products reach the ocean, and carbon can ultimately be buried in carbonate sediments. Warmer, wetter conditions can accelerate parts of this process. Its stabilizing influence operates far too slowly to offset an industrial pulse over decades, so calling weathering a thermostat must include its geological response time ([6]).

### Greenhouse physics is an altitude-dependent energy process

Earth absorbs solar energy, mostly at visible and near-infrared wavelengths, and emits thermal infrared energy because its surface and atmosphere are much cooler than the Sun. Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation in wavelength bands set by molecular structure. Increasing a well-mixed greenhouse gas raises the altitude from which radiation in affected bands escapes efficiently to space. Because the troposphere generally cools with height, that higher emitting level is colder and initially emits less energy. The resulting top-of-atmosphere energy imbalance warms the surface-troposphere system until outgoing energy again balances absorbed solar energy ([2] [15]).

This description is more accurate than saying greenhouse gases permanently trap photons or simply send half of each absorbed photon downward. Molecules collide, exchange energy, absorb, and emit repeatedly; convection and latent heat also transport energy vertically. Downward infrared radiation is real, but the climatic effect is diagnosed by the full radiative and convective energy budget, especially the change in outgoing radiation at the top of the atmosphere ([2]).

The natural effect raises mean surface temperature by roughly 33 degrees Celsius relative to a standard no-atmospheric-absorption thought experiment. That number is a useful baseline, not a directly observable alternate Earth, because removing atmospheric absorption could also change clouds, ice, water vapor, and reflectivity ([15]). The enhanced effect from additional CO2 is evaluated as radiative forcing and subsequent feedback response, not by assigning the entire 33-degree difference to one gas.

### Forcing and feedback are different roles

Water vapor supplies a large share of the present greenhouse effect, but its atmospheric abundance responds rapidly to temperature through evaporation, condensation, and precipitation. CO2, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone, and halogenated gases do not condense out under ordinary tropospheric conditions. Their concentrations can therefore impose radiative forcing, while water vapor acts mainly as a fast feedback: initial warming permits more atmospheric moisture, which adds further greenhouse warming ([2] [8]). Clouds, surface reflectivity, and the vertical temperature profile provide additional feedbacks with different signs and uncertainties.

Lacis and colleagues tested the control-knob concept with a climate model by removing non-condensing greenhouse gases. Water vapor and clouds declined as the modeled climate cooled, demonstrating that their large contribution to the current greenhouse effect does not make them independent long-term drivers ([8]). The model result does not imply that CO2 acts alone. It shows why the radiative influence of persistent gases must be evaluated together with fast feedbacks.

CO2 also acts within paleoclimate feedback loops. During glacial-interglacial cycles, orbital forcing altered regional and seasonal sunlight, oceans and land released or absorbed carbon, and the resulting CO2 changes amplified temperature changes globally ([1] [7]). Today, direct human emissions make CO2 an externally imposed forcing even though CO2 also participated as a feedback in past natural transitions. A gas can occupy different causal positions in different episodes.

### The anthropogenic perturbation is measured as a budget

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 estimated 2024 fossil CO2 emissions at 10.3 +/- 0.5 GtC per year and net land-use-change emissions at 1.3 +/- 0.7 GtC per year, for total anthropogenic emissions of 11.6 +/- 0.9 GtC per year. It estimated atmospheric accumulation at 7.9 +/- 0.2 GtC, ocean uptake at 3.4 +/- 0.4 GtC, and land uptake at 1.9 +/- 1.1 GtC for that year. The rounded components do not close exactly, and the study reported an unusually large budget imbalance, illustrating why annual sink estimates and land-use emissions carry uncertainty ([4]).

A single unusual year should not define the system. Over the 2015-2024 decade, the same assessment partitioned total emissions approximately 50% to atmospheric accumulation, 29% to ocean uptake, and 21% to land uptake, with a near-zero mean imbalance ([4]). These sinks have slowed the atmospheric increase; they have not canceled emissions. Describing them as finite containers is also misleading. Sink strength depends on concentration gradients, climate, circulation, nutrients, disturbance, and carbonate chemistry, and it can vary from year to year and under future warming ([1] [4]).

The 2025 budget estimated a global annual-average atmospheric concentration of 422.8 +/- 0.1 ppm in 2024 and a preliminary 2025 value near 425.6 ppm, about 53% above the approximately 278 ppm preindustrial reference ([4]). NOAA's Mauna Loa series reported an August 2026 monthly average of 427.55 ppm, but a site-specific monthly value must not be substituted for a global annual mean ([5]). Both measurements show the continuing rise; their different sampling frames explain why their numerical values need not match.

### Ocean uptake changes chemistry

When CO2 dissolves in seawater, it participates in reactions among dissolved CO2, carbonic acid, bicarbonate, carbonate, and hydrogen ions. Added CO2 increases hydrogen-ion concentration, lowers pH, and reduces carbonate-ion availability. Surface-ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1 unit since the industrial era; because pH is logarithmic, that corresponds to roughly a 26% increase in hydrogen-ion concentration and is commonly rounded to about a 30% increase in acidity ([1] [9]). The ocean remains alkaline at an average pH near 8.1, so acidification means movement toward lower pH, not that seawater has become acidic.

Carbonate buffering lets the ocean store far more carbon than would remain as physically dissolved CO2 alone, but buffering is not unlimited. The Revelle factor expresses how strongly seawater partial pressure of CO2 changes relative to dissolved inorganic carbon. As anthropogenic carbon accumulates, buffer capacity changes, and warming and stratification can also alter uptake. The 2025 carbon budget estimated that climate change reduced the modeled ocean sink by about 0.20 +/- 0.05 GtC per year during 2015-2024 relative to simulations driven only by rising atmospheric CO2 ([4] [12]).

Reduced carbonate-ion availability makes shell and skeleton construction more difficult for many calcifying organisms and can promote dissolution under sufficiently corrosive conditions ([1] [9]). Ocean acidification should not be described as the direct cause of coral bleaching: severe heat stress drives the expulsion of symbiotic algae that defines bleaching, while acidification separately impairs calcification and reef repair ([9]). The two stresses can interact, but their mechanisms are distinct.

### Carbon-cycle feedbacks alter, but do not erase, the budget logic

Warming affects respiration, wildfire, drought, vegetation, ocean solubility, mixing, and permafrost thaw. These responses can weaken land and ocean uptake or create additional emissions, producing positive carbon-climate feedbacks ([1]). Northern permafrost-region soils contain roughly 1,460-1,600 GtC, about twice the carbon currently in the atmosphere, but that stock is not a projection that all of it will enter the air. Thaw rate, soil moisture, microbial decomposition, fire, vegetation growth, and whether carbon is emitted as CO2 or methane determine the realized flux ([13]).

Regional evidence likewise requires qualification. Aircraft profiles collected over nine years showed that the more deforested and climate-stressed eastern and southeastern Amazon was a net CO2 source, especially because of fires, while wetter western and central regions were near balance in that study ([14]). That finding does not justify treating the entire Amazon as a uniform source. Spatial scale, time period, and whether fire emissions are included must accompany the claim.

The synthesis is that feedbacks change the fraction of emissions remaining in the atmosphere and the carbon budget consistent with a temperature outcome; they do not create a license to ignore primary emissions ([1] [3]). Uncertainty is consequential but bounded by mass balance, observations, and model evaluation. It should be represented as ranges and confidence levels rather than converted into a claim that sink behavior is either perfectly known or wholly unknowable.

## Evidence

### Direct atmospheric observations identify the trend and its scale

The Keeling record uses repeated calibrated measurements at a high-elevation, remote site, with procedures for excluding local volcanic or biological contamination. NOAA's global estimate combines observations from a distributed marine-boundary-layer network. Reporting dry-air mole fraction removes variable water vapor from the denominator, and overlapping NOAA and Scripps programs provide an independent continuity check ([5]). The method therefore tests both temporal persistence and geographic representativeness rather than relying on one instrument.

The result is a seasonal oscillation superimposed on a multi-decadal increase. Mauna Loa began near 315 ppm in 1958; by August 2026 its monthly mean was 427.55 ppm ([5]). Ice-core and modern observations show that the recent atmospheric increase is 100-200 times faster than the natural CO2 increase during the end of the last ice age ([16]). Concentration alone does not identify the source, but carbon-isotope changes, declining atmospheric oxygen during combustion, and the fact that observed accumulation is smaller than human emissions independently connect the rise to fossil carbon and land-use change ([1] [16]).

### Ice cores establish the preindustrial range and feedback context

Luthi and colleagues measured air extracted from the lowest 200 meters of the EPICA Dome C ice core using two analytical systems: laser absorption spectroscopy in Bern and gas chromatography in Grenoble. Standards tied the measurements to a common concentration scale, and replicate analyses addressed contamination and extraction effects. Combining those data with earlier cores extended the atmospheric record through eight glacial cycles to 800,000 years ([7]).

The study found a late-Quaternary range of about 172-300 ppm and a strong correlation between CO2 and Antarctic temperature. Modern concentrations are therefore outside that measured range, while the sequence of deglacial changes shows that the interpretation cannot stop at correlation. Orbital forcing starts regional energy changes; ocean, land, ice, and CO2 feedbacks magnify and redistribute the response ([1] [7]). This case supplies both a long baseline and a warning against the false choice that CO2 must be either a forcing or a feedback in all contexts.

### The global carbon budget closes independent measurements against one another

The Global Carbon Budget does not infer every component from the atmospheric rise. Fossil emissions are estimated from energy and cement statistics; land-use emissions use bookkeeping models and land-use data; atmospheric growth comes from surface-station concentration measurements; ocean uptake combines biogeochemical models with surface-ocean CO2 products and interior observations; land uptake combines vegetation models with atmospheric inversions and other constraints ([4]). The budget imbalance measures residual inconsistency among these independently estimated components.

For 2024 the assessment found total anthropogenic emissions of 11.6 +/- 0.9 GtC and atmospheric growth of 7.9 +/- 0.2 GtC, alongside ocean and land sinks. The components produced a large negative imbalance after rounding and full-precision analysis, so the authors did not present that year as perfectly closed. Over 2015-2024, however, the mean budget was near balance, with about half of emissions remaining atmospheric and the rest taken up by ocean and land ([4]). This combination of annual mismatch and decadal closure is evidence of a real mass-balance signal with quantified observational and model uncertainty, not evidence that every yearly sink estimate is exact.

### Radiative physics and attribution connect concentration to warming

Laboratory spectroscopy establishes that CO2, water vapor, methane, nitrous oxide, and other gases absorb infrared radiation in identifiable bands. Satellite and surface spectra observe the corresponding radiative structure, while energy-budget calculations quantify effective radiative forcing. Climate models then propagate forcing through ocean heat uptake, atmospheric circulation, water vapor, clouds, and surface reflectivity ([2]). These are linked but distinct tests: a model is not used to invent the molecular absorption property it is asked to represent.

Formal attribution combines observed spatial and temporal patterns with modeled responses to greenhouse gases, aerosols, solar variability, volcanic eruptions, and internal variability. The IPCC assessed that human activities, principally greenhouse-gas emissions, unequivocally caused global warming and that global surface temperature in 2011-2020 was 1.1 degrees Celsius above 1850-1900 ([3]). Its assessed likely contribution from well-mixed greenhouse gases was larger than the observed net warming because human-produced aerosols offset part of it, while natural solar and volcanic drivers contributed only a small net change over the assessed period ([3]). A comparison using natural forcing alone therefore fails not merely in total trend but across multiple fingerprints.

The control-knob experiment by Lacis and colleagues provides a mechanistic test of feedback classification. In their model, removing non-condensing greenhouse gases caused temperature, water vapor, and clouds to decline until the terrestrial greenhouse effect largely collapsed. The result supports the distinction between persistent gases that set radiative forcing and water vapor that strongly amplifies temperature changes ([8]). Because it is a model intervention rather than an observable experiment on the real planet, its evidential role is mechanism testing alongside, not instead of, observations.

### Ocean chemistry records a second, non-temperature consequence

Long-term ocean stations and repeated surveys measure dissolved inorganic carbon, alkalinity, partial pressure of CO2, pH, and related carbonate-system variables. The observed surface-ocean pH decline of about 0.1 unit is consistent with the chemical response expected when the ocean absorbs anthropogenic CO2 ([1] [9]). A recalculation from the logarithmic pH definition gives a 25.9% increase in hydrogen-ion concentration for an exact 0.1-unit decline, explaining the conventional statement of approximately 30% greater acidity.

Biological consequences vary among species and environments, so a universal response should not be asserted. The robust chemical result is that added hydrogen ions reduce carbonate-ion availability; field observations and experiments show impaired shell or skeleton formation and, under corrosive conditions, dissolution for some calcifiers ([1] [9]). Coral bleaching must be separately attributed to heat stress, even though acidification can reduce reef growth and recovery. This case is especially strong evidence for anthropogenic carbon-cycle disruption because the same transferred carbon is observed through atmospheric accumulation, ocean uptake, and predictable carbonate chemistry.

## Implications

### Cumulative emissions govern long-term warming

CO2-induced warming is approximately proportional to cumulative net CO2 emissions over the range relevant to modern climate targets. Each additional net tonne adds to cumulative emissions; stabilizing human-caused warming therefore requires at least net-zero global CO2 emissions, together with strong reductions in other greenhouse gases ([2] [3]). This relation converts the carbon cycle from a background diagram into a constraint: a temperature limit corresponds to a finite cumulative budget, not merely to a desired annual emissions rate.

Natural sinks are already incorporated in that relationship. It is incorrect to subtract an assumed future forest or ocean uptake twice, once in an Earth-system projection and again as an independent allowance. It is also incorrect to assume that today's airborne fraction is a fixed law. Land and ocean sinks respond to atmospheric CO2, climate, nutrients, disturbance, and circulation; the fraction absorbed can change even while the sinks continue taking up carbon in absolute terms ([1] [4]). Carbon-budget estimates must therefore state the period, uncertainty, and treatment of feedbacks.

### Net zero does not mean an immediate return to the old climate

The original claim that temperatures must continue rising for decades after CO2 emissions cease because the ocean releases stored heat is incomplete and generally wrong for CO2 alone. Reduced ocean heat uptake tends to warm the surface after emissions cease, but declining atmospheric CO2 as land and ocean continue absorbing carbon tends to cool it. In coordinated idealized model experiments, these effects approximately offset on multi-decadal timescales, with outcomes ranging from modest cooling to modest additional warming ([17]). The best estimate is therefore near stabilization, not a guaranteed large warming commitment.

This correction does not make past emissions reversible on human timescales. Atmospheric CO2 declines slowly after emissions cease, ocean acidification persists, deep-ocean heat and carbon redistribute, glaciers and ice sheets continue responding, and sea level can rise for centuries to millennia ([1] [3] [17]). Net zero concerns the approximate stabilization of global mean temperature; it is not a claim that every component of the Earth system stops changing.

The distinction between zero CO2 emissions and zero greenhouse-gas emissions also matters. Continued methane, nitrous oxide, aerosol, or land-use forcing can change temperature after fossil CO2 reaches net zero. Conversely, net negative CO2 emissions can lower atmospheric concentration and eventually reduce CO2-driven warming, but carbon-cycle response to removal is not guaranteed to be exactly symmetric with response to prior emissions ([1] [2]). Every statement about commitment should specify which emissions are set to zero and over what time horizon.

### Observation systems are part of the control problem

For atmospheric scientists, maintaining calibrated global networks is necessary to distinguish emission trends from year-to-year sink variability. A single station offers continuity; a distributed network establishes global representativeness. For oceanographers, repeated carbon-system surveys and autonomous sensors separate long-term uptake from circulation-driven variability. For ecologists and cryosphere scientists, regional flux measurements are needed because a global net sink can coexist with source regions such as fire-affected eastern Amazonia or seasonally emitting permafrost landscapes ([4] [5] [13] [14]).

These measurement systems also discipline models. A model that matches atmospheric CO2 but misallocates land and ocean uptake can project the wrong response to warming. Budget closure, interior-ocean carbon inventories, atmospheric oxygen, isotopes, vegetation observations, and flux towers constrain different components. The practical implication for Earth-system research is to preserve multiple independent measurements rather than optimize only one headline metric ([1] [4]).

### Carbon accounting must retain system boundaries

For emissions analysts, three accounting distinctions are essential. Gross ecosystem exchange is not the same as a net sink; territorial fossil emissions are not the same as global atmospheric accumulation; and GtC is not the same unit as GtCO2. Land-use estimates are particularly uncertain because definitions, baselines, regrowth, degradation, and bookkeeping conventions differ ([4]). A credible budget states whether cement process emissions and cement carbonation are included, how managed land is treated, and whether reported values are annual or cumulative.

The 2024 budget illustrates why false precision is hazardous. Rounded source and sink estimates do not close, and the reported imbalance was large relative to many annual changes ([4]). The correct response is not to force one component to make the equation balance or to dismiss the budget. It is to retain uncertainties, compare independent methods, and rely on multi-year behavior when annual climate variability temporarily changes sink strength.

### Ocean and ecosystem consequences follow carbon, not only temperature

For marine researchers and coastal managers, atmospheric CO2 is relevant even apart from warming because ocean uptake changes carbonate chemistry directly. Lower carbonate availability can reduce calcification and increase dissolution risk, while heat stress independently raises bleaching risk ([1] [9]). Monitoring only sea-surface temperature therefore misses a second exposure pathway. Local nutrients, freshwater, upwelling, and biological activity can intensify or mask the global acidification trend, so global and local measurements are complementary.

For ecosystem and permafrost researchers, stock estimates must not be reported as inevitable emissions. The approximately 1,460-1,600 GtC in northern permafrost soils defines material at risk, not a release forecast ([13]). Process measurements are required to estimate the thawed fraction, decomposition rate, hydrologic state, methane share, vegetation response, and time profile. This separation between vulnerable stock and realized flux is a general rule for forests, peat, soils, and methane hydrates.

### The governing mental model is a coupled budget

The most useful synthesis is a coupled budget with four linked tests. Matter must balance among emissions, atmospheric accumulation, land uptake, and ocean uptake. Energy must balance between absorbed sunlight and outgoing radiation. Chemistry must explain how added carbon changes seawater and atmospheric composition. Observations must constrain rates, stocks, feedbacks, and uncertainty across space and time ([1] [2] [4]).

This model prevents several recurring errors: comparing gross natural fluxes with net human inputs, equating molecular residence time with perturbation lifetime, treating water vapor as an independent cause of current long-term warming, equating the permafrost carbon stock with projected release, attributing coral bleaching directly to acidification, or assuming that zero CO2 emissions either instantly restore the old climate or guarantee decades of substantial further warming. Each error collapses a coupled process into one number. The evidence instead supports a simpler but stricter conclusion: fossil and land-use emissions add carbon to an incompletely absorbing system, the added atmospheric CO2 changes radiative transfer, and the resulting climate and chemical responses feed back on the sinks ([1] [3] [4]).
