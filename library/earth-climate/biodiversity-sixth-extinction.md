# Biodiversity and the Sixth Extinction -- Earth Is Losing Species at Rates Unparalleled in 66 Million Years, and the Primary Driver Is Human Activity

Earth is entering its sixth mass extinction event, with current
species extinction rates estimated at 100 to 1,000 times the natural
background rate. Unlike the five previous mass extinctions -- caused
by asteroid impacts, volcanic eruptions, and climate shifts -- this
one is driven overwhelmingly by human activity: habitat destruction,
overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, and anthropogenic
climate change. The consequences extend far beyond the loss of
individual species; biodiversity underpins the ecosystem services
that make human civilization possible, from pollination and water
purification to carbon sequestration and disease regulation.

## Background

The fossil record reveals five previous mass extinction events in
the last 500 million years, each defined by the loss of at least 75
percent of Earth's species within a geologically short interval. The
End-Ordovician (444 million years ago) eliminated roughly 85 percent
of marine species through a two-phase glaciation event. The Late
Devonian (360 mya) unfolded over millions of years and primarily
affected marine life. The End-Permian (250 mya), known as the Great
Dying, was the most severe: approximately 96 percent of marine
species and 70 percent of terrestrial vertebrates vanished, driven
by massive volcanic eruptions in Siberia that triggered runaway
global warming, ocean acidification, and anoxia. The End-Triassic
(200 mya) eliminated roughly 80 percent of species, again linked to
volcanic activity from the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province. The
End-Cretaceous (66 mya), famous for ending the age of dinosaurs,
killed approximately 76 percent of species through a combination of
asteroid impact and Deccan Traps volcanism.

What distinguishes the sixth extinction from these predecessors is
its cause and its speed. Previous mass extinctions were driven by
geological or astronomical forces operating over thousands to
millions of years. The current extinction event is driven by a
single species -- Homo sapiens -- and is compressing comparable
levels of biodiversity loss into centuries rather than millennia.
The term Anthropocene defaunation captures the specific pattern of
animal population declines and extinctions caused by human activity
since the Pleistocene, accelerating dramatically since the
Industrial Revolution.

The concept of a sixth mass extinction entered mainstream scientific
discourse with a series of landmark papers. Biologist E.O. Wilson
warned of biodiversity collapse throughout the 1980s and 1990s.
Barnosky et al. (2011) asked in Nature whether the sixth mass
extinction had already arrived, comparing current extinction rates
against the Big Five benchmarks. Ceballos, Ehrlich, and colleagues
(2015) provided the most cited evidence in Science Advances,
demonstrating that even under the most conservative assumptions,
vertebrate extinction rates over the last century were up to 100
times above background. Their 2017 and 2020 follow-up papers
introduced the concept of biological annihilation -- the
recognition that extinction begins with population decline, and that
thousands of species are already in the final stages of collapse
even if they have not yet been declared extinct.

## Core Concepts

### The Five Drivers of Biodiversity Loss

The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and
Ecosystem Services (IPBES) identifies five direct drivers of
biodiversity loss, ranked by global impact:

**Habitat destruction and land-use change** is the leading cause.
Approximately 40 percent of Earth's ice-free land surface has been
converted for agriculture, and agriculture drives 90 percent of
global deforestation. Tropical forests, which host an estimated 50
to 80 percent of terrestrial species despite covering less than 10
percent of land area, are being cleared at a rate of roughly 10
million hectares per year. When habitat is fragmented, populations
become isolated, genetic diversity collapses, and species that
require large continuous ranges -- large carnivores, migratory
birds, wide-ranging herbivores -- cannot survive.

**Overexploitation** of wild species through hunting, fishing, and
logging is the second major driver. Industrial fishing has reduced
large predatory fish populations by an estimated 90 percent since
1950. The bushmeat trade in Africa and Southeast Asia threatens
hundreds of mammal species. Overharvesting of timber, particularly
of high-value tropical hardwoods, degrades forest ecosystems even
when full deforestation does not occur.

**Climate change** is an accelerating and increasingly dominant
driver. As global temperatures rise, species must shift their ranges
poleward or upward in elevation to remain within their thermal
tolerances. Those that cannot move fast enough -- alpine species,
island endemics, coral reef organisms -- face extinction. Coral
reefs, which support roughly 25 percent of all marine species, are
particularly vulnerable: at 1.5 degrees Celsius of warming, 70 to
90 percent of warm-water corals are projected to decline; at 2
degrees, more than 99 percent are expected to be lost. Climate
change also acts as a threat multiplier, amplifying the effects of
habitat loss and invasive species.

**Pollution** affects biodiversity through multiple pathways.
Agricultural runoff of nitrogen and phosphorus creates dead zones in
coastal waters where oxygen levels drop too low to support most
marine life. Pesticides, particularly neonicotinoids, have been
implicated in widespread insect declines -- studies in Germany
documented a 75 percent decline in flying insect biomass over 27
years in protected areas. Plastic pollution entangles and is
ingested by hundreds of marine species. Light and noise pollution
disrupt the behavior of nocturnal and acoustically-communicating
species.

**Invasive alien species** are a leading cause of extinctions on
islands, where endemic species evolved without competition or
predators. The brown tree snake extirpated most of Guam's native
bird species after its accidental introduction. Invasive rats on
oceanic islands have caused an estimated 40 to 60 percent of all
recorded reptile and bird extinctions. Globally, invasive species
contribute to 60 percent of documented extinctions and cause an
estimated 423 billion USD in annual economic damage.

### Background Extinction Rates and the Extinction Debt

To understand why the sixth extinction is exceptional, one must
understand the baseline. The background extinction rate -- the
normal rate at which species disappear between mass extinction
events -- is estimated at 0.1 to 2 extinctions per million
species-years (E/MSY) for mammals. Under this rate, the world would
expect to lose roughly one mammal species every 500 to 1,000 years.

Current extinction rates are dramatically higher. Ceballos et al.
(2015) calculated that vertebrate extinction rates over the last
century were up to 100 times the background rate of 2 E/MSY, even
using the most conservative methodology. The IUCN Red List documents
338 vertebrate extinctions since 1500, with an additional 279
species classified as extinct in the wild or possibly extinct.
Extrapolating across all taxa, scientists estimate that dozens of
species go extinct every day, most of them insects, plants, and
microorganisms that were never formally described by science.

The concept of extinction debt is critical for understanding why the
current crisis is likely worse than documented numbers suggest.
Extinction debt refers to the time lag between habitat degradation
and the eventual extinction of species that remain in the degraded
habitat. A forest fragment may initially retain most of its species
after isolation, but small population sizes, inbreeding, and
stochastic events will cause a slow-motion collapse over decades or
centuries. The species still present but committed to extinction are
sometimes called the living dead. Current extinction rates may
therefore understate the true magnitude of biodiversity loss that
has already been locked in.

### Keystone Species and Trophic Cascades

Not all extinctions are ecologically equal. The loss of a keystone
species -- one whose impact on its ecosystem is disproportionately
large relative to its abundance -- can trigger a trophic cascade
that restructures the entire community. The classic example is the
sea otter. When sea otters were hunted to near-extinction for their
fur, sea urchin populations exploded and overgrazed kelp forests,
destroying the habitat for countless fish and invertebrate species.
When otters were reintroduced, kelp forests recovered, demonstrating
that keystone species restoration can reverse ecosystem collapse.

Wolves in Yellowstone provide another well-documented example. After
wolves were exterminated in the 1920s, elk populations grew
unchecked and overbrowsed riparian vegetation, causing streambank
erosion and declines in beaver and songbird populations. The
reintroduction of wolves in 1995 triggered a behavioral cascade: elk
avoided open riparian areas, willows and aspens recovered, beavers
returned, and the physical structure of streams changed. The
ecosystem rewilded itself.

The practical implication is that biodiversity loss is not a simple
counting exercise. Losing one keystone species can eliminate
hundreds of dependent species indirectly. Conservation strategies
that focus narrowly on charismatic megafauna while ignoring
ecosystem engineers, top predators, and keystone mutualists may fail
to prevent ecosystem collapse even while appearing to protect
individual species.

### Biodiversity Hotspots and the Geography of Loss

Extinction risk is not evenly distributed across the planet.
Biodiversity hotspots -- regions that harbor exceptional
concentrations of endemic species and have lost at least 70 percent
of their original habitat -- contain roughly 50 percent of all plant
species and 42 percent of terrestrial vertebrates in just 2.4
percent of Earth's land surface. The 36 recognized hotspots include
the Tropical Andes, Madagascar, the Caribbean Islands, the
Mediterranean Basin, the Western Ghats of India, and Sundaland in
Southeast Asia. These regions are disproportionately threatened
because they combine high species richness with intense human
pressure from agriculture, logging, and infrastructure development.

Coral reefs represent an analogous marine hotspot. Though covering
less than 0.1 percent of the ocean floor, they support roughly 25
percent of all marine species. An estimated 50 percent of the
world's coral reefs have already been lost or severely degraded, and
projections suggest that without dramatic emissions reductions,
functional coral reef ecosystems may largely disappear within this
century.

## Evidence

The empirical case for a sixth mass extinction rests on multiple
independent lines of evidence. Ceballos, Ehrlich, and colleagues
(2015) published the most rigorous quantitative assessment in
Science Advances. Using a background extinction rate of 2 E/MSY --
deliberately chosen to be twice as high as earlier estimates to
avoid charges of alarmism -- they found that vertebrate extinction
rates over the last century were 8 to 100 times higher than
background. The number of vertebrate species that went extinct in
the last 100 years would have taken 800 to 10,000 years to disappear
at natural background rates. The authors concluded that the evidence
for a sixth mass extinction is incontrovertible even under
assumptions designed to minimize the appearance of crisis.

A 2017 follow-up study in Proceedings of the National Academy of
Sciences mapped the geographic distribution of population losses for
177 mammal species and found that all had lost at least 30 percent
of their geographic ranges, and more than 40 percent had lost over
80 percent. The authors described this as biological annihilation
and argued that the focus on species extinctions alone obscures the
scale of the crisis, because massive population declines precede and
predict extinctions.

The Living Planet Index, published biennially by WWF and the
Zoological Society of London, tracks population trends for
approximately 32,000 populations of 5,230 vertebrate species. The
2022 report documented an average 69 percent decline in monitored
vertebrate populations between 1970 and 2018. Freshwater species
showed the steepest decline at 83 percent. In Latin America and the
Caribbean, the decline reached 94 percent. These figures do not mean
that 69 percent of all individual animals have died -- the index
aggregates population trends and can be influenced by a small number
of severely declining populations -- but the directional signal
across all regions and taxa is unambiguous.

Insect declines represent the most alarming and least understood
dimension of the crisis. A 2017 study by Hallmann et al. documented
a 75 percent decline in flying insect biomass across 63 nature
preserves in Germany over 27 years. A 2019 global meta-analysis by
Sanchez-Bayo and Wyckhuys estimated that over 40 percent of insect
species are threatened with extinction, with Lepidoptera
(butterflies and moths), Hymenoptera (bees, wasps, ants), and
dung beetles among the most affected. Insects account for
approximately 80 percent of all described animal species and perform
irreplaceable ecosystem functions: pollination of 75 percent of
global food crops, decomposition of organic matter, and serving as
the base of terrestrial food webs. A collapse of insect populations
would cascade through every terrestrial ecosystem.

The IUCN Red List, the most comprehensive global inventory of
species conservation status, classifies more than 48,600 species as
threatened with extinction as of 2025, representing roughly 28
percent of all evaluated species. Among assessed groups, 41 percent
of amphibians, 34 percent of conifers, 33 percent of reef-building
corals, 26 percent of mammals, and 13 percent of birds are
threatened. These figures are almost certainly underestimates
because the vast majority of species -- particularly invertebrates,
fungi, and microorganisms -- have never been assessed.

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List
Index, which tracks aggregate extinction risk over time, shows a
steady decline since 1993 for all major taxonomic groups, indicating
that species are moving toward extinction faster than conservation
successes are pulling them back. Corals show the steepest decline,
followed by amphibians, mammals, and birds. No major taxonomic group
shows an improving trend.

## Implications

Biodiversity loss is not only an environmental crisis; it is an
economic, health, and security crisis. The World Economic Forum
consistently ranks biodiversity loss and ecosystem collapse among
the top five global risks by likelihood and impact over the next
decade. The economic value of ecosystem services -- pollination,
water purification, soil formation, climate regulation, pest
control, nutrient cycling -- has been estimated at 125 to 140
trillion USD per year, exceeding global GDP.

The pollination services provided by insects, birds, and bats
underpin an estimated 235 to 577 billion USD in annual global crop
output. More than 75 percent of global food crops, including fruits,
vegetables, nuts, and oils, depend at least partially on animal
pollinators. A continued decline in pollinator populations would
directly reduce yields of nutrient-dense foods, increasing food
prices and exacerbating malnutrition in regions already facing food
insecurity.

Biodiversity is also a pharmaceutical reservoir. Over 50 percent of
modern medicines are derived from or inspired by natural compounds:
aspirin from willow bark, paclitaxel (a cancer drug) from the
Pacific yew tree, artemisinin (an antimalarial) from sweet
wormwood, penicillin from fungi. Every species lost represents a
potential medical treatment that will never be discovered. Marine
organisms, in particular, have yielded compounds active against
cancer, inflammation, and bacterial infections -- and the vast
majority of marine species have never been screened for
pharmaceutical potential.

Forests and wetlands provide water purification services that would
cost trillions to replicate with infrastructure. New York City
invested 1.5 billion USD in watershed protection in the Catskill
Mountains rather than building a 6 to 8 billion USD water filtration
plant, relying on natural ecosystem services that were far cheaper
than the technological alternative. Since 1970, 35 percent of global
wetlands have been lost, reducing nature's water filtration capacity
at precisely the moment when climate change is intensifying both
floods and droughts.

Biodiversity loss is a threat multiplier for climate change and vice
versa. Deforestation in the Amazon reduces both biodiversity and the
planet's capacity to absorb carbon dioxide. The loss of coastal
mangroves eliminates nursery habitat for fish while also removing
natural storm surge protection for coastal communities. Peatland
drainage for agriculture releases stored carbon while destroying
unique bog ecosystems. These feedback loops mean that biodiversity
loss and climate change cannot be addressed in isolation; they are
two symptoms of the same underlying driver -- the scale of human
appropriation of the biosphere.

For investors and business, biodiversity loss creates both
systemic risk and transition risk. Supply chains dependent on
ecosystem services -- agriculture, fisheries, forestry, tourism --
face direct physical risk from ecosystem degradation. The
Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, adopted in 2022,
commits 196 countries to protecting 30 percent of land and sea by
2030 (the 30x30 target), creating regulatory risk for industries
that depend on habitat conversion. The Taskforce on Nature-related
Financial Disclosures (TNFD) is building frameworks for companies
and financial institutions to report and manage nature-related
risks, analogous to the TCFD for climate. For value investors
applying a long-time-horizon lens, biodiversity risk will
increasingly function as a material financial risk that markets have
not yet fully priced.
