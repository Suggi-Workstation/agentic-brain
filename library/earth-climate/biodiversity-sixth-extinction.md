# Biodiversity Loss Is Accelerating, but a Sixth Mass Extinction Is a Trajectory, Not a Completed Threshold

Biodiversity is declining at rates unprecedented in human history, and the
assessed global species-extinction rate is already at least tens to hundreds
of times the average over the past 10 million years ([1]). Yet the
paleontological benchmark for a mass extinction is loss of at least 75 percent
of species in a geologically short interval, a threshold the modern episode
has not completed; "sixth mass extinction" therefore describes an incipient
trajectory rather than a finished event ([2] [3]). The immediate crisis is the
human-driven erosion of populations, geographic ranges, genetic variation,
and ecological functions that precedes final species extinction ([1] [11]).

## Background

Biodiversity includes variation within species, differences among species,
and the composition and functioning of ecosystems. These levels are related
but not interchangeable. A species can remain globally extant while losing
most of its populations, range, or genetic diversity, and an ecosystem can
lose functional interactions before its component species disappear. The
IUCN Red List therefore records not only extinction but categories of
extinction risk, while population and range indicators reveal deterioration
that a binary extant-or-extinct count misses ([1] [4] [11]).

Extinction itself is a normal evolutionary process, but mass extinction is a
specific paleontological category. The fossil record identifies five intervals
in the past roughly 540 million years when extinction greatly exceeded
origination and at least about three quarters of species disappeared within a
geologically short interval: the end-Ordovician, late Devonian, end-Permian,
end-Triassic, and end-Cretaceous events. Their triggers and sequences differed,
but each reorganized the biosphere on a scale far beyond ordinary background
turnover ([2]). The comparison matters because it separates two questions:
whether modern extinction rates are abnormal, and whether cumulative modern
losses have already crossed the fossil-defined mass-extinction magnitude.
Evidence strongly supports the first proposition; it does not require claiming
that the second threshold has already been completed ([2] [3]).

Comparing the present with deep time is difficult. Fossils usually resolve
genera better than species, sample some environments and organisms more
completely than others, and compress events that modern observations divide
into years or decades. Modern records, by contrast, can follow individual
species and populations but cover only a small fraction of Earth's species and
a very short interval. Barnosky and colleagues explicitly adjusted for these
differences and concluded that recent rates resemble mass-extinction rates,
while also finding that observed modern losses had not yet reached the
75-percent magnitude benchmark. Their central warning was conditional: if
many currently threatened species disappear and high rates persist, the
threshold could be reached within centuries to millennia ([2]).

The modern scientific case developed from several complementary approaches.
Paleontological comparisons established the magnitude benchmark and long-term
background. Red List records supplied documented recent extinctions and risk
categories. Rate studies converted losses into extinctions per million
species-years, or E/MSY, so groups with different species totals and observation
periods could be compared. Range and abundance studies then showed that
population decline is much broader than confirmed species extinction. Finally,
intergovernmental assessments synthesized thousands of studies across taxa,
regions, and drivers rather than treating any single indicator as a complete
measure of biodiversity ([1] [2] [3] [11]).

The E/MSY denominator is essential. A rate of 2 E/MSY means two extinctions per
million species per year, not two total extinctions per year. Ceballos and
colleagues deliberately used 2 E/MSY as a conservative background rate for
vertebrates, twice a commonly used earlier estimate, and counted only the most
secure modern losses in their strictest comparison. Even under those choices,
average vertebrate loss over the preceding century reached as much as 100
times background, depending on taxon and treatment of extinct-in-the-wild and
possibly extinct species ([3]). IPBES later used broader evidence to conclude
that the global species-extinction rate was already at least tens to hundreds
of times the average over the past 10 million years and accelerating ([1]).

Status counts and rates answer different questions. The IUCN Red List version
2026-1 reports more than 175,900 assessed species, more than 49,500 of them
threatened, or 28 percent of assessed species. The assessed shares differ
strongly among groups: 41 percent of amphibians, 44 percent of reef-building
corals, 38 percent of sharks and rays, 26 percent of mammals, and 11 percent of
birds are threatened ([4]). These percentages describe assessed taxa, not a
random sample of all life. Plants, invertebrates, fungi, and many marine groups
remain incompletely assessed, so the Red List is a rigorous inventory of
assessed species rather than a census of every species on Earth ([4]).

A further distinction is between global extinction and local extirpation.
Global extinction ends an evolutionary lineage; extirpation removes a
population from part of its range. Repeated local losses shrink abundance,
connectivity, ecological influence, and adaptive variation long before the
last individual dies. In a study of 27,600 terrestrial vertebrate species,
32 percent were decreasing in population size and range, including many still
classified as low concern. A detailed subset of 177 mammals showed pervasive
range contraction between 1900 and 2015 ([11]). This population-first view
explains why waiting for confirmed global extinctions systematically detects
the crisis late.

The most accurate summary is therefore conditional and measurable. Humanity
has caused an exceptionally rapid, globally distributed episode of biodiversity
loss. Modern rates and projected losses are consistent with movement toward a
sixth mass extinction, while the fossil-defined cumulative threshold remains a
future outcome that can still be altered. That distinction is not semantic
caution: it preserves both the severity of current evidence and the practical
fact that preventing threatened species from becoming extinct changes the
trajectory ([1] [2] [14]).

## Core Concepts

### Four complementary measurements

No single metric captures biodiversity loss. Extinction counts provide the
most final outcome but are conservative because declaring extinction requires
extensive evidence and because many species have never been formally described.
Threat categories estimate risk using population, range, trend, and other
criteria. Abundance indices track changes within monitored populations. Range
maps identify contraction and fragmentation, while genetic and functional
measures test whether populations retain evolutionary options and ecological
roles. A defensible diagnosis uses several measures and states each denominator,
time window, taxonomic coverage, and uncertainty ([1] [4] [11] [12]).

This distinction prevents a common statistical error. The Living Planet Index
is a geometric mean of relative abundance trends in monitored vertebrate
populations. It is not the percentage of all animals that have vanished, the
percentage of species that are extinct, or a census of unmonitored taxa. The
2024 technical supplement reports a 73-percent average decline in relative
abundance between 1970 and 2020 across 34,836 monitored populations of 5,495
vertebrate species, with weighting intended to reduce geographic and taxonomic
imbalance. The report also documents representation limits and sensitivity to
new data ([12]). Its signal is important, but its meaning depends on the index's
construction.

### Direct drivers and their interactions

IPBES ranks five direct drivers by their global impact to date: changes in land
and sea use, direct exploitation of organisms, climate change, pollution, and
invasive alien species. The ranking is global; the dominant driver for a
particular species or place can differ. These direct pressures arise from
underlying production, consumption, trade, governance, technology, and
demographic patterns, and they frequently act together rather than in isolation
([1]).

**Land- and sea-use change** removes, degrades, or fragments habitat. Conversion
to cropland, pasture, settlements, roads, mines, dams, and altered coastal uses
changes both habitat area and connectivity. FAO's sample-based Global Forest
Resources Assessment remote-sensing survey found that agricultural expansion
drove almost 90 percent of global deforestation during 2000-2018: cropland
expansion caused almost half and livestock grazing 38.5 percent ([5]). These
figures concern deforestation, not all habitat loss, but they show why food and
land systems are central to terrestrial biodiversity.

Fragmentation can matter even when some habitat remains. Smaller and more
isolated populations receive fewer immigrants, experience stronger demographic
and genetic stochasticity, and may lose access to seasonal or climate-shifting
habitat. The eventual loss can be delayed. Extinction debt is the future
extinction expected from past habitat reduction or fragmentation even without
additional habitat destruction. Long-lived species and populations near a
viability threshold may persist for years or decades before the demographic
consequences become visible, so current presence does not by itself demonstrate
long-term security ([8]).

**Direct exploitation** includes fishing, hunting, logging, harvesting, and
wildlife trade at rates that populations cannot replace. It can reduce a target
species directly and alter food webs when large predators, herbivores, or
habitat-forming organisms are removed. IPBES identifies direct exploitation as
the second-largest direct driver globally to date, while its importance is
especially high in marine systems ([1]). The relevant management variable is
not whether use occurs but whether total mortality, including illegal and
incidental take, remains compatible with recovery and ecological function.

**Climate change** shifts temperature, precipitation, seasonality, disturbance
regimes, ocean conditions, and the location of suitable habitat. Species can
persist through acclimation, adaptation, movement, or access to refugia, but
those routes are limited for isolated, slow-reproducing, mountain-top, island,
and reef organisms. Climate change also amplifies other drivers: fragmented
landscapes obstruct range shifts, heat stress increases disease and fire risk,
and altered conditions can favor invasive species ([1] [6] [7]). The IPCC
projected that warm-water coral reefs would decline a further 70-90 percent at
1.5 C of global warming and more than 99 percent at 2 C, illustrating the large
ecological difference associated with an additional half degree ([6]).

**Pollution** acts through nutrients, pesticides, toxic chemicals, plastics,
light, noise, and other stressors. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus can reorganize
freshwater and coastal food webs; toxic exposures can reduce survival or
reproduction below levels that immediately produce obvious extinction; and
light or noise can disrupt movement, feeding, mating, and predator avoidance.
Pollution often interacts with habitat and climate stress, so a concentration
that a robust population could tolerate may become consequential when numbers
and refuges are already reduced ([1] [10] [13]).

**Invasive alien species** are organisms introduced by human activity outside
their natural range that establish and cause harm. Their effects include
predation, competition, disease transmission, hybridization, and ecosystem
change. IPBES's 2023 assessment, which synthesized more than 13,000 references,
reported that invasive alien species had contributed to 60 percent of recorded
global plant and animal extinctions and were the sole driver in 16 percent.
More than 37,000 alien species were recorded worldwide, of which more than
3,500 were documented as invasive ([7]). Island endemics are especially
vulnerable because small ranges and evolutionary isolation can coincide with
novel predators, competitors, or pathogens.

The five-driver list is not five independent boxes. A forest fragment exposed
to hunting may lose seed dispersers; warming and drought may then increase fire;
roads may facilitate further extraction and introductions. Conversely, reducing
one pressure can raise resilience to another. The author's synthesis is that
conservation should diagnose interacting causal chains rather than assign every
population decline to one headline driver. This follows IPBES's finding that
multiple direct and indirect drivers compound across ecosystems ([1] [7]).

### Populations, functions, and trophic cascades

Species counts treat all losses as equal units, but ecological effects depend
on abundance, traits, and interactions. A keystone species has an effect on
community structure that is large relative to its abundance. An ecosystem
engineer changes physical habitat, while a foundation species creates much of
the habitat used by others. Removing a top consumer can release prey or
herbivores and propagate change across several trophic levels. Estes and
colleagues reviewed such trophic downgrading in marine, freshwater, and
terrestrial systems, linking losses of apex consumers to changes in vegetation,
disease, fire, carbon storage, invasive species, and nutrient cycles ([9]).

The sea otter-urchin-kelp system is a well-supported example. Sea otters consume
urchins; where otters are lost, urchin grazing can convert kelp forests into
urchin barrens, changing habitat and production for fishes and invertebrates.
The general lesson is not that every predator restoration will reproduce the
same chain. Food webs contain alternative prey, human harvest, climate effects,
and spatial variation. The evidence supports strong top-down effects in many
systems while requiring local tests before attributing every ecosystem change
to a single predator ([9]).

Functional erosion may therefore precede species extinction. A pollinator can
remain present but too scarce or poorly distributed to provide reliable
pollination; a large herbivore can persist in reserves while no longer shaping
most of its former landscape; a fragmented tree population can survive while
losing gene flow. Range, abundance, interaction strength, and demographic
structure are thus leading indicators of whether biodiversity still performs
its ecological roles ([1] [9] [10] [11]).

### Nature's contributions to people

Biodiversity supports food production, water regulation, soil formation,
carbon storage, coastal protection, disease regulation, and cultural values.
These contributions are produced by ecological processes, not by species names
alone. IPBES's pollination assessment found that more than three quarters of
leading global food-crop types benefit at least partly from animal pollination,
while 5-8 percent of global crop production by volume was directly attributable
to animal pollination. It valued the directly affected annual crop output at
USD 235-577 billion in 2015 dollars ([10]). The crop-type percentage should not
be misreported as three quarters of total food volume.

Dependence also does not mean that one service has one substitute. Managed
honey bees can provide substantial crop pollination, but diverse wild
pollinators stabilize pollination across weather, landscapes, and crop types.
Likewise, built infrastructure can replace selected water-control or coastal
functions but rarely duplicates the entire bundle supplied by intact wetlands,
forests, reefs, and floodplains. IPBES emphasizes that human-made substitutes
frequently fail to provide the full range of nature's contributions ([1] [10]).

### Geography, endemism, and representation

Extinction risk is spatially uneven. Species with small ranges, specialized
habitats, low reproductive rates, or isolated populations can lose a large
share of their global distribution from one local disturbance. Tropical
forests, islands, freshwaters, mountains, and coral reefs combine high
endemism or specialization with strong human pressures in many regions. This
is why protecting a percentage of area without representing unique species,
connecting habitats, or managing threats can leave major risk unchanged
([1] [6] [7] [15]).

Evidence is also spatially uneven. Birds and mammals are monitored more often
than many invertebrates, fungi, plants, and microorganisms; temperate regions
have denser time series than many tropical regions; and accessible or valued
species attract more study. The absence of a measured decline can therefore
mean stability, insufficient monitoring, or an unsuitable indicator. A sound
assessment states where evidence is strong and does not extrapolate a local
study into a global rate without an appropriate synthesis ([1] [4] [12] [13]).

## Evidence

### Extinction rates exceed conservative backgrounds

Ceballos and colleagues tested modern vertebrate losses against a deliberately
high background rate of 2 E/MSY. Their strict analysis counted 338 vertebrate
species recorded as extinct since 1500; a broader conservative treatment added
279 extinct-in-the-wild or possibly extinct species. The study compared losses
since 1900 with the number expected under background and found average modern
rates up to 100 times higher. At background rates, the vertebrate species lost
in the preceding century would ordinarily have taken roughly 800 to 10,000
years to disappear, depending on the taxon and treatment of uncertain losses
([3]).

This study's strength was not a precise census of all extinction. It was a
conservative rate comparison using well-documented vertebrates and an elevated
background. Its limits are equally important: vertebrates are not all life,
modern extinction designations lag reality, and E/MSY estimates depend on the
background and observation window. The result demonstrates exceptional recent
rates; it does not by itself measure the cumulative fraction of every species
lost or the future duration of those rates ([2] [3]).

Barnosky and colleagues approached the question from the fossil side. They
reviewed differences between fossil and modern data, compared rates and
magnitudes, and used the loss of at least 75 percent of species as the
conservative mass-extinction benchmark. They found modern rates outside normal
background ranges but modern cumulative losses still far short of 75 percent
for the well-assessed groups examined. Scenarios in which threatened species
were lost and those rates persisted reached the mass-extinction threshold in
centuries to millennia, which makes the modern episode a preventable trajectory
rather than a completed paleontological event ([2]).

### Global synthesis shows broad human causation

The 2019 IPBES Global Assessment integrated approximately 15,000 scientific and
government sources with additional knowledge, rather than extrapolating from
one taxon or region. It concluded that nature was declining globally at rates
unprecedented in human history, that around one quarter of species in
sufficiently assessed animal and plant groups were threatened, and that roughly
one million animal and plant species could face extinction, many within decades,
without reduced driver intensity. It estimated current global extinction rates
at least tens to hundreds of times the average over the past 10 million years
and identified land/sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change,
pollution, and invasive alien species as the leading direct drivers in that
order globally to date ([1]).

The one-million estimate is not a count of named species already scheduled to
disappear. It combines the threatened share in assessed groups with an estimate
of total animal and plant diversity and a conservative treatment of insects.
IPBES labels relevant components of this inference with confidence terms and
states that many outcomes depend on future action. The result is best read as a
scale estimate of risk under current pressures, not a dated extinction forecast
for one million individually identified species ([1]).

The current Red List offers a complementary, species-by-species snapshot.
Version 2026-1 reports more than 49,500 threatened species among more than
175,900 assessed. Threat is concentrated differently among assessed groups,
including 44 percent of reef-building corals and 41 percent of amphibians,
compared with 11 percent of birds. Because assessment coverage is incomplete
and nonrandom, these figures should be reported as proportions of assessed
species, not percentages of all species on Earth ([4]).

### Population and range loss reveal earlier stages

Ceballos, Ehrlich, and Dirzo analyzed IUCN trend information for 27,600
terrestrial vertebrate species, nearly half of known vertebrates at the time.
They found 8,851 species, or 32 percent of the sample, decreasing in population
size and range. In a more detailed analysis of 177 mammals from 1900 to 2015,
all had lost at least 30 percent of their geographic ranges and more than
40 percent had lost over 80 percent. The sampling focused on species with data
suitable for the analysis, so the exact percentages are not universal estimates
for all animals. The study nevertheless demonstrates that severe local and
population loss can be widespread while many affected species remain globally
extant ([11]).

The 2024 Living Planet Index supplies a different population measure. Its
global index used 34,836 time series for 5,495 native vertebrate species and
estimated a 73-percent average decline in relative population abundance from
1970 to 2020, with a 95-percent interval from 67 to 78 percent decline.
Freshwater, terrestrial, and marine sub-indices showed different trajectories.
The technical supplement explains that the index aggregates relative trends,
uses weights to address representation, and changes as data are added or
corrected. It therefore corroborates broad population deterioration but must
not be translated into "73 percent of animals died" or "73 percent of species
went extinct" ([12]).

### Focused studies expose mechanisms and limits

Hallmann and colleagues measured flying-insect biomass with standardized
Malaise traps across 63 protected areas in western Germany, yielding 96 unique
site-year combinations over 27 years. Their model estimated a 76-percent
seasonal decline and an 82-percent midsummer decline in flying-insect biomass.
The design provides strong evidence for the sampled lowland protected-area
network, and the authors tested weather, land use, and habitat covariates
without explaining the overall decline. It does not establish a global
75-percent insect decline or identify one universal cause; its importance is
that severe biomass loss occurred even inside protected areas embedded in a
human-dominated landscape ([13]).

The invasive-species evidence illustrates a different causal pathway. IPBES's
2023 assessment synthesized more than 13,000 references and found that invasive
alien species contributed to 60 percent of recorded global plant and animal
extinctions and were the sole driver in 16 percent. It recorded more than 3,500
harmful invasive species among more than 37,000 established alien species.
These findings distinguish "alien" from "invasive": origin outside a natural
range does not alone establish harm, while documented negative effects define
the management problem ([7]).

### Conservation changes outcomes

Biodiversity decline is not evidence that intervention is futile. Bolam and
colleagues used expert elicitation and documented conservation histories to
estimate counterfactual extinctions among birds and mammals. They concluded
that action prevented 21-32 bird and 7-16 mammal extinctions during 1993-2020.
Given 10 confirmed or suspected bird extinctions and five mammal extinctions in
the comparison, extinction rates would have been 2.9-4.2 times greater without
conservation. The ranges express uncertainty about a counterfactual, and many
saved species remained highly threatened, but the study directly shows that
site protection, invasive-species control, legislation, captive management,
and reintroduction can prevent final loss ([14]).

Taken together, the evidence is stronger than any single headline. Fossil
comparisons define magnitude; modern rate studies show acceleration; Red List
assessments measure risk; range and abundance studies reveal pre-extinction
erosion; driver assessments identify causes; and conservation counterfactuals
show that outcomes respond to action. The author's assessment is that the term
"sixth mass extinction" is scientifically useful only when accompanied by the
metric being claimed: abnormal rate, population decline, threatened share, or
cumulative species loss ([1] [2] [3] [4] [11] [12] [14]).

## Implications

### Diagnose the unit before choosing the remedy

A practical response begins by identifying what is declining and why. A species
with a small stable range faces a different problem from a formerly common
species declining across a continent. A population limited by harvest requires
different intervention from one limited by invasive predation, lost breeding
habitat, pollution, or climate-driven heat. Monitoring should therefore pair
status with abundance, range, demographic rates, habitat condition, and direct
pressure. This avoids treating protected-area designation, captive breeding,
or restoration as interchangeable solutions ([1] [4] [7]).

The same discipline applies to communication. Report extinction rates with the
background and denominator; report Red List percentages as shares of assessed
taxa; report population indices as average relative trends; and separate local
extirpation from global extinction. These rules prevent both understatement
and exaggeration. In particular, the evidence does not require saying that
75 percent of species have already vanished, and a 73-percent population index
decline does not mean that 73 percent of individual animals disappeared
([2] [4] [12]).

### Protect intact systems and reconnect fragments

Avoiding habitat conversion usually preserves more biological information and
function than attempting to reconstruct it after loss. Where conversion has
already occurred, increasing patch size, improving condition, restoring
corridors, and reducing edge pressures can lower isolation and help species
track climate. Extinction debt means restoration may be urgent even where
species remain present, because current occupancy can reflect past habitat
rather than future viability ([5] [8]). Protection must include management of
harvest, fire, pollution, invasive species, and other pressures; a boundary on
a map does not by itself maintain populations, as the German insect study
illustrates ([13]).

The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework's Target 3 reflects these
requirements more carefully than the shorthand "30x30." It calls for at least
30 percent of terrestrial and inland-water areas and at least 30 percent of
coastal and marine areas to be effectively conserved and managed by 2030,
especially areas important for biodiversity and ecosystem functions. It also
requires ecological representation, connectivity, equitable governance,
integration into wider landscapes and seascapes, and respect for the rights of
Indigenous peoples and local communities ([15]). Area alone is therefore not
the target's full ecological or social content.

### Reduce each driver at its source

Land-use intervention should focus on avoiding conversion of high-integrity and
high-biodiversity systems while improving production on already managed land
without exporting pressure elsewhere. FAO's finding that agricultural expansion
drove almost 90 percent of deforestation during 2000-2018 links biodiversity
outcomes to crop, livestock, and supply-chain decisions, not only to reserve
agencies ([5]). Marine and freshwater management likewise requires sustainable
harvest, protection of breeding and nursery habitat, maintenance of flows, and
reduction of incidental mortality and pollution ([1]).

Climate mitigation is biodiversity policy because each increment of warming
raises ecological risk. The IPCC coral projections show why a change that may
sound numerically small can separate severe loss from near-total loss in a
vulnerable ecosystem. Adaptation measures should maintain elevational,
latitudinal, river, and coastal pathways where possible, but movement and local
management cannot substitute for limiting warming when climatic niches or
physical habitats disappear ([6]).

Invasive-species management should distinguish prevention, early detection,
eradication where feasible, and long-term control. Prevention and rapid
response are generally most plausible before an introduced population spreads.
On islands and other bounded systems, eradication can remove a primary
extinction pressure; in widespread invasions, sustained containment and
protection of priority sites may be more realistic. The IPBES assessment shows
that biological invasions interact with trade, climate, and land use, so border
controls alone are incomplete ([7]).

Pollution control must address mixtures and chronic exposure as well as acute
toxicity. Nutrient management, pesticide risk reduction, wastewater treatment,
and limits on persistent pollutants protect species while also preserving
water and food-system functions. The pollinator assessment identifies habitat
diversity, reduced pesticide exposure, integrated pest management, disease
control, and better movement regulation for managed pollinators as practical
risk-reduction options ([10]).

### Protect functions as well as species totals

Prioritization should consider irreplaceability, threat, ecological function,
and feasibility. A narrowly endemic species represents irreplaceable
evolutionary history; a declining foundation species can alter habitat for many
others; a top consumer can structure food webs; and a pollinator community can
stabilize crop yield and wild-plant reproduction. Population abundance and
spatial distribution matter because a species cannot perform its former role
across landscapes from one small remnant ([9] [10] [11]).

Pollination illustrates the human consequence. More than three quarters of
leading crop types benefit at least partly from animal pollination, but the
directly attributable share of global production volume is estimated at
5-8 percent. The distinction matters for planning: losses disproportionately
affect many fruits, vegetables, nuts, seeds, and oils rather than all calories
equally. Maintaining diverse wild pollinators, habitat, and low-exposure farm
practices supports both crop production and reproduction of wild flowering
plants ([10]).

### Measure recovery and preserve uncertainty

Success should be measured as changed biological outcomes, not activities
alone. Useful measures include avoided conversion, improved habitat condition,
reduced mortality, increased recruitment, stabilized abundance, restored
range, genetic connectivity, and reduced Red List risk. Different indicators
move on different timescales, so a short-term population increase does not
prove durable recovery, while delayed improvement does not prove an
intervention failed ([4] [8] [12]).

Uncertainty should guide monitoring and precaution rather than paralysis. The
one-million-species estimate, the Living Planet Index, and conservation
counterfactuals each answer different questions with explicit assumptions.
Reporting those assumptions makes evidence more actionable. It also prevents a
false choice between certainty and action: the demonstrated rates, threatened
shares, driver mechanisms, and population losses are already sufficient to
justify measures whose benefits can be tested and adjusted ([1] [12] [14]).

Conservation can work, but prevented extinction is often a continuing
obligation rather than a permanent victory. The species identified by Bolam and
colleagues frequently remained dependent on habitat protection, invasive-species
control, legal safeguards, captive populations, or reintroduction. Recovery
therefore requires durable institutions and repeated measurement, not one-time
rescue ([14]).

The central implication is precise. Earth has not irreversibly completed the
fossil-defined sixth mass extinction threshold, but human activity has already
created extinction rates, threatened shares, and population declines far
outside recent natural baselines. Preventing additional habitat loss, reducing
direct pressures, limiting warming, reconnecting populations, and restoring
ecological interactions can still change both the rate and eventual magnitude
of loss. Calling the episode a trajectory preserves the urgency of the evidence
and the agency demonstrated by successful conservation ([1] [2] [6] [14]).
