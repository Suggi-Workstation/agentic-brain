# Soil Systems and the Critical Zone -- Why Soil Is a Slowly Renewable Earth-System Archive, Not Interchangeable Substrate

Soil is the weathered, biologically active upper part of the critical zone, the column that runs from the vegetation canopy through soil and weathered rock to fresh bedrock and the base of the groundwater [1]. Measured rates show that plowed fields lose soil one to two orders of magnitude faster than soil is produced, that soil organic carbon persists because of its physical and biological surroundings rather than its molecular chemistry, and that an estimated 59 percent of species live in soil [2][3][8]. Treating soil as interchangeable substrate therefore misstates how it works: its mechanisms are slow, coupled to rock, water, climate and organisms, and only partly reversible on human timescales.

## Background

Recognition that accelerated soil erosion harms agricultural societies dates back to Plato and Aristotle, and several now-classic studies attributed the bare rocky slopes of the classical world to ancient soil erosion [2]. Archaeological work later tied pronounced erosion episodes to the rise and decline of civilizations in the Middle East, Greece, Rome and Mesoamerica [2]. Most commentators blamed deforestation and neglected the role of agriculture in sustaining accelerated erosion on uplands [2]. In the 1950s the US Department of Agriculture set soil-loss tolerance values, called T values, against which to judge "acceptable" erosion. They correspond to about 5-12 tons per hectare per year, or about 0.4-1 mm of soil per year at a bulk density of 1,200 kg per cubic meter [2]. Some researchers argued that T values were set well above soil production rates for political and economic reasons, and a 1998 chapter quoted by Montgomery maintained that "seldom has such an important policy been based on such a dearth of defendable data" [2]. The data needed to test that argument, namely natural soil production rates and long-term geological erosion rates, had only recently become available when Montgomery compiled them in 2007 [2].

A second lineage is place-based observation. The Rothamsted Experimental Station in England was founded in 1843 to study the effects of fertilizers on crops [1], and its long-running plots later became the largest concentration of long-term soil organic carbon data in the world [5]. Paired small watersheds at Watson Wheel Gap, Colorado, began in 1910 to measure streamflow and sediment yield after land-use treatments, and the approach was replicated at Coweeta, Hubbard Brook, Reynolds Creek and the H.J. Andrews Experimental Forest [1]. The US National Science Foundation (NSF) created the Long-Term Ecological Research program in the early 1980s, the US Geological Survey began its Water, Energy, and Biogeochemical Budgets program in 1991, the Long-Term Agroecosystem Research program started in 2011, and the National Ecological Observatory Network was added later [1]. These programs measured water, ecosystems and agriculture, but Brantley and colleagues argue that no nationwide effort had treated the near-surface as one entity that included its deep geological underpinnings and long timescales [1].

The critical-zone concept filled that gap. In 2001 the US National Research Council defined the zone from the upper vegetative canopy through groundwater as the critical zone and named its study a basic research opportunity in the Earth sciences [1]. NSF launched the Critical Zone Observatory program in 2007 with three observatories, and by 2013 there were nine [1]. NSF describes the critical zone as the constantly evolving boundary layer where rock, soil, water, air and living organisms interact [11]. The observatory program was succeeded by the Critical-Zone Collaborative Network, and in August 2024 NSF announced that the network would be archived and stop accepting proposals starting with fiscal year 2025, stating that critical-zone research had outgrown the need for a special call for proposals because many NSF programs now support it [11]. By 2017, interdisciplinary field observatories hosting critical-zone science existed in more than 25 nations, including programs in France, Germany, the United Kingdom and China [1]. The sources used here do not establish the status of non-US critical-zone programs after 2017.

A third lineage concerns soil organic matter (SOM). For decades SOM was characterized by alkaline and acid extraction into operationally defined humic acids, fulvic acids and "humin" [8]. Doubts that humic substances are artifacts of extraction were recorded as early as the 1840s, and much of the SOM research community had dismissed the humic-substance view by the 2010s [10]. In 2011 a group of fourteen researchers argued that the persistence of soil organic carbon is primarily not a molecular property but an ecosystem property, because physical, chemical and biological conditions around organic matter reduce its probability of decomposition [8]. In a paper first published online in 2019, Lavallee, Soong and Cotrufo then proposed separating SOM into particulate (POM) and mineral-associated (MAOM) forms as a consistent, workable framework [10].

A fourth lineage concerns soil life. The earlier global estimate of soil biodiversity, by Decaens and colleagues, held that at least 25 percent of described animal species live in or on soil. That figure was widely adopted and often incorrectly cited as if it covered all species rather than only animals [3]. A 2023 enumeration across the tree of life estimated that 59 percent of species live in soil [3].

The stakes explain why these lineages converged. Soils hold at least three times as much carbon as the atmosphere or living plants [8], and roughly 50 million square kilometers of soil are managed by humans for food, fiber and livestock [7]. This topic covers the Earth-system mechanisms of soil. It does not give farming practice recommendations, discuss land regulation, or cover engineered remediation; where management experiments appear, they are used as evidence about soil mechanisms [5].

## Core Concepts

### The critical zone as one coupled system

The critical zone has three co-evolving surfaces: the top of the vegetative canopy, the ground surface, and a deep subsurface below which Earth materials are unweathered [1]. Critical-zone science measures fluxes of solutes, water, energy, gases and sediments, which Brantley and colleagues abbreviate as SWEGS, and compares them with the histories those fluxes left in landforms, regolith, soils and sediments across timescales from milliseconds to millions of years [1]. The central claim of the framework is that inputs at the surface, such as rain, dust, vegetation and land use, propagate downward into water, regolith structure, minerals and microbial activity at depth, and that the deep structure in turn governs the water available to plants and so influences aboveground ecosystems and regional climate [1]. Three network-level generalizations from up to ten years of observatory work illustrate this. Differences in natural and human inputs at the surface produce differences in water, regolith structure, minerals and biotic activity at depth. The deep weathering surface varies across landscapes in patterned ways that relate depth, fracture density, porosity and weathering. And mechanistic models now give quantitative predictions of the spatial structure of the deep surface relative to the ground-surface topography [1]. Interpretation: for soil, the practical consequence is that a soil profile is the thin top of a thicker weathered column, and its behavior cannot be read from the top tens of centimeters alone.

### Soil formation and the production-erosion balance

Soil forms where rock is converted into mobile, granular material. The state-factor view treats soil properties as a function of climate, organisms, relief, parent material and time, with human activity as a sixth factor [7][8]. Soil thickness at a location reflects a balance between soil production, the conversion of weathered bedrock to mobile soil, and erosion. Montgomery's global compilation of 1,673 measurements found that median soil production (0.017 mm per year), median erosion under native vegetation (0.013 mm per year) and median long-term geological erosion (0.029 mm per year) fall in similar ranges, which supports the concept that hillslope soils evolve toward a characteristic equilibrium thickness for a given climate and geological setting [2]. Soil production is not constant with depth. Production rates were long hypothesized, and only recently documented, to decline exponentially with increasing soil thickness, a relationship termed the soil production function [13]. A summary on the SERC (Science Education Resource Center) site gives the relation SPR = 47 e^(-0.02 H) for a southeastern Australian dataset attributed to Heimsath and colleagues, with SPR in meters per million years and H soil depth in centimeters, and describes a "humped" alternative in which production peaks beneath a thin soil [13]. As an illustration, and as the author's calculation from that formula rather than a measurement, production would fall from 47 m per million years at zero depth to about 17 at 50 cm and about 6 at 100 cm, so thin soils regenerate faster than thick ones, but only within the range of the fitted data.

When erosion E exceeds production P, the critical time to erode through a profile of initial thickness S is Tc = S / (E - P) [2]. Using E = 1 mm per year, the order-of-magnitude average net loss Montgomery infers for conventional agriculture, and P = 0.017 mm per year, the author's calculation gives about 305 years for a 30 cm soil and about 1,017 years for a 1 m soil. Montgomery reports the same order of magnitude, a few hundred to a few thousand years, for decimeter- to meter-thick soils [2]. The calculation shows why soil is a stock that renews on geological timescales yet can be spent on historical ones.

### Soil architecture: soil, saprolite and weathered bedrock

Rempe and Dietrich define soil as the physically mobile granular surface mantle that lacks relict rock structure. Beneath it lies saprolite, soil-like weathered rock that keeps the structure of the parent rock, and below that weathered bedrock whose fracturing and weathering decrease with depth down to fresh bedrock [9]. At their northern California hillslope, soil is 30-75 cm thick over up to 4 m of saprolite and up to 20 m of weathered bedrock, and the weathering profile thickens from 4 m near the channel to 25 m at the divide [9]. Soil thickness and weathered-bedrock thickness vary independently [9]. Classification schemes address the top of this column. USDA soil taxonomy groups soils into twelve orders: Alfisols, Andisols, Aridisols, Entisols, Gelisols, Histosols, Inceptisols, Mollisols, Oxisols, Spodosols, Ultisols and Vertisols [12]. The author's reading is that such a classification characterizes the soil layer only, and that the deeper structure described in [1] and [9] needs separate characterization by drilling and geophysics.

### Soil organic matter: persistence as an ecosystem property

Soil organic matter is the main soil carbon store, and understanding why it persists requires abandoning two older ideas. The first is that molecular "recalcitrance" explains persistence. Compound-specific isotope work found that molecules predicted to persist, such as lignin and plant lipids, turn over faster than bulk soil organic matter, while compounds that look labile, such as sugars, can persist for decades [8]. The second is that large, stable humic macromolecules dominate SOM. Direct in situ observation finds smaller, simpler molecules rather than the large macromolecules inferred from extraction, and the evidence does not support humic polymers as a quantitatively important route to humus [8]. Persistence instead results from interactions between organic matter and its environment, including reactive mineral surfaces, climate, water availability, acidity, redox state and the presence of decomposers in the immediate microenvironment [8]. Microorganisms occupy considerably less than 1 percent of soil volume, and physical disconnection between decomposers and organic matter is a likely reason deep SOM persists [8].

Lavallee and colleagues turn this view into an operational scheme. Particulate organic matter (POM) is made of relatively undecomposed lightweight fragments with a mean residence time of under 10 years to decades, a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of 10-40, and no saturation limit. Mineral-associated organic matter (MAOM) consists of small molecules or fragments of plant and microbial origin, enriched in microbial-derived compounds and protected by association with minerals, with a mean residence time of decades to centuries and a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of 8-13, and it can saturate [10]. MAOM forms by two routes: low-molecular-weight compounds released from plant litter attach directly to minerals, or microbes transform material and their necromass or exudates attach to minerals [10]. They propose operational definitions in which MAOM is denser than 1.6-1.85 g per cubic centimeter and smaller than 50-63 micrometers, and dissolved organic matter, typically under 2 percent of SOM, is not treated separately [10]. The framework matters because the two fractions respond differently: tillage and thawing quickly raise POM decomposition, while MAOM must first be destabilized, for instance by a change in redox chemistry that causes desorption [10].

Three further points from Schmidt and colleagues shape any soil-carbon account. Root-derived carbon is retained in soil much more efficiently than aboveground leaf and needle inputs [8]. Subsoil horizons hold more than half of global soil carbon stocks, with radiocarbon ages of 1,000 to more than 10,000 years common, and their response to land-use change can equal that of the top 30 cm [8]. Fire-derived organic matter can make up as much as 40 percent of SOM in grasslands and boreal forests, but it is not inert, and its decomposition pathways remain poorly understood [8].

### Water: soil moisture and rock moisture

Soil moisture has been studied intensively because it regulates evaporation and transpiration, but water for transpiration is often drawn from below the soil [9]. Rempe and Dietrich coined "rock moisture" for the exchangeable water stored in the matrix and fractures of the unsaturated weathered bedrock beneath soil, a term parallel to but distinct from soil moisture because weathered bedrock thickness is independent of soil thickness [9]. In the rock-moisture reservoir, storage accumulates through the wet season until a characteristic maximum is reached and further rain passes quickly to groundwater; in the dry season trees gradually deplete it [9]. The reservoir therefore mediates the onset and size of recharge and runoff, and it is missing from the hydrologic and land-surface models used to predict regional and global climate [9]. Observatory scientists also list, as emergent hypotheses rather than settled findings, that the deep microbial community is linked to overlying vegetation (agricultural field, brush, grassland, perennial or deciduous forest) and to lithology (granite, basalt, shale, sandstone) [1].

### Nutrients and weathering supply

Solutes are one of the five flux types that critical-zone observatories track, and Brantley and colleagues frame water quality regulation, soil development and carbon stabilization as "critical zone services" that depend on the function, evolution and architecture of the whole column [1]. Nitrogen couples tightly to carbon storage. MAOM has a lower carbon-to-nitrogen ratio than POM, so building MAOM requires more nitrogen, and turnover of MAOM can be an important nitrogen source for microbes and plants [10]. Nitrogen fertilizer can raise soil organic carbon where other nutrients and water are adequate, but the associated nitrous oxide and manufacturing emissions can offset the climate benefit [5]. Erosion carries away nitrogen, phosphorus and organic carbon, and global fertilizer consumption of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium has been growing by about 1.5, 2.2 and 2.4 percent per year respectively [4]. The sources read for this topic do not include a primary study of how phosphorus limitation changes with soil age on geological timescales, so that mechanism is not developed here.

### Soil life

Soil organisms mediate functions on which food production and biogeochemical cycles depend [3]. The 2023 enumeration estimates that 85 percent of plant species, 43 percent of bacterial species (central estimate, with a range of 22 to 89 percent), about 43 percent of nematode species, and 30 percent of arthropod species live in soil, whereas only 3.8 percent of mammal species do [3]. Termites (Isoptera) and millipedes (Diplopoda) are the most soil-specialized arthropod groups, at 84 and 77 percent of species respectively [3]. Between 22 and 67 percent of terrestrial plant biomass is belowground [3]. These numbers are model-based estimates with wide error ranges; the authors describe the field as highly fragmented and speculative [3]. Interpretation: the biological side of soil is not a layer on top of the physical side. Microbial products are themselves important components of SOM [8], so the biodiversity estimate and the carbon-persistence framework describe two views of the same living system.

## Degradation Mechanisms

### Erosion

Erosion is the removal and transport of soil by water or wind, and the FAO's 2026 assessment, as summarized in its news release, ranks it the most severe and interconnected threat to soil health: across seven regions, 81 percent of assessments judged erosion management poor or very poor and 65 percent reported a deteriorating trend since 2015 [14]. Quantitatively, Borrelli and colleagues estimate global soil erosion by water (sheet and rill processes) at 43 Pg per year for 2015, with a stated range of minus 7 to plus 9.2 Pg, of which main agricultural lands (annual crops, permanent crops and managed pasture) contribute 54 percent [4]. Annual crops cover about 16 percent of land and produce about 41 percent of the total [4]. Conventional plowing erodes fields at rates typical of steep alpine terrain, whereas conservation tillage and terracing erode at rates near the geological norm [2].

### Salinization

Soil salinization is excessive accumulation of soluble salts. Primary salinization arises from natural sources such as oceanic salts in rain and dust and weathering of parent rock, and secondary salinization arises from human interventions such as irrigation with brackish water, rising water tables, seawater intrusion and fertilizer overuse [6]. Soil with an electrical conductivity of the saturated paste extract (ECe) of at least 2 dS per meter is traditionally classed as saline; 2-4 dS per meter can reduce yields of sensitive plants, and above 8 dS per meter most crops decline severely [6]. Salinity also reduces the biological functioning of soil microorganisms enough to disturb the nitrogen cycle, respiration and organic matter input [6]. Global estimates of salt-affected soil area range from about 8.3 to 11.7 million square kilometers depending on the method [6].

### Loss of soil carbon

Conversion of native soil to agriculture typically lowers soil organic carbon, with a meta-analysis median loss of 26 percent in the top 30 cm and 16 percent in the top 100 cm, but with reported ranges from a 36 percent gain to a 78 percent loss in the top 30 cm [7]. Loss is not universal: modest gains occur where naturally infertile soil is improved, as in the Brazilian Cerrado [7]. Sanderman and colleagues also list accelerated erosion, desertification, salinization, acidification, compaction, biodiversity loss and nutrient depletion as consequences of human domination of soil resources [7].

### Threats not quantified here

Compaction, contamination and acidification are recognized soil threats, but this topic's sources provide no primary quantitative evidence on compaction or contamination, so they are named without figures. Acidification appears only in one Rothamsted comparison: a woodland site that stayed above pH 7 accumulated 56 percent more organic carbon than a nearby site whose pH fell from 7.1 to 4.4, although the passage read does not isolate pH as the cause [5].

## Evidence

### Montgomery (2007): a global compilation of erosion and production rates

Montgomery compiled 1,673 measurements from 201 studies of soil production, long-term geological erosion, erosion under native vegetation, and erosion from agricultural fields. The measurements came from cosmogenic beryllium-10 and aluminum-26 studies, stratigraphic constraints, lake sedimentation, caesium-137 and soil-loss plots, closed-basin deposition, soil profile truncation and elevated cemetery plots [2]. Sediment-yield studies of large drainage basins and floodplains were excluded because deposition obscures upland erosion [2]. Median rates were 1.537 mm per year for conventional agriculture (n = 448), 0.082 for conservation agriculture (n = 47), 0.013 for native vegetation (n = 65), 0.017 for soil production (n = 188) and 0.029 for geological erosion (n = 925) [2]. In 46 direct comparisons of the same or similar land, erosion under conventional agriculture exceeded erosion under native vegetation by a median 18-fold (mean 124, range 1.3 to more than 1,000). In 39 comparisons of tillage with no-till, no-till reduced erosion by a median 20-fold [2]. The author's calculation from the medians gives conventional-agriculture erosion about 90 times the median soil production rate (1.537 / 0.017), consistent with Montgomery's conclusion of a one to two orders of magnitude gap [2]. Limits: the data mix measurement methods and timescales, the universal soil loss equation was not used because it can over- or underpredict, geologists' preference for scenic alpine terrain probably overrepresents high-relief areas in the geological rates, and no-till was practiced on only about 5 percent of global cropland at the time [2].

### Borrelli and colleagues (2020): modeled global water erosion to 2070

The team ran the Global Soil Erosion Modeling platform, a RUSLE-based model at 250 m resolution covering about 95.5 percent of land, with land-use scenarios from three integrated assessment models and rainfall erosivity from 14 climate models [4]. The 2015 estimate was 43 Pg per year. Land-use change alone would change erosion by minus 10 percent under the low-emission scenario (SSP1-RCP2.6) and raise it under the other two, but combined with climate-driven rainfall erosivity the totals rise by 30, 51 and 66 percent by 2070 under SSP1-RCP2.6, SSP2-RCP4.5 and SSP5-RCP8.5 [4]. Offsetting the land-use-driven increase under the highest scenario would require conservation agriculture on about 60 percent of cropland, against a current 11-14 percent [4]. Validation was indirect, because long-term field-scale measurements are lacking: the authors compared output with sediment records at 398 gauging stations, and report that the estimates explain 64 percent of global variance in observed sediment load [4]. Limits: the model covers only sheet and rill erosion, not wind, gully or riverbank erosion, cannot represent deposition, and rests on an empirical equation whose parameters depend on the data used to fit it [4].

### Sanderman, Hengl and Fiske (2017): the soil carbon debt of human land use

The authors fitted machine-learning models to 158,147 soil profiles in the ISRIC database, relating organic carbon density to climate, topography, lithology and land use, then projected the model back through a historical land-use dataset to a world without agriculture [7]. The original estimate of cumulative loss to 2 m depth was 133 Pg C. A February 2018 correction reported two coding errors, a layer-thickness multiplier and a land-mask mismatch, and lowered the global total to 116 Pg C; stocks to 100 and 200 cm became about 5 percent larger, and the pixel-level patterns did not change [7]. Annual losses were below 0.05 Pg C per year until about AD 1800 and above 0.3 Pg C per year during the following century [7]. Cropland and grazing land contributed nearly equal totals, with grazing at least half in the original analysis [7]. The authors note that the widely repeated claim that two-thirds of lost carbon can be recovered would imply 88 Pg C, whereas bottom-up estimates of maximum sequestration potential on cropland and grazing land, 0.4 to 1.4 Pg C per year over about 20 years, imply 8-28 Pg C, which they judge to be at best 10-30 percent of the carbon sink that can be filled [7]. Limits: cross-validated R-squared was 45-54 percent, training data span 50 years and mixed methods, land-use data are coarse and exclude management practice and intensity, and the authors write that their estimate probably covers only a smaller fraction of the actual debt due to human influence [7].

### Poulton and colleagues (2018): Rothamsted long-term experiments and "4 per 1000"

The "4 per 1000" initiative, launched by the French Ministry of Agriculture in 2015, proposed raising soil organic carbon stocks by 0.4 percent per year in the top 40 cm [5]. Poulton and colleagues analyzed 114 treatment comparisons from 16 long-term experiments on three soil types in southeast England, lasting 7 to 157 years, correcting for changes in soil bulk density [5]. In 65 percent of cases topsoil carbon rose faster than 7 per mille per year in the top 23 cm, roughly equal to the 4 per mille goal across 40 cm. Annual farmyard manure at 35 tons fresh weight per hectare raised carbon by 18 and 43 per mille per year during the first 20 years in the two oldest experiments, stayed above the equivalent of the goal for 40 and 60 years, and reached a new equilibrium after 80-100 years at about 75-80 tons of carbon per hectare [5]. Only 11 percent of the 480 tons of carbon added as manure since 1843 on Broadbalk was retained to 46 cm depth [5]. Arable land reverting to woodland doubled or trebled its organic carbon in a little over a century [5]. The authors conclude that promoting the initiative as a major climate-mitigation contribution is unrealistic, because farmers lack the inputs, practices are already adopted, some changes would reduce food production or be uneconomic, and moving manure between fields transfers rather than removes carbon [5]. Limits: the sites are all in one region, many plots were not replicated or randomized, and the authors judged that small stock changes are hard to detect against the large background [5].

### Hassani, Azapagic and Shokri (2021): projected primary salinization in drylands

The authors trained ensembles of regression trees on 44,708 non-cropland salinity samples from 11,517 profiles in the WoSIS database, using 14 predictors including precipitation frequency, evapotranspiration, soil class, texture and sea-salt deposition, with climate inputs from 16 model runs under moderate and high emission pathways [6]. Ten-fold cross-validation gave a mean R-squared of 72.8 percent [6]. For 2071-2100 relative to 1961-1990, drylands in South America, southern Australia, Mexico, the southwestern United States and South Africa were projected to become saltier, while the northwestern United States, the Horn of Africa, eastern Europe, Turkmenistan and western Kazakhstan were projected to stay constant or fall [6]. Under the high pathway, the area of dryland soil with ECe of at least 2 dS per meter was projected to grow by 3.4 percent in Australia and 6.7 percent in South America relative to the twentieth-century average [6]. Limits: the study covers only primary salinization and excludes irrigation, sea-level rise and groundwater effects; training data are concentrated in North America and Australia; and CMIP5- and CMIP6-based projections disagree in sign for Africa, North America and parts of Russia [6].

### Rempe and Dietrich (2018): direct measurement of rock moisture

At a forested hillslope in the Eel River Critical Zone Observatory in northern California, which receives about 2,042 mm of rain per year, the authors surveyed nine boreholes repeatedly with a neutron probe between 2013 and 2016, quantifying water stored between the base of the soil and the water table [9]. Maximum seasonal rock-moisture storage averaged 280 mm (plus or minus 140), 4.7 times the maximum soil-moisture storage, and returned to the same low value each dry season regardless of rainfall [9]. In the dry year 2014 (1,027 mm of rain), up to 27 percent of annual rainfall was stored and then transpired; in 2016 (2,168 mm) the figure was up to 13 percent [9]. After mid-June, the rock-moisture decline of 164-194 mm far exceeded runoff of 13-20 mm, and roots were observed to 16 m depth [9]. High-frequency sampling by other researchers, as reported in the paper, attributed 43-74 percent of stream solute fluxes to the rock-moisture zone [9]. Limits: the result comes from one hillslope with deep weathering; no systematic large-area measurements exist, which makes incorporation into large-scale models difficult [9].

### Anthony and colleagues (2023): how much of life lives in soil

The authors reviewed published global species-richness estimates for each major group, from viruses to mammals, set lower, central and upper estimates, and used global sequence datasets to compute the share of bacterial, archaeal, protist and viral taxa detected in soil versus other habitats [3]. The unweighted mean share of species in soil was 58.5 plus or minus 14.7 percent excluding phages, which the authors round to 59 percent, and 43.2 percent using only central estimates [3]. This is more than twice the earlier 25 percent animal-only figure [3]. Limits: bacterial and viral richness estimates span orders of magnitude, sampling is biased toward non-soil habitats and northern latitudes, and the authors call current knowledge fragmented and speculative [3].

## Implications

### For Earth-system modelers and carbon accountants

The persistence evidence changes how soil carbon should be represented. Widely used SOM models are structured around pools with intrinsic decay rates, use simple proxies such as texture and litter chemistry, and treat microbial biomass as a carbon pool rather than as an agent, whereas the observational evidence points to environmental controls on persistence [8]. Model intercomparisons show large disagreement about soil carbon fluxes in a warmer world [8]. Lavallee and colleagues argue that a model splitting SOM into POM and MAOM has a physical referent, can be measured by size and density fractionation, captures two different vulnerabilities, and is the most parsimonious and easiest-to-validate structure, and several newer models already use it [10]. The author's interpretation is that this also changes what a soil-carbon "stock" means: a large stock held mainly as saturating MAOM has a ceiling, a stock held as POM responds quickly to disturbance, and a stock held at depth is poorly constrained by topsoil measurements because subsoils hold more than half of the global total [8][10].

The numerical evidence sets realistic bounds on mitigation claims. The Rothamsted experiments show that high accumulation rates are real but temporary (under the heavy manure regime tested, the stock reached equilibrium within 80-100 years), and manure-driven increases often reflect transfer of carbon from elsewhere [5]. The corrected soil carbon debt of 116 Pg C bounds what could be re-stored, and bottom-up sequestration potentials of 0.4-1.4 Pg C per year imply recapture of only a fraction [7]. For anyone reading a carbon-removal claim, three checks follow from the sources: whether stock rather than concentration was measured and corrected for bulk density, whether the carbon was newly fixed or moved from another location, and whether nitrous oxide and manufacturing emissions were counted [5]. Permafrost soils are a related stock. Schmidt and colleagues give up to 1,672 Pg C for permafrost soils, as much as was once believed to exist in all soils, and flag its fate as a major uncertainty in climate-carbon feedbacks [8]; the permafrost carbon range used in the Library's carbon-cycle topic (1,460-1,600 GtC) is consistent with that upper bound.

### For hydrologists, ecologists and land-surface modelers

Rock moisture shows that the root zone is deeper than soil maps imply. If up to 27 percent of annual rainfall can be stored in weathered bedrock and returned by transpiration in a dry year, then drought response, recharge timing and stream chemistry at such sites depend on bedrock weathering structure, not only on soil depth [9]. Current land-surface models rely on mechanisms such as capillary flux from groundwater to give plants access to water in the absence of rainfall, and Rempe and Dietrich saw no upward capillary flux at their site [9]. The author's inference is that such models would misallocate water between evapotranspiration and runoff where rock moisture dominates. For forest and drought forecasting, Rempe and Dietrich suggest that rock-moisture storage capacity, and whether it is refilled each year, should be considered in forecasting large-scale forest cover response to droughts [9]. Interpretation: the same logic extends to the soil-moisture and climate relationship treated in the Library's hydrology topic, because the soil layer is one reservoir in a deeper chain [1][9].

For ecologists, the biodiversity estimate reframes conservation accounting. Anthony and colleagues note that biodiversity monitoring and conservation programs such as the US Endangered Species Act and the IUCN Red List largely exclude species inhabiting soil [3]. With an estimated 59 percent of species living in soil, this exclusion is a large gap (interpretation). The authors argue that quantitative figures help advocate for soil life in the biodiversity crisis [3]. Because the underlying estimates are fragmented, the more defensible reading is relative: the soil share is 43.2 percent using central estimates alone and 63 plus or minus 5 percent when both phages and bacteria are excluded, so it remains large under the variants the authors report [3].

### For people who interpret land-degradation and erosion statistics

Global erosion figures are model outputs with stated limits and should be quoted with their basis. The 43 Pg per year estimate covers water erosion by sheet and rill processes only, and earlier models gave much larger totals (about 132 and 172 Pg per year), so differences between sources probably reflect method as much as change over time (interpretation) [4]. Ratio statements also depend on basis. Cropland erosion is about 90 times soil production by the medians in [2] (author's calculation), whereas another Library topic states a global topsoil loss 10 to 40 times formation without a source in that passage. Interpretation: the two figures may refer to different land bases, the basis of the second is not stated, and neither should be applied to land use outside its own basis. Projected erosion increases are dominated by climate-driven rainfall erosivity rather than land use, and the mitigation share that conservation agriculture could supply is limited [4].

The direction of travel matters as much as the level. FAO's 2026 assessment summary reports that more than half of reviewed regional assessments show low or very low adoption of sustainable soil management, that 58 percent report a deteriorating trend since 2015 and about 10 percent report improvement, and that since 2015 global population grew by 800 million and harvested area of major crops by 80 million hectares [14]. The author's assessment is that the physical findings in [2] and [4] and the monitoring findings in [14] point the same way, but the 2026 report itself was not available to the author, so only its release was used.

### For infrastructure, hazard and water analysts

Salinization projections give a spatial prior. Hassani and colleagues excluded secondary salinization from irrigation, rising water tables and seawater intrusion [6]. Interpretation: for dryland regions flagged as likely to become saltier, their map is therefore a partial view that omits those additional human-driven pathways. Observatory scientists hypothesize that depth to fresh bedrock may be predictable from models that account for regional stress fields, advancing reaction fronts, drainage and freeze-thaw fracturing [1]; if confirmed, that would bear on where water storage and recharge are likely to be high. Detailed ground engineering for foundations and slopes belongs to geotechnical practice, covered in a separate Library topic.

## Common Pitfalls

- Reading a soil taxonomic order as a description of the whole critical zone. The order names the soil layer, not the weathered column below it [12][9].
- Quoting "133 Pg C" for the human soil carbon debt without the 2018 correction to 116 Pg C [7].
- Citing "25 percent of life lives in soil" for all species. That figure was for described animal species only [3].
- Treating a high accumulation rate in a young experiment as a sustained rate. Rothamsted rates fell to zero as stocks approached equilibrium [5].
- Treating bulk soil organic carbon as one pool with one turnover time, which hides the POM versus MAOM contrast [10].
- Treating soil moisture as the only subsurface water store available to plants [9].

## Criticisms and Limits

The persistence framework is qualitative more than quantitative: the 2011 synthesis says the new knowledge tells researchers what is important and suggests model structures, but not how to parameterize them [8]. The POM versus MAOM scheme is coarse by design and leaves fast-cycling MAOM and aggregate effects uncharacterized [10]. The Rothamsted analysis itself acknowledges that the "4 per 1000" initiative has been described variously as a target, an aspiration and a slogan, and that other researchers criticized examples used to support it [5]. The global erosion and salinity models rely on empirical or machine-learning structure and training data biased toward certain regions [4][6]. The soil carbon debt paper was corrected after publication and its authors expect the estimate to be conservative [7]. Observatory evidence is concentrated in a small number of instrumented sites, and the US observatory network never emphasized social science [1]. Finally, this topic's evidence base is largely peer-reviewed work from 2007-2023 plus institutional web pages and announcements from 2024 and 2026; it does not substitute for the full 2026 FAO assessment.
