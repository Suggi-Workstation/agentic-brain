# Race and Ethnicity as Social Systems -- How Institutions Turn Social Categories Into Unequal Life Chances

Race and ethnicity operate as social systems when classifications, group boundaries, institutional decisions, and unequal access to resources become connected over time. In the United States, federal statistical agencies explicitly treat race as a social definition rather than a biological, anthropological, or genetic definition, while research documents that racialized treatment can affect hiring, housing, and intergenerational economic outcomes. [2] [4] [5] [6] [7] The topic therefore examines categories and institutions at the social level; it does not treat a category as an explanation of an individual's abilities, values, or destiny.

## Background

The sociological study of race begins from a distinction between human variation and the social meanings assigned to it. A social category is not made insignificant by being socially constructed. It can organize identities, legal classifications, administrative data, residential patterns, and access to institutions precisely because people and organizations act through it. The U.S. Census Bureau states that its racial categories generally reflect a social definition recognized in the United States and are not an attempt to define race biologically, anthropologically, or genetically. It also states that responses are based on self-identification and that people may report more than one race. [2] These official practices show why sociology treats race as a social relation: a category can be constructed, revised, and self-reported while still carrying institutional consequences.

Michael Omi and Howard Winant developed racial formation theory to explain this combination of historical change and durable consequence. Their account treats racial formation as the sociohistorical process through which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and sometimes abandoned. Their concept of a racial project links representation and interpretation of race to the organization and distribution of resources. [1] The theory rejects two simplifications. It rejects biological essentialism, which treats race as a fixed natural hierarchy. It also rejects the claim that race is only an illusion and therefore irrelevant to social analysis. The sociological question is how racial meanings become connected to institutions, social movements, ordinary classifications, and unequal outcomes. [1]

Ethnicity overlaps with race but should not be used as an interchangeable label. In ordinary and institutional usage, ethnicity can refer to a claimed or assigned connection through ancestry, culture, language, origin, or historical community. Race can likewise include ancestry and origin in official classifications, but it has often been organized through visibly marked and historically racialized group boundaries. The Census Bureau's older standards separated a Hispanic or Latino ethnicity question from race and recognized that a person of Hispanic or Latino origin may be of any race. [2] The 2024 federal standards instead establish a combined race and ethnicity question with co-equal categories, including a Middle Eastern or North African category, and encourage respondents to select all applicable options. [3] These revisions demonstrate that the administrative categories used to measure populations are historical institutional choices, not timeless scientific containers.

The development of federal standards illustrates a broader institutional problem. Governments need categories to collect data, administer programs, monitor civil-rights compliance, and describe population change. Yet any category system must decide which identities are listed, how detailed responses are recorded, whether multiple responses are allowed, and how responses are tabulated. The Census Bureau reports that the 2024 standards require additional detailed collection in most circumstances and set a March 2029 implementation deadline for federal statistical agencies. [3] These decisions affect what inequalities can be observed. A broad category can make a population visible at one level while concealing variation within it; a detailed category can reveal differences while creating smaller analytic cells and new comparability problems. This is an inference from the measurement design described by the Census Bureau, not a claim that one classification is universally superior. [2] [3]

Sociology also shifted attention from prejudice understood only as an individual attitude to discrimination understood as a process. Pager and Shepherd define discrimination broadly enough to include decisions and processes without explicit racial content when they produce or reinforce racial disadvantage. Their review distinguishes evidence from self-reports, statistical analyses, audit studies, and organizational or institutional research, while warning that each method answers a different question. [4] This approach matters because a persistent group disparity does not, by itself, identify a cause. At the same time, an absence of explicit hostile language does not establish that an institutional process has equal effects. The appropriate analysis identifies the outcome, the comparison group, the mechanism under examination, and the evidentiary design. [4]

The candidate topic belongs in sociology-demography because it examines group relationships, stratification, institutions, and population-level patterns. It does not attempt a broad history of every racial classification, a clinical account of individual prejudice, or a legal prescription for particular policy disputes. The domain anchor explicitly includes race and ethnicity as social systems and social inequality, while assigning individual cognition to psychology-behavior and formal policy design to political-science-public-policy. `library/sociology-demography/anchor-sociology-demography.md` The United States evidence used below supplies concrete cases and methodological examples; it should not be read as establishing identical racial categories, institutions, or causal mechanisms in every society.

## Core Concepts

### Categories, Identities, and Social Classification

A category is a classification used to sort observations, people, or records; an identity is a person's self-description or affiliation; and a social system is the pattern of relationships and institutions through which classifications become consequential. These concepts are related but not identical. The Census Bureau asks people to self-identify, permits more than one race, and recognizes that race categories can include racial, national-origin, or sociocultural groups. [2] An administrative category therefore cannot be assumed to capture every aspect of a person's history, ancestry, culture, or experience. Conversely, the fact that a person has a complex identity does not prevent institutions from sorting records using a limited set of categories.

This distinction prevents two common analytical errors. The first is essentialism: treating a group label as evidence of a shared intrinsic capacity, culture, or behavior. The second is nominalism: treating a label as inconsequential because its boundaries are socially made. The sociological position is narrower and testable. A classification has social force when it changes how people are counted, perceived, sorted, or treated, or when it records the result of those processes. The Census Bureau's description of race as a social definition, and its continuing work to revise collection standards, support this focus on classification as an institutional practice. [2] [3]

A practical example is survey reporting. If an agency changes whether it uses separate or combined questions, permits multiple selections, or requests detailed origins, the reported population counts can change without an equivalent change in anyone's biological characteristics. That does not make either result fraudulent. It means comparisons across time require attention to question wording, response options, tabulation rules, and the purposes for which the data were collected. The 2024 standards' combined question and co-equal categories create such a comparability task for future federal data users. [3]

### Racial Formation and Racial Projects

Racial formation theory supplies a framework for linking cultural meanings to material organization. Omi and Winant describe racial formation as a process rather than a single event: racial categories are interpreted and reinterpreted through historical conflicts, institutions, and social action. A racial project, in their usage, connects a representation of race with an effort to organize or redistribute resources along racial lines. [1] The framework is useful because it makes neither ideas nor institutions sufficient on their own. A classification can be sustained by everyday meanings, official data practices, organizational rules, and political conflict at the same time.

The framework also directs analysis toward change. If categories are formed historically, analysts should not assume that a label has one fixed meaning across periods or settings. Federal standards show this directly. The 1997 standards used separate race and ethnicity questions; the 2024 revision moved toward a combined question, recognized Middle Eastern or North African as a minimum category, and required more detailed data collection in most cases. [3] These changes do not prove that every racial boundary has weakened or strengthened. They establish that the institutional representation of population categories changes and that researchers must specify the version of the classification they analyze.

An application is the study of a housing market. An analyst can ask whether an identity signal in an inquiry affects a landlord's response, whether residential locations are distributed differently by group, and whether those patterns are associated with later outcomes. Each question operates at a different level. The first concerns a decision at a point of contact; the second concerns population distribution; the third concerns an association across linked outcomes. Racial formation theory provides a vocabulary for relating such levels without claiming that a single action or attitude explains the whole system. [1] [4] [6]

### Ethnic Boundaries and Group Relations

Ethnic boundaries are the distinctions through which membership in an origin, cultural, linguistic, religious, or historical community is recognized, claimed, or assigned. The author's synthesis is that boundaries should be analyzed as relationships rather than as permanent properties of persons. Their salience may differ by setting: a language can matter in one institution, ancestry in another, and a broad administrative category in a third. The Census Bureau's need to update definitions, terminology, and the collection of detailed identities illustrates that a single population label can combine heterogeneous origins and experiences. [3]

Boundary analysis asks concrete questions. Who is classified together or separately? Which distinctions determine eligibility, representation, exposure, or access? How do people identify themselves, and how are they identified by others? What institutional rules make a boundary consequential? These questions avoid presuming that ethnic attachment is either wholly voluntary or wholly imposed. They also avoid treating all forms of group solidarity as equivalent. A community connection may provide mutual support and political voice, while a boundary can also exclude outsiders or restrict access to resources. The empirical task is to identify the setting and mechanism rather than attribute a uniform effect to ethnicity.

Race and ethnicity can interact without becoming the same thing. In the older federal standards, Hispanic or Latino origin was treated separately from race, while the newer standards require co-equal treatment of combined race and ethnicity categories for collection and tabulation. [2] [3] This institutional change is not a universal sociological definition of ethnicity. It is evidence that official systems make choices about how categories relate, and those choices affect what can be measured. Research should therefore state whether it analyzes self-identified ethnicity, assigned racial appearance, ancestry, national origin, language, migration history, or an administrative category.

### Stratification, Institutions, and Categorical Inequality

Stratification is the patterned distribution of valued resources, status, security, and authority. In a racialized social system, group classifications may become associated with different exposure to opportunities and constraints across institutions. Pager and Shepherd review evidence on discrimination in employment, housing, credit, and consumer markets and emphasize that discrimination may include processes that reproduce disadvantage even without explicit racial content in a particular decision. [4] The key claim is not that all group inequality is caused by discrimination, nor that every organization has the same mechanism. It is that organizational and institutional processes are part of the causal field that must be investigated.

This perspective differs from a model that explains group differences only by individual attributes. Education, experience, household resources, neighborhood conditions, organizational rules, and discriminatory treatment can coexist and interact. A sound study does not merely list all possible explanations. It uses a design able to distinguish among some of them. For example, a matched audit can test the response to a signal while holding selected application characteristics constant. A longitudinal administrative study can measure trajectories across generations but may not isolate every decision point that produced those trajectories. [4] [5] [7]

Cumulative disadvantage is an analytic concept for examining how unequal conditions at one stage can affect later stages. Pager and Shepherd argue that indirect pathways and self-fulfilling processes should be considered when assessing discrimination's full effects. [4] A housing search that reduces access to a location can alter proximity to jobs, schools, networks, or credit, but any particular downstream effect must be measured rather than presumed. Christensen, Sarmiento-Barbieri, and Timmins find that stronger discriminatory constraints in their rental correspondence study are associated with higher residential segregation and larger intergenerational-income-mobility gaps. Their result establishes an association at the city level alongside evidence on rental responses; it does not prove that rental discrimination alone caused each later outcome. [6]

### Direct, Indirect, and Structural Discrimination

Direct discrimination is differential treatment in a decision that is tied to a protected or socially salient category. Indirect or structural discrimination refers to processes, rules, or accumulated conditions that produce or reinforce disadvantage even when a single decision lacks explicit racial language. Pager and Shepherd's definition includes both kinds of processes and explicitly notes that the boundaries of discrimination depend on theory and method. [4] This distinction is useful because an organization can comply with a formally neutral rule while still generating unequal exposure, but unequal outcomes alone remain insufficient to prove that the rule is the cause.

The distinction also clarifies evidence. Resume or correspondence audits create matched or randomized comparisons and can identify differential treatment at a defined decision point. Quillian and colleagues synthesized U.S. field experiments in hiring that compared callback rates for otherwise matched applicants. Their meta-analysis assesses the point of hire, not wages after employment, neighborhood conditions, or every possible labor-market mechanism. [5] An administrative data analysis can describe broader patterns and link family, place, and later income, but it carries different assumptions about confounding and mechanism. [7] Strong analysis describes these limits rather than using one design as proof of an entire social system.

### Intersectionality

Intersectionality is a framework associated with Kimberle Crenshaw's analysis of how antidiscrimination doctrine can fail to recognize harms experienced at the intersection of race and sex. Crenshaw's 1989 article used legal cases and feminist and antiracist critique to show why a framework that treats race and gender as mutually exclusive categories can miss the position of Black women. [8] The concept does not mean that every category has the same effect in every context. It directs attention to whether a research design or institutional rule assumes a single-axis group comparison when the relevant social position is jointly structured.

For sociology, intersectionality is useful when it changes the unit of analysis. An inquiry into employment, family, housing, or mobility may need to distinguish groups by race, gender, class position, migration history, disability, or family status when theory and data indicate that these positions interact. The author's synthesis is that intersectionality is an anti-simplification rule: it prevents an average result for one category from being treated as the complete experience of every person placed in that category. It does not authorize unsupported claims about any subgroup. [8]

### Measurement, Mechanism, and Inference

Measurement answers how a population, behavior, or outcome is recorded. Mechanism answers what process connects conditions to an outcome. Inference answers what a particular research design can establish. These three tasks are often conflated in public debate. Census categories make population tabulations possible, but they do not by themselves establish why any disparity exists. [2] [3] An observed wage, housing, or mobility gap can describe an outcome; an audit study can identify differential treatment at a selected stage; a broader theory is needed to connect evidence across stages without overstating certainty. [4] [5] [6] [7]

The distinction has practical consequences. A researcher who treats race as a fixed biological variable makes a claim that conflicts with the Census Bureau's social-definition approach. [2] A researcher who treats every disparity as direct discrimination overextends descriptive evidence. A researcher who treats a single audit result as proof of every historical and institutional mechanism also overextends the design. The better procedure is to report the category definition, population, time period, comparison, method, observed result, and limits of inference. That procedure allows evidence from different studies to compound rather than compete through vague generalization. [4] [5] [6] [7]

## Evidence

### Federal Classification as an Institutional Case

Federal statistical standards provide direct evidence that race and ethnicity are administratively constructed and periodically revised. The Census Bureau states that its race data are based on self-identification and that the categories generally reflect a social definition in the United States, not a biological, anthropological, or genetic definition. [2] The older standards permitted multiple race responses and separated Hispanic or Latino origin from race. [2] The updated 2024 standards moved to a combined race and ethnicity question, added Middle Eastern or North African as a minimum category, required additional detail in most settings, and directed agencies to treat the seven minimum categories as co-equal. [3]

This case does not test discrimination. Its evidentiary contribution is different: it documents institutional decisions about how a population is counted. The Census Bureau reports that the updates followed scientific research, public engagement, and federal review, and that implementation is planned for the 2027 American Community Survey and 2030 Census. [3] The case demonstrates why historical comparisons require metadata about the question and tabulation rules. A change in a category can change what is reported even if respondents' lived identities do not change in the same way or at the same rate. This is a measurement inference grounded in the documented standards, not a claim about the appropriate identity of any respondent. [2] [3]

### Hiring Audits and Meta-Analysis

Quillian, Pager, Hexel, and Midtboen conducted a meta-analysis of available U.S. field experiments testing hiring discrimination against African American and Latino applicants. The study included 28 experiments, 55,842 applications, and 26,326 positions; its main analysis of post-1989 studies found that White applicants received, on average, 36 percent more callbacks than African American applicants and 24 percent more callbacks than Latino applicants. The authors reported no change in discrimination against African Americans over the period examined and modest evidence of decline for Latino applicants. [5]

The method is important. Field experiments use matched resumes or matched in-person applicants and compare employer responses when race or ethnicity is signaled while selected qualifications are held comparable. This supplies stronger causal leverage for a hiring-stage decision than a raw comparison of employed workers. [5] It does not establish how every employer decides, whether an individual employer intended to discriminate, or the entire causal history of racial inequality. Its appropriate conclusion is narrower: across the studies and period synthesized, callback differences remained a measurable feature of the selected hiring process. [5]

Pager and Shepherd's review places audit studies among several methods. They note that self-reports can capture experience but are affected by attribution and reporting differences; statistical studies can show residual associations but may omit relevant variables; and audits identify treatment at a defined stage while not measuring every later outcome. [4] Together, the review and meta-analysis illustrate a general research principle: a specific causal design should be used to make a specific causal claim, and it should be combined with other evidence only after its scope is made explicit.

### Rental-Housing Correspondence Study

Christensen, Sarmiento-Barbieri, and Timmins conducted a rental-market correspondence study using more than 25,000 interactions with property managers in the 50 largest U.S. cities. The study reported discriminatory constraints for African American and Hispanic or Latino renters in a majority of the sampled cities, with regional variation. It also matched the correspondence evidence to actual rental outcomes and reported that the correspondence measure predicted those outcomes. [6]

This design gives the evidence two linked components. First, it examines a contact-stage response in a large rental sample, where matched inquiries can estimate unequal treatment under controlled conditions. Second, it assesses whether the correspondence measure aligns with later observed rental outcomes at the properties. [6] The study also reported that stronger discriminatory constraints were associated with higher residential segregation and larger intergenerational-income-mobility gaps. This association is informative about the city-level context, but it does not identify rental discrimination as the sole cause of segregation or mobility gaps. [6]

The case shows why housing belongs in a social-systems account. Housing affects where people can live, and place is connected to institutions such as schools, labor markets, transport, social networks, and local public services. This statement identifies possible connections, not a causal estimate for any household. The documented result is narrower: the rental experiment found discriminatory constraints in its sampled market and linked its correspondence measure to actual rental outcomes. [6] Further claims about downstream effects require evidence that measures those pathways directly.

### Intergenerational Mobility Evidence

Chetty, Hendren, Jones, and Porter analyzed anonymized longitudinal data covering nearly the entire U.S. population from 1989 through 2015 to study racial disparities in income across generations. They reported lower rates of upward mobility and higher rates of downward mobility for Black Americans and American Indians relative to White Americans, while Hispanic Americans had intergenerational mobility rates more similar to White Americans in their analysis. [7] Conditional on parent income, the study reported that the Black-White income gap was driven by differences in wages and employment rates between Black and White men in its data, and that parental marital status, education, and wealth explained little of that conditional gap. [7]

The study also compared outcomes across neighborhoods. It found that Black boys had lower adult incomes than White boys in 99 percent of census tracts after controlling for parental income, and that the few areas with relatively small gaps tended to have low poverty, lower measured racial bias among White residents, and high father-presence rates among Black residents. [7] The authors' design combines population-scale longitudinal records with place-based comparisons. Its contribution is to show persistent differences in mobility trajectories and to identify contextual correlates of smaller gaps; it cannot by itself isolate a complete mechanism for every neighborhood or person. [7]

This evidence is relevant to racial systems because it connects group categories, family resources, labor-market outcomes, and places across generations. It also warns against a single-factor explanation. The study's finding that several common family characteristics explained little of a conditional gap does not eliminate the importance of families, education, workplaces, neighborhoods, or policy. It establishes a result within a specified model and sample. [7] A social-systems interpretation is strongest when it retains that specificity rather than using a population average to characterize an individual or an entire community.

### Evidence Synthesis and Limits

The studies above examine distinct levels: category construction in federal administration, employer responses in hiring, property-manager responses in rental housing, and intergenerational income trajectories. They should not be combined by adding percentages or treating them as independent estimates of one quantity. Each uses a different population, outcome, method, and time range. [2] [3] [5] [6] [7] Their joint value is conceptual and cumulative: they show that classification systems, decision points, residential contexts, and life-course outcomes can all be examined empirically.

Pager and Shepherd's review supports this disciplined synthesis because it treats discrimination research as methodologically plural. [4] The author's assessment is that a social-systems account is warranted when evidence demonstrates patterned links across institutional settings, but claims about a particular mechanism must retain the scope of the design that produced them. That standard avoids both denial of measurable unequal treatment and overstatement that turns every disparity into proof of one cause. [4] [5] [6] [7]

## Implications

### For Sociological Research

A sociological analysis of race and ethnicity should begin by specifying the object being studied. It should state whether a category is self-identified, externally assigned, administratively coded, inferred from an experimental signal, or measured through ancestry, language, origin, or another construct. This requirement follows from the Census Bureau's documentation of changing federal standards and self-identification practices. [2] [3] Without that specification, studies can appear to disagree when they are measuring different aspects of categorization.

Researchers should also match causal language to evidence. A descriptive disparity reports a difference; it does not identify its cause. A matched audit can estimate a decision-stage effect under its design. A longitudinal study can document trajectories and associations over time. [4] [5] [6] [7] The author's synthesis is that a credible research program connects these designs through clearly stated mechanisms rather than demanding that one design answer every question. For example, audits can identify a barrier at a contact point, while longitudinal data can show whether group outcomes persist across generations; neither alone establishes the complete pathway between them.

Intersectionality supplies a further design check. If a study treats race and gender, class, migration, family status, or other positions as separate axes without testing their combination, it may conceal the population most affected by the mechanism under study. Crenshaw's analysis demonstrates why a single-axis framework can miss a joint form of disadvantage. [8] The implication is not that every study needs every interaction term. It is that the choice of categories and comparisons must follow the theory, population, and available evidence rather than administrative convenience alone.

### For Data Systems and Public Interpretation

Data systems make social patterns visible, but they also embed definitions. The 2024 federal standards' combined question, detailed-identity collection, and co-equal categories create a need for careful documentation when users compare data across surveys or years. [3] Analysts should record the question wording, response options, coding rules, treatment of multiple responses, geographic level, and denominator before drawing conclusions about a trend. This is especially important when a change in measurement can alter counts or apparent disparities.

Public interpretation should separate population statistics from claims about persons. A group average is not a prediction about an individual, and an administrative category is not a complete account of culture, ancestry, or experience. The Census Bureau's social-definition approach supports this caution. [2] The author's synthesis is that the most useful way to discuss group data is conditional: identify what was measured, in which population, over what period, compared with whom, and with what uncertainty. That practice makes data more informative and reduces the risk of essentialist interpretation.

Detailed categories involve trade-offs. More detail can reveal heterogeneity that a broad category masks, as the Census Bureau notes in explaining the demand for disaggregated data. [3] At the same time, finer categories can create small samples, disclosure risks, and instability in estimates. These are not reasons to avoid detail or to demand it universally. They are design questions that require appropriate confidentiality procedures, sample sizes, and transparent aggregation rules. The source evidence establishes the federal move toward detail; the trade-off analysis is an author's synthesis about statistical use. [3]

### For Organizations, Employment, and Housing

Organizations that want to assess unequal treatment need measures appropriate to the decision they control. In hiring, this may mean examining who applies, who advances, how job requirements are defined, who conducts screening, and whether similarly situated applicants receive different responses. The Quillian meta-analysis shows that matched field experiments can detect callback differences at the initial point of hire. [5] The result does not prove that a particular organization discriminates, but it demonstrates why an organization should not treat the absence of an explicit complaint as conclusive evidence about its selection process.

In housing, the rental correspondence evidence identifies a measurable contact-stage question: whether matched prospective renters receive different responses. [6] Organizations and researchers must distinguish this question from later issues such as lease terms, neighborhood sorting, affordability, or actual occupancy. Each stage may require a different dataset and method. This discipline matters because a broad statement that a market is fair or unfair is less useful than a diagnosis specifying where, for whom, and through what observable process constraints arise. [4] [6]

The same logic applies to workplace and housing metrics. An observed disparity may initiate investigation, but it does not by itself establish intent or a particular mechanism. Pager and Shepherd's methodological review explains why self-reports, residual statistical estimates, and field experiments provide different forms of evidence. [4] The operational implication is to preserve data and audit trails sufficient to distinguish application, screening, offer, assignment, retention, pricing, and promotion stages. A system cannot be evaluated rigorously if its decision stages are collapsed into a single outcome measure.

### For Inequality and Mobility Analysis

The mobility evidence shows why racial inequality cannot be reduced to a one-period income comparison. Chetty and colleagues document divergent upward and downward mobility patterns across groups and find persistent Black-White gaps for boys across nearly all census tracts after conditioning on parent income. [7] The implication for analysts is to study life-course sequences: family resources, neighborhood context, school access, hiring, housing, employment, and wealth may interact across time. This is a research agenda, not proof that every pathway has the same size or operates everywhere.

Housing evidence reinforces the importance of place. Christensen and colleagues report that stronger rental-market discriminatory constraints were associated with more segregation and larger mobility gaps across sampled cities. [6] That result makes residential context a relevant variable in explanations of opportunity, but it does not justify treating a neighborhood label as destiny. The appropriate next question is which features of a place, institutions, and group relations are actually measured and which are assumed. The authorial synthesis is that durable inequality is best analyzed as an accumulation of specified processes, not as a property of a group or place.

The present topic adds an account of how racial and ethnic categories enter these systems. This cross-reference is a synthesis of the domain's topic structure, not new empirical evidence.

### For Ethical Reasoning and Institutional Design

An ethical account of racial and ethnic inequality must avoid two symmetrical failures. One is to naturalize a group category, assigning intrinsic traits to persons based on membership. The other is to deny measurable unequal treatment or unequal opportunity because categories are socially constructed. The Census Bureau's statement that race is socially defined, combined with audit and longitudinal evidence of patterned outcomes, supports rejecting both failures. [2] [5] [6] [7] Social construction describes how a category is made and maintained; it does not erase the consequences of acting through that category.

Institutional design also requires clarity about the boundary between analysis and prescription. The research reviewed here can identify classification changes, hiring-stage disparities, rental-market constraints, and mobility patterns. [2] [3] [5] [6] [7] It cannot by itself select one legal rule, program, or organizational intervention for every setting. A defensible decision must state its objective, jurisdiction, population, evidence, expected trade-offs, and evaluation plan. This topic therefore supplies an analytic foundation for adjacent public-policy work rather than a substitute for democratic or legal judgment.

The author's final synthesis is that race and ethnicity should be studied as social systems of classification, boundary making, institutional action, and unequal opportunity. The system is neither an immutable biological hierarchy nor a single causal machine. It is a set of historically changing, empirically investigable relationships. Good analysis identifies the specific category, institutional setting, decision point, population, evidence, and limit of inference before making a claim about inequality or reform.
