# Social Stratification and Mobility -- Why the Hierarchical Arrangement of Society Determines Who Rises and Who Stays

Social stratification is the hierarchical arrangement of individuals
and groups in a society based on their access to wealth, power,
status, and opportunity. Social mobility is the mechanism by which
people move between these strata -- upward, downward, or
horizontally -- within a lifetime (intragenerational) or across
generations (intergenerational). Together they form the
architecture of opportunity: stratification describes the structure
of inequality, and mobility describes how rigid or permeable that
structure is. The central finding of a century of sociological
research is that societies with high inequality tend to exhibit low
mobility -- the more unequal a society, the more strongly a child's
adult position is determined by the position of their parents.

## Background

The systematic study of social stratification emerged in the
mid-nineteenth century as classical sociology sought to explain the
dramatic inequalities produced by industrialization. Before this
period, hierarchical social arrangements were generally treated as
natural or divinely ordained. Aristotle, in his *Politics* (circa
350 BCE), wrote of the natural ranking of free people and slaves.
Feudal society organized individuals into estates -- clergy, nobility,
and commoners -- with position determined by birth and legitimated by
religious ideology. The Enlightenment challenged these
justifications: Locke, Rousseau, and Montesquieu wrote of feudal
inequities and the social contract, arguing that hierarchical
arrangements were human constructions, not natural law (Zeitlin,
1968; Strasser, 1976).

The classical sociological theorists -- Karl Marx, Emile Durkheim, and
Max Weber -- developed the analytical frameworks that still structure
stratification research. Marx, writing in the mid-1800s, provided the
first systematic economic theory of class. He defined class by
relationship to the means of production: the bourgeoisie owned
capital, the proletariat sold labor, and the conflict between them
was the engine of history. Marx died before completing his planned
detailed analysis of class in the third volume of *Capital*
(Dahrendorf, 1959). His exclusively economic definition proved
influential but incomplete -- it could not explain why stratification
persisted in societies that had abolished private ownership, nor
why prestige and political power sometimes diverged sharply from
economic position.

Weber, working half a century later, addressed these gaps directly.
In his essay "Class, Status, Party" (written circa 1914, published
posthumously in 1922 in *Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft*), Weber
developed a three-component theory of stratification that treated
class, status, and power as distinct but interacting dimensions.
Class referred to economic position determined by market situation --
what one has to sell and on what terms. Status referred to social
honor or prestige, which could diverge from class: aristocrats
lacking wealth still commanded deference, and wealthy Jewish
families in Germany lacked social prestige. Party referred to the
ability to influence political outcomes, a dimension Weber
predicted would grow in importance as modern bureaucracies expanded
(Gerth and Mills, 1946: 181-94). Weber's multidimensional model
became the dominant framework for twentieth-century stratification
research because it could explain the growing middle class and the
persistence of inequality under communism -- when one dimension
(private ownership) was suppressed, another (bureaucratic power)
intensified.

The mid-twentieth century produced two major competing theoretical
traditions. The functionalist approach, articulated by Kingsley
Davis and Wilbert Moore in "Some Principles of Stratification"
(1945), argued that inequality was functionally necessary: societies
must attach differential rewards to positions to motivate the most
talented individuals to undergo the training required for the most
functionally important roles. This thesis generated sustained
criticism, most notably from Melvin Tumin in 1953, who argued that
the theory assumed meritocracy that did not exist, ignored the role
of power in determining rewards, and could not explain why
essential workers received low compensation (Davis and Moore, 1945;
Tumin, 1953; Springer, 2003).

The conflict tradition, rooted in Marx but elaborated by Ralf
Dahrendorf and others, viewed stratification as the product of power
and domination rather than functional necessity. Dahrendorf noted the
tendency toward an enlarged middle class in technological economies
but argued that authority relations within organizations reproduced
class conflict in new forms. C. Wright Mills, drawing from Pareto
and Mosca, argued in *The Power Elite* (1956) that power in
mid-century America was concentrated in an interconnected network of
corporate, political, and military leaders -- a structural dominance
that could not be explained by functional merit.

Pierre Bourdieu, working in France in the 1960s and 1970s, introduced
cultural capital as a mechanism of social reproduction. In *Les
Heritiers* (1964, with Passeron) and *Reproduction in Education,
Culture and Society* (1977, with Passeron), Bourdieu argued that the
education system transforms inherited cultural advantage into
apparent academic merit, reproducing class hierarchy across
generations while appearing meritocratic. His concepts of habitus,
field, and symbolic violence expanded stratification theory beyond
economics into the cultural and symbolic domain.

The empirical study of mobility was transformed by Peter Blau and
Otis Dudley Duncan's *The American Occupational Structure* (1967),
which used the 1962 Current Population Survey and path analysis to
model the mechanisms linking fathers' occupational status to sons'
outcomes through education and first job. Their finding that
achievement (education) was more important than ascription (family
background) in determining occupational status in mid-century
America established the status attainment research program. The
Wisconsin model extended this work by incorporating social
psychological variables -- parental encouragement, peer influences,
and educational aspirations -- as mediating mechanisms.

In the twenty-first century, Raj Chetty and colleagues used
administrative tax records covering over 40 million parent-child
pairs to map intergenerational mobility with unprecedented
geographic and demographic detail. Their work revealed substantial
variation in mobility across regions within the United States and
identified the structural factors -- segregation, inequality, school
quality, social capital, family stability -- associated with higher
or lower mobility. The Great Gatsby Curve, popularized by Alan
Krueger in 2012, formalized the cross-national relationship between
income inequality and intergenerational persistence: more unequal
societies exhibit lower mobility.

## Core Concepts

### The Dimensions of Stratification: Class, Status, and Power

Weber's three-component theory remains the foundational analytical
framework for stratification. Class is an economic concept: it
refers to a person's position in the market, which determines their
life chances -- the probability of accessing health, education,
security, and advancement. Class is not defined by ownership alone
(as Marx argued) but by market situation: what one brings to the
market (property, skills, labor) and on what terms one can sell it.
Weber distinguished property classes (stratified by ownership),
acquisition classes (stratified by the marketability of services),
and social classes (aggregates within which intergenerational
mobility is typical and easy).

Status is a social concept: the degree of deference or social honor
accorded to an individual or group. Status is related to class but
not determined by it. A professional basketball player and a nuclear
physicist may both enjoy high prestige while occupying different
class positions. Status inconsistency occurs when class and status
positions diverge -- for example, a wealthy individual denied entry
into elite social circles, or a middle-class African American family
facing housing discrimination. Status groups maintain boundaries
through patterns of association, marriage, and consumption that
signal belonging.

Power is a political concept: the ability to achieve one's will
despite the resistance of others. Weber distinguished the possession
of power from its exercise and identified parties as organizations
that aggregate power for collective purposes. The power dimension
has grown in analytical importance as modern societies have
developed large bureaucratic organizations -- corporations,
government agencies, labor unions -- that concentrate authority
independently of ownership or status. Erik Olin Wright extended
Marxian class analysis by incorporating control over labor and
organizational assets, producing a four-class model (capitalists,
managers, workers, petty bourgeoisie) that integrates Marxian and
Weberian insights (Wright, 1978, 1997).

### Open and Closed Stratification Systems

Stratification systems vary along a continuum from closed to open.
Closed systems -- slavery, estates, caste -- accommodate little
movement between strata. In caste systems, all aspects of social
position are ascribed at birth: occupation, marriage partner,
residence, and social standing are determined by caste membership
and sustained by religious ideology emphasizing fate and destiny.
The Hindu caste tradition is the paradigmatic example: individuals
are socialized to accept their position as a moral duty, and
cultural beliefs reinforce the system's legitimacy. Closed systems
exhibit high status consistency -- an individual's rank is uniform
across all dimensions of stratification.

Open systems -- class systems -- permit movement based on achievement.
Class position reflects a combination of social factors (family
background, education) and individual accomplishment. While family
and social networks guide individuals toward particular careers,
personal choice and opportunity play a role. Open systems exhibit
lower status consistency: an individual may rank high on education
but low on income, or high on occupational prestige but low on
wealth. No society has achieved a pure meritocracy in which social
position is determined entirely by merit, but modern class systems
incorporate meritocratic elements -- academic performance, skill
certification, competitive labor markets -- alongside ascriptive
ones (OpenStax, Introduction to Sociology 3e).

The distinction between open and closed systems is analytical rather
than empirical: real societies mix elements. Contemporary India
combines constitutional commitments to equality with persistent caste
effects on marriage and occupation. The United States combines
formally open class mobility with racial and economic barriers that
partially close the system in practice. The degree of openness is
itself a variable that stratification research measures and
explains.

### Types of Social Mobility

Social mobility is classified along two axes: temporal scope and
direction. Intergenerational mobility compares an individual's
social position to that of their parents. Intragenerational mobility
compares an individual's position at different points in their own
lifetime. Vertical mobility involves movement up or down the
stratification hierarchy. Horizontal mobility involves movement
between positions of similar rank -- changing occupations at the
same status level.

Intergenerational income elasticity (IGE) is the most commonly used
quantitative measure of intergenerational mobility. It is estimated
by regressing log child income on log parent income: an IGE of 0.0
means no association between parent and child income (perfect
mobility), while an IGE of 1.0 means child income is entirely
determined by parent income (perfect immobility). Estimates for the
United States range from 0.26 to 0.70 depending on specification,
with a central estimate around 0.45-0.50 for children born in the
1980s (Chetty et al., 2014a; Solon, 1999). Rank-rank slopes --
correlating parent and child percentile ranks -- provide a more
stable measure: Chetty et al. found a rank-rank slope of 0.34,
meaning a 10 percentile increase in parent income is associated
with a 3.4 percentile increase in child income.

Absolute mobility -- whether children earn more than their parents --
is conceptually distinct from relative mobility. Chetty et al.
(2017) found that absolute mobility in the United States declined
from approximately 90% for children born in 1940 to 50% for children
born in the 1980s. This decline was driven less by changes in
relative mobility (the rank-rank association remained stable) than
by changes in the marginal income distribution: slower economic
growth and rising inequality meant that children needed to climb
further to exceed their parents' positions, even given the same
rank mobility.

### The Great Gatsby Curve

The Great Gatsby Curve describes the positive empirical relationship
between cross-sectional income inequality and intergenerational
income persistence across countries. Countries with higher Gini
coefficients (more inequality) tend to have higher IGE values (lower
mobility). The relationship was recognized by several researchers
(Hassler, Mora, and Zeira; Andrews and Leigh; Bjorklund and Jantti;
Corak) and popularized by Alan Krueger in a 2012 speech at the
Center for American Progress. Krueger coined the term "Great Gatsby
Curve" and used it to predict that rising inequality in the United
States would produce lower mobility in subsequent generations
(Krueger, 2012; Corak, 2013).

The curve has been documented at subnational levels as well: a
subnational Great Gatsby Curve exists within the United States,
Italy, and China, with more unequal regions exhibiting lower
mobility. A 2023 study found the subnational curve already present
in US counties at the beginning of the twentieth century
(Battiston et al., 2023). Germany provides a temporal analogue:
researchers found that intergenerational income mobility in Germany
decreased for cohorts born in the late 1970s and early 1980s,
coinciding with rising cross-sectional inequality -- evidence for
an "Intertemporal Great Gatsby Curve" within a single country
(ifo Institute, 2025).

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the curve. Family
investment models emphasize that wealthier parents can invest more
in their children's education, health, and development, and that
the gap in investment widens as inequality increases. Social models
emphasize residential segregation: rising inequality increases
economic sorting across neighborhoods and school districts,
concentrating disadvantage and reducing peer effects, social
networks, and school quality for children from low-income families.
The relative importance of these mechanisms remains debated, but
the cross-national correlation is robust.

### Cultural Capital and Social Reproduction

Bourdieu's theory of social reproduction identifies cultural capital
as a key mechanism through which class inequality persists across
generations. Cultural capital exists in three forms: embodied (as
habitus -- internalized dispositions, skills, and tastes acquired
through socialization), objectified (cultural goods such as books,
artworks, instruments), and institutionalized (educational
credentials and qualifications). The education system transforms
inherited cultural capital into academic credentials, creating an
appearance of meritocracy while reproducing class advantage.

The mechanism operates as follows. Middle-class families socialize
children into linguistic styles, behavioral norms, and cultural
knowledge that align with school expectations. Teachers, themselves
products of the dominant culture, recognize and reward these
competencies as "natural" ability rather than inherited advantage.
Working-class children, lacking this cultural alignment, face
systematic disadvantage that appears as individual deficiency rather
than structural inequality. Bourdieu termed this process "symbolic
violence" -- the imposition of a cultural arbitrary that is
recognized as legitimate by both dominant and subordinate groups
through a process of "misrecognition" (Bourdieu and Passeron, 1977).

Empirical evidence for Bourdieu's thesis is mixed. Tzanakis (2011)
reviewed key quantitative studies and found that cultural capital
has limited confirmed power as a mechanism of class reproduction in
large-scale representative data. Other scholars argue that
cultural capital and habitus, taken together with practice and
field, retain significant explanatory potential when incorporated
into a realist explanatory framework. The concept has been
influential in educational research and policy despite the mixed
empirical record, particularly in studies of parenting practices,
school interactions, and curriculum design (Sullivan, 2001; Lareau
and Weininger, 2003; Farkas, UC Irvine).

### Status Attainment and the Blau-Duncan Model

The Blau-Duncan status attainment model, published in *The American
Occupational Structure* (1967), was the first large-scale
quantitative study of social mobility in the United States. Using
data from the 1962 Current Population Survey, Blau and Duncan
modeled the stratification process as a path from father's
education and occupational status, through son's education and
first job, to son's adult occupational status. Their model
explained 26% of variance in educational attainment, 33% of
variance in first job, and 42% of variance in adult occupational
attainment.

The central finding was that achievement (education) was more
important than ascription (family background) in determining
occupational status: the ratio of the effect of education to the
direct effect of father's occupation on current occupation was
approximately 2.9 to 1. However, family background exerted
substantial indirect effects through education: father's
occupational status influenced son's educational attainment, which
in turn influenced occupational outcomes. The model thus
demonstrated that ascription and achievement were not opposing
forces but linked processes -- advantage was transmitted partly
through the educational system.

The Wisconsin model, developed by Sewell, Hauser, and colleagues,
extended the Blau-Duncan framework by incorporating social
psychological mediating variables: parental encouragement,
significant others' influence, and educational aspirations. The
Wisconsin model showed that family background affected educational
and occupational outcomes partly through its effects on these
psychological mediators, and that academic ability had independent
effects on educational attainment. Featherman and Hauser replicated
the Blau-Duncan study in *Opportunity and Change* (1978), finding
similar results and documenting that most mobility was short in
distance and occurred in the middle of the occupational hierarchy.

### Intersectionality and Multiple Stratification Axes

Contemporary stratification research recognizes that class is one
of several intersecting axes of inequality. Race, gender, ethnicity,
and age operate alongside class to structure access to resources,
opportunities, and prestige. Feminist theory analyzes gender
stratification through the intersection of gender, race, and class,
arguing that these dimensions cannot be understood in isolation.
The concept of intersectionality, developed by Kimberle Crenshaw
and elaborated by Patricia Hill Collins, holds that the effects of
multiple stratification axes are interactive rather than additive:
the experience of being a working-class woman of color cannot be
decomposed into separate effects of class, gender, and race.

Empirical research supports this position. Chetty et al. (2020)
documented sharp changes in economic mobility by race and class in
recent decades: between the 1978 and 1992 birth cohorts, earnings
gaps by parental income ("class") grew by 30% for white children,
while white-Black earnings gaps for children from low-income
families shrank by 30%. These divergent trends were caused by
differential changes in childhood environments -- parental
employment rates within local communities defined by race, class,
and childhood county -- rather than by changes in individual
characteristics. The findings demonstrate that stratification
dynamics operate differently across racial groups and that class
and race interact in shaping mobility outcomes.

## Evidence

### The Geography of Intergenerational Mobility in the United States

Chetty, Hendren, Kline, and Saez (2014a) used de-identified federal
income tax records covering over 40 million parent-child pairs
(born 1980-1982) to characterize intergenerational mobility in the
United States with unprecedented precision. Their methodological
innovation was the use of percentile rank correlations rather than
log-log IGE regressions, which proved unstable due to nonlinearity
and sensitivity to zero-income observations. They found a
rank-rank slope of 0.34: a 10 percentile increase in parent income
was associated with a 3.4 percentile increase in child income at
age 30. IGE estimates ranged from 0.26 to 0.70 depending on
specification, with a central estimate of 0.45 when restricting
to the 10th-90th percentile range of parent income.

The study's most striking finding was the geographic variation in
mobility. The probability that a child born to a family in the
bottom quintile reaches the top quintile of the national income
distribution ranged from 4.4% in Charlotte, North Carolina, to
12.9% in San Jose, California. This variation was larger than the
differences between the United States and other developed countries,
leading the authors to conclude that the United States is better
described as "a collection of societies, some of which are 'lands of
opportunity' with high rates of mobility across generations, and
others in which few children escape poverty."

Five factors were correlated with upward mobility: (1) less
residential segregation, (2) lower income inequality (a smaller
middle-class gap), (3) better primary schools (higher test scores,
lower class sizes), (4) greater social capital (measured by
civic participation and neighborhood cohesion), and (5) greater
family stability (measured by the fraction of children with single
parents). The authors noted that these correlations are descriptive
and do not identify causal mechanisms, but they pointed toward
neighborhood and school quality as the most promising targets for
policy intervention.

### Trends in Absolute and Relative Mobility

Chetty et al. (2014b, AER P&P) examined trends in intergenerational
mobility using administrative earnings records for children born
between 1971 and 1993. They found that rank-based measures of
relative mobility -- the correlation between parent and child
income percentile ranks -- remained stable across cohorts. Children
entering the labor market in the 2000s had the same chances of
moving up the income distribution relative to their parents as
children born in the 1970s. However, income inequality increased
substantially over the same period.

This distinction between relative and absolute mobility is critical.
Relative mobility (rank-rank association) was stable, meaning the
process by which family background determines position in the
income distribution did not change. But absolute mobility -- the
share of children earning more than their parents -- declined
sharply. Chetty et al. (2017) estimated that approximately 90% of
children born in 1940 earned more than their parents, compared to
approximately 50% of children born in the 1980s. The decline was
driven primarily by the widening marginal income distribution:
slower GDP growth and greater inequality meant that children needed
to climb higher in the relative distribution to exceed their
parents' absolute earnings, even given the same rank mobility. The
authors estimated that restoring absolute mobility to 1940s levels
would require either much faster growth or a substantial reduction
in inequality.

### The Great Gatsby Curve: Cross-National Evidence

Corak (2013) compiled intergenerational income elasticity estimates
and Gini coefficients for dozens of countries, demonstrating the
positive cross-national relationship between inequality and
immobility. Countries with low inequality (Denmark, Norway, Sweden)
exhibited IGE values around 0.15-0.20, indicating high mobility.
Countries with high inequality (United States, United Kingdom,
Brazil) exhibited IGE values around 0.40-0.60, indicating low
mobility. The relationship is not perfectly linear, but the
correlation is robust across multiple data sources and
specifications.

Bavaro, Carranza, and Nolan (2024) extended the Great Gatsby Curve
framework to poverty persistence, examining 30 European countries
using EU-SILC data. They found that intergenerational poverty
persistence tended to be stronger in countries with higher current
or past poverty rates, analogous to the income inequality-mobility
relationship. This finding suggests that the Great Gatsby Curve
operates not only at the top of the distribution (income mobility)
but also at the bottom (poverty persistence), supporting the
interpretation that inequality and immobility are structurally
linked across the full distribution.

### Status Attainment: Blau and Duncan's Quantitative Evidence

Blau and Duncan's 1967 study provided the first national-level
empirical evidence on the mechanisms of stratification in the
United States. Using the 1962 Current Population Survey (the first
national mobility survey in the US), they constructed path models
linking father's education and occupational status to son's
education, first job, and adult occupation. The total effect of
father's occupational status on son's occupational status was
approximately 0.405, decomposed into an indirect effect via
education of 0.227 (57%) and a direct effect of 0.178 (43%). The
ratio of education's effect to father's direct effect was 2.9:1,
supporting the conclusion that achievement was more important than
ascription in mid-century America.

However, the model also demonstrated that ascription operated
through achievement: family background was a strong determinant of
educational attainment, which in turn determined occupational
outcomes. The model explained 42% of variance in adult occupational
status -- a substantial figure, but one that left the majority of
variance unexplained. Subsequent research (Featherman and Hauser,
1978; the Wisconsin studies) found that adding social psychological
mediators (aspirations, significant others' influence) improved
explanatory power and revealed that family background operated
partly through these psychological channels.

### Bourdieu's Cultural Capital: Mixed Empirical Support

Bourdieu's social reproduction thesis has generated extensive
empirical research with mixed results. On the supportive side,
studies have found that parental cultural participation (museum
visits, reading, concert attendance) is associated with children's
educational attainment, controlling for parental education and
income. Lareau (1987, 2003) documented class differences in
family-school relationships, showing that middle-class parents
engage in "concerted cultivation" -- organized activity
participation, institutional intervention -- while working-class
parents practice "natural growth" -- unstructured time, deference
to institutional authority. These differences in parenting
practices produce differential school success that appears
meritocratic but is partly rooted in class background.

On the critical side, Tzanakis (2011) reviewed key quantitative
studies and concluded that cultural capital, strictly defined
within Bourdieu's framework, has limited confirmed power as a
mechanism of class reproduction in large-scale representative
longitudinal data. The role of teachers and schools as agents of
exclusion could not be confirmed in quantitative research, and
cultural capital's analytic potential appeared restricted when
bound to Bourdieu's original class-analytic framework. Goldthorpe
argued that habitus is not formed once and for all in the family
but is also shaped by schools, and that the empirical record shows
substantial upward educational mobility out of the working class --
contradicting the strong reproduction thesis. The consensus among
contemporary researchers is that cultural capital retains
explanatory value as one mechanism among several, but that it
cannot alone account for the persistence of stratification
(Sullivan, 2002; Van de Werfhorst, 2010; Farkas, UC Irvine).

## Implications

### For Economic Analysis and Policy

Social stratification and mobility research directly informs
economic policy debates about inequality, taxation, education, and
social insurance. The Great Gatsby Curve implies that policies
tolerating high inequality produce low mobility in subsequent
generations -- inequality is not merely an outcome but a cause of
rigidified social structure. This finding challenges the view that
equal opportunity can be achieved without addressing unequal
outcomes. If the curve holds, reducing inequality is a prerequisite
for increasing mobility, not an alternative to it.

For macroeconomic analysis, stratification research connects to
human capital theory: if family background determines educational
attainment, and education determines productivity and earnings,
then unequal access to education produces aggregate efficiency
losses. Children from low-income families who would have been high
productivity workers do not reach their potential, reducing total
output. This is the "wasted talent" critique of stratified
societies: inequality is not only unjust but inefficient. The
Chetty et al. (2014a) finding that mobility varies more across
regions within the United States than across countries suggests
that subnational institutional differences -- school funding
formulas, zoning laws, social capital -- are policy levers with
large potential returns.

The distinction between relative and absolute mobility has
implications for growth policy. If relative mobility is stable and
absolute mobility is declining, restoring the "American Dream"
(understood as children doing better than their parents) requires
either faster growth or less inequality. Chetty et al. (2017)
estimated that even with GDP growth rates matching the postwar
golden age, absolute mobility would not return to 1940s levels
without distributional change. This finding reframes the mobility
debate: growth alone is insufficient; the distribution of growth
matters.

### For Education and Human Capital

Education is the primary institutional mechanism through which
stratification is transmitted and potentially disrupted. The
Blau-Duncan model showed that family background affects
occupational outcomes largely through education. Bourdieu argued
that education reproduces stratification by rewarding inherited
cultural capital. Chetty et al. (2014a) found that school quality
(measured by test scores and class size) was one of the five
factors correlated with upward mobility.

These findings have direct implications for education policy.
School funding equalization, early childhood education, and
reductions in residential segregation all target the mechanisms
through which family background determines educational outcomes.
The mixed evidence on Bourdieu's cultural capital thesis suggests
that schools are not purely agents of reproduction: they can also
compensate for family disadvantage, as Goldthorpe argued. The
policy question is under what conditions schools become engines of
mobility rather than reproducers of stratification. The Chetty et
al. (2014a) geographic variation suggests that some local
educational systems achieve substantially better mobility outcomes
than others, indicating that institutional design matters.

### For Political Behavior and Social Cohesion

Stratification shapes political behavior and social cohesion.
Societies with low mobility and high inequality tend to exhibit
lower political participation among lower strata, higher
polarization, and weaker social trust. The perception that the
system is rigged -- that effort does not translate into advancement
-- erodes the legitimacy of democratic institutions. The Great
Gatsby Curve's challenge to the American Dream is a case in point:
if the belief that anyone can succeed through hard work is
empirically false, the ideological foundation of American political
culture weakens.

Research on social capital (Putnam; Coleman) connects
stratification to civic engagement. Chetty et al. (2014a) found
that social capital was one of the five factors correlated with
upward mobility, suggesting that community networks, civic
participation, and neighborhood cohesion are not only outcomes of
stratification but inputs to mobility. This creates a potential
virtuous or vicious cycle: communities with high social capital
produce mobile individuals who contribute to further social
capital, while communities with low social capital produce
immobility that further degrades social ties.

For comparative politics, stratification research connects to
democratic stability. Democracies with high mobility can absorb
inequality because citizens believe their children will have
opportunities. Democracies with low mobility face pressure to
redistribute, but high inequality may also produce political power
concentration that blocks redistribution. The C. Wright Mills power
elite thesis, updated with contemporary data on campaign finance
and lobbying, suggests that stratification can become
self-reinforcing through political channels: those at the top use
their resources to preserve the structure that placed them there.

### For Demographic and Population Research

Stratification interacts with demographic processes in multiple
ways. Fertility varies by social class: in most developed
countries, women with higher education and income have fewer
children, later in life. This differential fertility can amplify
or attenuate stratification, depending on whether low-fertility
groups are concentrated at the top or bottom of the distribution.
The demographic transition, documented in the companion topic on
demographic transition, unfolds differently across social strata:
mortality declines first among higher-status groups with better
access to healthcare, nutrition, and sanitation, and fertility
declines follow as women gain education and labor market
opportunities.

Migration is both a cause and consequence of stratification.
International migration often represents intergenerational mobility
-- children of migrants may exceed their parents' position in the
destination country's stratification system. But migration also
creates new stratification axes: migrant status, ethnicity, and
legal status interact with class to shape opportunity. Internal
migration within countries (rural-to-urban, regional) has
historically been a major mobility channel, but residential
segregation can limit its effectiveness: Chetty et al. (2014a)
found that less residential segregation was associated with higher
mobility, suggesting that the quality of destination neighborhoods
matters as much as the act of moving.

## Common Pitfalls

A frequent error is conflating inequality with stratification.
Inequality refers to the dispersion of outcomes (income, wealth);
stratification refers to the hierarchical structure that produces
and reproduces those outcomes. A society can have high inequality
but high mobility (outcomes are dispersed but individuals move
freely among positions) or low inequality but low mobility (outcomes
are compressed but position is inherited). The Great Gatsby Curve
shows these are empirically related, but they are conceptually
distinct and should not be treated as synonyms.

A second error is treating class as a unidimensional economic
construct. Weber's contribution was to show that class, status, and
power are distinct dimensions that can diverge. Reducing
stratification to income or wealth alone misses status hierarchies
based on ethnicity, religion, or occupation, and power hierarchies
based on organizational position or political office. Status
inconsistency -- high class position with low status, or vice versa
-- is a common and analytically important phenomenon that a
unidimensional framework cannot capture.

A third error is assuming that mobility is inherently upward.
Downward mobility is equally part of the process: some children
fall below their parents' positions. In highly mobile societies,
substantial upward and downward movement coexist. Policy
discussions often focus on upward mobility alone, but the
analytical concept encompasses both directions. The rate of
downward mobility from the top is as informative about system
openness as the rate of upward mobility from the bottom.
