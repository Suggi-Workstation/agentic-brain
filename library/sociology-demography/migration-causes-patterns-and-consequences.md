# Migration -- The Demographic Process That Reshapes Sending and Receiving Societies

Migration is the movement of people across a boundary -- a household, a
city, a region, or a national border -- to establish a new place of
residence. It is one of the three demographic processes, alongside
fertility and mortality, that determine how populations change in size,
structure, and distribution, and it is the only one of the three that
operates across administrative and political borders. The central finding
of a century and a half of migration research is that movement is not
random: it is patterned by economic gradients, social networks, policy
regimes, and environmental shocks, and its cumulative effects transform
both the places people leave and the places they arrive. Migration
therefore functions as a demographic engine that redistributes labour,
capital, ideas, and population structure across space, with consequences
that compound over generations.

## Background

The systematic study of migration begins with the work of the
Anglo-German geographer Ernest Georg Ravenstein, who in 1885 read a
paper before the Statistical Society of London titled "The Laws of
Migration." Ravenstein's originality was not the observation that people
move -- that had long been obvious -- but his insistence that the
movement obeyed discoverable regularities. He was provoked by a remark
of the statistician William Farr, who had noted that migration
"appeared to go on without any definite law." Ravenstein set out to
prove Farr wrong, and to do so he turned to the most reliable
quantitative instrument available to a nineteenth-century social
scientist: the census. Using the birthplace tables from the 1871 and
1881 censuses of the British Isles, Ravenstein compared the county of
birth with the county of residence for tens of millions of individuals,
a method that allowed him to trace the direction, distance, and volume
of internal population flows with a precision previously unattainable
(Ravenstein, 1885; Rees and Lomax, 2020).

From this analysis Ravenstein extracted a set of empirical
generalizations he called "laws." The most consequential of these held
that the great body of migrants proceed only a short distance, producing
currents of migration that set in toward the great centres of commerce
and industry which absorb them; that each main current produces a
compensating countercurrent; that the natives of towns are less
migratory than the natives of rural areas; and that females are more
migratory than males. He extended the analysis in an 1889 second paper
to continental Europe and North America, mapping the foreign-born
populations of the United States and Canada and confirming that the
same spatial regularities operated at the international scale (Ravenstein,
1889). Ravenstein was careful to note that his "laws" were not immutable
rules but empirical generalizations specific to the time and place of
the evidence -- a caveat that later scholars would sometimes forget
(Rees and Lomax, 2020).

Ravenstein's laws anticipated by nearly a century the conceptual
vocabulary that would dominate mid-twentieth-century migration
scholarship: the distinction between push factors (conditions at the
origin that motivate departure) and pull factors (conditions at the
destination that attract arrivals). The push-pull framework was
formalized by Rudolf Heberle in 1938 for rural-urban migration and
elaborated by Everett Lee in 1966, who added the mediating role of
intervening obstacles and personal characteristics. Lee's model
distinguished four sets of factors: push factors associated with the
area of origin, pull factors associated with the area of destination,
intervening obstacles between the two, and personal factors that
condition the individual's perception of all three (Lee, 1966). The
push-pull model remained the dominant heuristic for decades, and
although it would later be criticized for its individualism and its
neglect of structural and network determinants, it captured an
enduring truth: migration is simultaneously an individual decision and
a response to spatial inequalities in opportunity.

The field underwent a theoretical revolution in the 1980s and 1990s as
scholars moved beyond the push-pull framework toward a set of
complementary theories operating at different levels of analysis. The
landmark synthesis was Douglas Massey and colleagues' 1993 review,
"Theories of International Migration: A Review and Appraisal," which
organized the fragmented literature into a set of distinct but
compatible theories: neoclassical economics at the individual and
macro levels, the new economics of labour migration at the household
level, dual labour market theory and world systems theory at the
structural level, and network theory and the theory of cumulative
causation at the community level (Massey et al., 1993). The
contribution of this synthesis was to show that these theories are not
rivals but explanations of different stages and facets of the migration
process, and that no single theory could account for the full
complexity of why people move, where they go, and why migration
persists long after the original causes have faded.

Parallel to the theory of migration causes, a separate intellectual
tradition developed around its consequences: the incorporation of
migrants into receiving societies. The classical statement was Milton
Gordon's 1964 book "Assimilation in American Life," which proposed a
linear seven-stage model of assimilation from acculturation through
structural integration and intermarriage to the eventual absence of
prejudice and value conflict. Gordon's model reflected the experience
of early-twentieth-century European immigration to the United States
and assumed a relatively homogeneous host society. By the 1990s,
scholars working with the post-1965 wave of immigration from Asia and
Latin America found that Gordon's linear model did not fit the new
evidence. Alejandro Portes and Min Zhou responded in 1993 with the
theory of segmented assimilation, which holds that the host society is
stratified and that immigrants may assimilate into different segments of
it -- into the middle class, into an urban underclass, or into a
deliberately preserved ethnic community -- with sharply different
outcomes for the second generation (Portes and Zhou, 1993; Xie and
Greenman, 2011). This reframing made the consequences of migration
contingent on context rather than deterministic.

The empirical backdrop to these theoretical developments is the
increasing scale and complexity of human movement. The International
Organization for Migration estimates that 281 million people were
international migrants in 2022, the highest figure in modern records,
and that internal displacement -- movement that does not cross an
international border -- affected an additional 117 million people by the
end of that year, driven by conflict, violence, and disaster (IOM,
2024). The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre reported that 75.9
million people were living in internal displacement at the end of 2023,
with an annual average of 21.9 million disaster-related movements over
the preceding decade (IDMC, 2024). Remittances -- the money migrants
send home -- grew from 128 billion United States dollars in 2000 to
905 billion dollars in 2024, exceeding foreign direct investment to
low- and middle-income countries as a source of external finance
(IOM, 2024; IOM, 2026). These magnitudes make migration a first-order
demographic and economic phenomenon, not a peripheral one.

## Core Concepts

### Push and Pull Factors

The push-pull framework remains the most widely taught and intuitively
accessible model of migration causation. Push factors are negative
conditions at the place of origin that motivate departure: unemployment,
low wages, political persecution, environmental degradation, war, and
famine. Pull factors are positive conditions at the destination that
attract arrivals: higher wages, labour demand, political freedom, family
connections, and educational opportunity. The framework's strength is
its parsimony: it reduces the bewildering variety of migration motives
to two categories anchored in the geography of inequality. Its weakness,
recognized from the outset, is that it treats the decision to move as an
individual cost-benefit calculation and neglects the structural,
household, and network determinants that the later theoretical
synthesis would foreground (Massey et al., 1993). A second limitation is
that push and pull factors are necessary but not sufficient: millions
of people experience push conditions without migrating, and many
attractive destinations never generate sustained inflows. The model
therefore explains why migration is more likely where gradients are
steeper, but not why it occurs at all in some cases and not others.

The push-pull framework also presupposes a degree of agency that not
all migrants possess. Refugees fleeing war and disaster are driven
primarily by push factors so severe that the destination is a matter of
survival rather than attraction. The distinction between voluntary and
forced migration, central to international law and policy, rests on the
relative dominance of push over pull: a refugee is a person who has a
well-founded fear of persecution and has crossed an international border,
while an internally displaced person has been forced to move but has not
crossed such a border. The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees
and the Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre maintain separate
counting systems for these populations, reflecting the legal and
operational significance of the distinction even where the underlying
experience of displacement is continuous (IDMC, 2024).

### Ravenstein's Laws and the Gravity of Distance

Ravenstein's first and most durable law -- that most migrants proceed
only a short distance -- formalizes what geographers later called the
distance decay of migration: the probability of moving to a destination
declines as the distance to it increases, all else being equal. This
regularity underpins the gravity model of migration, which predicts the
volume of flow between two places as a function of their populations and
the distance separating them, by analogy with Newton's law of
gravitational attraction. The gravity model has been validated across
countries and centuries and remains a workhorse of migration
forecasting and spatial interaction analysis (Rees and Lomax, 2020;
Tobler, 1995). Ravenstein's observation that towns are less migratory
than rural areas reflects the same mechanism: urban residents already
live near the centres of absorption that draw migrants, so they have
shorter effective distances to the opportunities that motivate
movement. His law that each main current produces a compensating
countercurrent anticipates the finding that return migration and
counter-flows are systematic features of migration systems, not
anomalies.

### The New Economics of Labour Migration

Oded Stark and David Bloom's 1985 article "The New Economics of Labour
Migration" reframed the unit of analysis from the individual to the
household. Under the neoclassical model, an individual migrates to
maximize expected lifetime earnings, comparing wages at the origin and
destination net of migration costs. The new economics argues that
migration decisions are made jointly by households, not individuals,
and that the motive is not only income maximization but risk
diversification. A household sends one member to work in a distant or
foreign labour market not simply because the wages there are higher but
because the income stream from that market is imperfectly correlated
with the household's income at home. Migration thus functions as an
insurance mechanism: it spreads the household's income sources across
markets that are subject to different shocks, reducing the variance of
household income in the presence of incomplete or absent credit and
insurance markets (Stark and Bloom, 1985). This reframing explains why
migration occurs even when wage differentials are small and why it
persists when they narrow: the household is diversifying risk, not
merely chasing higher wages.

A second contribution of the new economics is the concept of relative
deprivation. Stark and Bloom observed that migration behaviour responds
not only to absolute income but to a household's income relative to a
reference group. A household that is poor in absolute terms but not
poor relative to its neighbours has less incentive to migrate than a
household with the same absolute income that is relatively deprived
within its community. Migration becomes a strategy for improving the
household's relative position, not only its absolute standard of
living. This insight connects migration to the sociology of inequality
and reference-group behaviour and helps explain why emigration rates
can rise during periods of economic growth if the growth is unevenly
distributed: rising average incomes can increase the relative
deprivation of those left behind, strengthening their incentive to
move (Stark and Bloom, 1985).

### Migration Networks and Cumulative Causation

Network theory and the theory of cumulative causation, both
synthesized in Massey et al. (1993), explain why migration becomes
self-sustaining over time. Migrant networks are sets of interpersonal
ties -- kinship, friendship, and shared community origin -- that
connect migrants in the destination to non-migrants and former migrants
in the origin. These ties reduce the costs and risks of migration:
someone with a relative or friend at the destination has access to
housing, job leads, and practical assistance, and thus faces lower
costs and uncertainty than someone without such connections. As each
migrant establishes ties to the destination, the cost of migrating
falls for the next person in the network, who in turn extends the
network further. This process produces cumulative causation: each act
of migration increases the likelihood of subsequent migration, so
that a flow that begins for one set of reasons may continue and expand
for entirely different reasons (Massey et al., 1993). The theory
explains why migration from a particular origin to a particular
destination can persist across generations and through changes in
economic conditions, policy regimes, and political circumstances.

Cumulative causation operates through several reinforcing mechanisms.
Remittances sent home finance the migration of additional household and
community members. Returning migrants transmit information, reduce
uncertainty for would-be migrants, and may demonstrate success that
shifts the local culture toward migration as a normal rite of passage.
The redistribution of income within the sending community alters the
reference group and increases relative deprivation for non-migrant
households, raising their incentive to send a member abroad. Together
these mechanisms produce what scholars have called a "migration
hump": emigration rates rise as a sending country develops from very
low income levels, peak at middle-income levels, and then decline as
the country approaches the income levels of destination countries. The
implication is that development in the poorest countries can initially
increase emigration rather than reducing it, a finding with direct
relevance to migration policy (de Haas, 2010, as cited in Massey et
al., 1993).

### Internal versus International Migration

A distinction fundamental to demography separates internal migration
(movement within national borders) from international migration
(movement across them). The distinction is consequential for
measurement, law, and theory. Internal migration is far larger in
volume: the great majority of human movement occurs within countries,
from rural to urban areas, between regions, and between cities.
International migration, though smaller in absolute numbers, carries
greater political, legal, and economic significance because it crosses
the institutional boundary of the state and subjects the migrant to a
different legal regime, labour market, and welfare system. Internal
migrants retain citizenship and most legal rights at the destination;
international migrants may acquire, lose, or remain in limbo with
respect to legal status, with profound consequences for their economic
incorporation and personal security (IOM, 2024).

The measurement of internal migration presents distinctive challenges.
Census-based measures rely on birthplace-residence comparisons, as
Ravenstein pioneered, or on place of residence at a fixed prior date.
Population registers in countries that maintain them provide
continuous records of moves. Survey-based measures estimate migration
rates from retrospective questions. Each method captures a different
aspect of movement and is subject to different biases, and the lack of
comparable cross-national data on internal migration long hampered
comparative research. Recent efforts to build international databases of
internal migration flow data have produced indicators for countries
covering roughly 80 percent of the world's population, enabling
systematic comparison for the first time (Rees and Lomax, 2020).

### Remittances and Social Ties

Remittances -- financial transfers from migrants to households and
communities at the origin -- are the most measurable and economically
significant direct effect of migration on sending societies. Global
remittances reached 905 billion United States dollars in 2024,
including 685 billion to low- and middle-income countries, a flow
larger than foreign direct investment to those countries (IOM, 2026).
India was the largest single recipient, with 137.67 billion dollars in
inflows, followed by Mexico and the Philippines. For the most
remittance-dependent countries, remittances constitute a substantial
share of gross domestic product: Tajikistan at 47.2 percent, Tonga at
39.2 percent, and Nepal at 26.0 percent (IOM, 2026). At the household
level, remittances smooth consumption, fund education and healthcare,
finance housing and small businesses, and provide insurance against
shocks. At the macroeconomic level, they stabilize the balance of
payments and can appreciate the real exchange rate, a phenomenon known
as "Dutch disease" that complicates the assessment of remittances as
purely beneficial.

The concept of social remittances, developed by Peggy Levitt, extends
the analysis beyond money to the transfer of ideas, practices,
identities, and social capital that migrants carry home through visits,
communication, and return. Social remittances can reshape gender norms,
political participation, entrepreneurial practices, and attitudes
toward education and health in sending communities. Research on
post-transition economies documents migrants acting as agents of
change who transfer knowledge and practices affecting entrepreneurship,
politics, and everyday life, with effects that depend on the receiving
community's capacity to absorb and implement them (MDPI, 2022). The
concept of cognitive remittances further refines this analysis to
capture changes in self-perception and political identity that
returning migrants bring home, including heightened sensitivity to
discrimination and new expectations of governance (Springer, 2022).

## Evidence

The empirical study of migration rests on a combination of census
data, population registers, household surveys, administrative records,
and -- increasingly -- geolocated displacement data and big-data
sources. The following cases illustrate the range of evidence and the
conclusions it supports.

### Ravenstein's Census Analysis and Its Legacy

Ravenstein's foundational evidence was the birthplace data from the
1871 and 1881 British censuses. By comparing county of birth with
county of residence for the populations of England, Wales, Scotland,
and Ireland, he was able to map the direction and volume of internal
migration and to derive his laws. The key methodological finding was
that population growth rates in counties did not match the growth rates
of county natives: the difference was attributable to in- and
out-migration. This decomposition allowed Ravenstein to identify
counties of absorption (net in-migration) and dispersion (net
out-migration) and to show that the dominant direction of movement was
toward the centres of commerce and industry -- the industrializing
cities of England and Scotland (Ravenstein, 1885; Rees and Lomax,
2020). His 1889 extension to continental Europe and North America
confirmed that the same spatial regularities operated at the
international scale: foreign-born populations concentrated in the
industrializing regions that offered employment, and the volume of
inflow was inversely related to distance from the origin. Later
research calibrated the distance-decay relationship formally through
gravity models, and the concentric logic of absorption was refined by
Hagerstrand's longitudinal migration records and by the multiple-nuclei
and sector models of urban form. Ravenstein's data were limited to
lifetime migrants and to the United Kingdom, but subsequent
replications across countries and centuries have confirmed the
robustness of his central regularities (Tobler, 1995; Rees and Lomax,
2020).

### Massey et al.: The Cumulative-Causation Evidence

The 1993 synthesis by Massey and colleagues assembled the evidence for
each of the theories it reviewed. For the new economics of labour
migration, the evidence came from household surveys in Mexico that
showed migration decisions were made by households, not individuals, and
that the motive was risk diversification as much as income maximization.
Households with more assets and more diversified income sources were
less likely to send a migrant, consistent with the insurance function.
For network theory, the evidence showed that the probability of
migration rose with the number of prior migrants from the same
community already at the destination, and that this effect persisted
after controlling for wage differentials and economic conditions. For
cumulative causation, the evidence showed that each act of migration
altered the distribution of income and the culture of the sending
community in ways that increased the odds of subsequent movement,
producing a self-reinforcing dynamic that could sustain migration
independently of the original causes. The synthesis concluded that the
various theories were complementary rather than competing, each
capturing a different level and stage of the migration process, and
that no single theory could account for the full complexity of observed
flows (Massey et al., 1993).

### The Brain Drain or Brain Gain Evidence

A long-running debate concerns whether the emigration of skilled
workers from developing countries depletes the human capital of sending
societies (brain drain) or increases it (brain gain). The traditional
brain-drain argument holds that the emigration of the educated removes
human capital, reduces tax revenue, and creates shortages in critical
sectors such as healthcare. A 2024 review in Science synthesized the
recent causal evidence and found a more nuanced picture: the direct
brain-drain effect exists, but it is offset and sometimes outweighed by
indirect brain-gain channels. Migration prospects incentivize investment
in education, and many who invest in education for migration
ultimately do not emigrate, increasing the domestic human capital
stock. Remittances fund further education. Diaspora networks create
trade and investment linkages. Returning migrants bring education and
work experience acquired abroad. The review concluded that the largest
welfare gains accrue to the migrants themselves, who often more than
double their incomes, and that migration produces broader positive
effects on origin communities through remittances, knowledge transfer,
and return (Batista et al., 2024). The IZA World of Labor synthesis
adds that whether a country experiences net brain drain or brain gain
depends on its level of development, population size, and the
probability of emigration: the human-capital-maximizing brain-drain rate
is around 10 percent, above which losses tend to exceed gains. Of 108
developing countries studied, 88 were net losers and 20 net winners,
with the largest developing countries -- Brazil, China, India --
experiencing moderate gains and the small states of sub-Saharan Africa
and Central America experiencing substantial losses (Docquier, 2014).

### Internal Displacement: The IDMC Data

The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre's 2024 Global Report on
Internal Displacement provides the most comprehensive quantitative
evidence on forced internal migration. At the end of 2023, 75.9 million
people were living in internal displacement worldwide, the highest
figure on record. Of these, 68.3 million were displaced by conflict
and violence and 7.6 million by disasters. The year 2023 saw 26.4
million new conflict displacements and 3.4 million new disaster
displacements. Over the preceding decade, weather-related hazards drove
an average of 21.9 million internal displacements per year, a figure
that climate change is expected to increase as extreme weather events
become more frequent and intense. The data show that disaster
displacement is concentrated in the Horn of Africa and Asia, and that
its socioeconomic profile varies by hazard type: storm and flood
displacement affects farming communities, drought displacement affects
pastoral communities, and wildfire displacement affects urban
communities. The 2025 report updated the stock to 84.3 million people
living in internal displacement at the end of 2024, continuing the
upward trend (IDMC, 2024; IDMC, 2025).

### Remittance Flows: The IOM and World Bank Data

The IOM World Migration Reports, drawing on World Bank data, document
the growth and distribution of remittances. International remittances
rose from 128 billion United States dollars in 2000 to 831 billion in
2022 and 905 billion in 2024, with 685 billion of the 2024 total going
to low- and middle-income countries -- exceeding foreign direct
investment to those countries as a source of external finance. India
received 137.67 billion dollars in 2024, the first country to exceed
100 billion in annual remittances. The United States was the largest
sending country. Remittance transfer costs remain high, averaging 6.6
percent globally against a Sustainable Development Goal target of 3
percent, with sub-Saharan Africa the most expensive corridor at
roughly 8 percent. The IOM notes that official data do not capture
unrecorded formal and informal flows, so actual totals are likely
higher than the estimates (IOM, 2024; IOM, 2026).

### Segmented Assimilation: The Second-Generation Evidence

Empirical tests of segmented assimilation theory have examined the
educational and economic outcomes of the children of post-1965
immigrants in the United States. Xie and Greenman (2011) tested the
theory's central implication -- that the consequences of assimilation
depend on local social context -- and found support: immigrant children
living in low-socioeconomic-status inner-city communities faced higher
risks of downward assimilation, while those in high-socioeconomic-status
communities followed patterns closer to classical straight-line
assimilation. The evidence also supported the theory's emphasis on
selective acculturation -- the deliberate preservation of the immigrant
community's language and values alongside economic integration -- as a
protective factor, particularly in disadvantaged contexts. The
dissonant-acculturation dynamic, in which children acculturate faster
than parents, was associated with parent-child conflict and elevated
risk of downward mobility. These findings challenged the assumption
that assimilation is uniformly beneficial and established that its
outcomes are conditional on the segment of society into which
immigrants are incorporated (Portes and Zhou, 1993; Xie and Greenman,
2011).

## Implications

### For Demographic Theory and Population Structure

Migration alters population structure in ways that fertility and
mortality cannot. It redistributes the age composition: migrants are
disproportionately of working age, so emigration ages the sending
population and immigration rejuvenates the receiving population. This
selectivity by age means that migration's demographic effects are not
symmetric: a country that loses young workers experiences an increase
in its dependency ratio and a contraction of its labour force, while a
country that gains them experiences the opposite. The connection to
the demographic transition is direct: as fertility falls and
populations age in the later stages of the transition, immigration
becomes one of the few mechanisms that can replenish the working-age
population and stabilize the dependency ratio. Countries of
immigration in Europe and North America have come to rely on
immigration for population and labour-force growth as their native-born
populations stagnate or decline, making migration a structural rather
than a residual component of their demographic futures (see
library/sociology-demography/demographic-transition.md). Migration also
selects on education, health, and ambition, so its demographic effects
are filtered through compositional change: the people who move are not
a random sample of the origin population, and their departure and
arrival alter the skill, health, and behavioural composition of both
ends of the flow.

### For Sending Societies

The effects of emigration on sending societies are double-edged. On
the one hand, remittances provide a substantial and relatively stable
income stream that smooths consumption, funds education and health
expenditure, and supports small business formation. For the most
remittance-dependent countries, remittances are macroeconomically
significant and constitute the largest source of external finance,
exceeding foreign direct investment and official development
assistance. On the other hand, the emigration of skilled workers can
deplete human capital, create fiscal losses, and generate shortages in
critical sectors, with effects that vary by country size and
development level: large middle-income countries can absorb and
compensate for skilled emigration, while small and poor countries
cannot. The brain-gain evidence suggests that migration prospects can
increase domestic human capital formation, but only where education
systems can produce replacements and where the probability of
emigration is not so high that nearly all who train depart. The
broader consequences include the transformation of gender roles when
women become heads of household in the absence of male migrants, the
emergence of a culture of migration that normalizes departure as a
rite of passage, and the political effects of diaspora engagement
with the homeland through remittances, investment, and advocacy
(Stark and Bloom, 1985; Batista et al., 2024; Docquier, 2014).

### For Receiving Societies

The incorporation of immigrants into receiving societies is the central
policy and sociological question of the destination end of the
migration process. Classical assimilation theory predicted a gradual
convergence of immigrants toward the norms, language, and
socioeconomic position of the host society, a pattern supported by
the early-twentieth-century European experience in the United States.
Segmented assimilation theory, developed to account for the post-1965
immigration from Asia and Latin America, holds that assimilation is
not uniform: the outcomes for immigrant children depend on the segment
of society into which they are incorporated, the human capital of
their parents, the context of reception, and the preservation or loss
of ethnic community ties. The policy implication is that the
receiving society's institutions -- schools, labour markets, housing
markets, and anti-discrimination regimes -- are not passive backdrops
but active determinants of assimilation outcomes. Where these
institutions are inclusive and well-resourced, immigrants and their
children tend to follow trajectories of upward mobility; where they are
segmented and under-resourced, the risk of downward assimilation rises
(Gordon, 1964; Portes and Zhou, 1993; Xie and Greenman, 2011). The
labour-market effects of immigration on native workers have been
extensively studied and generally found to be small on average, with
distributional effects concentrated among the native workers whose
skills are closest substitutes for immigrant labour. The fiscal
effects depend on the immigrant's age, skill, legal status, and
duration of residence: working-age immigrants tend to be net
contributors to public finances, while the calculation is more
ambiguous for elderly or low-skill immigrants (IOM, 2024).

### For Policy

Migration policy sits at the intersection of demography, economics,
and politics, and its design must contend with the fact that the
determinants of migration extend beyond any single country's control.
Push factors in the origin and pull factors in the destination are
shaped by wage differentials, labour demand, conflict, environmental
change, and network ties that cross borders. Policy interventions that
attempt to stop migration by closing borders often fail to address the
underlying drivers and can redirect flows into irregular channels,
increasing the risks to migrants and the costs of enforcement. The
migration hump -- the empirical regularity that emigration rates rise
with development up to a point and then decline -- implies that
economic development in origin countries will increase, not decrease,
emigration in the short to medium term, a finding that complicates
the common assumption that development assistance will reduce
migration pressure. The remittance evidence implies that reducing
transfer costs, which remain well above international targets, would
return substantial sums to receiving households at no fiscal cost to
sending countries. The internal displacement evidence implies that
climate change is becoming a structural driver of migration that
existing legal and institutional frameworks -- built around the
voluntary-forced distinction and the international-internal boundary --
are not fully equipped to manage (IOM, 2024; IDMC, 2024). The
assimilation evidence implies that integration outcomes are not fixed
by the characteristics of immigrants alone but are shaped by the
receiving society's institutions and choices, giving destination
countries substantial policy leverage over the long-run consequences
of the migration they have already received.

### For Urbanization and Spatial Structure

Migration is the proximate mechanism of urbanization: the growth of
cities is driven by rural-to-urban migration as much as by natural
increase. Ravenstein identified the dominant direction of internal
migration as movement toward centres of commerce and industry, and
this pattern has intensified globally as the share of humanity living
in urban areas has risen toward the United Nations projection of 68
percent by 2050. The connection between migration and urbanization is
not merely quantitative but qualitative: the social structure of
cities, their stratification, segregation, and collective organization,
are shaped by the composition and sequencing of the migration flows
that build them. International migration adds a further layer, producing
the global cities that serve as nodes in the international migration
system and whose social structure reflects the diversity of their
immigrant populations (see
library/sociology-demography/urbanization-and-city-life.md). The
implication is that migration policy and urban policy are
inseparable: the housing, infrastructure, schooling, and labour-market
institutions that determine whether migrants and their children
prosper or founder are urban institutions, and the cities that receive
migrants are themselves products of the migration that preceded them.
