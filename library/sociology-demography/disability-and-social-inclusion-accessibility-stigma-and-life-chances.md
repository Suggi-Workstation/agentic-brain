# Disability and Social Inclusion -- How Institutions, Accessibility, and Stigma Turn Impairment into Unequal Life Chances

Disability is measured and experienced as a relationship between people with long-term impairments and the environments, institutions, and expectations they meet, not as a property of bodies alone. In the United States and in many other settings, adults with disabilities are far less often employed and more often poor than other adults, and many live in homes without basic accessibility features; the evidence identifies hiring behavior, stigmatizing classification, unmeasured extra costs, and the design of buildings and services as mechanisms alongside impairment itself. Competing models of disability disagree about how much of this disadvantage the environment can remove, and that disagreement determines what counts as inclusion and how it is measured.

## Background

The default understanding of disability in everyday speech is biomedical. Tossebro describes the common assumption that "everyone knows" what disability means: a body with functional limitations caused by a health condition. He notes that political and professional conceptualizations of disability traditionally followed the same logic [3]. Mont's World Bank paper describes the same medical model as linking disability to medical conditions and treating it as a problem residing solely in the affected individual, with interventions consisting of medical rehabilitation and social assistance [7]. The sociological interest in disability begins where this default is challenged: if the same impairment produces different life outcomes in different settings, the setting belongs in the explanation.

The challenge took several forms. Tossebro dates an early official statement to a 1967 Norwegian White Paper, which argued that disability policy should address not only how to change disabled people so that they cope better in their environment but also how to change the environment to fit people with impairments. He calls the resulting reorientation an "environmental turn" and reports that it is visible in social science, in national policy documents, in the United Nations Standard Rules of 1994, and in the World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) of 2001 [3].

In Britain a more radical formulation came from disabled people themselves. The Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) and the Disability Alliance met on 22 November 1975, and the booklet that records the meeting (1976) contains the Union's statement that it is society which disables physically impaired people, that disability is something imposed on top of impairments by the way people are unnecessarily isolated and excluded from full participation in society, and that disabled people are therefore an oppressed group [2]. The Union defined impairment as lacking part or all of a limb, or having a defective limb, organ or mechanism of the body, and defined disability as the disadvantage or restriction of activity caused by a contemporary social organisation that takes no or little account of people with physical impairments and so excludes them from the mainstream of social activities [2].

The same booklet records a disagreement that anticipates later debates. The Union held that low income is a consequence of isolation and segregation in education, work, mobility, and housing, and that poverty is "one symptom of our oppression, but it is not the cause"; it opposed the Alliance's plans to assess a "degree of disability" and put its main effort into organising disabled people to change society [2]. The Alliance's revised aims committed it to persuading society to pay an income as of right to all disabled persons, whatever their age, cause, type or place of disablement, or working capacity, and its commentary argued that the absence of an income as of right is fundamental to the way society penalises people who are "not as others" [2]. The dispute over whether income or integration is the first lever is an early version of a question that recurs throughout this topic: how much disadvantage is produced by the environment, how much by lack of resources, and which intervention should come first.

Shakespeare and Watson trace the "social model of disability" to UPIAS in the 1970s and to its academic elaboration by Finkelstein (1980, 1981), Barnes (1991), and Oliver (1990, 1996), and report that it became the ideological litmus test of disability politics in Britain [1]. They distinguish it from the North American tradition, which developed the notion of people with disabilities as a minority group within US political thought without necessarily redefining disability as social oppression [1]. They credit the British model with identifying a political strategy, barrier removal, and with being liberating for individuals who learned that society, not they, was at fault; campaigners for anti-discrimination legislation also drew on the civil-rights model of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) [1].

Legal institutions then gave these ideas force. In the United States the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 and the Developmentally Disabled Assistance and Bill of Rights Act of 1975 preceded the ADA of 1990, whose findings state that society has historically tended to isolate and segregate individuals with disabilities and that such discrimination remains a serious and pervasive social problem [15]. In Olmstead v. L. C. (1999) the Supreme Court held that unjustified isolation is properly regarded as discrimination based on disability under Title II of the ADA [15]. Internationally, the United Nations adopted the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) in 2006 [7], and its text opened for signature at United Nations Headquarters on 30 March 2007 [5]. The CRPD preamble recognizes disability as an evolving concept that results from the interaction between persons with impairments and attitudinal and environmental barriers [5].

Disability is also a demographic fact tightly linked to age. In the 2010 Survey of Income and Program Participation, 8.4 percent of children under 15 had a disability compared with 70.5 percent of people aged 80 and older [8]. In 2025 half of all people with a disability in the United States were aged 65 or older, compared with about 18 percent of people without a disability [9]. The WHO estimates that 1.3 billion people, 16 percent of the world's population, experience significant disability, and attributes the growth of this number to the rise of noncommunicable diseases and longer lives [6]. These figures connect the topic to population aging: aging changes how many people meet environments built for a narrower range of bodies. The sociological question, as distinct from the clinical one, concerns how those environments are built, classified, and governed, and what group-level consequences follow.

## Core Concepts

### Impairment, Disability, and Three Families of Models

Three families of explanation organize the literature. The medical (biomedical) model locates disability in a body with functional limitations that results from a health condition; its policy implication is prevention or treatment of the individual and, where that fails, the construction of special environments [3]. The strong social model holds that disability is caused solely by the environment: a society not adapted to all people systematically creates barriers for groups such as people with impairments, human variation is taken for granted, and the political task is to change the environment so that it fits more of that variation [3]. The relational model, characteristic of Nordic policy documents, treats disability as a relationship between person and environment. Norwegian policy has defined disability as a mismatch between the person's capabilities and the functional demands of the environment, so that disability is relative and situational: Tossebro's example is that a blind person is not disabled when speaking on the telephone and is exceptionally able when the lights go out [3].

The relational family is embedded in international instruments. Lid reads the CRPD description of persons with disabilities as those with long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments which "in interaction with various barriers" may hinder full and effective participation as a relational conception, and she notes that the WHO and World Bank World Report on Disability uses a similar one [4][5]. The WHO fact sheet likewise states that disability results from the interaction between health conditions such as dementia, blindness, or spinal cord injury and a range of environmental and personal factors [6]. In the ICF, as Mont summarizes it, disability is not an all-or-nothing concept: people are classified by their functioning in the domains of body function and structure, activities, and participation, and a given level of impairment at the body level will not necessarily become an activity or participation limitation if the environment accommodates the person's functional status [7]. The Census Bureau, citing the ICF, describes disability as a gradient on which every person functions at different levels owing to personal and environmental factors [8].

Each family has been criticized from within the field. Shakespeare and Watson argue that the British social model neglects impairment, that its sharp impairment/disability dualism cannot be sustained (it is difficult to say where impairment ends and disability starts), and that many people with impairments do not identify as disabled [1]. They add that the "barrier-free environment" is an unsustainable myth, because removing an obstacle for one impairment can create one for another and because some limitations, such as constant pain, are not generated by the environment; they conclude that the model has been an excellent basis for a political movement but is now an inadequate grounding for a social theory [1]. Tossebro raises a logical objection: if disabled people are by definition discriminated against, then disability does not lead to fewer opportunities, it is fewer opportunities, and disability becomes an outcome concept that leaves no vocabulary for asking whether impairment leads to poorer living conditions [3]. He also reports Thomas's argument that the social-relational kernel of the social model concerns power relations between social groups, analogous to class structures, a sense of "relational" that she distinguishes from the Nordic person-environment sense [3].

Lid proposes that a relational model preserves what is useful in both older models and avoids a relapse into the medical/social dichotomy, because it directs research at the interaction itself and at individual, social, political, economic, and material factors together [4]. She draws on Nussbaum's political conception of the person, which treats embodiment and vulnerability as universal and so transcends the dichotomy of disabled and non-disabled people [4]. That position parallels Shakespeare and Watson's claim that everyone is impaired, although only a proportion of people experience the additional disabling processes of society [1]. The capability approach as a body of theory was not read for this entry; its connection here is limited to Lid's use of Nussbaum. The author's assessment is that the three families answer different questions: the medical model explains the need for care and treatment, the strong social model explains why barriers are an appropriate target of collective action, and the relational model supplies a measurement logic in which the same person can be more or less disabled across settings.

### Measurement: Disability as a Threshold on a Continuum

Disability prevalence depends on how disability is operationalized. Mont reports national prevalence rates ranging from under 1 percent in Kenya and Bangladesh to 20 percent in New Zealand, and shows that instruments within a single country diverge: Canadian adult rates in 2001 ranged from 13.7 percent to 31.3 percent depending on the survey [7]. The question format matters most. A direct question such as "Do you have a disability?" typically produces positive responses of 1 to 3 percent even when functional surveys of the same populations produce 10 to 20 percent, because the word carries negative connotations, because people with moderate limitations may not regard their situation as severe enough, and because people judge themselves against a cultural standard of normal functioning [7]. In Zambia a census-style question gave about 1 percent while a functional approach using Washington Group questions gave over 13 percent; Brazil's census recorded 0.9 percent with a list-of-conditions question and 14.5 percent in the later census that asked about difficulty with activities [7]. In a representative sample in Thai Binh province, Vietnam, the rate was 31.6 percent with any difficulty in any of six domains, 10.0 percent with "a lot of difficulty," and 2.6 percent with "unable" [7].

The UN Washington Group set recommended for censuses asks about difficulty seeing, hearing, walking or climbing stairs, remembering or concentrating, self-care, and communicating, with response categories of no difficulty, some difficulty, a lot of difficulty, and unable [7]. The United States Current Population Survey uses a set of six yes/no questions on hearing, vision, concentrating or remembering, walking or climbing stairs, dressing or bathing, and doing errands alone, and classifies a person as having a disability if the answer to any is yes [9]. The Census Bureau's Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP) instead uses a broader battery of functional, activity, and participation items and classifies disability as severe or nonsevere [8]. The author's assessment is that the gap between the SIPP estimate of 18.7 percent (2010) [8] and the CPS-based figure of about 13 percent of the population (2025) [9] illustrates the definitional point and should not be read as a trend; the Census report itself warns that estimates from other data sources use different criteria [8].

Mont argues that the threshold should follow the purpose of measurement. For general prevalence in a census, the purpose is "equalization of opportunities," which calls for questions on basic activities rather than on participation. A question about being limited in the kind or amount of work one can do is affected by the environment itself: as a society becomes more accessible, fewer people answer yes, and the measured disabled population could appear to become more limited even as inclusion improves [7]. Basic-activity measures can instead be correlated with outcomes such as employment and education; a negative correlation indicates that people with limitations do not have equal opportunities, which makes the prevalence statistic a gauge of social inclusiveness [7]. Mont recommends reporting at least two prevalence rates, one at a moderate and one at a severe threshold [7].

Two further features matter for interpretation. Household surveys exclude people living in institutions: the SIPP covers the civilian noninstitutionalized population, and the Census report notes that American Community Survey estimates suggest that over half of the population in institutional group quarters had a disability in 2010, so including them would likely enlarge the estimates [8]. The BLS estimates likewise cover the civilian noninstitutional population [9]. And disability counts are strongly age-structured, so comparisons between groups with different age profiles can mislead; the BLS notes that the low employment-population ratio of people with disabilities reflects in part their older age profile, but that people with disabilities were much less likely to be employed across all age groups [9].

### Accessibility and Universal Design

Accessibility is the institutional counterpart of the relational definition. The CRPD defines "reasonable accommodation" as necessary and appropriate modification and adjustment, not imposing a disproportionate or undue burden, where needed in a particular case, and defines "universal design" as the design of products, environments, programmes, and services to be usable by all people to the greatest extent possible without the need for adaptation or specialized design [5]. Article 9 extends accessibility to the physical environment, transportation, and information and communications, including information and communications technologies, and lists buildings, roads, housing, medical facilities, workplaces, and electronic and emergency services among the settings covered [5]. Article 2 treats denial of reasonable accommodation as a form of discrimination [5].

Lid argues that universal design reflects a shift from accessibility for people with disabilities toward the principle that there is one population comprising people with various abilities and impairments [4]. Her examples show why accessibility is relational and situational. In Norway, kerb cuts at pedestrian crossings are set at two centimetres, which lets people with sight loss detect the crossing and lets wheelchair users negotiate the kerb; yet in "shared space" street designs that remove kerbs and signs, the loss of safety and predictability can itself become the disabling barrier for road users with visual impairments [4]. A restaurant on a second floor reached by a 1:20 ramp or a staircase serves wheelchair users and people without mobility limits, but a person who walks slowly finds the ramp too long and the stairs too steep, and needs a lift [4]. Lid also stresses that accessibility has attitudinal as well as material dimensions: even when physical barriers are removed, negative attitudes and prejudice can remain [4].

### Stigma, Labels, and Classification

Stigma is a social process that operates through classification. Shifrer applies Link and Phelan's definition, under which stigma exists when powerful entities allow the elements of labeling, stereotyping, separating, status loss, and discrimination to co-occur [13]. She notes that students are referred for evaluation for learning disabilities on the basis of socially rooted and subjective characteristics and diagnosed on the basis of inconsistent criteria, and that students with disabilities were historically educated first in separate schools and then in separate classrooms within mainstream schools [13]. Shakespeare and Watson make a related point with a historical example: learning difficulties became salient only once a complex social order demanded literate workers and citizens, because in the medieval period being unable to read was not a problem when social processes did not demand literacy [1].

Classification also shapes self-presentation. Mont lists stigma, shame, and the severity connotation of the word as reasons why direct questions undercount disability, and notes that in some cultures disability is understood as punishment for past transgressions [7]. Lid describes a Norwegian practice in which applicants for public-administration posts are invited to tick a box if they identify as disabled, intended to secure an interview; she argues that this depends on a medical-model categorization and can lead to a more diffuse form of discrimination, because a later rejection can be explained as the choice of a better applicant, and that people may therefore be careful about disclosing their status [4]. Shakespeare and Watson add that many people with impairments refuse to define themselves by impairment or disability and seek a mainstream identity, which limits any politics that assumes disability is the key to everyone's identity [1].

### Institutions, Segregation, and Inclusion

The sociological concept of inclusion contrasts with segregation, and the legal and empirical literatures treat the contrast carefully. In Olmstead the Court identified two judgments behind treating unjustified institutional isolation as discrimination: institutional placement of persons who can handle and benefit from community settings perpetuates unwarranted assumptions that they are incapable or unworthy of participating in community life, and confinement in an institution severely diminishes everyday life activities, including family relations, social contacts, work options, economic independence, educational advancement, and cultural enrichment [15]. The Court also stated that nothing in the ADA condones terminating institutional settings for persons unable to handle or benefit from community settings, that community-based treatment is not required for those who do not desire it, and that the State's duty is limited by reasonable modification and the obligation to administer services with an even hand [15]. The CRPD states a broader right: Article 19 recognizes the equal right of all persons with disabilities to live in the community with choices equal to others, including the opportunity to choose where and with whom to live and access to in-home, residential, and community support services, including personal assistance [5]. Article 24 requires an inclusive education system at all levels and states that persons with disabilities are not to be excluded from the general education system on the basis of disability [5].

### Poverty, Extra Costs, and Income Support

Disability and poverty are intricately interlinked in Mont's phrase: poverty can cause disability through malnutrition, poor health services, and unsafe living and working conditions, and disability can trap people in poverty through barriers to education, employment, and social participation [7]. Two measurement problems complicate the link. The first is cost. Mitra and colleagues, reviewing 20 studies of the direct costs of living with a disability, find that extra costs are sizeable and vary with severity, life cycle, and household composition, and that low observed spending may signal unmet needs rather than low need [12]. The second is directionality. Banks and colleagues found that 83 percent of included studies were cross-sectional; the thirteen cohort studies and one pre-post study examined how poverty affected the risk of developing a disability, and no longitudinal study examined whether disability could lead to poverty, so the review cannot settle the direction of the association [11]. The UPIAS and Alliance dispute over whether poverty is a symptom or a cause thus remains partly an empirical question [2][11].

### Heterogeneity and Intersection

Disability is not a single experience. The WHO notes that persons with disabilities are a diverse group and that factors such as sex, age, gender identity, sexual orientation, religion, race, ethnicity, and economic situation affect their experiences [6]. The CRPD preamble expresses concern about multiple or aggravated discrimination on the basis of race, colour, sex, language, religion, and other statuses, notes that women and girls with disabilities are often at greater risk of violence, injury, or abuse, and Article 6 recognizes that women and girls with disabilities are subject to multiple discrimination [5]. The BLS data show differences within the disability population: in 2025 the unemployment rate for people with a disability was 11.8 percent for Black or African American workers, 9.5 percent for Hispanic or Latino workers, 7.9 percent for Asian workers, and 7.3 percent for White workers [9]. Shakespeare and Watson caution that theoretical responses must be situated in specific contexts, such as disabled children in education or black and minority ethnic disabled people, and that it is tempting to generalize [1].

## Evidence

### Population Surveys: Income, Employment, and Public Assistance

Brault's report for the Census Bureau analyzes the 2008 panel of the Survey of Income and Program Participation, with interviews conducted from May through August 2010 and estimates representative of the civilian noninstitutionalized population [8]. About 56.7 million people (18.7 percent of 303.9 million) had a disability, 38.3 million (12.6 percent) had a severe disability, and 12.3 million people aged 6 and older (4.4 percent) needed assistance with activities of daily living or instrumental activities of daily living [8]. Among adults aged 21 to 64, 41.1 percent of those with a disability were employed in the interview month compared with 79.1 percent of those without [8]. Median monthly earnings were $1,961 for adults with any disability and $2,724 for adults without; median monthly family income was $2,856 for people with disabilities, about 60 percent of the $4,771 median for people without disabilities [8]. Among people aged 15 to 64, 28.6 percent of those with severe disabilities were in poverty, compared with 17.9 percent with nonsevere disabilities and 14.3 percent with no disability; over a 24-month period 10.8 percent of those with severe disabilities experienced persistent poverty, against 4.9 and 3.8 percent respectively [8]. More than half of adults aged 15 to 64 with severe disabilities (59.0 percent) received some form of public assistance, and 28.1 percent received SNAP benefits compared with 8.3 percent of adults with no disability [8].

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports the same pattern from the Current Population Survey for 2025. The employment-population ratio was 22.8 percent for people with a disability and 65.2 percent for people without; for ages 16 to 64 the ratio was 38.1 percent for people with a disability against 74.8 percent for people without, and for ages 65 and over it was 7.8 percent against 22.8 percent [9]. The unemployment rate was 8.3 percent for people with a disability and 4.1 percent for people without, and among people with a bachelor's degree or higher it was 5.3 percent against 2.5 percent [9]. About 75 percent of people with a disability were outside the labor force compared with about 32 percent of people without, and of those outside the labor force about 3 percent of people with a disability wanted a job, compared with nearly 7 percent of people without [9]. Workers with a disability were more likely to work part time (about 30 percent against about 17 percent) and to be self-employed (9.1 percent against 5.9 percent), and less likely to hold management and professional occupations (37.5 percent against 43.9 percent) [9]. The BLS cautions that 2025 annual estimates average 11 months because the October 2025 survey was not collected during the federal government shutdown [9]. At the global level, the WHO reports that some persons with disabilities die up to 20 years earlier than those without, have twice the risk of conditions such as depression, asthma, diabetes, stroke, obesity, or poor oral health, and find inaccessible and unaffordable transportation 15 times more difficult [6].

### A Field Experiment on Hiring

Survey gaps cannot by themselves separate supply-side from demand-side causes. Ameri and colleagues addressed this with a field experiment in the style of Bertrand and Mullainathan's audit study. They submitted applications to 6,016 advertised US accounting positions in 2013 from well-qualified fictional applicants; one-third of cover letters disclosed a spinal cord injury, one-third disclosed Asperger's syndrome, and one-third mentioned no disability, and half of the resumes portrayed a novice and half an experienced accountant [10]. The disabilities were chosen because they would not be expected to limit productivity in accounting [10]. Applicants with disabilities received expressions of employer interest from 4.87 percent of employers compared with 6.58 percent for applicants without disabilities, a gap of 1.71 percentage points or 26 percent; the gap for the stricter measure of an interview callback was 0.28 points (11 percent) and was not statistically significant [10]. The gap was three times larger for experienced applicants (2.57 points, 34 percent) than for novices (0.86 points), and was concentrated among small, closely held employers that were not federal contractors, including employers with fewer than 15 employees, who are not covered by the ADA [10]. Comparisons above and below legal coverage thresholds pointed to a possible positive effect of the ADA but no clear effect of state laws [10]. The authors state that their method likely gives a lower bound on the true gap, because cover letters may not be read, and that they cannot clearly ascribe the gap to discrimination [10].

### Labels and Expectations

Shifrer tests whether the learning disability label itself carries stigma, using the Education Longitudinal Survey of 2002. Her analytic sample comprises about 11,740 adolescents, of whom about 690 (6 percent) held a school learning disability designation; students in 202 schools that reported no information on such designations were excluded, so the sample is not nationally representative [13]. Net of academic background, achievement, attitudes, and behavior, teachers' odds of perceiving a labeled student as disabled were 18.5 times, and parents' 10.6 times, those for unlabeled students, and the odds that a teacher expected a labeled student to complete a bachelor's degree rather than no college were 82 percent lower, and a parent's odds 48 percent lower [13]. In her mediation analysis, 32.1 percent of the label's negative effect on teachers' expectations and 84.9 percent of its negative effect on parents' expectations ran through the perception that the adolescent was disabled, and the label's negative effect on adolescents' own expectations ran partly through teachers' (20.7 percent) and parents' (8.4 percent) expectations [13]. Shifrer concludes that special education services may engender stigma while simultaneously improving students' opportunities to learn [13]. The study is observational and cannot rule out unmeasured differences between labeled and unlabeled students [13].

### Poverty and the Cost of Disability

Banks, Kuper, and Polack screened 15,500 records and included 150 studies from low- and middle-income countries published between 1990 and March 2016. Almost half (70, or 47 percent) were conducted in China, India, or Brazil and most (124, or 83 percent) were cross-sectional [11]. A total of 122 studies (81 percent) found a positive association between disability and a poverty marker, 23 (16 percent) found no significant association, 3 (2 percent) found a negative association, and 2 had mixed findings; the share of positive findings was 69 percent for older adults and 86 percent for working-age adults, and 61 of 89 studies (69 percent) that disaggregated by severity or poverty level found a dose-response pattern [11]. The authors note that widely cited statements that disabled people are twice as likely to live in poverty are, upon tracing, often based on decidedly weak evidence [11].

Mitra and colleagues' systematized review searched three databases (11,316 articles screened) and found 20 studies from 10 countries, mostly high-income. Extra costs as a share of average income ranged from 12 percent in Vietnam to 40 percent for elderly households in Ireland; in the United Kingdom they were 11 percent of income for mild, 34 percent for moderate, and 64 percent for severe disability, and in the United States out-of-pocket health costs were 65 percent higher for working-age adults with disabilities, all else equal [12]. In the United Kingdom and China, public transfers fell significantly short of estimated disability costs [12]. A New Zealand study found actual transportation spending of USD 577 against USD 1,822 required, illustrating unmet need [12].

### Community Living and Quality of Life

McCarron and colleagues' systematic review of deinstitutionalisation searched seven databases to September 2017 and included 13 studies (eight quantitative, two qualitative, two mixed-methods, and one case study), all from high-income countries, with sample sizes of 1 to 76 [14]. In a meta-analysis of five studies (246 participants) quality of life increased up to one year after moving from an institution to a community setting (standardised mean difference 2.03; 95 percent confidence interval 1.21 to 2.85), and in three studies (160 participants) beyond one year (2.34; 0.49 to 4.20), with moderate certainty of evidence [14]. In one study that compared matched groups, people moving to dispersed community homes scored higher than those moving to cluster centres at 12 months (mean difference 26.9) and 24 months (39.2) [14]. Qualitative studies reported a sense of freedom and privacy, but also that improvement is not inevitable: some residents did not feel integrated into the local community, some appeared more isolated than before, and family contact showed a downward trend [14].

### Built Environments: Housing and the Web

The Department of Housing and Urban Development's analysis of the 2019 American Housing Survey estimated that 19 percent of US households (23.1 million of 124.1 million) included someone with a mobility-related disability, a mobility-assistive device, or difficulty entering or using parts of the home [16]. Forty-seven percent of homes required climbing stairs to enter; among households with accessibility needs, 40 percent (9.2 million) required climbing stairs to enter and had no ramp or lift, and only 14 percent had a ramp or lift [16]. About 58 percent of households with accessibility needs had both a bedroom and a full bathroom on the entry level [16]. Households with accessibility needs were more common among those with incomes under $20,000 (about one-third) and among households with only grade-school education (31 percent) than among households with graduate degrees (13 percent) [16]. Only 5 percent of all owner households planned accessibility improvements in the next two years [16].

On the web, the 2026 WebAIM Million analysis ran the WAVE evaluation tool on the home pages of the top one million sites in February 2026, detecting 56.1 errors per page on average (up 10.1 percent from 2025) and WCAG 2 failures on 95.9 percent of pages [17]. Six categories accounted for 96 percent of errors: low-contrast text (83.9 percent of pages), missing image alternative text (53.1 percent), missing form labels (51 percent), empty links (46.3 percent), empty buttons (30.6 percent), and missing document language (13.5 percent) [17]. Government home pages averaged 42.4 errors, below the 56.1 average [17]. WebAIM cautions that automated tools detect only a subset of failures, so the absence of detected errors does not show that a page is accessible [17].

## Implications

### For Researchers and Statistical Agencies

Choose the disability measure to fit the question. Mont's analysis implies that a participation-based item such as "limited in the kind or amount of work" is appropriate for administrative eligibility but unsuitable for assessing inclusion, because it changes as environments change; basic-activity items allow inequality in outcomes to be read as a measure of social inclusiveness [7]. Report severity bands and age-specific rates, because the same population contains very different circumstances: for example, the poverty rate among people aged 15 to 64 was 28.6 percent with severe disabilities and 17.9 percent with nonsevere disabilities [8], and Mont recommends reporting at least two prevalence rates for this reason [7]. Name the excluded population: household surveys omit people in institutions, who may have high disability rates [8]. The author's assessment is that these cautions apply with particular force to cross-national comparisons, where Mont's Zambia and Brazil examples show that the wording of the question can move measured prevalence by an order of magnitude [7]. For the broader discipline, see the census and survey measurement topic in this library.

### For Employers and Labor-Market Institutions

The hiring experiment implies that qualifications alone do not erase the gap: the gap in employer interest was largest for experienced, well-credentialed applicants and was concentrated among small, closely held firms outside ADA coverage [10]. Comparing groups on both sides of a legal threshold suggested that statutory coverage may matter, but the authors could not detect clear effects of state laws, and they considered the possibility that awareness of the laws is limited [10]. The authors conclude that more attention needs to be paid to employer behavior and to the demand side of the labor market for people with disabilities [10]. The author's assessment is that organizations seeking inclusion should audit their own screening and response practices for disability-related gaps instead of assuming that supply-side explanations account for employment differences. The Norwegian tick-box example shows that disclosure mechanisms can backfire when they require self-categorization without protection against later discrimination [4]. See the sociology of work topic for labor-market segmentation.

### For Schools and Educators

Labels open access to services but also trigger perception and expectation effects. Shifrer's mediation results indicate that a large share of the label's association with lower expectations runs through the perception that the student is disabled, particularly for parents [13]. The author's assessment is that schools should treat the decision to label as consequential and should monitor expectations separately from services, for example by tracking whether labeled students are routed to and encouraged toward postsecondary pathways. For the instructional side of this issue, see the learning disabilities topic. The CRPD standard of inclusive education places the burden on the system rather than the child: students are not to be excluded from the general system on the basis of disability, and reasonable accommodation of individual requirements is to be provided [5].

### For Housing, Design, and Digital Product Teams

The HUD data imply that accessibility is a stock problem: homes last for decades, and in 2019 households with accessibility needs largely lived in homes without features such as ramps or an entry-level bedroom and bathroom, while few owners planned to add them [16]. Universal design, in the CRPD sense of usable by all people to the greatest extent possible without adaptation, is cheaper to apply at construction than to retrofit; this is the author's inference and is not directly tested in the sources. Lid's examples add that accessibility features should be evaluated in context rather than by checklist, since a measure that helps in one setting, such as a kerb cut, can fail in another, such as a shared-space street [4]. For web teams, the WebAIM data show that 96 percent of detected errors fall into six categories that have been the same for seven years, and that higher page complexity and more ARIA code correlated with more detected errors; WebAIM concludes that improving accessibility at scale will require both better practices and simpler systems [17].

### For Policymakers and Service Systems

Olmstead and the CRPD frame community participation as a right, while the McCarron review shows that it is not self-executing: quality-of-life gains followed moves to community settings, but integration into the community was mixed and depended on staff support, housemate compatibility, and family contact [14][15]. The legal analysis in Olmstead also limits the demand: it recognizes that some individuals may need institutional care at times, that a state may rely on the assessments of its professionals, and that a comprehensive and effectively working plan with a reasonably moving waiting list can satisfy the reasonable-modification standard [15]. On income support, the review of extra costs found that transfers fell short of estimated disability costs in the United Kingdom and China, and Banks and colleagues note that poverty measures that ignore extra costs may underestimate poverty among people with disabilities [11][12]. The author's assessment is that the UPIAS and Alliance dispute need not be an either/or: income support addresses extra costs, while barrier removal addresses exclusion, and the evidence reviewed here supports both levers [2][10][12][14].

### For Demographers and Planners of Aging Societies

Because disability prevalence rises steeply with age, population aging raises the number of people who meet environments built for a narrower range of bodies. The age gradient in the Census data runs from 8.4 percent of children under 15 to 70.5 percent of people aged 80 and older [8], and the WHO attributes the growth in global disability partly to longer lives [6]. HUD found that 35 percent of US households that included someone aged 65 or older also included a person with accessibility needs, against 11 percent of households without such a person [16]. The author's assessment is that long-term housing, transport, and service planning should therefore treat accessibility as a core demographic input rather than a niche requirement; see the population aging topic for the demographic side.

### For Readers Evaluating Claims

Check the denominator and the definition. Statements that a given share of a population is disabled, that disabled people are "twice as likely" to be poor, or that employment has risen or fallen may rest on different instruments, age mixes, or institutional exclusions [7][8][9][11]. Distinguish description from mechanism: the survey gaps in employment and income are large, but separating discrimination from differences in education, health, extra costs, or benefit incentives requires designs like the audit study [10]. Finally, distinguish inclusion from placement: the same sources that document benefits from community living also document loneliness and thin social networks in community settings, so inclusion needs support, not only relocation [14].

## Criticisms and Limits of the Evidence

Several limits qualify the picture above. The social model has been criticized as an inadequate theory of the body and identity, and as an outcome concept that cannot test whether impairment leads to disadvantage [1][3]. The survey evidence is largely descriptive and from the United States; the BLS figures partly reflect the older age profile of people with disabilities [9]. The Banks review found that most studies were cross-sectional and that no longitudinal study examined whether disability leads to poverty, and its sample was concentrated in China, India, and Brazil [11]. The Mitra review found that most extra-cost studies came from high-income countries and that no study used participatory methods [12]. The hiring experiment covers two disabilities and one occupation and is restricted to well-qualified male applicants, and its cover-letter disclosure design may understate employer behavior; the version read here is an NBER working paper that NBER states has not been peer reviewed, and revised published versions were not read [10]. Shifrer's analysis excluded students in schools that did not report disability designations and cannot establish causation [13]. The McCarron review included only observational studies from high-income countries and could not apply a fixed definition of "institution" or "community" [14]. The WebAIM data are from automated testing of home pages only and describe the top one million sites, not all sites [17]. The Census and BLS figures come from different surveys with different definitions and should not be pooled [8][9].
