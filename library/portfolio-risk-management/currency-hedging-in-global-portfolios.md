# Currency Hedging in Global Portfolios -- The Right Hedge Preserves the Asset Diversification an Investor Actually Wants

A global asset position also creates a currency position whenever the asset and the investor's reporting currency differ, so an unhedged portfolio combines the intended asset exposure with a second return source [1][2]. Currency hedging can remove much of that second source, but the risk-minimizing ratio is usually closer to full hedging for bonds than for equities and still depends on base currency, liabilities, covariance, horizon, costs, and implementation [1][2][3]. The central portfolio decision is therefore not whether currencies are good or bad investments; it is how much incidental exchange-rate risk to retain without turning risk control into a directional foreign-exchange strategy.

## Background

International diversification separates the location or denomination of an investment from the currency in which an investor measures wealth and pays obligations. A U.S. investor buying a euro-denominated bond receives the bond's local-currency return and the dollar return on the euro. A euro-area pension fund buying a dollar bond receives the bond return and the euro return on the dollar, while its benefits may still be owed in euros. The home-currency result is therefore generated by both the asset and the exchange rate, even when the security analyst formed no view about currencies [1][2]. Currency hedging developed as the portfolio-level process for controlling that incidental exposure.

For a one-period investment, let `r_L` be the local-currency asset return and let `c` be the home-currency return on one unit of the foreign currency. The exact gross home-currency return is `(1 + r_L)(1 + c) - 1`; for modest changes it is approximately `r_L + c`. The interaction term matters during large moves, and the quotation convention determines the sign of `c` [1][2]. A hedge adds a position that gains when the foreign currency loses value against the home currency, usually by selling the foreign currency forward or establishing an economically equivalent position. The investment then contains two deliberately separable decisions: which assets to own and which currencies to retain.

The academic literature moved from simple full-hedge rules toward portfolio-specific hedge ratios. Early international portfolio models showed that foreign currency can be treated as another return source whose covariance with the asset portfolio affects total variance. Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros, and Viceira formalized this risk-management demand for seven developed-market currencies and distinguished it from speculative currency demand based on expected returns [1]. Schmittmann examined German, Japanese, British, and U.S. investors over 1975-2009 and compared no hedge, half hedge, full hedge, and estimated minimum-variance ratios over several horizons [2]. Boudoukh, Katz, Richardson, and Thapar later separated a minimum-variance hedge from an alpha-seeking currency portfolio, making explicit that reducing asset risk and forecasting currency returns are different activities [3].

This literature repeatedly finds an asset-class asymmetry. Currency volatility can be large relative to the ordinary volatility of high-quality bonds, so an unhedged bond allocation may behave more like a currency position than the fixed-income exposure the investor selected. For global equities, local stock volatility is larger and some currencies have historically moved against equity markets, so retaining selected currency exposure has sometimes reduced rather than increased portfolio variance [1][2]. These are empirical covariance results, not permanent laws. A currency that acted as a safe haven in one sample, for one base currency and one equity portfolio, need not do so in another regime.

Institutional practice reflects both this asymmetry and operational constraints. Research using mutual-fund positions finds extensive use of short-dated currency forwards by international fixed-income funds, while custodian and regulatory data show that fixed-income investors generally hedge more than equity investors [4][5][6]. Actual ratios remain heterogeneous across investors, countries, and currencies [5][6]. Different liabilities, benchmarks, regulations, tax rules, cost tolerances, and beliefs can justify different choices even when two institutions own similar foreign assets.

The market architecture also changed the meaning of a hedge. An outright forward locks a future exchange rate. A currency future standardizes a similar commitment on an exchange. An FX swap combines a spot exchange with a reversing forward exchange and is economically close to collateralized cross-currency borrowing. A longer-dated currency swap can exchange principal and interest streams, while an option buys asymmetric protection for an upfront premium [7][9][12]. Each instrument can reduce exchange-rate exposure, but each creates a different path of margin, collateral, basis, liquidity, settlement, and roll obligations.

After the global financial crisis, covered interest parity ceased to be a costless empirical identity for major currencies. Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan documented persistent cross-currency bases linked to intermediary balance-sheet costs and imbalances in funding and investment demand [8]. Later research shows that the economic return on a hedged foreign bond depends not only on the bond yield but also on the short-term interest-rate differential and cross-currency basis embedded in the forward or swap [5][7]. Calling all forward points a fee is therefore misleading: much of the difference between spot and forward rates reflects the two currencies' money-market rates, while the basis and trading spread reflect additional market and balance-sheet conditions [7][8].

The most consequential implementation problem is maturity mismatch. Long-term foreign bonds and equities are often hedged with one- or three-month forwards and FX swaps because the short end is liquid. The hedge must then be renewed repeatedly. BIS evidence from Asian institutional investors shows that this practice converts part of exchange-rate risk into rollover and dollar-funding risk; disruptions in March 2020 made that dependence visible [10]. A terminal hedge can be economically correct yet operationally fail if the investor cannot post collateral, settle principal, or replace the contract during market stress.

Synthesis: This topic therefore treats currency hedging as portfolio risk management. It covers return decomposition, hedge ratios, full and partial policies, dynamic adjustment, instruments, carry, basis, collateral, roll risk, and diversification. It does not attempt to forecast individual currencies or recommend speculative carry, value, momentum, or macro trades. Those positions may be legitimate investments, but they should be budgeted and evaluated separately from the hedge that preserves the intended global asset allocation.

## Core Concepts

### Currency exposure is an additional portfolio weight

For a foreign asset measured in the investor's home currency, the unhedged return can be approximated as follows [1][2]:

```text
home-currency return = local-asset return + currency return
                       + interaction term
```

The approximation is useful for risk decomposition, but the exact multiplicative relationship should be used for large moves and formal performance calculation. The sign of the currency return must follow one fixed quote. In this topic, a positive `c` means that the foreign currency appreciates against the investor's home currency, increasing the home-currency value of the foreign asset [1][2].

Let `h` be the share of the foreign-currency exposure sold forward against the home currency. Ignoring carry for the moment, the retained currency exposure is `1 - h` and the hedged return is approximately [1][2]:

```text
r_H(h) = r_L + (1 - h)c
```

The common policy labels follow directly:

- `h = 0`: unhedged; the investor retains the full currency exposure.
- `h = 0.5`: half hedged; half the foreign-currency exposure remains.
- `h = 1`: fully hedged; the first-order currency exposure is removed.
- `h > 1`: overhedged; the investor is net short the foreign currency.
- `h < 0`: the investor adds foreign-currency exposure beyond the asset holding.

A ratio is always relative to a defined exposure base. It may be calculated separately by currency, as an aggregate ratio across all foreign assets, or relative to a liability benchmark. A 100 percent portfolio-level ratio can still leave cross-currency mismatches if the currencies netted in the numerator differ from those in the assets. Synthesis: governance should report both the aggregate ratio and the currency-by-currency amounts, because one headline percentage can conceal offsetting long and short positions [4][6].

### The minimum-variance ratio depends on covariance

Using the approximation above, the variance of the hedged return is [1][2][3]:

```text
Var[r_H(h)] = Var[r_L]
              + (1 - h)^2 Var[c]
              + 2(1 - h)Cov[r_L,c]
```

Differentiating with respect to `h` gives the unconstrained minimum-variance hedge ratio [1][2]:

```text
h* = 1 + Cov[r_L,c] / Var[c]
```

The formula explains why full hedging is a benchmark rather than a theorem. If local asset returns and currency returns are uncorrelated, `Cov[r_L,c] = 0` and `h* = 1`. If the foreign currency tends to appreciate when the local asset falls, the covariance is negative and the risk-minimizing investor retains some currency exposure, so `h* < 1`. If the currency tends to fall when the asset falls, the covariance is positive and the estimated minimum-variance ratio can exceed 100 percent [1][2]. In a multi-country portfolio, covariances among assets and among currencies make the solution a matrix problem rather than a set of isolated bilateral ratios [1][3].

This is a risk result, not an expected-return result. A currency can lower equity variance because of negative covariance even if its average return is low. Conversely, a high-yield currency can increase portfolio risk despite attractive historical carry. Campbell and coauthors separate risk-minimizing currency demand from speculative demand for this reason [1]. Boudoukh and coauthors make the same separation operational by constructing a hedge portfolio first and adding an alpha-seeking currency portfolio only as a separately sized component [3].

Estimated optimal ratios are uncertain. Currency volatility, asset-currency covariance, and cross-currency covariance change across samples and regimes. The optimization can also produce large long or short positions from small estimation differences [2][3]. Synthesis: a ratio should be treated as an estimate with error bands and constraints, not as a precise property of an asset class. A robust policy can shrink an estimated ratio toward a strategic benchmark, cap overhedging, require a minimum improvement in stressed loss, and compare the optimized result with simple 0, 50, and 100 percent alternatives.

### The base currency and liability benchmark define the risk

A hedge has no meaning without a reporting or liability currency. The same dollar asset is domestic to a U.S. investor, foreign to a euro investor, and potentially a partial liability hedge to an institution that owes dollars despite reporting in another currency. Schmittmann's multi-country results show that conclusions cannot simply be transferred from one base currency to another [2]. Campbell and coauthors find that risk-minimizing currency demands for a given global asset portfolio have structural commonalities, but each resident still treats the home currency differently [1].

Synthesis: The relevant benchmark may also differ from the accounting currency. A pension fund reports in the sponsor's currency but should evaluate hedges against the currencies and sensitivities of promised benefits. A sovereign wealth fund may have no single near-term liability currency and may deliberately retain foreign-currency diversification. A household may spend in one currency yet hold a foreign home, education obligation, or business income that changes the economic exposure. The liability-driven-investing principle applies: risk is the mismatch between assets and what capital must fund, not the volatility of assets viewed alone.

Horizon changes the question but does not remove it. Long horizons allow purchasing-power adjustment and mean reversion to matter, yet they also expose the investor to repeated forward rolls and changing interest-rate differentials. Schmittmann found that substantial risk reduction from hedging remained over horizons up to five years in the 1975-2009 sample, especially for bonds [2]. Campbell and coauthors did not find that the case for leaving currency unhedged necessarily strengthened with horizon in their post-1975 data [1]. These findings reject a universal rule that long-term investors can ignore currencies, while still leaving room for liability-specific long-run exposures.

### Full, partial, and dynamic hedges solve different governance problems

A full hedge sets `h = 1` and removes first-order currency exposure. It is transparent, easy to benchmark, and often close to minimum variance for high-quality international bonds [1][2]. It does not remove basis, transaction, counterparty, collateral, or roll risk. Nor does it hold exactly between reset dates: asset prices, accrued income, subscriptions, redemptions, and currency moves change the amount being hedged [4][6]. A policy must specify whether it hedges beginning-period market value, current value, principal only, or principal plus expected income.

A partial hedge fixes a strategic ratio such as 50 percent. It accepts some exchange-rate variance while reducing turnover, estimation dependence, and the risk of repeatedly trading a full notional during stressed markets. A partial ratio may also preserve exposure to a currency that diversifies equity or liabilities. Its weakness is that a round number is not evidence of optimality. Synthesis: a partial policy is defensible when its purpose is explicit -- for example, balancing volatility reduction against liquidity and model risk -- rather than when 50 percent is treated as a neutral fact.

A dynamic hedge changes the ratio in response to estimated covariance, volatility, asset value, carry, valuation, momentum, risk limits, or liability changes. Dynamic minimum-variance models can reduce volatility beyond a static rule in some samples [3]. Actual fund data also show time-varying ratios, particularly among equity managers [4][6]. But dynamic rules trade more, depend on estimates, and can quietly become currency strategies. A rule that hedges more because a currency is expected to fall is making an expected-return decision; a rule that adjusts notional because the foreign asset rose is maintaining a risk target.

The author's synthesis is to separate three layers. The strategic hedge is the long-run ratio justified by asset and liability risk. The maintenance rule restores that ratio when market values drift outside a band. The tactical currency budget permits deliberate deviations under separate limits, attribution, and review. This separation prevents a manager from labeling an unsuccessful FX view as a hedge and prevents risk control from being judged against a speculative return target.

### Instruments change the cash-flow path of the same objective

| Instrument | Basic economic use | Main strengths | Main residual risks |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Outright FX forward | Exchange two currencies at a future date and rate | Customized currency, amount, and maturity; no spot principal exchange | Bilateral counterparty exposure, collateral, settlement, spread, basis, and repeated roll |
| Currency future | Standardized exchange-traded currency commitment | Central clearing, transparent price, transferable position, daily mark-to-market | Contract-size and date mismatch, daily margin liquidity, exchange basis, and roll |
| FX swap | Exchange currencies now and reverse the exchange later | Simultaneously funds and hedges a foreign asset; deep short-term institutional market | Full-principal settlement, off-balance-sheet obligation, counterparty exposure, short-term rollover, and funding squeeze |
| Currency swap | Exchange principal and interest streams across currencies | Better maturity matching for long liabilities or funding programs | Bespoke legal terms, counterparty and collateral exposure, valuation complexity, and lower exit liquidity |
| FX option or option spread | Buy asymmetric protection against an adverse rate while preserving some favorable movement | Bounded premium for the buyer; can place a floor on home-currency value | Premium and volatility-skew cost, strike and maturity mismatch, path risk, counterparty or clearing demands |

The table is a synthesis of official derivative definitions, BIS market analysis, and settlement guidance [7][9][10][12][13]. An outright forward is usually the simplest overlay when the investor already owns the foreign asset and only needs a future offset. A future can serve the same direction of exposure but standardization creates residual mismatch in notional and date. Because futures are marked to market, they convert changes in hedge value into current cash flows rather than leaving all settlement to maturity [13]. A position that is economically protective can therefore create a margin call before the foreign asset is sold.

An FX swap differs from an outright forward because it includes a spot leg as well as a forward reversal. BIS describes it as effectively collateralized cross-currency borrowing: one currency is lent and another borrowed, with the future reversal fixed at inception [7][9]. Once the spot exchange is complete, the remaining obligation resembles a forward. This structure is useful when an investor must both obtain foreign currency to buy an asset and remove the exchange-rate mismatch. It also creates a full-notional repayment obligation that can be invisible in ordinary balance-sheet debt measures [9][11].

Longer-dated currency swaps exchange interest streams and often principal, making them suitable for foreign debt or stable long-duration cash flows [10][11]. They can reduce the repeated renewal of short hedges but are more bespoke and may be harder to resize as portfolio values change. Options differ because the buyer pays for a right rather than a symmetric obligation. BIS evidence from 2022-2025 shows that some investors increased option use when wide interest-rate differentials made forward hedging expensive; collars can reduce premium by surrendering some favorable currency movement [15]. This changes the payoff rather than making the protection free.

### Carry, forward points, and basis are part of the hedged return

Under covered interest parity, the forward rate reflects the difference between comparable short-term interest rates in the two currencies. For a non-U.S. investor hedging a dollar asset, the forward discount or premium therefore embeds the dollar rate relative to the home rate. A cross-currency basis adds a deviation associated with funding imbalances and intermediary balance-sheet capacity [7][8]. In simplified form [7]:

```text
forward minus spot
    = interest-rate differential + cross-currency basis
```

The exact sign depends on the quote convention. Governance should avoid the ambiguous statement that hedging "costs" the interest-rate differential. If the investor gives up the higher short-term rate in one currency and receives the lower rate in another, the forward points reduce the hedged return relative to the unhedged spot position. The reverse configuration can produce a hedge benefit. Neither outcome is an implementation fee; it is part of the relative financed return. Bid-ask spread, commissions, market impact, custody, collateral funding, and operational expense are separate implementation costs [5][7][8].

Du and Huber estimate that persistent post-crisis bases imposed measurable additional costs on sampled foreign pensions and insurers hedging dollar assets, while emphasizing that investors continuously rolled short-term hedges [5]. Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan show that the basis widens when supplying FX hedges consumes scarce intermediary balance sheet [8]. Synthesis: expected hedged return should be decomposed into local asset return, home cash rate, forward carry, basis, trading cost, and collateral funding. Combining them into one unexplained "hedge drag" makes policy sensitive to accounting labels rather than economics.

### Rebalancing and roll rules determine realized exposure

A hedge established against today's foreign asset value drifts when the asset or exchange rate changes. If a foreign equity position rises from 100 to 120 while the short forward remains 100, a nominally full hedge becomes an 83.3 percent hedge before any other change. Subscriptions, redemptions, dividends, coupons, and security trades create the same mismatch [4][6]. A daily rebalance tracks exposure closely but raises turnover, spread, collateral, and market-impact costs. A monthly or threshold rule tolerates temporary under- or overhedging in exchange for lower friction.

Short contracts also expire. A roll closes or settles one hedge and establishes another for a later date. The new forward points, basis, spread, collateral terms, and dealer capacity may differ from the old contract [5][7][10]. Futures require an explicit move from the expiring contract to another listed maturity; the trader offsets the old contract and opens the new one [14]. Synthesis: the realized hedge is a sequence of contracts and cash flows, not one ratio selected at inception.

The worst implementation error is to measure only terminal offset. A forward or swap can match the final currency amount but generate collateral or full-principal needs before the asset can be liquidated. BIS and Basel guidance require attention to replacement cost, legally enforceable netting, margin, eligible collateral, settlement timing, and liquidity [10][12]. A hedge that depends on rolling short dollar funding during the same event that disrupts dollar markets has exchanged visible FX volatility for a less visible refinancing dependency.

### A hedge policy is not a currency forecast

Currency exposure can earn return, and managers may have informed views. The governance problem is classification. A risk-minimizing hedge is justified by covariance, liability alignment, or a stated tolerance for home-currency loss. A directional currency position is justified by expected return. A carry trade is justified by compensation for holding higher-yield currencies and bears crash and liquidity risk; it is not automatically the residual of an incomplete hedge [1][5].

A clean policy records the strategic hedge ratio before the currency view, calculates the residual exposure, and attributes every tactical deviation to a separate FX risk budget. Performance reports then show asset return, currency return, strategic hedge return, tactical FX return, transaction cost, basis, and collateral funding. Synthesis: this separation preserves accountability. It allows the hedge to be successful when it reduces the intended risk even if its standalone derivative return is negative, and it prevents a profitable currency bet from being mistaken for evidence that the original asset allocation was well hedged.

## Evidence

### Global bond portfolios generally support high hedge ratios

Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros, and Viceira study monthly data for developed equity, bond, currency, and short-rate markets from 1975 through 2005. Their mean-variance framework holds the global asset portfolio fixed and chooses currency positions to minimize total variance. They find that global bond returns are nearly uncorrelated with most currency returns, so the risk-minimizing policy is close to full hedging, with a modest residual U.S. dollar exposure in their sample [1]. They also find little evidence that a risk-minimizing bond investor should vary currency positions merely because interest differentials change [1].

Schmittmann extends the comparison through 2009 for German, Japanese, British, and U.S. investors and evaluates single-country and multi-country portfolios. Full hedging reduces bond risk more than half hedging in nearly all reported cases, and estimated bond hedge ratios cluster near one across base currencies and horizons [2]. His horizon tests retain a strong case for bond hedging out to five years rather than finding that long holding periods reliably diversify currency risk away [2]. The study also warns that optimized ratios can be unstable, supporting the practical use of simple strategic benchmarks [2].

Levich and Thomas analyze U.S.-dollar returns on international ten-year government bonds over 1977-1990. In their sample, the fully hedged global bond portfolio had a higher Sharpe ratio than the unhedged version, and active currency rules improved the reported ratios further before full allowance for data-mining and implementation concerns [16]. Their transaction-cost estimates show that repeated futures trades and monthly hedge rolls consumed a material portion of active-rule profits [16]. This older sample is evidence that currency management can change bond outcomes; it is not evidence that historical trading rules will repeat.

Fund holdings provide a separate test of practice. Sialm and Zhu combine U.S. international fixed-income mutual-fund portfolios with currency-forward positions and report that around 90 percent of the sampled funds used currency forwards [4]. They find that managers use forwards both to reduce incidental exposure and to make active currency decisions, and that the short maturity of forward markets requires frequent renewal [4]. This distinction supports the governance separation between strategic hedging and tactical FX positions.

### Equity hedging is beneficial on average but less uniform

Campbell and coauthors find that the risk-minimizing currency portfolio for global equities differs from the bond solution because some currencies moved against world equity markets in their 1975-2005 sample. The U.S. dollar, euro, and Swiss franc supplied diversification in parts of the sample, while several other currencies were best hedged or overhedged [1]. Their result is covariance-based: the currency can reduce equity risk even when its average return is not high. It does not imply that those currencies will remain permanent safe havens.

Schmittmann likewise reports larger cross-country dispersion in equity hedge ratios than in bond ratios. Some estimated equity ratios are below zero or above one, depending on investor base and foreign market, while full hedging still reduces risk in most cases [2]. The variation confirms that a single global rule for equities can be convenient without being minimum variance for every investor. It also demonstrates the estimation problem: large optimal positions can emerge from historical covariance that may not persist.

Boudoukh and coauthors evaluate international equities over 1981-2017. Full hedging reduced average volatility from 16.5 percent to 14.9 percent across the G10 investor cases they report, while their robust dynamic minimum-variance basket reduced it further to 14.3 percent [3]. Their larger Sharpe-ratio improvement came only after adding a separately modeled alpha-seeking currency portfolio [3]. The decomposition is important: the incremental return was not a free by-product of hedging but compensation from additional currency risk under model assumptions.

Current custodian evidence shows that managers do not converge on the theoretical ratios. Cheema-Fox and Greenwood study more than 25 years of matched holdings and FX-forward positions from a global custodian. In their U.S.-dollar sample, 27 percent of equity investors and 51 percent of fixed-income investors hedged; among hedgers, the average ratios were 33 percent for equity and 60 percent for fixed income [6]. Their estimated minimum-variance ratios were materially higher, including averages near full hedging for fixed income [6]. The gap indicates that risk minimization is only one objective and that costs, mandates, delegated incentives, client preferences, and currency views affect actual behavior.

### Hedging practice is large, heterogeneous, and balance-sheet dependent

Du and Huber assemble filings for insurers, pension funds, mutual funds, and other institutions holding dollar assets. They estimate average dollar hedge ratios of 44 percent for insurance companies, 35 percent for pension funds, and 21 percent for mutual funds in the reported cross-sector sample, with more than $2 trillion of collective hedging demand by 2019 [5]. They also find suggestive evidence that bonds are hedged more than equities and substantial variation within sectors and currencies [5]. These facts reject both extremes: institutions do not leave all foreign assets unhedged, and they do not uniformly follow a full-hedge rule.

The same study links ratios to currency volatility, asset-currency covariance, interest differentials, and cross-currency basis [5]. A persistently negative basis made dollar hedging more expensive for many non-U.S. investors after the crisis. Their estimated additional basis cost for sampled pensions and insurers averaged about $2.7 billion per year over 2017-2020, around 0.1 percent of the relevant underlying dollar assets [5]. The percentage is small relative to some market moves but large enough to matter for high-grade bond portfolios with modest expected excess returns.

Nenova, Schrimpf, and Shin connect portfolio allocations with outstanding FX swaps and forwards across countries. Their model and evidence treat the hedged foreign bond return as the destination bond term premium plus the cross-currency basis after the interest differential is incorporated through the swap [7]. They find that relative bond opportunities, hedge cost, financial conditions, and exchange-rate valuation affect both foreign investment and the associated derivative position [7]. The evidence shows why currency policy cannot be chosen independently from the assets: a hedge changes the net return that makes the foreign bond attractive.

Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan document persistent violations of covered interest parity after the global financial crisis using unsecured rates, collateralized repo rates, and bonds issued by the same highly rated borrower in multiple currencies [8]. The basis co-moves with bank balance-sheet constraints and international funding imbalances, so it is a market price of intermediation rather than a guaranteed arbitrage available without capital [8]. For portfolio hedgers, the implication is direct: the cost of transferring FX risk can widen when dealer balance sheet is scarce, especially when many investors want the same side.

### Rollover, collateral, and hedging flows become risk factors

BIS annual-report evidence describes FX swaps as collateralized borrowing operations and reports $111 trillion of outstanding FX swaps, forwards, and currency swaps at end-2024, with more than three quarters of FX swap contracts maturing within one year [9]. Long-term asset owners that repeatedly use short contracts face a maturity mismatch between the investment and the hedge [9]. The notional obligation is off balance sheet under common accounting treatment, but it still has to be settled or renewed.

McGuire, Shim, Shin, and Sushko study outward portfolio investment from emerging Asia. They find that long-term dollar assets were predominantly hedged with short-term derivatives supplied by banks; in their markets, average forward and swap maturities were roughly one to three months [10]. The March 2020 dollar-funding disruption raised hedge costs and made renewal more difficult, demonstrating that an investor can reduce exchange-rate risk while becoming more exposed to dealer funding and rollover conditions [10]. Currency swaps with longer maturities can reduce this mismatch, but they are less liquid and more bespoke.

Borio, McCauley, and McGuire estimate that non-U.S. non-banks had approximately $26 trillion of off-balance-sheet dollar obligations through FX swaps, forwards, and currency swaps in 2022 [11]. They emphasize that pension funds and insurers often roll contracts monthly or quarterly against long-term assets [11]. The statistic measures gross forward payment obligations rather than an equal net loss exposure, but it reveals the full-notional liquidity that becomes relevant when contracts cannot be rolled or netted as expected.

Basel Committee guidance requires banks to manage settlement, replacement-cost, legal, and liquidity risk in physically settled FX forwards, swaps, options, and currency swaps. It recommends legally enforceable netting, explicit collateral policies, and frequent variation margin for covered financial counterparties [12]. This evidence does not mean every end investor faces identical margin terms. It establishes that the derivative system supporting the hedge requires collateral, valuation, legal enforceability, and operational access to liquidity.

### Costs can change both hedge ratios and market dynamics

BIS analysis of the April 2025 dollar decline reports that non-U.S. investors retained U.S. assets while increasing swap and forward overlays after an unusual joint fall in U.S. equities, bonds, and the dollar [17]. The authors interpret timing, basis movements, and trading-hour evidence as consistent with hedging flows contributing to dollar weakness, while distinguishing that explanation from wholesale liquidation of U.S. securities [17]. The episode shows that a hedge is not only a private portfolio decision when many large investors adjust the same exposure.

Huang, Krohn, and Sushko report that higher short-term U.S. rates widened forward hedging costs from 2022 onward and left some investors relatively underhedged entering April 2025 [15]. In their examples, annualized three-month forward premia rose from 0.7 percent to 3.5 percent for EUR/USD and from 0.3 percent to 5.5 percent for USD/JPY during 2022 [15]. They also document increased use of currency options as some participants sought alternatives to costly symmetric forward hedges [15]. These are period-specific market observations, not stable cost estimates.

The evidence as a whole supports four bounded conclusions. First, hedging generally reduces the volatility of international bonds and often reduces equity volatility [1][2][3]. Second, the appropriate equity ratio is more covariance- and base-currency-dependent than the bond ratio [1][2][3]. Third, actual investors use partial and dynamic policies because risk minimization competes with return objectives, costs, constraints, and views [4][5][6]. Fourth, the hedge itself creates basis, collateral, settlement, dealer, and rollover risks that must be budgeted alongside the exchange-rate exposure it removes [7][8][9][10][11][12].

## Implications

### Start from the portfolio objective, not from a forecast

Synthesis: The first practical implication is procedural. An investor should specify the reporting currency, liability currencies, foreign asset values, relevant benchmark, horizon, and loss measure before selecting a ratio. A global bond sleeve held to diversify duration should not accidentally become a dominant currency bet. A global equity sleeve may reasonably retain some currencies when their covariance lowers total risk. A liability-driven investor may hedge a currency even when the asset-only covariance suggests otherwise because the obligation, not the asset benchmark, defines loss.

The author's synthesis is that every policy should answer one sentence: "The portfolio hedges X percent of Y exposure into Z currency because doing so controls this stated risk." `X` is the ratio, `Y` is the exposure base, `Z` is the home or liability currency, and the final phrase identifies the objective. If any element is missing, the policy cannot be reproduced or audited.

### Use asset-class-specific defaults, then test exceptions

For developed-market, high-quality bonds, full or near-full hedging is the evidence-based default when the objective is to preserve fixed-income risk in the investor's home or liability currency [1][2]. The exception requires evidence: a foreign currency may hedge liabilities, diversify another portfolio component, or offer an intentionally budgeted return exposure. The burden should be on retaining the currency because its volatility can dominate the bond return.

For global equities, no equally universal ratio follows from the evidence. A full hedge often reduces volatility, but selected unhedged currency positions have also diversified equity risk in historical samples [1][2][3]. A reasonable governance process compares at least no hedge, half hedge, full hedge, and a constrained minimum-variance estimate. It evaluates volatility, drawdown, expected shortfall, turnover, carry, basis, and stressed collateral rather than selecting the ratio from average return alone.

For alternatives, private assets, and emerging-market exposures, reported price volatility may understate asset risk while currency markets may be less liquid or subject to capital controls. The author's synthesis is that the hedge ratio and instrument must be conditioned on valuation frequency, exit horizon, convertibility, derivative depth, and legal access. A mathematically attractive ratio that cannot be traded or collateralized is not an implementable risk control.

### Build the hedge in layers

Synthesis: A robust program can use five layers. First, create a currency map of assets, liabilities, income, commitments, and collateral. Second, choose strategic ratios by asset class and liability role. Third, select instruments and maturities that match the exposure closely enough without creating unacceptable liquidity dependence. Fourth, define maintenance bands, roll dates, eligible counterparties, collateral sources, and fallback actions. Fifth, place tactical currency positions in a separate risk and performance budget.

The currency map should be both gross and net. Netting euro assets against euro liabilities is economically meaningful when timing, legal entity, and convertibility align. Netting a liquid euro bond against a private euro capital call may be misleading if one cannot fund the other when due. Gross reporting also reveals settlement obligations and counterparty concentration that disappear in a net currency total [11][12].

Synthesis: Strategic ratios should be ranges rather than point estimates when model uncertainty is material. A committee might approve a target with a tolerance band and require review when estimated minimum variance leaves the band, when liabilities change, or when basis and liquidity costs become abnormal. This design is reversible: ordinary drift is corrected mechanically, while large policy changes require evidence and approval.

### Match the instrument to the exposure and the institution

Outright forwards fit investors that already own foreign assets and need a customized future currency offset. Their advantages are precision and operational familiarity; their risks are bilateral terms, settlement, and repeated roll. Futures fit liquid major-currency exposures when standard contract sizes and maturities are acceptable. Central clearing reduces bilateral credit dependence, but daily margin creates a direct cash requirement and the listed expiry can create basis and roll risk [13][14].

FX swaps fit portfolios that must obtain foreign currency funding and hedge the same amount. They are especially useful for bond investment, but their short maturities can turn a long-duration allocation into a recurring funding decision [7][9][10]. Currency swaps fit stable, longer-dated funding or liability cash flows and reduce frequent renewal, at the cost of bespoke valuation, legal, and exit complexity [10][11]. Options fit objectives that require a floor while retaining favorable currency participation. Their premium, strike, maturity, volatility surface, and possible collar obligations must be compared with the symmetric forward alternative [15].

The author's synthesis is that instrument selection should minimize the total mismatch across amount, currency, maturity, payoff, collateral, and legal entity. Choosing the cheapest quoted spread while ignoring those dimensions can make the hedge less reliable than a nominally more expensive contract.

### Treat liquidity as part of hedge effectiveness

A hedge is effective only if the investor can maintain it through the adverse path. The liquidity budget should include variation margin, initial-margin changes, full-principal settlement, option premium, counterparty thresholds, asset-sale timing, and the possibility that the new roll is priced at a wider basis. Collateral should be counted after haircuts, encumbrance, settlement delay, and competing uses [9][10][12].

Synthesis: Stress tests should apply at least four paths. The first is a sharp foreign-currency decline before the next rebalance, creating underhedging after the asset also falls. The second is a home-currency decline, which produces derivative losses and collateral calls while the foreign asset rises in home-currency terms. The third combines basis widening, dealer retreat, and a roll date. The fourth combines a currency move with asset redemptions or liability payments. The test should show mark-to-market loss, cash by date, remaining unencumbered collateral, hedge ratio after the shock, and required trade size.

Synthesis: The single worst outcome is a sound foreign asset sold to meet the liquidity demands of its hedge. Preventing that failure may justify a lower strategic ratio, longer maturity, broader counterparty set, greater collateral reserve, or a staged roll. This is not evidence against hedging. It is evidence that exchange-rate variance and hedge-funding risk must be optimized together.

### Rebalance enough to control risk, not enough to chase noise

Synthesis: The maintenance rule should specify the valuation time, exposure base, frequency, and threshold. A beginning-of-month full hedge is not continuously full. The portfolio can use calendar rebalancing, percentage bands, cash-flow netting, or a combination. Contributions and withdrawals should be used first when they can reduce the required derivative trade.

More frequent rebalancing lowers exposure drift but increases bid-ask spread, market impact, collateral movements, and operational load. Dynamic covariance models add another source of turnover and estimation error [2][3]. The author's synthesis is to separate notional maintenance from model-driven ratio changes. The first follows observable asset values; the second should cross a higher evidence threshold because it changes the risk policy itself.

Synthesis: Rolls should be staggered when concentration on one fixing or maturity creates market and operational risk. A ladder of maturities can reduce one-day refinancing dependence, although it complicates valuation and attribution. The committee should record the forward curve, basis, spread, collateral, and counterparty concentration at each roll. A persistent cost increase may justify policy review, but changing the hedge merely because the derivative recently lost money confuses the hedge payoff with the combined portfolio objective.

### Separate risk attribution from return attribution

Synthesis: Performance reporting should decompose at least six items: local asset return, currency return, strategic hedge payoff, tactical FX payoff, forward carry and basis, and explicit implementation cost. The combined result should then be compared with the declared benchmark. A negative derivative payoff can be the expected offset to a positive currency translation gain; evaluating the derivative alone reverses the economic conclusion.

Synthesis: Risk attribution should separately show retained currency exposure, asset-currency covariance, contribution to total variance, stressed loss, collateral requirement, and rollover concentration. A hedge can lower ordinary volatility while increasing liquidity risk. Another can have modest variance effect but materially reduce liability mismatch. One ratio cannot summarize both outcomes.

The author's synthesis is that a strategic hedge succeeds when it controls the stated portfolio risk at an acceptable all-in cost. It should not be rewarded for a lucky currency forecast or condemned because the foreign currency appreciated. Tactical FX positions should be judged by return, risk, and forecasting evidence in their own budget.

### Design governance for model disagreement

The evidence contains legitimate disagreement. Full hedging is a strong bond default, but equity results vary by base currency, sample, and model [1][2][3]. Dynamic models can reduce volatility in sample, but actual ratios are lower and more heterogeneous than minimum-variance estimates [4][5][6]. Long horizons do not automatically remove exchange-rate risk, yet purchasing-power and liability effects can make long-run currency exposure valuable [1][2]. Governance should surface these contradictions rather than resolve them with one permanent slogan.

Synthesis: A committee should approve the objective, strategic range, instruments, leverage and collateral limits, counterparties, rebalancing rule, tactical budget, stress tests, and review triggers. Independent risk should verify exposure and cash-flow calculations. Operations should confirm settlement instructions and collateral mobility. The portfolio manager should own the asset thesis but should not be able to reclassify an out-of-policy currency position after the result is known.

Synthesis: Review triggers can include a material liability change, a persistent shift in asset-currency covariance, a new capital-control regime, basis or spread outside a defined range, counterparty deterioration, loss of market liquidity, repeated band breaches, or stress liquidity below the approved reserve. The response should be proportional. A temporary basis widening may call for a staged roll, while a permanent liability change may call for a strategic ratio change.

### Apply the framework by investor type

Synthesis: An individual using a currency-hedged fund delegates forwards, rolls, collateral, and operational controls to the fund. The decision is therefore between hedged and unhedged share classes or funds, with attention to expense differences, tracking, tax, and the role of the asset in the household plan. The investor should not assume that a hedged share class removes asset risk; it mainly changes the currency component.

Synthesis: An asset manager needs currency-by-currency exposure, derivative attribution, cash forecasts, counterparty limits, and an explicit mandate for tactical deviations. A pension or insurer must integrate the program with liabilities and collateral at the correct legal entity. A sovereign or endowment without one dominant liability currency may value currency diversification more highly, but should still distinguish strategic exposure from unmanaged drift.

Synthesis: For all investors, the correct conclusion is conditional. Hedge foreign bonds heavily when the objective is domestic-currency fixed-income behavior and implementation is resilient. Evaluate global equity hedging as a portfolio covariance and liability problem rather than a binary doctrine. Retain currency exposure only when it serves a documented diversification, liability, or return objective. The right hedge is the one that removes incidental risk without creating a funding structure or speculative position that the investor did not intend.

## Common Pitfalls

### Calling the forward discount a management fee

Most forward points reflect short-term interest-rate differences, while the cross-currency basis and trading spread add separate market costs [7][8]. Reporting the entire forward discount as an avoidable fee makes hedged and unhedged returns incomparable.

### Using one aggregate ratio to hide currency mismatches

A 100 percent aggregate hedge can combine overhedges and underhedges in different currencies. Report currency-by-currency asset value, hedge notional, retained exposure, and liability offset [4][6].

### Optimizing on one covariance sample

Minimum-variance ratios can be unstable and extreme. Compare simple ratios, use constraints and shrinkage, test other windows and regimes, and require improvement after costs and stress [2][3].

### Letting a hedge become an unreported FX strategy

Changing the ratio because a manager expects appreciation or depreciation is a tactical currency decision. Place it in a separate budget with separate attribution [1][3][4].

### Ignoring collateral because the hedge offsets at maturity

Forwards, futures, swaps, and options can require margin, collateral, or principal before the asset produces cash. Stress the path and legal entity, not only the terminal offset [9][10][12][13].

### Assuming the next roll will resemble the last

Short hedges against long assets create repeated exposure to forward points, basis, spreads, dealer capacity, and settlement. Test a failed or expensive roll and maintain collateral and counterparties accordingly [9][10][11].

### Treating full hedging as continuous

Market values and cash flows change between reset dates. Define the exposure base, valuation time, tolerance band, and use of contributions or withdrawals before calculating compliance [4][6].
