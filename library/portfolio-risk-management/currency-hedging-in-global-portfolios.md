# Currency Hedging in Global Portfolios -- The Right Hedge Preserves the Asset Diversification an Investor Actually Wants

A global asset position also creates a currency position whenever the asset and the investor's reporting currency differ, so an unhedged portfolio combines the intended asset exposure with a second return source [1][2]. Currency hedging can remove much of that second source, and the developed-market evidence generally places the risk-minimizing ratio closer to full hedging for government bonds than for equities [1][2][3]. The exact ratio depends on the starting asset portfolio, exposure definition, covariance, horizon, constraints, costs, and implementation; the central decision is how much incidental exchange-rate risk to retain without turning risk control into a directional foreign-exchange strategy [1][2][3].

## Background

International diversification separates the location or denomination of an investment from the currency in which an investor measures wealth and pays obligations. A U.S. investor buying a euro-denominated bond receives the bond's local-currency return and the dollar return on the euro [1][2]. Synthesis: a euro-area pension fund buying a dollar bond receives the bond return and the euro return on the dollar, while its benefits may still be owed in euros. In either case, the home-currency result is generated by both the asset and the exchange rate even when the security analyst formed no view about currencies [1][2]. Currency hedging developed as the portfolio-level process for controlling that incidental exposure.

For a one-period investment, let `r_L` be the local-currency asset return and let `c` be the home-currency return on one unit of the foreign currency. The exact gross home-currency return is `(1 + r_L)(1 + c) - 1`; for modest changes it is approximately `r_L + c`. The interaction term matters during large moves, and the quotation convention determines the sign of `c` [1][2]. A hedge adds a position that gains when the foreign currency loses value against the home currency, usually by selling the foreign currency forward or establishing an economically equivalent position. The investment then contains two deliberately separable decisions: which assets to own and which currencies to retain.

The academic literature moved from simple full-hedge rules toward portfolio-specific hedge ratios. Early international portfolio models showed that foreign currency can be treated as another return source whose covariance with the asset portfolio affects total variance. Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros, and Viceira formalized this risk-management demand for seven developed-market currencies and distinguished it from speculative currency demand based on expected returns [1]. Schmittmann examined German, Japanese, British, and U.S. investors over 1975-2009 and compared no hedge, half hedge, full hedge, and estimated minimum-variance ratios over several horizons [2]. Boudoukh, Richardson, Thapar, and Wang later separated a minimum-variance hedge from an alpha-seeking currency portfolio, making explicit that reducing asset risk and forecasting currency returns are different activities [3].

This literature repeatedly finds an asset-class asymmetry within its studied developed-market samples. Currency volatility can be large relative to the ordinary volatility of long-term government bonds, so an unhedged allocation may behave more like a currency position than the fixed-income exposure the investor selected. For global equities, local stock volatility is larger and some currencies have historically moved against equity markets, so retaining selected currency exposure has sometimes reduced rather than increased portfolio variance [1][2]. These are empirical covariance results, not permanent laws or direct evidence for every credit, inflation-linked, emerging-market, or private-debt portfolio. A currency that acted as a safe haven in one sample, for one equity portfolio and set of constraints, need not do so in another regime.

Institutional practice reflects both this asymmetry and operational constraints. Sialm and Zhu document widespread short-dated forward use but low average hedge ratios among U.S. international fixed-income mutual funds [4]. Sector filings and custodian portfolios also show that fixed-income investors generally hedge more than equity investors, while actual ratios remain heterogeneous across investors, countries, and currencies [5][6]. Synthesis: different liabilities, benchmarks, regulations, tax rules, cost tolerances, and beliefs can justify different choices even when two institutions own similar foreign assets.

The market architecture also changes the path of a hedge. An outright forward fixes a future currency exchange [12][13]. A currency future standardizes a forward-like commitment on an exchange and is marked to market daily [13][14]. An FX swap combines a spot exchange with a reversing forward exchange and is economically close to collateralized cross-currency borrowing [9][11][12]. A longer-dated currency swap exchanges interest streams and generally principal, while a deliverable option gives its buyer asymmetric protection for an upfront premium [9][10][12][13]. Each can reduce exchange-rate exposure, but the instruments differ in margin, collateral, principal settlement, basis, liquidity, and roll obligations.

In their 2009-2016 evidence, Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan document persistent G10 covered-interest-parity deviations after the global financial crisis. Their quarter-end results support a balance-sheet-constraint channel, while nominal-rate relationships are consistent with the proposed role of international funding and investment imbalances [8]. Later work models the excess return over an investor's local cash rate on a fully hedged foreign bond as the destination bond term premium plus the cross-currency basis [7], and estimates that basis deviations imposed additional costs on some pensions and insurers hedging dollar assets [5]. Calling all forward points a fee is therefore misleading: matched-tenor interest-rate differentials and cross-currency basis are return components, while trading spreads and operational expenses are separate implementation costs [5][7][8].

The most consequential implementation problem is maturity mismatch. McGuire, Shim, Shin, and Sushko study resident institutional investors and asset managers in six emerging Asian economies whose outward portfolios were predominantly long-term and mainly dollar-denominated, while currency risk was hedged mainly through short-term derivatives supplied by banks [10]. Market-wide average forward and swap maturities were about one month for MYR, nearly two months for PHP, and about three months for KRW and TWD; these are currency-market averages rather than investor-level estimates. In March 2020, FX-swap borrowing costs rose and dealer capacity tightened, especially in Korea, although effects differed across markets [10]. A terminal hedge can be economically correct yet operationally fail if the investor cannot post required collateral, settle principal, or replace the contract during market stress [10][12].

Synthesis: This topic therefore treats currency hedging as portfolio risk management. It covers return decomposition, hedge ratios, full and partial policies, dynamic adjustment, instruments, carry, basis, collateral, roll risk, and diversification. It does not attempt to forecast individual currencies or recommend speculative carry, value, momentum, or macro trades. Those positions may be legitimate investments, but they should be budgeted and evaluated separately from the hedge that preserves the intended global asset allocation.

## Core Concepts

### Currency exposure is an additional portfolio weight

For a foreign asset measured in the investor's home currency, the unhedged return can be approximated as follows [1][2]:

```text
home-currency return = local-asset return + currency return
                       + interaction term
```

The approximation is useful for risk decomposition, but the exact multiplicative relationship should be used for large moves and formal performance calculation. The sign of the currency return must follow one fixed quote. In this topic, a positive `c` means that the foreign currency appreciates against the investor's home currency, increasing the home-currency value of the foreign asset [1][2].

Let `h` be the share of the foreign-currency exposure sold forward against the home currency. Ignoring carry for the moment, the retained currency exposure is `1 - h` and the hedged return is approximately [1][2]:

```text
r_H(h) = r_L + (1 - h)c
```

The common policy labels follow directly, but this scalar expression is a derived one-period special case that holds forward carry fixed or deliberately ignores it and assumes a beginning-period hedge notional. Empirical work generally estimates the hedge against currency excess returns or the forward leg rather than raw spot returns alone, and sources [1] and [3] express the equivalent solution in retained-currency-exposure notation rather than the `h` convention used here:

- `h = 0`: unhedged; the investor retains the full currency exposure.
- `h = 0.5`: half hedged; half the foreign-currency exposure remains.
- `h = 1`: fully hedged; the first-order currency exposure is removed.
- `h > 1`: overhedged; the investor is net short the foreign currency.
- `h < 0`: the investor adds foreign-currency exposure beyond the asset holding.

A ratio is always relative to a defined exposure base. It may be calculated separately by currency, as an aggregate ratio across all foreign assets, or relative to a liability benchmark. A 100 percent portfolio-level ratio can still leave cross-currency mismatches if the currencies netted in the numerator differ from those in the assets. Synthesis: governance should report both the aggregate ratio and the currency-by-currency amounts, because one headline percentage can conceal offsetting long and short positions [4][6].

### The minimum-variance ratio depends on covariance

Using the approximation above, the variance of the hedged return is [1][2][3]:

```text
Var[r_H(h)] = Var[r_L]
              + (1 - h)^2 Var[c]
              + 2(1 - h)Cov[r_L,c]
```

Differentiating with respect to `h` gives the unconstrained minimum-variance hedge ratio [1][2]:

```text
h* = 1 + Cov[r_L,c] / Var[c]
```

The formula explains why full hedging is a benchmark rather than a theorem. If local asset returns and currency returns are uncorrelated, `Cov[r_L,c] = 0` and `h* = 1`. If the foreign currency tends to appreciate when the local asset falls, the covariance is negative and the risk-minimizing investor retains some currency exposure, so `h* < 1`. If the currency tends to fall when the asset falls, the covariance is positive and the estimated minimum-variance ratio can exceed 100 percent [1][2]. In a multi-country portfolio, covariances among assets and among currencies make the solution a matrix problem rather than a set of isolated bilateral ratios [1][3].

This is a risk result, not an expected-return result. A currency can lower equity variance because of negative covariance even if its average return is low. Conversely, a high-yield currency can increase portfolio risk despite attractive historical carry. Campbell and coauthors separate risk-minimizing currency demand from speculative demand for this reason [1]. Boudoukh and coauthors make the same separation operational by constructing a hedge portfolio first and adding an alpha-seeking currency portfolio only as a separately sized component [3].

Estimated optimal ratios are uncertain. Currency volatility, asset-currency covariance, and cross-currency covariance change across samples and regimes. The optimization can also produce large long or short positions from small estimation differences [2][3]. Synthesis: a ratio should be treated as an estimate with error bands and constraints, not as a precise property of an asset class. A robust policy can shrink an estimated ratio toward a strategic benchmark, cap overhedging, require a minimum improvement in stressed loss, and compare the optimized result with simple 0, 50, and 100 percent alternatives.

### The base currency and liability benchmark define the risk

A hedge has no meaning without a reporting or liability currency, but the empirical papers operationalize that benchmark in different ways. Schmittmann obtains base-currency-sensitive ratios in single-country and constrained multi-country exercises [2]. By contrast, Campbell and coauthors show that unconstrained optimal currency exposures for a fixed global asset portfolio are invariant to base currency when investors share the same currency opportunity set and can hold a real risk-free asset [1], and Boudoukh and coauthors obtain nearly identical flexible optimized solutions across G10 investors [3]. Apparent base-currency differences therefore depend on the starting portfolio, exposure definition, available assets, and constraints.

Synthesis: The relevant economic benchmark can differ from the accounting currency. A pension fund reports in the sponsor's currency but should evaluate hedges against the currencies and sensitivities of promised benefits. A sovereign wealth fund may have no single near-term liability currency and may deliberately retain foreign-currency diversification. A household may spend in one currency yet hold a foreign home, education obligation, or business income that changes the economic exposure. The liability-driven-investing principle applies: risk is the mismatch between assets and what capital must fund, not the volatility of assets viewed alone.

Horizon changes the question but does not remove it. Long horizons allow purchasing-power adjustment and mean reversion to matter, yet they also expose the investor to repeated forward rolls and changing interest-rate differentials. Schmittmann found that substantial risk reduction from hedging remained over horizons up to five years in the 1975-2009 sample, especially for developed-market government bonds [2]. Campbell and coauthors did not find that leaving currency unhedged became systematically more attractive as the horizon increased from one month to one year in their post-1975 sample [1]. These findings reject a universal rule that long-term investors can ignore currencies, while still leaving room for liability-specific long-run exposures.

### Full, partial, and dynamic hedges solve different governance problems

A full hedge sets `h = 1` and removes first-order currency exposure. It is transparent, easy to benchmark, and often close to minimum variance for the developed-market government-bond portfolios studied in [1] and [2]. It does not remove basis, transaction, counterparty, collateral, or roll risk. Nor does it hold exactly between reset dates when the local-currency value or quantity of the underlying asset changes while the forward notional stays fixed [4][6]. A pure exchange-rate move does not change a consistently measured bilateral hedge ratio, although it can change an aggregate portfolio ratio by changing currency weights [6]. A policy must specify whether it hedges beginning-period market value, current value, principal only, or principal plus expected income.

A partial hedge fixes a strategic ratio such as 50 percent. It accepts some exchange-rate variance while reducing turnover, estimation dependence, and the risk of repeatedly trading a full notional during stressed markets. A partial ratio may also preserve exposure to a currency that diversifies equity or liabilities. Its weakness is that a round number is not evidence of optimality. Synthesis: a partial policy is defensible when its purpose is explicit -- for example, balancing volatility reduction against liquidity and model risk -- rather than when 50 percent is treated as a neutral fact.

A dynamic risk-minimizing hedge changes with the foreign-asset exposure and estimates of currency volatility, asset-currency covariance, and cross-currency covariance [3]. Expected-return signals such as carry, value, momentum, and fundamental momentum belong to a separately identified tactical or alpha-seeking currency portfolio [3]. Actual fund data also show time-varying ratios [4][6], but dynamic rules trade more, depend on estimates, and can quietly become currency strategies. A rule that hedges more because a currency is expected to fall is making an expected-return decision; a rule that adjusts notional because the foreign asset rose is maintaining a risk target.

The author's synthesis is to separate three layers. The strategic hedge is the long-run ratio justified by asset and liability risk. The maintenance rule restores that ratio when market values drift outside a band. The tactical currency budget permits deliberate deviations under separate limits, attribution, and review. This separation prevents a manager from labeling an unsuccessful FX view as a hedge and prevents risk control from being judged against a speculative return target.

### Instruments change the cash-flow path of the same objective

| Instrument | Basic economic use | Main strengths | Main residual risks |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Outright FX forward | Commit to exchange two currencies at a future date and rate [12][13] | Customized currency, amount, and maturity; no spot principal exchange at inception | Counterparty and replacement-cost exposure, collateral, principal settlement, spread, basis, and repeated roll |
| Currency future | Standardized exchange-traded currency commitment [13] | Central clearing, transparent price, and daily mark-to-market [13] | Listed amount or date mismatch, daily liquid-collateral needs, settlement choice, and roll [13][14] |
| FX swap | Exchange currencies now and reverse the exchange later [9][11][12] | Simultaneously funds and hedges a foreign asset; widely used at short maturities | Full-principal reversal, counterparty exposure, off-balance-sheet obligation, short-term rollover, and funding squeeze |
| Currency swap | Exchange principal and coupons across currencies, generally at longer maturities [9][10][12] | Can match foreign-currency bond liabilities and reduce repeated short rolls [10][11] | Contract-specific valuation, counterparty, collateral, settlement, and replacement risk |
| Deliverable FX option | Buy a right to exchange currencies at a strike for an upfront premium [12][13] | Asymmetric protection can place a floor on home-currency value | Premium, strike and maturity mismatch, path dependence, and counterparty or clearing demands |

The table combines official contract definitions and implementation evidence [9][12][13][14]. The residual-risk column is the author's synthesis of how the cited mechanics can affect a portfolio. Synthesis: an outright forward is usually the simplest overlay when the investor already owns the foreign asset and only needs a future offset, while a future can serve the same direction of exposure but leave notional or date mismatch. Because futures are marked to market, accumulated losses can require cash or eligible securities collateral before the foreign asset is sold [13]. A position that is economically protective can therefore create a liquidity demand before the offsetting asset produces cash.

An FX swap differs from an outright forward because it includes a spot leg as well as a forward reversal. BIS describes it as economically similar to collateralized cross-currency borrowing: one currency is lent and another borrowed, with the future reversal fixed at inception [9][11]. Once the spot exchange is complete, the remaining obligation resembles a forward. Synthesis: this structure is useful when an investor must both obtain foreign currency to buy an asset and remove the exchange-rate mismatch. It also creates a full-notional repayment obligation that can be absent from ordinary balance-sheet debt statistics [9][11].

Longer-dated currency swaps exchange coupons and generally principal and are used, among other purposes, to hedge foreign-currency bond liabilities. Their longer maturity can reduce reliance on repeated short-term rolls [10][11]. Options differ because the buyer pays for a right rather than a symmetric obligation. BIS turnover evidence for 2022-2025 shows sharp growth in currency-option trading, especially in currencies whose forward premia rose; the authors interpret this as suggesting some substitution from forwards toward options while noting that option trading can also be speculative [15]. When interest-rate differentials are wide and FX volatility is low, option spreads may reduce hedging expense; [15] gives a collar -- a purchased out-of-the-money put combined with a sold out-of-the-money call -- as the basic example.

### Carry, forward points, and basis are part of the hedged return

Under covered interest parity, the forward rate reflects matched-tenor interest rates under a defined quotation and funding convention. A cross-currency basis measures the residual deviation associated with market segmentation, funding demand, and intermediary balance-sheet capacity [7][8]. The equation must use proportional or log forward points rather than subtracting rate levels. With `S` and `F` quoted as local-currency units per U.S. dollar, `s = ln(S)`, `f = ln(F)`, tenor `T`, and continuously compounded rates, one convention writes [7][8]:

```text
(f - s) / T = r_local - r_US + x
```

Here `x` is the cross-currency basis under that convention; another quotation or basis definition can reverse signs. At covered interest parity, `x = 0`. Forward points therefore decompose into the matched-tenor interest-rate differential and basis, while bid-ask spread is separate. Their relative magnitudes vary by currency, maturity, and period [7][8]. It is misleading to call the entire forward premium or discount an avoidable fee. If the investor gives up the higher short-term rate in one currency and receives the lower rate in another, forward carry reduces the hedged return relative to the unhedged spot position; the reverse configuration can produce a benefit. Synthesis: commissions, market impact, custody, collateral funding, and operational expense should be reported separately from carry and basis.

Du and Huber estimate that persistent basis deviations imposed measurable additional costs on sampled foreign pensions and insurers hedging dollar assets. Their estimate averages $2.7 billion annually during 2017-2020, about 0.1 percent of all dollar securities held by those two sectors, and assumes continuously rolled short-term hedges [5]. Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan find larger deviations for contracts crossing quarter-end reporting dates, evidence consistent with more costly intermediary balance-sheet capacity [8]. Synthesis: expected hedged return should be decomposed into local asset return, home cash rate, forward carry, basis, trading cost, and collateral funding. Combining them into one unexplained "hedge drag" makes policy sensitive to accounting labels rather than economics.

### Rebalancing and roll rules determine realized exposure

A bilateral hedge established against today's foreign asset value drifts when the local-currency value or quantity of the asset changes while the forward notional stays fixed. If a foreign equity position rises from 100 to 120 in local-currency value while the short forward remains 100, a nominally full hedge becomes an 83.3 percent hedge [6]. A pure exchange-rate move cancels from a consistently measured bilateral ratio, although it can alter an aggregate ratio through currency weights [6]. Synthesis: subscriptions, redemptions, dividends, coupons, and security trades can also change the exposure base. A daily rebalance tracks exposure closely but raises turnover, spread, collateral, and market-impact costs; a monthly or threshold rule tolerates temporary under- or overhedging in exchange for lower friction.

Short contracts also expire. A roll closes or settles one hedge and establishes another for a later date. The new forward carry, basis, dealer capacity, and rollover availability may differ from the old contract [5][7][10]. To maintain a futures hedge beyond expiry, the trader offsets the expiring position and opens a later-dated contract; otherwise the contract proceeds to settlement [14]. Synthesis: the realized hedge is a sequence of contracts and cash flows, not one ratio selected at inception.

The worst implementation error is to measure only terminal offset. A forward or swap can match the final currency amount but generate collateral or full-principal needs before the asset can be liquidated. The Basel Committee's 2013 guidance directs banks and supervisors to manage principal, replacement-cost, liquidity, operational, and legal risks arising between FX trade execution and final settlement. It recommends legally enforceable netting and collateral arrangements and frequent full variation margin for physically settling FX swaps and forwards with financial institutions and systemically important non-financial entities [12]. Payment-versus-payment can eliminate principal risk but does not eliminate replacement-cost or liquidity risk, and collateral against mark-to-market exposure does not by itself fund the principal settlement obligation [12]. BIS evidence separately shows that short dollar hedges against long assets can create rollover dependence during market stress [10].

### A hedge policy is not a currency forecast

Synthesis: currency exposure can earn return, and managers may have informed views; the governance problem is classification. A risk-minimizing hedge is justified by covariance, liability alignment, or a stated tolerance for home-currency loss, while a directional currency position is justified by expected return. Carry, value, momentum, and fundamental-momentum signals belong to an alpha-seeking currency strategy rather than to the minimum-variance hedge itself [3]. Fund evidence also links retained exposure to expected currency returns and carry [4][5]. Such a position is not automatically the residual of an incomplete hedge.

A clean policy records the strategic hedge ratio before the currency view, calculates the residual exposure, and attributes every tactical deviation to a separate FX risk budget. Performance reports then show asset return, currency return, strategic hedge return, tactical FX return, transaction cost, basis, and collateral funding. Synthesis: this separation preserves accountability. It allows the hedge to be successful when it reduces the intended risk even if its standalone derivative return is negative, and it prevents a profitable currency bet from being mistaken for evidence that the original asset allocation was well hedged.

## Evidence

### Global bond portfolios generally support high hedge ratios

Campbell, Serfaty-de Medeiros, and Viceira study developed equity, government-bond, currency, and short-rate markets with monthly underlying data from July 1975 through December 2005 and overlapping quarterly returns as the baseline. Their mean-variance framework holds the global asset portfolio fixed and chooses currency positions to minimize total variance. They find that global government-bond returns are nearly uncorrelated with most currency returns, so the risk-minimizing policy is close to full hedging, with a modest residual U.S. dollar exposure in their sample [1]. They also find little evidence that a risk-minimizing bond investor should vary currency positions merely because interest differentials change [1].

Schmittmann extends the comparison through 2009 for German, Japanese, British, and U.S. investors and evaluates single-country and multi-country portfolios. Full hedging reduces bond risk more than half hedging in nearly all reported cases [2]. At the quarterly horizon, estimated government-bond hedge ratios are generally close to one. At longer horizons, full hedging continues to reduce variance strongly, but estimated optimal ratios sometimes depart materially from one, including both underhedging and overhedging [2]. The study also warns that optimized ratios can be unstable, supporting comparison with simple strategic benchmarks.

Levich and Thomas analyze U.S.-dollar returns on an equal-weighted portfolio of approximately ten-year government bonds in Deutsche marks, Canadian dollars, British pounds, and Japanese yen over 168 months in 1977-1990. Reported Sharpe ratios were 0.29 unhedged, 0.41 fully hedged, 0.75 under a tactical hedge, and 1.23 with an active currency overlay; the active-strategy results were before transaction costs [16]. The paper assumes 2.5-4.0 basis points per futures transaction and estimates 50-80 basis points of annual cost for its active bond strategies. It says costs substantially eroded one especially high-turnover currency rule but did not materially affect most other individual rules [16]. This older in-sample evidence does not establish that the trading rules will repeat.

Fund holdings provide a separate test of practice. Sialm and Zhu study 302 U.S.-domiciled, actively managed international fixed-income funds from 2010Q2 through 2018Q4. Eighty-nine percent used currency forwards in at least one reporting quarter, while the mean fund-quarter hedge ratio was only 18 percent [4]. The 476,622 observed contracts had 63.7 mean and 48.2 median days remaining to settlement, so maintaining a stable hedge generally required frequent renewal [4]. Managers used forwards both to reduce incidental exposure and to make active currency decisions, supporting the governance separation between strategic hedging and tactical FX positions.

### Equity hedging is beneficial on average but less uniform

Campbell and coauthors find that the risk-minimizing currency portfolio for global equities differs from the bond solution because some currencies moved against world equity markets in their 1975-2005 sample. The U.S. dollar, euro, and Swiss franc supplied diversification in parts of the sample, while several other currencies were best hedged or overhedged [1]. Their result is covariance-based: the currency can reduce equity risk even when its average return is not high. It does not imply that those currencies will remain permanent safe havens.

Schmittmann likewise reports larger cross-country dispersion in equity hedge ratios than in bond ratios. Some estimated equity ratios are below zero or above one, depending on investor base and foreign market, while full hedging still reduces risk in most cases [2]. The variation confirms that a single global rule for equities can be convenient without being minimum variance for every investor. It also demonstrates the estimation problem: large optimal positions can emerge from historical covariance that may not persist.

Boudoukh, Richardson, Thapar, and Wang evaluate international equities over 1981-2017. Full hedging reduced average volatility from 16.5 percent to 14.9 percent across the G10 investor cases they report; their Robust dynamic minimum-variance hedge reduced it to 14.3 percent, and the fully Optimized solution reduced it to 12.7 percent [3]. The large Sharpe-ratio improvement arose only after adding the separate style-based `FXALPHA` portfolio, not from the hedge itself, and the reported returns are gross of fees and transaction costs [3]. The decomposition is important: risk reduction and alpha seeking are distinct decisions under the model.

Cheema-Fox and Greenwood analyze screened, matched custodian portfolios over February 1998 through December 2025. Inclusion is conditional on observed FX-forward activity, so the reported incidence is not an unconditional population share [6]. Using their Table 2 fund-currency denominator, the full-period cross-currency averages for U.S.-dollar-based funds are 27 percent hedged for equity and 51 percent for fixed income, with conditional hedge ratios of 33 percent and 60 percent. At the portfolio level, the corresponding incidence is 36 percent and 84 percent, with conditional ratios of 30 percent and 61 percent [6]. Their estimated minimum-variance ratios are materially higher, including an average of 97 percent for U.S.-dollar fixed income [6]. The gap is consistent with risk minimization being only one objective; the paper does not establish one causal explanation for every observed ratio.

### Hedging practice is large, heterogeneous, and balance-sheet dependent

Du and Huber estimate aggregate U.S.-dollar hedge ratios as of June 2020 of 43 percent for insurance companies, 35 percent for pension funds, and 21 percent for mutual funds [5]. Their December 2019 table reports $1.971 trillion of hedging demand across those actively managed sectors, approximately $2 trillion. The mutual-fund ratio is constructed partly from share-class hedging designations rather than a complete aggregation of observed forward notionals, and the authors describe bond-versus-equity differences as suggestive because separate ratios are reported by relatively few investors [5].

The same study links ratios to currency volatility, asset-currency covariance, interest differentials, and cross-currency basis [5]. For sampled pensions and insurers, the estimated additional cost attributable to short-term basis deviations averaged $2.7 billion annually during 2017-2020, around 0.1 percent of all dollar securities held by those two sectors. This is a basis-deviation estimate, not total all-in hedging cost; it assumes continuously rolled short-term hedges and includes imputations [5].

Nenova, Schrimpf, and Shin model the excess return over local cash on a fully hedged foreign bond as the destination bond term premium plus the cross-currency basis [7]. In aggregate BIS data, changes in other financial institutions' gross swap and forward notionals are associated with yield-curve slopes, financial conditions, and spot valuation and co-move with bilateral long-term debt holdings. The data do not reveal contract direction or exact hedge ratios, and the estimated basis relationship is not robustly causal [7]. The evidence supports treating currency policy and foreign-bond allocation as connected decisions, not assigning every observed derivative position to hedging.

Du, Tepper, and Verdelhan document persistent G10 covered-interest-parity deviations in data ending in 2016, using unsecured rates, collateralized repo rates, and bonds issued by the same highly rated borrower in multiple currencies [8]. Quarter-end patterns support a balance-sheet-constraint channel, while nominal-rate relationships are consistent with their proposed international funding-imbalance channel. The paper reports deviations after selected transaction-cost adjustments but does not show a costless arbitrage available without capital, funding, or shorting constraints [8]. For portfolio hedgers, the implication is direct: the price of transferring FX risk can widen when intermediary balance sheet is costly, especially when many investors demand the same side.

### Rollover, collateral, and hedging flows become risk factors

BIS Annual Economic Report data put global over-the-counter notional amounts outstanding in FX swaps, outright forwards, and currency swaps at $110.9 trillion on 31 December 2024, rounded to $111 trillion [9]. Of the aggregate notional for which maturity was reported, 76.2 percent was due within, including, one year. These are notionals, not market values, debt losses, or contract counts. Long-term asset owners that use short contracts repeatedly therefore face a maturity mismatch between the investment and the hedge, and the future full-principal obligation can remain outside ordinary balance-sheet debt statistics [9].

McGuire, Shim, Shin, and Sushko study resident institutional investors and asset managers in Chinese Taipei, Indonesia, Korea, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Thailand. They find that outward portfolios were predominantly long-term and mainly dollar-denominated, while currency risk was hedged mainly with short-term bank-supplied derivatives [10]. Their one-to-three-month maturity evidence is currency-market-wide rather than an investor-level estimate. In March 2020, FX-swap borrowing costs rose and dealer capacity tightened, especially in Korea, while Thai stress was shorter and some funds allowed hedge ratios to decline rather than rolling [10]. The event demonstrates that reducing exchange-rate risk can increase dependence on dealer funding and rollover conditions.

Using end-June 2022 derivatives data, Borio, McCauley, and McGuire estimate that non-banks outside the United States likely owed about $26 trillion in off-balance-sheet dollar payment obligations through FX swaps, forwards, and currency swaps [11]. The location is inferred because the statistics lack a counterparty-country breakdown, and the amount is a gross forward-payment obligation rather than net exposure, expected loss, or a simultaneous cash call. The monthly or quarterly rolling examples concern selected Dutch pension funds and Japanese insurers, not every entity included in the estimate [11].

The Basel Committee's 2013 guidance applies to banks and their supervisors. It covers spot, forwards, FX swaps, deliverable FX options, and currency swaps involving principal exchange, while excluding one-way-settlement instruments such as non-deliverable forwards [12]. It directs banks to manage principal, replacement-cost, liquidity, operational, and legal risks; use payment-versus-payment where practicable; and maintain legally enforceable netting and collateral arrangements. Its full variation-margin recommendation is specific to physically settling FX swaps and forwards with financial institutions and systemically important non-financial entities [12]. This establishes the infrastructure demands supporting many hedges without implying that every end investor faces identical margin terms.

### Costs can change both hedge ratios and market dynamics

Shin, Wooldridge, and Xia argue that ex post hedging by non-U.S. investors appears to have contributed materially to dollar weakness in April-May 2025. Many investors retained U.S. assets while increasing FX swap and forward overlays; basis changes and Asian-hours price movements support that mechanism [17]. The authors also state that hedging was not the sole driver and cannot be cleanly disentangled from speculation or some disinvestment. Their evidence is consistent with hedging flows adding to dollar pressure, not with a claim that no securities were sold.

Huang, Krohn, and Sushko report that monetary tightening in 2022-2023 raised the cost of hedging dollar exposures and contributed to relatively low hedge ratios entering April 2025 [15]. Between January and December 2022, the annualized U.S.-dollar forward premium embedded in three-month FX swaps rose from 0.7 percent to 3.5 percent for EUR/USD and from 0.3 percent to 5.5 percent for USD/JPY [15]. They also document sharp growth in option turnover between the April 2022 and April 2025 surveys and interpret the pattern as suggesting some substitution from forwards toward options, while acknowledging that not every option trade was a hedge [15]. These are period-specific market observations, not stable cost estimates.

The evidence as a whole supports four bounded conclusions. First, hedging generally reduces the volatility of developed-market international government bonds and often reduces equity volatility in the studied samples [1][2][3]. Second, the appropriate equity exposure is more covariance- and constraint-dependent than the bond exposure [1][2][3]. Third, actual investors use partial and dynamic policies in association with multiple objectives, costs, constraints, and views [4][5][6]. Fourth, depending on the instrument and settlement arrangement, a hedge can introduce basis, collateral, principal-settlement, dealer-capacity, and rollover risks [7][8][9][10][11][12].

## Implications

### Start from the portfolio objective, not from a forecast

Synthesis: The first practical implication is procedural. An investor should specify the reporting currency, liability currencies, foreign asset values, relevant benchmark, horizon, and loss measure before selecting a ratio. A global bond sleeve held to diversify duration should not accidentally become a dominant currency bet. A global equity sleeve may reasonably retain some currencies when their covariance lowers total risk. A liability-driven investor may hedge a currency even when the asset-only covariance suggests otherwise because the obligation, not the asset benchmark, defines loss.

The author's synthesis is that every policy should answer one sentence: "The portfolio hedges X percent of Y exposure into Z currency because doing so controls this stated risk." `X` is the ratio, `Y` is the exposure base, `Z` is the home or liability currency, and the final phrase identifies the objective. If any element is missing, the policy cannot be reproduced or audited.

### Use asset-class-specific defaults, then test exceptions

Synthesis: for developed-market long-term government bonds of the kind studied in [1] and [2], full or near-full hedging is the historical risk-minimizing default when the objective is to preserve fixed-income behavior in the investor's home or liability currency. The exception requires separate evidence: a foreign currency may hedge liabilities, diversify another portfolio component, or offer an intentionally budgeted return exposure. The result should not be transferred mechanically to credit, emerging-market debt, inflation-linked bonds, or private debt.

For global equities, no equally universal ratio follows from the evidence. A full hedge often reduces volatility, but selected unhedged currency positions have also diversified equity risk in historical samples [1][2][3]. A reasonable governance process compares at least no hedge, half hedge, full hedge, and a constrained minimum-variance estimate. It evaluates volatility, drawdown, expected shortfall, turnover, carry, basis, and stressed collateral rather than selecting the ratio from average return alone.

For alternatives, private assets, and emerging-market exposures, reported price volatility may understate asset risk while currency markets may be less liquid or subject to capital controls. The author's synthesis is that the hedge ratio and instrument must be conditioned on valuation frequency, exit horizon, convertibility, derivative depth, and legal access. A mathematically attractive ratio that cannot be traded or collateralized is not an implementable risk control.

### Build the hedge in layers

Synthesis: A robust program can use five layers. First, create a currency map of assets, liabilities, income, commitments, and collateral. Second, choose strategic ratios by asset class and liability role. Third, select instruments and maturities that match the exposure closely enough without creating unacceptable liquidity dependence. Fourth, define maintenance bands, roll dates, eligible counterparties, collateral sources, and fallback actions. Fifth, place tactical currency positions in a separate risk and performance budget.

Synthesis: The currency map should be both gross and net. Economic netting of euro assets against euro liabilities is meaningful only when timing, legal entity, and convertibility align. Netting a liquid euro bond against a private euro capital call may be misleading if one cannot fund the other when due. Gross reporting also reveals settlement obligations and counterparty concentration that disappear in a net currency total. Separately, contractual derivative netting reduces exposure only where it is legally enforceable, and a settlement disruption can cause liquidity needs measured on a net basis to move back toward gross payment needs [12].

Synthesis: Strategic ratios should be ranges rather than point estimates when model uncertainty is material. A committee might approve a target with a tolerance band and require review when estimated minimum variance leaves the band, when liabilities change, or when basis and liquidity costs become abnormal. This design is reversible: ordinary drift is corrected mechanically, while large policy changes require evidence and approval.

### Match the instrument to the exposure and the institution

Synthesis: Outright forwards fit investors that already own foreign assets and need a customized future currency offset. Their advantages are precision and operational familiarity; their risks include bilateral replacement cost, settlement, and repeated roll [12]. Futures fit liquid major-currency exposures when standardized contract sizes and maturities are acceptable. Central clearing reduces bilateral credit dependence, but daily margin creates a liquid-collateral requirement that can generate cash calls [13]. To maintain the hedge beyond expiry, the position must be rolled into a later contract; otherwise it proceeds to settlement [14].

Synthesis: FX swaps fit portfolios that must obtain foreign currency funding and hedge the same amount, but short maturities can turn a long-duration allocation into a recurring funding decision [7][9][10]. Longer-term currency swaps exchange coupons and generally principal and can reduce repeated short rolls for foreign-currency bond liabilities [10][11]. Deliverable options fit objectives that require asymmetric protection, but the premium, strike, and maturity determine the protection actually purchased [12][13]. When rate differentials are wide and FX volatility is low, an option collar can reduce the upfront expense by combining a purchased out-of-the-money put with a sold out-of-the-money call [15].

The author's synthesis is that instrument selection should minimize the total mismatch across amount, currency, maturity, payoff, collateral, and legal entity. Choosing the cheapest quoted spread while ignoring those dimensions can make the hedge less reliable than a nominally more expensive contract.

### Treat liquidity as part of hedge effectiveness

A hedge is effective only if the investor can maintain it through the adverse path. BCBS guidance supports testing full-principal payments, variation-margin and collateral calls, eligible-collateral haircuts, settlement timing, and collateral mobilization [12]; BIS evidence supports adding basis and rollover stress [9][10]. Synthesis: the broader investor liquidity budget should also include any initial margin, option premium, counterparty thresholds, asset-sale delay, encumbrance, and competing uses of collateral. Resources should be counted after contractual, legal-entity, and settlement constraints rather than at unconstrained market value.

Synthesis: Stress tests should apply at least four paths. The first combines a foreign-currency decline with a fall in the local-currency asset value before the next rebalance, potentially leaving the fixed hedge overhedged against a smaller asset position. The second is a home-currency decline, which produces derivative losses and collateral calls while the foreign asset rises in home-currency terms. The third combines basis widening, dealer retreat, and a roll date. The fourth combines a currency move with asset redemptions or liability payments. The test should show mark-to-market loss, cash by date, remaining unencumbered collateral, hedge ratio after the shock, and required trade size.

Synthesis: The single worst outcome is a sound foreign asset sold to meet the liquidity demands of its hedge. Preventing that failure may justify a lower strategic ratio, longer maturity, broader counterparty set, greater collateral reserve, or a staged roll. This is not evidence against hedging. It is evidence that exchange-rate variance and hedge-funding risk must be optimized together.

### Rebalance enough to control risk, not enough to chase noise

Synthesis: The maintenance rule should specify the valuation time, exposure base, frequency, and threshold. A beginning-of-month full hedge is not continuously full. The portfolio can use calendar rebalancing, percentage bands, cash-flow netting, or a combination. Contributions and withdrawals should be used first when they can reduce the required derivative trade.

More frequent rebalancing lowers exposure drift but increases bid-ask spread, market impact, collateral movements, and operational load. Dynamic covariance models add another source of turnover and estimation error [2][3]. The author's synthesis is to separate notional maintenance from model-driven ratio changes. The first follows observable asset values; the second should cross a higher evidence threshold because it changes the risk policy itself.

Synthesis: Rolls should be staggered when concentration on one fixing or maturity creates market and operational risk. A ladder of maturities can reduce one-day refinancing dependence, although it complicates valuation and attribution. The committee should record the forward curve, basis, spread, collateral, and counterparty concentration at each roll. A persistent cost increase may justify policy review, but changing the hedge merely because the derivative recently lost money confuses the hedge payoff with the combined portfolio objective.

### Separate risk attribution from return attribution

Synthesis: Performance reporting should decompose at least six items: local asset return, currency return, strategic hedge payoff, tactical FX payoff, forward carry and basis, and explicit implementation cost. The combined result should then be compared with the declared benchmark. A negative derivative payoff can be the expected offset to a positive currency translation gain; evaluating the derivative alone reverses the economic conclusion.

Synthesis: Risk attribution should separately show retained currency exposure, asset-currency covariance, contribution to total variance, stressed loss, collateral requirement, and rollover concentration. A hedge can lower ordinary volatility while increasing liquidity risk. Another can have modest variance effect but materially reduce liability mismatch. One ratio cannot summarize both outcomes.

The author's synthesis is that a strategic hedge succeeds when it controls the stated portfolio risk at an acceptable all-in cost. It should not be rewarded for a lucky currency forecast or condemned because the foreign currency appreciated. Tactical FX positions should be judged by return, risk, and forecasting evidence in their own budget.

### Design governance for model disagreement

The evidence contains legitimate disagreement. Full hedging is a strong historical default for the developed-market government-bond portfolios studied, while equity estimates vary with the starting portfolio, currency opportunity set, constraints, sample, and model [1][2][3]. Under Campbell's assumptions, the unconstrained optimal currency portfolio for a fixed global asset portfolio is invariant to base currency [1]. Dynamic models can reduce volatility in sample, but actual ratios are lower and more heterogeneous than minimum-variance estimates [4][5][6]. Longer horizons do not automatically remove exchange-rate risk [1][2]. Synthesis: purchasing-power and liability effects can nevertheless make long-run currency exposure valuable. Governance should surface these conditional findings rather than resolve them with one permanent slogan.

Synthesis: A committee should approve the objective, strategic range, instruments, leverage and collateral limits, counterparties, rebalancing rule, tactical budget, stress tests, and review triggers. Independent risk should verify exposure and cash-flow calculations. Operations should confirm settlement instructions and collateral mobility. The portfolio manager should own the asset thesis but should not be able to reclassify an out-of-policy currency position after the result is known.

Synthesis: Review triggers can include a material liability change, a persistent shift in asset-currency covariance, a new capital-control regime, basis or spread outside a defined range, counterparty deterioration, loss of market liquidity, repeated band breaches, or stress liquidity below the approved reserve. The response should be proportional. A temporary basis widening may call for a staged roll, while a permanent liability change may call for a strategic ratio change.

### Apply the framework by investor type

Synthesis: An individual using a currency-hedged fund delegates forwards, rolls, collateral, and operational controls to the fund. The decision is therefore between hedged and unhedged share classes or funds, with attention to expense differences, tracking, tax, and the role of the asset in the household plan. The investor should not assume that a hedged share class removes asset risk; it mainly changes the currency component.

Synthesis: An asset manager needs currency-by-currency exposure, derivative attribution, cash forecasts, counterparty limits, and an explicit mandate for tactical deviations. A pension or insurer must integrate the program with liabilities and collateral at the correct legal entity. A sovereign or endowment without one dominant liability currency may value currency diversification more highly, but should still distinguish strategic exposure from unmanaged drift.

Synthesis: For all investors, the correct conclusion is conditional. Hedge developed-market government-bond currency exposure heavily when the objective is domestic-currency fixed-income behavior, the evidence is applicable, and implementation is resilient. Evaluate global equity hedging as a portfolio covariance and liability problem rather than a binary doctrine. Retain currency exposure only when it serves a documented diversification, liability, or return objective. The right hedge is the one that removes incidental risk without creating a funding structure or speculative position that the investor did not intend.

## Common Pitfalls

### Calling the forward discount a management fee

Forward points decompose into the matched-tenor interest-rate differential and cross-currency basis under a stated quotation and sign convention; trading spread is separate, and their relative magnitudes vary [7][8]. Reporting the entire forward discount as an avoidable management fee makes hedged and unhedged returns incomparable.

### Using one aggregate ratio to hide currency mismatches

A 100 percent aggregate hedge can combine overhedges and underhedges in different currencies. Report currency-by-currency asset value, hedge notional, retained exposure, and liability offset [4][6].

### Optimizing on one covariance sample

Minimum-variance ratios can be unstable and extreme [2][3]. Synthesis: compare simple ratios, use constraints and shrinkage, test other windows and regimes, and require improvement after costs and stress.

### Letting a hedge become an unreported FX strategy

Changing the ratio because a manager expects appreciation or depreciation is a tactical currency decision [1][3][4]. Synthesis: place it in a separate budget with separate attribution.

### Ignoring collateral because the hedge offsets at maturity

Physically settled FX forwards, swaps, deliverable options, and principal-exchanging currency swaps can create principal-settlement exposure [12]. Futures can require daily liquid collateral [13], while BCBS recommends frequent full variation margin for physically settling FX swaps and forwards with specified financial and systemically important counterparties [12]. Stress the path and legal entity, not only the terminal offset.

### Assuming the next roll will resemble the last

Short hedges against long assets create repeated exposure to changing basis, dollar-funding conditions, dealer capacity, and rollover availability [9][10][11]. Synthesis: test an expensive or failed roll and identify committed liquidity and alternative counterparties before the next maturity.

### Treating full hedging as continuous

The local-currency value or quantity of the underlying asset can change between reset dates while the forward notional remains fixed [6]. A pure exchange-rate move does not change a consistently measured bilateral ratio, although it can change an aggregate ratio through currency weights. Synthesis: define the exposure base, valuation time, tolerance band, and treatment of contributions or withdrawals before calculating compliance.
