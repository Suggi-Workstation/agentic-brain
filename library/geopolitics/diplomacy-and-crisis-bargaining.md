# Diplomacy and Crisis Bargaining Work When Signals, Enforcement, and Exit Options Align

Diplomacy does not end conflict merely by opening talks: it works when parties can identify a bargain preferable to continued fighting, communicate enough credible information to find it, and trust that its terms can survive changes in power and incentives.[1][2][7] Threats, assurances, backchannels, mediators, ceasefires, and guarantees are therefore parts of one bargaining system whose design determines whether pressure produces agreement, delay, or escalation.[3][4][9][13]

## Background

Modern crisis-bargaining theory begins with a puzzle. War destroys lives and resources, so two actors that expect those costs should normally prefer some negotiated allocation to paying the costs and then receiving the battlefield result. Fearon formalized this as the inefficiency puzzle: a satisfactory rational explanation must show why leaders cannot locate or implement a settlement that both prefer to war, not merely why each side values the issue or expects benefits from victory.[1] His framework identified private information combined with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and rare indivisibilities as mechanisms that can remove an otherwise available bargain.[1] The framework did not claim that emotion, ideology, error, or domestic politics are irrelevant. It supplied a baseline against which those forces can be located: they matter when they alter beliefs, preferences, political survival, the feasible terms, or the credibility of implementation.

Diplomacy and coercion are often described as opposites, but the strategic literature treats them as intertwined. Schelling distinguished brute force, which takes or destroys what an actor wants, from coercion, which seeks to change another actor's choice by manipulating expected costs and benefits.[3] Deterrence asks an opponent not to begin an action; compellence asks it to stop, reverse, or undertake one. Compellence is generally harder because the target must visibly change behavior, the coercer must communicate what compliance requires, and both sides face questions about deadlines, proportionality, and how pressure will end.[3] A threat without a feasible bargain is an ultimatum; a concession without an enforcement structure may be temporary; force that leaves no decision to the adversary is no longer bargaining in the same sense.

The growth of formal bargaining models shifted analysis away from treating credibility as a reputation that leaders either possess or lack. Credibility depends on the signal, the stakes, the sender's capability, the receiver's beliefs, and the costs each side expects from action or retreat.[1][3][4] Public mobilization, deployed forces, alliance coordination, economic measures, domestic authorization, and visible deadlines can make a threat harder to abandon. The same devices can also trap the sender or make the target's retreat humiliating. Kurizaki's model of public and private threats shows why secrecy can be efficient even when a private threat is less informative: private communication can shield both leaders from domestic punishment if compromise becomes preferable to war.[4] Diplomacy therefore has both an informational function and a political-space function.

War does not suspend bargaining. Fighting changes territory, capabilities, beliefs about resolve, coalition cohesion, domestic survival, and estimates of future costs. Goemans treats termination as a process in which battlefield information changes expected outcomes and war aims, while leaders' domestic political risks affect whether they can reduce demands or accept defeat.[12] The practical implication is that an offer rejected at one point can become acceptable later, but continued costs do not mechanically create peace. Leaders may expect improvement, fear punishment for compromise, distrust future compliance, or believe that delay changes bargaining power in their favor.[2][12]

Intrastate war sharpens the enforcement problem. Belligerents that sign an interstate settlement may retain separate governments and armed forces, but a civil-war settlement often requires at least one side to disarm, demobilize, enter state institutions, or submit to authorities previously controlled by its enemy. Walter's study of civil wars from 1940 through 1990 argued that negotiations frequently failed because the parties could not credibly guarantee security during that vulnerable transition; outside guarantees were associated with successful settlement in her cases.[7] Later work qualified claims that one mechanism is universally necessary; Hartzell and Hoddie, for example, found that settlements sharing or dividing power across more political, territorial, military, and economic dimensions were associated with more durable peace.[17] The underlying problem remains: a signed bargain is not durable if compliance destroys one party's capacity to survive defection.[7][17]

Mediation developed as more than neutral message carrying. A mediator can organize contact, protect confidential exploration, diagnose incompatible beliefs, propose packages, coordinate external actors, supply rewards or pressure, and help arrange monitoring or guarantees.[5][6][13] Kydd's model shows that impartiality and credibility are not identical. A mediator known to favor one side may be especially credible when telling that side to concede, because the recommendation runs against the mediator's presumed bias.[5] Beardsley and colleagues separate facilitation, formulation, and manipulation, finding different associations with agreement, crisis abatement, and longer-run tension reduction.[6] The mediator's relevant quality is therefore not a label of neutrality but the ability to transmit believable information, alter incentives without destroying consent, and remain engaged through implementation.

Timing also entered the field through the concept of ripeness. Zartman argues that negotiations become more likely when parties perceive a mutually hurting stalemate and a way out.[11] The concept is perceptual rather than a mechanical casualty threshold: objective pain may exist without leaders accepting that victory is unavailable, and recognition of stalemate may still produce paralysis if no plausible formula is visible.[11] Ripeness identifies an opportunity, not a guarantee. Mediators can help reveal costs, develop a way out, or preserve a channel until perceptions change, but premature pressure for a comprehensive bargain may harden positions or provide cover for military preparation.

Ceasefire practice joined these theories to operational design. The United Nations treats a ceasefire as both a political and technical arrangement: it may protect civilians and humanitarian access, support negotiations, or implement a final settlement, but its purpose, prohibited acts, command responsibilities, monitoring, verification, dispute handling, and implementation resources must fit the conflict.[13] Clayton and Sticher distinguish cessations of hostilities, preliminary ceasefires with compliance mechanisms, and definitive ceasefires linked to political settlement and demobilization.[10] This distinction prevents a common analytical error. A pause can succeed at reducing violence without resolving the disputed issue, while a nominally permanent agreement can fail if it lacks the mechanisms needed for the transition it promises.

The field's development leads to one central conclusion. Diplomacy is not speech added after strategy; it is the process by which actors exchange information, manipulate risk, define reciprocal actions, and construct enforceable transitions under uncertainty.[1][2][3][13] Its success cannot be measured only by whether representatives meet or sign a document. The relevant tests are whether the process changes beliefs and incentives, whether the agreement maps obligations to observable behavior, whether parties can comply without becoming defenseless, and whether violations can be managed without automatically restarting war.

## Core Concepts

### The Bargaining Range Is the Starting Point, Not the Settlement

If war has expected costs for both sides, there is normally a range of allocations that each should prefer to fighting. The boundaries of that range depend on each side's expected battlefield outcome, expected costs, risk tolerance, political survival, and valuation of the disputed issue.[1][12] A rise in one side's expected victory or a fall in its expected costs moves its minimum acceptable offer. A humanitarian disaster can widen the range by increasing costs, while a new ally or expected weapons delivery can narrow it by raising anticipated battlefield returns. The range is analytical; it does not announce itself to negotiators.

Agreement can fail even when a range exists because the parties disagree about its location. Each has an incentive to exaggerate strength or resolve to obtain better terms. A state may conceal logistical weakness, overstate alliance support, or publicly rule out compromise. If all such statements were accepted literally, bluffing would always pay; if none were believed, genuine warnings would fail.[1] Crisis bargaining is therefore a problem of communicating information when the speaker benefits from distortion.

The author's synthesis is that negotiation should separate discovery from commitment. Exploratory channels, hypothetical packages, mediator shuttling, and confidential technical talks can test whether a range exists without immediately binding leaders to public positions.[4][5] Once a feasible zone is clearer, formal commitments, reciprocal sequencing, verification, and public explanation can stabilize it. Combining discovery and final commitment in one public ultimatum destroys information precisely when leaders most need it.

### Signals Are Credible When Their Costs and Meaning Are Diagnosable

A costly signal changes the receiver's inference because actors with low resolve should be less willing or able to send it. Mobilization, deployment, alliance consultation, sanctions, evacuation, legislative authorization, and accepting economic loss can serve this function.[1][3] Yet cost alone does not make a signal clear. A mobilization can indicate resolve, fear, preparation for attack, or a domestic political need. The receiver must infer both capability and intent, and its own response can change the meaning of the signal.

Threats and assurances solve different halves of the same problem. A threat specifies costs for noncompliance; an assurance specifies what restraint or benefit follows compliance. A compellent threat without assurance leaves the target unsure whether concession will end pressure. An assurance without a credible threat may not change incentives. Effective coercive diplomacy therefore defines the demanded behavior, the consequences of refusal, the benefits or restraint attached to compliance, and the mechanism for confirming that compliance occurred.[3]

Public signals can tie a leader's hands, but they can also tie the opponent's hands by raising the domestic price of retreat. Kurizaki demonstrates formally that private threats can avoid this reciprocal audience-cost problem and allow a target to comply without public humiliation.[4] Private channels are not automatically superior. They can be denied, misunderstood, manipulated, or disconnected from the agencies that must implement the bargain. A sound message architecture uses public communication for broad principles and reassurance while reserving private channels for conditional trades, clarification, and face-saving where secrecy does not conceal obligations from those who must carry them out.

Backchannels are valuable because they reduce performance before audiences and permit candid questions. They become dangerous when one small group promises terms that military commands, coalition partners, legislatures, or affected populations cannot deliver. The operational test is not secrecy itself but authorization: who speaks for each party, which commitments are exploratory, who must ratify them, and how will the confidential understanding become an executable order?

### Commitment Problems Concern Tomorrow's Incentives

An information problem asks what the other side can do or intends today. A commitment problem asks whether it will still honor the bargain after power, vulnerability, or incentives change. Powell shows that rapid shifts in relative power can make war rational even under complete information if the currently weaker side cannot trust the rising side to respect today's deal tomorrow.[2] First-strike advantages and issues that alter future bargaining power can create the same logic.[2]

A promise can be sincere and still not credible. A government may intend to protect a former rebel group but lose office; a coalition may be unable to discipline all commanders; a stronger state may later demand revision; a disarmed party may have no remedy if guarantees fail. Negotiators must therefore ask not only whether leaders want compliance but whether the post-agreement structure gives future actors reasons and capacities to comply.

Commitment devices change those future incentives. They include phased implementation, reciprocal steps, demilitarized zones, separation or redeployment of forces, third-party monitoring, peacekeeping, guarantees, escrowed benefits, joint commissions, automatic review, dispute-resolution procedures, and reversibility before irreversible disarmament.[7][9][13] None is universally sufficient. A weak monitor can record violations without deterring them, while an overbroad guarantee may not be politically credible. The mechanism must address the specific feared defection and identify who can act when it occurs.

### Deterrence, Compellence, and Bargaining Must Not Be Confused

Deterrence preserves a condition by threatening a response if the adversary changes it. Compellence seeks an observable change and normally requires initiative, a deadline, and a stopping rule.[3] This difference affects diplomacy. A deterrent message can remain conditional indefinitely; a compellent campaign creates continuing pressure and repeated decisions about whether to escalate, pause, or accept partial compliance.

Brute force seeks to seize or destroy the objective regardless of the adversary's decision. Coercion seeks to influence that decision.[3] Military action can contain both elements: a strike may degrade capability and communicate future risk. Analysts should ask what behavior the action seeks, how the target can demonstrate it, and what the coercer will do next. If no compliant path exists, calling the action coercive obscures that the strategy has become denial or destruction.

Promises and face-saving are not softness added to coercion. They are part of its causal logic. A target that expects unlimited demands after its first concession has little reason to comply. A leader who expects removal, prosecution, or political collapse after agreement may rationally prefer continued risk.[12] A bounded demand, reciprocal concession, confidential sequencing, or neutral formulation can lower the cost of compliance without changing the underlying distribution of material power.[4][12]

### Mediation Works Through Information, Formulation, Leverage, and Implementation

Facilitative mediation improves communication without imposing a detailed solution. It can arrange contact, clarify positions, reduce misperception, and carry messages that parties cannot exchange directly. Formulative mediation adds proposals, frameworks, sequencing, and drafting. Manipulative or directive mediation uses rewards, pressure, guarantees, or control over external support to change the bargain.[6]

These styles solve different problems. Facilitation is useful when a bargaining range probably exists but communication is dangerous or distrusted. Formulation helps when parties cannot convert general interests into compatible provisions. Leverage matters when one or more actors prefer continued conflict or seek to shift costs onto others. Implementation support matters when the signed terms create vulnerable transitions.[6][7][13]

Mediator bias has two dimensions: preferences and information. Kydd's model shows that a mediator believed to favor one party may credibly convey adverse information to that party, while a nominally neutral mediator that always recommends concession may be ignored.[5] Access to both sides, independent information, and the capacity to deliver or withhold benefits can matter more than ceremonial impartiality. However, bias that becomes unrestricted patronage can make the mediator unable to assure the opposing party or police violations by its client.

Coordination among mediators is itself a bargaining problem. Competing sponsors can offer parties alternative forums, shield clients from pressure, or issue incompatible guarantees. The UN guidance emphasizes coherence, coordination, consent, inclusion, and national ownership because a technically sound text cannot survive if external actors reward defection or excluded constituencies can veto implementation through violence.[13]

### Ripeness Is a Perceived Condition Plus a Feasible Way Out

A mutually hurting stalemate exists when relevant decision-makers believe continued conflict is costly and unilateral escalation will not deliver an acceptable victory.[11] Objective losses help shape that perception but do not determine it. Leaders can interpret the same battlefield as temporary adversity, a reason to mobilize, or evidence that compromise is necessary. Intelligence, ideology, domestic survival, and external promises affect the interpretation.

The second element is a perceived way out. Pain without a formula can increase desperation rather than negotiation. A mediator can help by developing principles, sequencing, interim arrangements, or reciprocal assurances that show how movement is possible.[11] Limited agreements can also create a way out incrementally: humanitarian access, prisoner exchanges, incident-prevention channels, local pauses, and protected infrastructure arrangements may test compliance without pretending to settle the whole conflict.[13]

Ripeness is not a license to wait passively. Diplomatic preparation can map actors, preserve channels, develop options, coordinate sponsors, and identify observable indicators before leaders request mediation. Nor is ripeness proof that a final agreement is available. The author's assessment is that it should be used as a diagnostic hypothesis -- which leaders feel which costs, what victory they still expect, and what formula they consider survivable -- rather than as a declaration that a conflict is "ready."

### Ceasefires Have Different Purposes and Therefore Need Different Designs

A cessation of hostilities may be deliberately lean: stop specified violence for a defined time or area, protect civilians, permit aid, or create space for talks. A preliminary ceasefire adds compliance mechanisms because the parties want negotiations to continue under lower violence. A definitive ceasefire implements a political settlement through force separation, demobilization, security-sector arrangements, territorial provisions, or other changes that end the status of war.[10][13]

The design should state geographic and temporal scope, covered actors, prohibited and permitted activities, effective time, command notification, contact points, humanitarian rules, monitoring access, reporting, investigation, dispute resolution, consequences, review, and termination or extension procedures.[13] Ambiguous language can facilitate initial signature but move the conflict into implementation. Excessive detail can also fail if parties lack command control, maps, communications, resources, or political consent.

Monitoring reduces uncertainty only when parties trust the process and information reaches decision-makers in time. Verification asks whether reported conduct occurred; attribution asks who controlled it; adjudication asks whether it violated the agreement; enforcement asks what follows. Combining these functions in one body can speed response but politicize fact-finding. Separating them can preserve neutrality but create dangerous delay. The design must specify the chain rather than merely promise "monitoring."

A violation does not automatically prove that the agreement failed or that a leader ordered renewed war. Local commanders, spoilers, accidents, unclear lines, displaced civilians, or communication failures can create incidents.[9][13] Joint commissions, hotlines, investigation timelines, and proportionate remedies prevent one disputed event from becoming a justification for general remobilization. They also make deliberate violations more visible.

### Spoilers and Fragmentation Change the Unit of Bargaining

Stedman defines spoilers as leaders or parties that believe an emerging peace threatens their power, worldview, or interests and use violence to undermine it.[8] Some are inside negotiations and conceal obstruction; others are excluded and attack the process. Their goals, constituencies, and tolerance for settlement differ, so inducement, socialization, coercion, or exclusion cannot be applied mechanically.[8] Critics of the typology argue that an actor's commitment to an accord shifts with its capabilities and opportunities, so the material power to coerce or co-opt matters more than a fixed diagnosis of spoiler type.[20]

Fragmented conflicts contain more than two reservation values. Political leaders may not control all armed units; external patrons may pay the costs of continued fighting; local groups may fear a national bargain; and actors that profit from war may prefer disorder. A settlement among central delegations can therefore leave an active bargaining table outside the room. Inclusion expands legitimacy and information, but unlimited participation can create veto points or reward violence with status.[8][13]

The practical task is actor mapping. Negotiators should distinguish signatories, commanders, constituencies, patrons, affected communities, potential veto players, and implementers. For each, they should identify interests, control, dependencies, internal divisions, and the behavior needed for the agreement to work. The author's synthesis is that "the parties" is often the most dangerous undefined term in a peace document.

### War Termination Requires Converging Expectations and Survivable Politics

Fighting reveals information, but learning can be slow and biased. Each side observes different battles, attributes losses to temporary causes, and may expect allies, mobilization, technology, or leadership change to improve its position.[1][12] War aims can also expand to justify sunk costs or contract when leaders become pessimistic. A bargaining range emerges only when expectations and demands overlap enough for an implementable settlement.

Domestic politics shape this process because leaders bear different costs for defeat, compromise, or continued war. Goemans argues that leaders in different regime structures face different postwar risks and that some may gamble for resurrection rather than accept a settlement that threatens their tenure or safety.[12] This does not mean regime type mechanically predicts termination. It means diplomatic proposals must account for who can authorize compromise, who can block it, and what political narrative or institutional process makes compliance survivable.

War termination is therefore broader than a ceasefire and narrower than reconciliation. It requires ending organized hostilities under terms the relevant actors can implement. Long-term justice, governance, reconstruction, and social repair may continue for decades. Loading all of those aims into the first cessation can prevent immediate relief; omitting them entirely can leave the incompatibility and commitment problem untouched. Sequencing must connect a bounded stop in violence to a credible political process without pretending that one document completes both tasks.[10][13]

## Evidence

### Formal Models Identify Mechanisms but Do Not Predict a Case by Themselves

Fearon's 1995 article uses bargaining models to show why anarchy, positive expected utility, or preventive motives alone do not solve the inefficiency puzzle. Its contribution is deductive: when issues are divisible and war is costly, leaders should prefer some bargain unless private information and misrepresentation, commitment problems, or genuine indivisibility block it.[1] The model clarifies what evidence a case study should seek -- hidden capabilities, incentives to bluff, future power shifts, or absent enforcement -- but it does not establish which mechanism caused a particular war.

Powell's 2006 formal analysis develops the commitment branch. It links preventive war, first-strike or offensive advantages, and disputes over objects that affect future bargaining power through a common mechanism: large, rapid changes can make the future stronger actor unable to commit to today's division.[2] The finding explains why transparent intentions do not always produce peace. Its scope is conditional; analysts must demonstrate a relevant shift and a bargain that future incentives would undermine rather than label every broken promise a commitment problem.

Kurizaki's 2007 crisis-bargaining model compares public and private threats when both leaders face domestic audiences. It finds that private threats can be as compelling in equilibrium because secrecy protects a target that capitulates and a sender that decides not to escalate.[4] The Alaska Boundary Dispute illustrates the logic in the article. This challenges the simple inference that public threats are always more credible, but it does not show that secrecy is universally stabilizing. Unauthorized, unverifiable, or leaked messages can create their own risks.

Kydd's 2003 cheap-talk model analyzes mediation under uncertainty about resolve. An unbiased mediator interested mainly in avoiding war may have an incentive to counsel restraint even when the warning is untrue; a mediator biased toward one side can be credible when advising that favored side to concede.[5] The result is theoretical and counterintuitive: neutrality of preference is not the same as credibility of information. In practice, a biased mediator still needs access, knowledge, and enough restraint to remain acceptable to the other side.

### Cross-Case Studies Show That Mediation Style and Enforcement Matter

Beardsley, Quinn, Biswas, and Wilkenfeld analyze mediation episodes in the International Crisis Behavior data for 1918 through 2001. They classify facilitation, formulation, and manipulation and evaluate formal agreement, post-crisis tension reduction, and contribution to crisis abatement.[6] Manipulation has the strongest association with formal agreement and crisis abatement, while facilitation has the greatest influence on lasting tension reduction.[6] The different outcomes matter: leverage may obtain a document quickly, whereas communication may better address information and relationships that affect later tension. The observational design cannot remove every selection problem because mediators and styles are not randomly assigned.

Walter examined 41 civil wars between 1940 and 1990 and found that negotiated settlements were much rarer than in interstate wars: 55 percent of interstate wars in the period were resolved at the bargaining table, against 20 percent of civil wars. In her cases, once adversaries agreed to negotiate, every treaty that an outside party stepped in to guarantee produced a successful settlement.[7] The proposed mechanism is vulnerability during disarmament and integration. The evidence established commitment and enforcement as central research questions, though later cases and scholarship show that guarantees vary in strength and that domestic institutions, monitoring, or other arrangements can sometimes substitute for coercive external enforcement.[10][17] The robust lesson is not "always intervene" but "identify who becomes vulnerable and what can protect compliance." Hartzell and Hoddie's survival analysis of 38 civil wars settled by negotiation between 1945 and 1998 points to one such domestic arrangement: the more dimensions of power sharing an agreement specified, the more likely peace was to endure.[17]

Fortna uses hazard analysis of ceasefires in interstate wars since 1946 to test whether ceasefire content affects the duration of peace while controlling for conditions such as decisive victory, war costs, and relative capabilities.[9] Stronger agreements are associated with more durable peace, especially provisions such as demilitarized zones, explicit third-party guarantees, peacekeeping, and joint dispute-resolution commissions.[9] This finding rejects the claim that agreements are only scraps of paper reflecting an already easy peace. It remains an association from observational data, but the controls and mechanism-specific provisions make agreement design a testable variable rather than ceremonial wording.

Werner and Yuen qualify the agreement-strength finding. They argue that enforcement provisions are often inadequate when a belligerent believes renewed war could yield better terms, and they find that "unnatural" ceasefires produced by third-party pressure are significantly more likely to fail, while ceasefires reached after wars with consistent battle outcomes last longer.[18] Noting that later studies disagreed on whether such pressure shortens peace or has no effect, Pechenkina coded outside pressure for ceasefires initiated between 1914 and 2001 and distinguished its cost: diplomatic pressure was associated with ceasefire breakdown, economic pressure had no long-run effect, and military pressure was associated with longer peace; matching on similar cases did not change these results.[19] The combined evidence does not show that outside pressure always helps or always harms.[18][19] The author's synthesis is that a pressured settlement is more likely to last when it reflects expected military outcomes or when the pressure keeps the cost of renewed war high.

Clayton and Sticher analyze written civil-war ceasefires from 1990 through 2019 using PA-X agreement provisions and georeferenced Uppsala conflict-event data. They estimate ceasefire survival across 82 conflict dyads with conflict deaths as the main indicator.[10] Definitive ceasefires containing demobilization and incompatibility provisions, followed by preliminary ceasefires with compliance mechanisms, are associated with longer suspensions of violence than lean cessations of hostilities.[10] Among compliance provisions, monitoring and verification appeared to be the main driver of longer suspensions in preliminary ceasefires.[10] Their interpretation is not that every ceasefire should be maximally detailed. Different bargaining problems motivate different designs, and the agreement's purpose must be considered when judging duration and success.

Stedman's comparative analysis of 1990s peace processes constructs a typology of limited, greedy, and total spoilers and distinguishes insiders from outsiders.[8] The cases show that misdiagnosis can make inducement reward escalating demands or make indiscriminate coercion fracture a viable coalition. He treats the findings as provisional rather than a universal algorithm.[8] Greenhill and Major re-examined three of his cases -- Angola, Mozambique, and Cambodia -- and argued that changes in actors' willingness to comply tracked shifts in capabilities and opportunities more closely than fixed spoiler types.[20] The evidence supports actor-specific strategies and coordinated international custodianship, not the loose practice of calling every critic or violator a spoiler.[8][20]

### Contemporary Conflicts Illustrate the Mechanisms Without Proving Them

Current diplomacy shows why ceasefire labels require mechanism-level analysis. In February 2026 the UN Secretary-General called for an immediate, full, and unconditional ceasefire in Ukraine while also stating that peace must conform to the UN Charter and Ukraine's sovereignty, independence, and territorial integrity within its internationally recognized borders.[14] This official position distinguishes an urgent suspension of violence from the disputed political and territorial terms of final settlement. By October 2026, US-mediated talks had stalled: the US Secretary of State was reported as saying that a negotiated settlement was out of reach because Russia insisted on full control of Donetsk Oblast without fighting, while Ukraine called for a ceasefire along the current front line.[21] The author's assessment is that the case displays both a bargaining-range problem and a commitment problem, but public statements alone cannot establish which mechanism dominates private decision-making.

A September 2025 joint statement by Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States called for a three-month humanitarian truce in Sudan leading toward a permanent ceasefire and an inclusive transition, while arguing that external military support intensifies and prolongs the conflict.[15] The sequence illustrates the difference between immediate humanitarian purpose, permanent security arrangements, and political transition. It also demonstrates a coordination challenge: external actors that can mediate may also affect belligerents' expectations of future resources. The statement is a diplomatic position, not evidence that its timetable or causal diagnosis was accepted by all Sudanese actors. A year later the sequence remained unimplemented. Reuters reported in September 2026 that the army-led government had not accepted a US proposal for a 90-day ceasefire followed by negotiations, and that its chief requested change was an internationally monitored withdrawal of the Rapid Support Forces from urban areas; UN reporting in October 2026 described diplomacy as stuck on that proposal.[22][23] The author's assessment is that a dispute over who withdraws, from where, and under whose monitoring is a commitment problem about the vulnerable transition, not only a disagreement over whether to pause the fighting.

In April 2026 UN reporting described the Gaza ceasefire as increasingly fragile amid continuing humanitarian and political disputes.[16] At a Security Council meeting in September 2026, UN reporting stated that the plan's later phases, including Hamas' disarmament and Israel's full withdrawal, had yet to begin, and that 1,394 Palestinians had been killed since the October 2025 ceasefire, according to UNRWA citing local health authorities.[24] The bounded lesson is not a judgment on the parties' legal claims or preferred final status. It is that a ceasefire's durability depends on implementation, access, verification, reciprocal obligations, and a political process capable of addressing incompatible expectations.[13][16] Calling a pause either "peace" or "failure" without specifying its purpose obscures what it achieved and which mechanism is breaking down.

The strongest empirical conclusion across these literatures is conditional. Mediation can improve outcomes, but style affects which outcome; guarantees can solve vulnerability, but only if credible; detailed provisions can prolong peace, but design must match purpose; and ripeness can open negotiations, but it cannot implement them.[6][7][9][10][11] Evidence does not support a single universal sequence from pressure to talks to agreement. It supports diagnosing the bargaining failure and selecting instruments that address it.

## Implications

### For Political Leaders: Define the Bargain and the Worst Failure

Leaders should begin crisis planning with four separate questions. What behavior must change or remain unchanged? What outcome does each side expect from continued conflict? What concession or assurance could make compliance preferable? What future change could cause either side to defect?[1][2][3] Mixing these questions produces vague demands, open-ended threats, and settlements that solve today's confrontation while creating tomorrow's vulnerability.

The worst plausible diplomatic failure is an irreversible escalatory step taken because each side misread the other's signal while no authorized channel could clarify it. Prevention requires protected communications, explicit decision authority, redundant contact methods, and pre-agreed procedures for incidents. Military readiness and diplomacy should be connected: deployments need an explanation of purpose, and messages need forces consistent with them. Contradictory signals from leaders, commands, allies, or unofficial intermediaries increase uncertainty rather than strategic flexibility.

Demands should include a stopping rule. The target needs to know what counts as compliance, how it will be verified, what pressure will cease, and what commitments remain. Partial compliance should have a defined treatment rather than forcing a choice between declaring victory and escalating. Reciprocal steps can preserve reversibility until trust and verification improve. The author's assessment is that ambiguity is useful only when it preserves options without obscuring the behavior needed to avoid war.

Public rhetoric should leave room for implementation. Maximal language can mobilize support and signal resolve, but it can also raise the domestic cost of accepting a feasible bargain. Backchannels can explore trade-offs and face-saving formulations, yet final terms must be communicated to those who command forces, allocate resources, or ratify the agreement.[4] A secret understanding that cannot become an executable order is not an off-ramp.

### For Mediators: Match the Intervention to the Bargaining Failure

When the central problem is private information, mediators should obtain independent assessments, ask each side to specify contingencies, and carry messages in forms that reveal confidence and conditions. When the problem is commitment, more dialogue is insufficient; the process needs guarantees, sequencing, monitoring, or institutions that alter future incentives.[5][7] When leaders still expect military improvement, a mediator may need to clarify costs, coordinate external patrons, or preserve a channel rather than force a premature comprehensive package.[11]

Mediator mandates should distinguish facilitation, formulation, leverage, and implementation support. A process that advertises neutral facilitation but secretly applies one-sided pressure will lose informational credibility. A process asked to enforce a settlement without resources or political backing will create false assurance. Beardsley and colleagues' findings imply that styles can be combined, but the trade-off between a rapid formal agreement and durable tension reduction should be explicit.[6] Pressure applied to obtain a signature also needs a theory of what keeps the settlement acceptable once the pressure eases, because Werner and Yuen found ceasefires produced by third-party pressure more likely to fail, while Pechenkina found diplomatic pressure associated with ceasefire breakdown and military pressure associated with longer peace.[18][19]

Bias should be managed rather than denied. A mediator with influence over one party can be valuable precisely because it can credibly deliver that party, but the other side needs evidence that the mediator will transmit information accurately and support reciprocal compliance.[5] Co-mediation can pair access and balance, provided the mediators share objectives, information rules, and a division of labor. Uncoordinated forums allow parties to shop for better terms and external patrons to shift blame.

Inclusion must be tied to implementation. Women, affected communities, local authorities, civil society, displaced people, and minority groups can identify security and access problems invisible to armed delegations; commanders and external patrons may control whether violence stops.[13] Not every actor requires an identical seat at every stage. The process should state who participates, who is consulted, who validates technical terms, and who monitors implementation. This is process design, not symbolic representation.

### For Ceasefire Designers: Write an Operating System, Not a Slogan

A ceasefire text should define the purpose before its provisions. A short humanitarian pause requires precise timing, routes, protected activities, liaison, and restart or extension rules. A preliminary ceasefire supporting talks needs monitoring, incident management, and protection against using the pause for military advantage. A definitive ceasefire requires detailed security arrangements, sequencing, demobilization or force reconfiguration, guarantees, and links to the political settlement.[10][13]

Every obligation should answer six questions: who acts, what behavior is required, where it applies, when it begins, how it is observed, and what happens after compliance or violation. Maps, coordinates, command orders, language versions, communications, and resources are part of the agreement. A provision that cannot be observed or implemented by the relevant unit is aspirational rather than operational.

Monitoring bodies need access, safety, technical capacity, reporting rules, and a route from fact-finding to decision. Reports should separate observation, attribution, legal or political judgment, and remedy. A hotline can stop an accidental encounter while a joint commission investigates; neither replaces consequences for deliberate repeated violations. Fortna's findings support demilitarized zones, guarantees, peacekeeping, and joint commissions because they alter incentives, uncertainty, and accident management, not because more clauses are inherently better.[9]

Implementation should protect the most vulnerable transition. If one side disarms first, who protects its members and constituencies? If forces withdraw, who controls the vacated area? If aid or commerce resumes, who verifies access? If sanctions are lifted, can they be restored? If elections occur, what prevents a loser from remobilizing? Phasing and reciprocal verification preserve leverage until irreversible steps become safer.[7][13]

### For Alliances and External Patrons: Control the Principal-Agent Problem

External support can deter aggression and make a client secure enough to negotiate, but it can also raise expectations of battlefield improvement, shield obstruction, or make guarantees unbelievable to the opponent.[2][15] Sponsors should connect assistance to defined political objectives, consultation duties, civilian control, compliance with agreements, and reporting on incidents. Open-ended support without an agreed theory of termination can narrow the bargaining range by lowering a client's perceived cost of continued war.

Allies should pre-negotiate the process for limited crises: consultation triggers, evidence standards, authorized messages, economic and military options, and off-ramps. The existing alliance topic explains how credibility depends on interests, capability, resolve, and institutions. Crisis bargaining adds a further requirement: commitments must deter the covered action without convincing either the client or the adversary that escalation is automatic under every ambiguous incident.

Patrons that become mediators must disclose their role and demonstrate what they can deliver. Influence over a client can make recommendations credible, as Kydd's logic suggests, but coercive mediation may secure formal talks or an agreement without producing durable consent.[5][6] The test is whether pressure changes only the signature decision or also the incentives and security conditions of implementation.

### For Analysts: Diagnose Mechanisms and Denominators

Analysts should not infer resolve from rhetoric, success from signature, or failure from every violation. They should track offers, conditions, capabilities, battlefield expectations, domestic constraints, command control, external support, and implementation behavior over time.[1][10][12] A rejected offer may fall outside the other side's expected bargaining range, threaten future vulnerability, or be politically undeliverable. These explanations imply different remedies.

Ceasefire assessment needs a stated denominator. Was the objective a six-hour evacuation window, a reduction in violence, support for negotiations, or permanent termination? How many covered actors and locations complied, over what period, and compared with what baseline?[10][13] A limited pause can be successful by its own purpose and inadequate as a peace settlement. A durable frozen line can suppress fighting while leaving coercion, displacement, or unresolved claims. Measures should include civilian protection, humanitarian access, battle deaths, geographic coverage, violations, investigation speed, negotiation progress, and implementation of political terms.

Claims about ripeness should name the evidence. Which decision-makers perceive a stalemate? What costs do they consider decisive? Which escalation options do they still expect to work? What way out can each explain to its coalition?[11] Repeatedly declaring a conflict ripe because it is destructive substitutes an observer's judgment for the parties' incentives.

Contemporary cases should be used as tests of general mechanisms, not as shortcuts to policy advocacy. Ukraine, Sudan, and Gaza differ in actors, legal context, territorial control, external involvement, command structure, and proposed settlement. The shared analytical questions concern information, credible commitments, coercive leverage, mediator coordination, and agreement design.[13][14][15][16] Similar vocabulary does not establish similar causes or justify identical remedies.

### A Practical Diagnostic

A disciplined crisis-bargaining assessment can proceed in ten steps. First, define the disputed behavior and current status quo. Second, identify all actors able to authorize, implement, or spoil an agreement. Third, estimate each actor's expected outcome and cost of continued conflict. Fourth, identify private information and incentives to misrepresent. Fifth, identify future power shifts and vulnerable transitions. Sixth, classify the instrument as deterrence, compellence, persuasion, denial, or brute force. Seventh, design both threat and assurance, including a stopping rule. Eighth, select channels and mediator roles that can carry credible information and leverage. Ninth, match ceasefire provisions and guarantees to the specific failure modes. Tenth, define observable indicators for compliance, violation, review, and termination.[1][2][3][5][9][13]

The author's final assessment is that diplomacy succeeds when it makes a safer choice both visible and durable. Signaling without an off-ramp can create a contest of resolve; an off-ramp without enforcement can postpone conflict; enforcement without legitimate consent can impose a brittle pause. Effective crisis bargaining aligns information, incentives, implementation, and political survival closely enough that restraint remains preferable after the immediate pressure recedes.
