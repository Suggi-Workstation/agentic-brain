# Undersea Cables Convert Connectivity into Strategic Dependence, but Resilience Limits Coercion

Undersea telecommunications and power cables turn seabed routes, landing sites, repair capacity, and private operating decisions into instruments of international dependence.[1][3][4] The author's synthesis is that their geopolitical importance does not mean every fault is sabotage or every diversified network is coercible: leverage depends on concentrated failure domains, slow restoration, limited substitutes, credible attribution, and the target's ability to coordinate public and private response.[1][2][3][4]

## Background

Submarine telecommunications cables carry approximately 99 percent of intercontinental or transoceanic digital communications, including ordinary internet traffic, voice, government communications, and financial transactions.[1][3] The Congressional Research Service reported in 2023 that more than 500 commercial systems formed this global network and that private companies or multinational consortia owned and operated most of them.[3] The physical infrastructure is therefore global in function but fragmented in ownership, jurisdiction, and responsibility. A route can cross international waters and several exclusive economic zones before reaching a privately operated landing station whose terrestrial power, network-management equipment, and onward connections are governed under national rules.[3]

The strategic object is not the glass fiber alone. A working telecommunications system includes the wet cable and repeaters, branching units, landing stations, power-feeding and transmission equipment, network-management systems, terrestrial backhaul, spare cable, repair depots, specialized crews, and vessels able to recover and splice a damaged segment.[1][2][3][7] The 2024 New York Joint Statement expressly defined undersea cable infrastructure to include construction, operation, surveillance, maintenance, software, terrestrial components, repair centers, and deployment and repair vessels.[7] This end-to-end definition matters because an adversary, natural hazard, or administrative failure does not need to sever a deep-ocean span if a clustered landing point, power supply, management system, permit process, or repair resource is the weaker dependency.[1][2]

Submarine power cables create a related but different form of dependence. Electricity interconnectors link national markets, contribute to security of supply, and bring offshore generation to shore; unlike packet traffic, electric power cannot simply be routed through any telecommunications path.[4] The author's synthesis is that continuity must be evaluated differently: a power-cable outage is managed within the connected electricity system, whereas telecommunications traffic may be rerouted over surviving communications paths.[3][4] Both systems can be geographically concentrated, share landing corridors, depend on specialized vessels, and become politically salient during a crisis, but their service physics and restoration requirements are not interchangeable.[1][4]

Cable faults are routine enough that damage alone is weak evidence of hostile intent. ENISA reported roughly 150 to 200 accidental faults annually and identified fishing and shipping activity as the dominant causes, with natural hazards such as earthquakes also producing breaks.[2] The ITU-ICPC International Advisory Body reported in 2026 that submarine telecommunications cables experience approximately 150 to 200 faults annually and that more than 80 percent are attributable to human maritime activity, principally fishing and dragged anchors.[1] Different datasets and classifications produce different shares, but both sources establish a large accidental baseline. A responsible geopolitical assessment must therefore separate observed damage, physical cause, vessel involvement, intent, sponsorship, and legal responsibility rather than move directly from outage to sabotage.[1][2]

Security attention nevertheless increased because dependence has grown while several failures can share one practical domain. The ITU-ICPC report identified geographical concentration, limited redundancy in vulnerable states, long repair timelines, and scarce repair vessels as structural concerns.[1] European institutions responded with an all-hazards policy cycle covering prevention, detection, response and repair, and deterrence; the EU framework covers telecommunications cables and power interconnectors and includes risk mapping, stress testing, monitoring, repair capacity, sanctions, and external cable diplomacy.[4][5] NATO separately created coordination mechanisms with governments and industry and launched Baltic Sentry in January 2025 after damage to energy and communication cables in the Baltic Sea.[6]

The intellectual shift is from treating cables only as engineering assets to treating them as security-governance systems. Bueger and Liebetrau's review argues that cable protection spans maritime security, infrastructure governance, technical resilience, and regulation rather than fitting inside one established policy field.[10] A European Parliament study by Bueger, Liebetrau, and Franken similarly found that responsibilities cross maritime law enforcement, military protection, cyber security, digital policy, and private operation.[16] This fragmentation creates a coordination problem: those who own operational data, those who issue permits, those who monitor ships, those who investigate crime, and those who can deploy security forces are often different organizations.[1][3][16]

The author's synthesis is that undersea cables create power through controlled dependence, not through physical vulnerability alone. A state, firm, or coalition gains leverage when it can influence a route, landing permission, supplier, repair access, traffic handling, or evidentiary process that another actor cannot replace within the relevant time. Conversely, route diversity, spare capacity, independent landings, transparent ownership, available repair assets, trusted information sharing, and credible enforcement reduce the expected benefit of coercion.[1][5][7] This framing keeps the topic inside geopolitics: engineering defines the constraints, but the central question is how states and private actors convert those constraints into bargaining power, resilience, deterrence, or strategic alignment.

## Core Concepts

### The Strategic Unit Is an End-to-End Service Path

A cable map can mislead because a colored line does not reveal the full service path. The wet plant must connect through a landing station to domestic or regional networks; it also needs power, management systems, physical access, compatible spares, and a maintenance arrangement.[2][3][7] A country shown with several international cables may still depend on one beach corridor, one landing facility, one terrestrial backhaul route, one power substation, or one operator. Conversely, two cables in the same general sea can provide meaningful resilience if their seabed routes, landings, terrestrial paths, suppliers, and repair arrangements do not share the same failure mode.[1][3]

This distinction separates nominal redundancy from independent redundancy. Counting systems answers how many assets exist. Resilience asks whether failure of one hazard, decision, supplier, or site disables several assets together. The ITU-ICPC report treats geographical diversity as a reduction in reliance on heavily trafficked corridors and chokepoints, while the EU Expert Group combines mapping with risk scenarios and stress tests to identify dependencies that a simple inventory misses.[1][5] The author's synthesis is that every resilience claim should name the hazard against which the routes are independent: an anchor, landslide, port closure, landing-station outage, cyber compromise, hostile state decision, or regional shortage of repair vessels.

Telecommunications networks add a second distinction between traffic continuity and physical restoration. Routing and spare optical capacity may move traffic around one fault, so customers can retain service even while the damaged cable remains on the seabed.[3] That successful rerouting does not prove that the physical network is secure. Surviving paths may have higher latency, less spare capacity, different jurisdictional exposure, or another shared chokepoint. A second fault during the repair window can therefore be more consequential than the first. The author's assessment is that restoration time is part of deterrence because a short vulnerability window reduces the opportunity for sequential disruption, whereas a long queue permits repeated faults to accumulate.[1][13]

Power interconnectors require a different continuity test. An outage changes cross-border transfer capacity and may force generation, reserve, or market adjustments elsewhere in the grid.[4] The strategic significance depends on season, load, alternate interconnectors, domestic generation, and repair duration, not merely on the rated capacity of the failed cable. The shared geopolitical principle is portfolio resilience: no one route should become an unpriced assumption behind essential communications or energy security.[4][5]

### Geography Creates Chokepoints, but Networks Determine Their Severity

Seabed geography, shipping density, coastlines, and permissible landing sites concentrate cables into corridors. The ITU-ICPC report warns that a substantial share of global traffic passes through a limited number of maritime chokepoints and major cable corridors, creating potential for disruption across several regions.[1] Concentration can arise offshore where routes converge, near shore where cables enter shallow water, or on land where diverse systems join one backhaul or data-center cluster.[1][3] The consequence of a cut therefore depends on network architecture and available substitutes, not on geographical drama alone.

A telecommunications chokepoint differs from a canal. A blocked canal compels ships to wait or sail a longer route; telecommunications traffic may be rerouted over compatible capacity without waiting for physical cable repair.[1][3] Yet the analogy becomes stronger when several nominal routes share a landing corridor, when alternate paths are already heavily loaded, or when a state or small island has only one or two systems.[1][3][9] The Tonga case shows the extreme: damage to the 827-kilometer cable connecting Tonga and Fiji isolated international telecommunications, while volcanic ash also constrained satellite-phone use.[9] The case demonstrates that satellite phones can provide only partial continuity: ash affected their use, reports said only dial-out calls were possible, and high costs restricted access to government officials and some businesses.[9]

A chokepoint can also be administrative. Repairs may require maritime, environmental, customs, immigration, security, or cabotage approvals across several agencies.[1] The ITU-ICPC report cites Singapore's reduction of repair approval from about one week to three to five working days and reports an average of approximately eight days to commence repair in the United Kingdom compared with a global average of 50 days.[1] These figures are jurisdiction-specific examples rather than universal service guarantees. They show that state procedure can determine outage duration even when the vessel, cable, and technical repair method are available.

### Ownership Is a Bundle of Rights and Capabilities

Ownership should not be treated as a binary label. Relevant roles include equity ownership in the system, ownership of capacity, operation of the wet plant, ownership of a landing station, control of network management, equipment supply, maintenance contracting, financing, and national authority over landing and repair.[1][3][7] Consortium and single-owner systems allocate cost, capacity, and operating rights differently.[3][12] The author's assessment is that either structure can create resilience or dependency depending on who controls capacity, landings, management systems, maintenance, and operational data. A coastal state may own none of the cable yet control permits, territorial access, or the landing license.[1][3]

The political economy has changed over time. Lai, Flensburg, and Sick analyzed 804 global cable projects dated from 1989 through 2028 and report a shift from national and regional telecommunications companies operating through large consortia toward concentration around global platform and data businesses building networks for their own services.[12] This is evidence about project ownership, not proof that any owner automatically controls all traffic or can unilaterally interrupt service. The author's synthesis is that control must be traced function by function: who can authorize capacity allocation, inspect equipment, change software, grant landing access, dispatch a repair vessel, or disclose operational data?

Private ownership produces both resilience and governance gaps. Operators have commercial reasons to protect assets, reroute traffic, and restore service, and they hold telemetry that governments may lack.[3] Governments hold law-enforcement, intelligence, diplomatic, military, and permitting powers that firms do not.[1][6] The problem is not that one side should replace the other. It is that incident response fails when responsibilities, information-sharing rules, evidentiary standards, and emergency authorities are improvised after a fault.[1][5][7]

### Surveillance and Cyber-Physical Risk Extend Beyond the Seabed

Cable security includes confidentiality and control as well as physical availability. Landing stations contain transmission, reception, power, and network-management equipment, making them interfaces between an international physical system and domestic networks.[2][3] ENISA identifies landing stations and network-management systems as potential targets for physical intrusion, power interruption, espionage, or cyber compromise; access to management systems may reveal network structure or enable traffic disruption and diversion.[2] The New York Joint Statement accordingly calls for secure and verifiable providers, transparent ownership, cyber security, route diversity, and protection against compromise of communications and data.[7]

These risks require calibrated language. Physical proximity to a cable does not prove interception, and foreign participation in construction does not by itself prove a backdoor. A credible assessment identifies the access path, affected equipment, technical capability, governance rights, and evidence supporting the allegation.[7][16] The author's synthesis is that supplier risk is best managed through verifiable controls - architecture review, access logging, separation of duties, component assurance, encryption, and incident evidence - rather than nationality alone. Geopolitical exposure remains relevant because national law, state-firm relationships, and crisis alignment can affect access and cooperation, but those factors should guide verification rather than substitute for it.[7][11]

Cyber and physical attack paths can interact. An actor may damage a wet cable, interrupt power at a landing station, compromise network management, corrupt operational information, or obstruct repair logistics.[2][5] A coordinated campaign could combine limited physical incidents with disinformation about responsibility or network condition. This is why cable protection belongs partly in cyber policy even when the initiating event is an anchor on the seabed: operators and authorities still depend on trusted telemetry, communications, and control systems to detect, classify, reroute, investigate, and restore.[2][5]

### Attribution Is a Layered Judgment

An outage establishes loss of service, not intent. ENISA distinguishes physical fault mechanisms and notes that many causes remain unknown, while the Finnish police statement shows that a vessel's involvement can remain under investigation after a rupture.[2][14] The author's analytical framework separates technical diagnosis, physical causation, vessel involvement, intent, sponsorship, and legal responsibility; each proposition can carry a different confidence level and can change with later evidence.

The large accidental baseline raises the standard for claiming sabotage. Fishing and anchoring account for most known faults, while natural hazards and equipment failures produce additional events.[1][2][3] Deliberate damage can use ordinary maritime tools and can therefore resemble negligence. The author's assessment is that ambiguity can create gray-zone value by imposing costs while leaving allies divided over whether the evidence supports criminal enforcement, sanctions, collective defense, or no public attribution.[4][6]

Deniability and uncertainty are not identical. Deniability is an actor's ability to contest responsibility; uncertainty is the analyst's actual evidentiary condition. A vessel may be physically associated with a cut while intent or state direction remains unresolved. The author's assessment is that policy should map responses to evidence tiers. Immediate actions such as rerouting, inspection, protection of nearby assets, and preservation of evidence do not require final attribution. Punitive measures require a stronger, disclosed basis proportionate to their consequences.[2][4][14]

### Repair Capacity Is Strategic Capacity

Repair converts resilience from a design promise into restored service. A repair operation must locate the fault, obtain permits, mobilize a vessel, load compatible cable and jointing equipment, reach the site, recover the cable, splice and test it, and return the system to service.[1][2] Weather, distance, water depth, disputed jurisdiction, customs rules, crew access, and simultaneous faults can all extend the interval.[1] A country with diverse routes but no practical path to repair can remain exposed after redundancy is consumed.

The 2026 ITU-ICPC report estimated that about 70 vessels had technical cable-repair capability in 2025, but only about 20 to 25 were dedicated to repair under active maintenance agreements or long-term charters; not all were continuously ready, and many vessels were aging.[1] ENISA's earlier industry survey listed approximately 35 installation vessels and 30 maintenance vessels, using a different classification.[2] The apparent difference is not necessarily a contradiction: vessel counts vary with whether a ship is technically capable, contractually committed, active, or immediately deployable. The policy variable is available capacity in the relevant region and time, not a global headline count.[1][2]

Maintenance agreements allocate scarce attention before a crisis. Contracts can specify priority among systems, base ports, spares, and mobilization; a ship engaged in another operation may not be immediately available.[2] The ITU-ICPC report describes cross-zone sharing of cable ships, spare cable segments, and jointing materials and recommends regional spare pools and shared vessels, especially where repair resources are limited.[1] The author's synthesis is that repair access resembles strategic lift: it is rarely visible when capacity is ample, but becomes decisive when multiple actors need the same specialized asset at once.

Insurance and financing transmit physical and political risk into project and repair decisions. CSIS reports that insurance for cable-repair ships can become difficult to obtain in conflict zones and recommends strategic financing for otherwise noncommercial but geopolitically important projects, stronger landing-site standards, information sharing, and pooled resources for repair and monitoring.[13] The author's assessment is that public subsidy is justified most clearly where a route, landing, depot, or standby vessel produces regional redundancy whose benefits cannot be fully captured by one owner.

### Cable Diplomacy Shapes the Network Before a Crisis

Cable diplomacy is competition and cooperation over which routes are financed, which suppliers are trusted, where systems land, and which standards govern their operation and repair. The 2024 New York Joint Statement linked secure providers, transparent ownership, route diversity, permitting, and government-industry coordination as elements of a shared approach.[7] Australia created a Cable Connectivity and Resilience Centre as part of the Quad partnership to provide technical assistance, research, and policy support across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific.[8] These measures treat institutional capacity as infrastructure: a new line is less resilient if the recipient lacks workable licensing, incident coordination, or repair access.

Route projects can also alter alignment. The United States described the Humboldt cable between Chile and Australia via French Polynesia as a trusted, multi-stakeholder route intended to add resilience and connect South America directly to the Indo-Pacific.[15] EUISS describes Indo-Pacific competition over suppliers, permits, route choices, and repair capability, while warning that several allegations remain uncertain and that regional coordination is fragmented.[11] The author's synthesis is that a cable project simultaneously allocates capacity, contracts, technical access, landing relationships, and long-lived political dependence. The geopolitical question is therefore not only who paid for the cable, but which relationships become difficult to replace after it is built.[7][8][11][15]

## Evidence

### Fault Data Show Routine Damage and Concentrated Consequences

ENISA's 2023 report used the International Cable Protection Committee's fault and repair data, including maintenance-zone datasets covering approximately nine to fourteen years.[2] It reported 185 repairs in 2021 across 44 coastal jurisdictions and concluded that accidental damage from fishing and shipping was the dominant observed problem.[2] The method is operationally useful because maintenance organizations record faults that require repair, but the categories do not eliminate uncertainty: cause may remain unknown, reporting practices can differ, and a repair count is not a measure of service impact. One fault on a well-meshed route and one fault isolating an island enter the count as events even though their social consequences differ.[2]

The 2026 ITU-ICPC report describes three working groups on repair, risk, and geographic diversity that brought together more than 175 expert participants nominated by governments, industry, academia, and international organizations.[1] Its synthesis reports approximately 150 to 200 annual faults, identifies maritime activity as the dominant source, and finds that route concentration, permitting delay, vessel scarcity, and unequal redundancy make consequences highly uneven.[1] This is a multi-stakeholder expert and operational review rather than a controlled causal study. Its strength is system knowledge across jurisdictions and maintenance practice; its limitation is that participating institutions can have different incentives and definitions. The findings are strongest as a description of failure mechanisms and policy bottlenecks, not as proof that one reform produces a specified reduction in outages.[1]

The two sources agree on the central empirical pattern: ordinary maritime activity creates most faults, but structural concentration and restoration capacity determine whether routine damage becomes a strategic crisis.[1][2] This evidence contradicts both sensational and complacent accounts. Sabotage is not required for serious disruption, and a high global repair rate does not prove that every country is resilient.

### The EU Converted Mapping into Scenario-Based Security Policy

The EU Submarine Cable Infrastructure Expert Group combined a map of existing and planned data cables with a coordinated risk assessment and stress-test guidance.[5] The 2025 report identified seven main scenarios built from threats, vulnerabilities, and dependencies; in related announcements that day, the Commission announced funding opportunities for regional cable hubs intended to aggregate data and improve near-real-time situational awareness.[5] The method moves beyond asset counting by asking how a scenario propagates through infrastructure and institutions. It also creates a repeatable basis for testing response, although public summaries do not disclose every sensitive route or vulnerability.[5]

The wider EU Action Plan applies four linked functions: prevention, detection, response and recovery, and deterrence.[4] Its measures include stronger risk requirements, diversified and smart cables, sea-basin monitoring, crisis coordination, an EU cable-vessel reserve, sanctions, and external cable diplomacy.[4] The inclusion of both telecommunications and electricity cables is evidence that European policy treats connectivity and energy interdependence as one critical-undersea-infrastructure problem while retaining sector-specific operating regimes.[4]

This policy architecture also exposes a potential error. A monitoring program can detect anomalous ship behavior without proving intent; a sanctions instrument can raise costs only if attribution and jurisdiction are adequate; a vessel reserve shortens restoration only if compatible spares, permits, crews, and contracts are ready.[1][4] The author's assessment is that the value of the EU model lies in linking the whole cycle, not in any single surveillance technology or legal tool.

### Tonga Demonstrates the Difference Between Redundancy on a Map and Available Substitution

The World Bank's Global Rapid Post-Disaster Damage Estimation for the January 2022 Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai eruption combined remote sensing, available government information, sector data, and modeled estimates while explicitly reporting uncertainty caused by disrupted communications and limited ground assessment.[9] The report states that damage to the 827-kilometer cable connecting Tonga with Fiji isolated Tonga's international telecommunications and that repair was expected to take more than four weeks; volcanic ash also constrained satellite-phone use.[9]

This case was a natural disaster, not coercion, which makes it especially useful. It shows the consequence of dependence without requiring assumptions about hostile intent. The cable failure impaired not only users but also the information needed to assess the wider disaster.[9] The author's synthesis is that connectivity is part of response capacity: when communications fail during a physical emergency, uncertainty about damage, logistics, and need rises at the same time that coordination becomes harder.

The case also limits exaggerated conclusions. Tonga's outage does not establish that every island or coastal state can be isolated by one cut; topology and alternate capacity vary. It does establish that states with few international paths face a different risk class from large hubs with many landings.[1][9] Financing a second genuinely independent route, compatible emergency satellite service, pre-arranged repair, and resilient domestic backhaul addresses that risk more directly than generic naval patrols alone.[1][8][9]

### Baltic Incidents Show How Damage Becomes an Alliance Problem Before Attribution Is Final

On December 26, 2024, Finnish police announced an investigation into the rupture of the Estlink 2 power cable in Finland's exclusive economic zone and examined possible involvement of the tanker Eagle S; the suspected offense at that stage was aggravated criminal mischief.[14] That statement established an investigation and a suspected vessel, not a final finding of intent or state sponsorship.[14] The distinction is essential because policy and media narratives can harden before forensic and legal processes are complete.

NATO launched Baltic Sentry on January 14, 2025, using frigates, maritime patrol aircraft, naval drones, and integrated national surveillance to improve protection and response for critical undersea infrastructure.[6] NATO also used its Critical Undersea Infrastructure Network to engage industry.[6] The response illustrates a collective-action mechanism: an alliance can raise monitoring, readiness, and expected enforcement costs while a criminal investigation continues. It does not demonstrate that permanent military presence can physically guard every cable or prevent ordinary anchoring accidents.[6][14]

The author's assessment is that Baltic Sentry's strongest contribution is coordination under uncertainty. It connects maritime awareness, national authorities, alliance assets, and private infrastructure owners before a case reaches final attribution.[6] Its worst failure mode would be to treat ambiguous evidence as automatic proof of hostile sponsorship, creating escalation from a negligent event. The opposite failure would be to wait for courtroom-level certainty before protecting adjacent infrastructure or preserving evidence. A tiered response avoids both errors.[6][14]

### Matsu Shows the Evidentiary and Resilience Problems in a Contested Region

An EUISS brief reports that two cables serving Taiwan's Matsu Islands were cut in February 2023 by a fishing vessel and a cargo ship and that the islands experienced limited internet access for 50 days.[11] The same source states that deliberate action remained unclear.[11] The case is secondary evidence rather than a completed attribution record, so it supports conclusions about outage duration and strategic concern more strongly than claims about motive.

Matsu matters because geography, sparse routes, heavy maritime activity, and cross-strait tension coexist. The author's assessment is that unresolved damage in this setting can produce gray-zone signaling or public-intimidation effects even when intent is not established.[11] The author's synthesis is that resilience policy should not wait for certainty about a past motive. A second route, protected landings, backup capacity, repair access, and public communication reduce harm under accidental, criminal, and state-directed scenarios alike.[1][8][11]

### Ownership and Corridor Studies Show That Network Design Is Political

Lai, Flensburg, and Sick's study of 804 cable projects from 1989 through 2028 identifies phases of ownership change, from telecommunications consortia toward a smaller set of global platform and data firms building networks around proprietary services.[12] The study's unit is the cable project, so it does not by itself measure traffic control, state access, or outage risk. It does show that the institutions deciding where new capacity is built have changed, which affects bargaining over routes, landing sites, and interoperability.[12]

Government programs confirm that route selection is an instrument of external policy. The Quad-backed Australian centre provides regulatory and technical assistance across the Indo-Pacific, while the Humboldt announcement linked public diplomacy, private investment, route diversity, and trusted infrastructure.[8][15] EUISS describes parallel competition over suppliers and corridors in the South China Sea and Pacific, including projects intended to bypass contested or concentrated routes.[11] These sources contain policy preferences as well as evidence; terms such as "trusted" express a government's security judgment, not a neutral technical certification.[7][11][15]

Taken together, the evidence supports a bounded conclusion. Cable geopolitics is real because states influence finance, permits, suppliers, monitoring, repair, and alliance response, while firms control much of the infrastructure and operational knowledge.[1][3][7][12] It is not a theory that every outage is strategic action. The strongest analysis begins with the service path and observed failure, then adds ownership, dependence, evidence, and political response without collapsing those layers.[1][2][14]

## Implications

### Governments Should Measure Failure Domains, Not Cable Counts

A national inventory should map the entire service chain: wet routes, branching points, landing stations, terrestrial backhaul, power, management systems, capacity commitments, maintenance contracts, spares, vessel coverage, and responsible authorities.[1][5][7] Sensitive detail need not be public, but government and operators require a shared operational picture. The first test is whether apparently diverse cables share one beach, conduit, power source, supplier, or repair arrangement. The second is whether surviving capacity can carry priority traffic for the expected restoration interval.[1][3]

Stress tests should work backward from severe but plausible failures. Scenarios should include one accidental cut during peak demand, simultaneous faults in a corridor, loss of a landing station, compromise of network management, denial of a repair permit, unavailability of the contracted vessel, and a natural disaster that also damages terrestrial infrastructure.[2][5] For power interconnectors, the scenario must include generation and grid consequences; for telecommunications, it must include capacity and routing consequences.[4] The worst outcome is a plan that counts an alternate asset which shares the initiating failure.

Public investment should target uncovered regional benefits. A commercially marginal second landing, spare depot, standby contract, or training program may have high social value because it reduces isolation for several states.[1][8] Governments should publish the resilience objective, require independent failure domains where feasible, and avoid subsidizing duplicate routes that reproduce the same chokepoint. The author's synthesis is that the correct metric is expected essential service preserved per unit of public support, not kilometers of cable announced.

### Repair Readiness Should Be Contracted Before Damage

States and operators should treat repair as a planned capability rather than a procurement task that begins after a cut. Agreements should identify priority rules, mobilization time, compatible cable and jointing stock, vessel and crew availability, customs treatment, port access, survey data, security support, and authority to enter relevant waters.[1][2] Pre-authorization can preserve government oversight while reducing administrative delay, and regional mutual-assistance agreements can pool expensive assets.[1]

A global vessel count is insufficient for planning. Decision-makers need to know which ships are under active maintenance contracts, where they are, whether they are on another installation job, what depth and equipment they can handle, and how long mobilization and transit will take.[1][2] Aging fleets create a capital-allocation problem because standby repair capacity earns value through avoided outage rather than continuous visible use.[1] Public guarantees, availability payments, shared maintenance zones, or co-financing can be justified where ordinary project revenue will not sustain socially necessary readiness.

Exercises should test the administrative chain as well as the splice. A technically capable vessel that waits for cabotage relief, customs clearance, visas, environmental approval, or security coordination does not provide rapid restoration.[1] The author's assessment is that repair time should be measured from fault detection to restored service and decomposed into diagnosis, decision, permit, mobilization, transit, physical repair, and testing. That decomposition identifies whether the marginal investment belongs in ships, spares, procedures, data, or authority.

### Alliances Need a Division of Labor with Industry

Private operators own much of the network, possess system telemetry, and execute rerouting and repair.[3] National authorities control policing, maritime surveillance, intelligence, border procedures, and legal process. Alliances can aggregate awareness, coordinate patrols, align response thresholds, and distribute burdens.[6] A durable arrangement should define what information operators provide, how governments protect commercial and security-sensitive data, who leads an incident, and how evidence moves from technical diagnosis to criminal and political assessment.[1][5][6]

Military assets should be assigned functions they can perform. Persistent presence and patrol aircraft may deter conspicuous interference, correlate ship behavior, and accelerate response, but they cannot continuously guard every kilometer of cable.[6] Sensors and automated anomaly detection can narrow the search area but still require validation. Law enforcement can board or seize vessels only within applicable authority and evidence. The author's synthesis is that layered protection works when monitoring, commercial telemetry, investigation, and repair reinforce one another; it fails when technology is presented as a substitute for governance.

Alliance consultation should distinguish service severity from attributed intent. A major outage may justify mutual technical assistance even when cause is unknown. A pattern of suspicious conduct may justify heightened surveillance before public attribution. Sanctions or collective-defense decisions require stronger evidence and political agreement.[4][6] This graduated model prevents an adversary from exploiting procedural delay while reducing the risk that an accident triggers disproportionate escalation.

### Attribution Should Be Designed as an Evidence Pipeline

ENISA reports substantial uncertainty in fault causes, and the Finnish police initially treated Eagle S's involvement as under investigation.[2][14] The author's proposed incident protocol preserves cable samples, seabed imagery, vessel tracks, anchor and hull evidence, weather and fishing records, operator logs, and network effects under documented custody. It then separates six propositions: a service failed; a physical cable was damaged; a mechanism caused the damage; a vessel or person performed the act; the act was intentional; and a state or organization directed it. Confidence should be stated for each proposition rather than assigned once to the whole narrative.

Public communication should disclose what can be supported without exposing sensitive methods. Saying that a vessel was near a fault is different from saying its anchor caused damage, and both are different from proving intent or sponsorship.[14] Authorities should correct early claims when evidence changes. The author's assessment is that calibrated language strengthens deterrence because partners and adversaries can distinguish verified attribution from political assertion.

The same discipline applies to surveillance allegations and supplier risk. Decision-makers should ask what access was possible, what control failed, whether data were encrypted, what logs or artifacts exist, and whether the alleged actor had legal or operational authority.[2][7] National origin and geopolitical alignment affect the risk model, but they do not replace technical evidence. Procurement restrictions should therefore be connected to explicit, auditable security requirements.[7]

### Operators and Investors Should Price Correlated Risk

Operators should evaluate route diversity across seabed, landing, terrestrial, power, software, supplier, and repair layers.[1][3] A second cable that shares the same landing trench or regional repair queue may add capacity without adding equivalent resilience. Contracts should identify priority during simultaneous faults and should disclose enough ownership and maintenance structure for regulators and counterparties to understand dependencies.[2][7]

Investors, lenders, and insurers should scenario-test the duration and distribution of outages rather than use cable presence as a binary indicator. Material questions include spare capacity, alternate landing jurisdictions, repair coverage, permit history, political relations, exposure to contested waters, and the customer's tolerance for degraded service.[1][13] A business that claims geographic diversification because it buys capacity from several cables should verify whether those systems share routes or operators.

The author's synthesis is that resilience expenditure resembles an option. Its value is low in ordinary periods and rises sharply when several risks coincide. This makes it vulnerable to underinvestment by firms evaluated on near-term utilization. Procurement standards, insurance terms, disclosure, and public co-financing can reward independent routes and repair readiness without requiring the state to operate the network.[7][13]

### Small and Island States Need Portfolio Resilience, Not Symbolic Connectivity

For a state with one international system, the first cable creates transformative capacity but also a single point of isolation.[1][9] A second system adds resilience only if its route, landing, and maintenance arrangement are sufficiently independent. Emergency satellite service, local caching, and prioritized low-bandwidth channels can bridge an outage, but the Tonga evidence shows that disaster conditions can also impair or ration those substitutes.[9]

Development finance should include long-term maintenance and governance, not only construction. Partner governments may need technical help with landing licenses, open access, cyber security, route planning, repair permits, and incident leadership.[1][8] The Australian Quad centre is one model for demand-driven assistance, while the ITU-ICPC report recommends capacity-building and regional cooperation for underserved regions.[1][8] The author's assessment is that a cable project is incomplete until the recipient can detect failure, maintain service, authorize repair, and negotiate with owners and suppliers from an informed position.

Corridor diplomacy should preserve recipient agency. Offers framed as trusted connectivity can still create dependence on the financier, platform, supplier, or landing partner.[7][12][15] Governments should compare lifetime cost, ownership, capacity rights, upgrade path, maintenance, data governance, and exit options rather than choose solely on grant value or geopolitical pressure. Transparent terms make strategic alignment visible and reduce the chance that security claims become cover for vendor protectionism.

### Europe and the Indo-Pacific Require Different but Connected Models

Europe has dense institutions, NATO mechanisms, EU regulation, regional funding, and a developing cable-vessel reserve.[4][5][6] The Indo-Pacific spans wider distances, more small island states, contested maritime areas, uneven regulatory capacity, and fewer region-wide security institutions.[8][11] Copying the European model wholesale would ignore those differences. The transferable elements are shared risk assessment, pre-arranged repair, public-private coordination, evidence standards, and financing for route diversity.[1][8][11]

In Europe, the priority is to integrate data and power infrastructure protection without confusing their operating requirements. Sea-basin monitoring, cable hubs, and alliance patrols should connect to operators and investigators, while repair capacity must cover both telecommunications and energy assets under appropriate technical arrangements.[4][5][6] In the Indo-Pacific, regional repair access and permitting may have higher marginal value because distance and fragmented authority lengthen restoration.[1][8][11]

Humboldt and the proposed Far North Fiber Arctic route are intended to add or diversify connectivity.[11][15] The author's assessment is that any new corridor can also create landing, environmental, or supplier dependencies. Route diversification should therefore be assessed as a network change, not celebrated as an isolated line. The author's synthesis is that a strategically useful corridor adds an independent path, sustainable maintenance, transparent governance, and reciprocal benefit; a longer map line alone is not resilience.

### A Practical Geopolitical Assessment Uses Eight Questions

A disciplined assessment can begin with eight questions. First, what essential service depends on the cable? Second, which physical and institutional components form the end-to-end path? Third, which failures can disable several nominally separate assets? Fourth, what capacity and service remain after the loss? Fifth, who owns, operates, licenses, supplies, and repairs each component? Sixth, how long would restoration take under current contracts and permits? Seventh, what evidence distinguishes accident, negligence, crime, and state direction? Eighth, which reversible measures reduce harm before attribution is complete?[1][2][3][5]

These questions prevent the single worst analytical failure: turning an ambiguous outage into an unsupported geopolitical conclusion. They also prevent the inverse failure of treating routine faults as evidence that strategic risk is imaginary. The author's synthesis is that undersea cable power is conditional. It grows with concentrated dependence, opaque control, scarce substitutes, and slow repair; it shrinks with independent routes, verified security, available restoration, coordinated institutions, and honest attribution.[1][5][7]
