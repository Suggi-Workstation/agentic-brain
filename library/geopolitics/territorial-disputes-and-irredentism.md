# Territorial Disputes Escalate When Borders Become Tests of Nationhood, Power, and Resolve

Territorial disputes are contests over who may exercise sovereignty in a specified place, but their danger depends less on area alone than on the strategic, economic, historical, and identity claims attached to it. Irredentism is a narrower form in which a state seeks territory associated with ethnic kin outside its borders; it is politically potent but empirically uncommon relative to the number of possible cross-border kin claims. The central geopolitical problem is therefore not that every unsettled border produces war, but that salient claims, prior coercion, domestic incentives, and fear of future disadvantage can turn a negotiable boundary into a recurring escalation system.[1][2][3][4]

## Background

The modern territorial order developed through conquest, dynastic inheritance, imperial partition, decolonization, negotiated boundary making, and the breakup of multinational states. A line that appears fixed on a contemporary map may therefore represent several incompatible histories: an older administrative frontier, a treaty boundary, a military ceasefire line, a colonial division, a national homeland, or the former reach of a state. Abramson and Carter's geospatial study of European historical boundaries finds that competing border precedents help explain where later territorial claims emerge. Their mechanism is institutional as well as symbolic: historical borders coordinate settlement, administration, trade, and expectations, so past lines can continue to organize behavior after formal sovereignty changes.[6]

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries multiplied these ambiguities. Empires converted frontier zones into surveyed boundaries, while their dissolution converted internal administrative divisions into international borders. Decolonization preserved many inherited lines because reopening all of them would have exposed newly independent states to cascading revision, yet the preserved boundaries sometimes divided communities or joined territories with different political histories. The post-First World War settlement similarly tried to reconcile self-determination, security, infrastructure, and economic viability in regions where the objectives pointed toward different maps. These processes did not make later disputes inevitable, but they supplied rival precedents and populations through which political leaders could construct claims.[1][6][7]

The post-1945 order placed a strong normative and legal barrier against changing borders by force. Article 2 of the United Nations Charter requires members to settle disputes peacefully and refrain from the threat or use of force against another state's territorial integrity or political independence; Article 33 lists negotiation, mediation, arbitration, judicial settlement, and other peaceful means.[8] The 1975 Helsinki Final Act reinforced sovereign equality, the inviolability of frontiers, territorial integrity, non-use of force, and peaceful settlement among its participating states.[9] Zacher's historical analysis describes a territorial-integrity norm that increasingly delegitimized interstate conquest even though violations and attempted revisions persisted.[7]

This order changed the form of territorial competition rather than abolishing it. Frederick, Hensel, and Macaulay identify 843 interstate territorial claims from 1816 through 2001 in the Issue Correlates of War, or ICOW, data. Adjusted for the growing number of states, involvement in territorial claims declined markedly after 1945, and fatal violence over claims was lower in the short post-Cold War period covered by their data than in earlier eras. Yet 107 claims in the dataset remained ongoing at the end of 2001, and the regional concentration had shifted toward Asia and Oceania.[1] The evidence supports two propositions at once: territorial revision became less accepted as a general practice, and unresolved claims remained consequential security problems.[1][7]

ICOW provides a disciplined definition. A territorial claim requires explicit competing claims to sovereignty over specific territory, made or adopted by officials authorized to conduct foreign policy. A vague aspiration for access to the sea, a private nationalist statement, a demand concerning minority treatment, or a secessionist demand for independence does not by itself constitute an interstate territorial claim under this coding rule.[1][14] This distinction prevents analysts from treating every rhetorical map, communal grievance, or resource disagreement as an active sovereignty contest.

Irredentism is related but narrower, and the literature uses the term at different levels of intensity. Siroky and Hale examine actual and potential cases in a triad consisting of a possible irredentist state, a coethnic enclave across the border, and the host state. Their 2017 analysis asks why some governments pursue annexation on ethnic grounds while most show restraint.[3] Hale and Siroky's later institutional study uses a deliberately strict outcome: military force by a central government against a neighboring state to annex ethnic kin and their territory.[4] Rhetorical concern for cross-border kin, cultural support, autonomy advocacy, secession, and armed annexation should therefore not be collapsed into one category. The strict definition isolates the geopolitical act most likely to internationalize communal politics.[3][4]

The empirical rarity of armed irredentism is as important as its dramatic cases. Hale and Siroky report that less than 4 percent of potential cases in their 1946-2014 universe produced irredentist conflict, while many efforts remained rhetorical.[4] The existence of coethnics across a border is thus an opportunity structure, not a sufficient cause. Leaders still face the costs of war, resistance from the host state and local population, sanctions or external intervention, the fiscal burden of incorporation, and changes to their own domestic coalition.[3][4]

The background problem is consequently one of selective activation. Borders can remain stable despite mixed populations and rival histories when governments accept the status quo, communities value autonomy or existing citizenship, institutions channel claims peacefully, and outside powers discourage revision. The same geographic configuration can become dangerous when elites convert history into an exclusive entitlement, military or economic shifts alter expectations, coercive precedents accumulate, or domestic institutions reward uncompromising claims. The author's synthesis is that territorial conflict should be studied as a process linking a place, a claim, a political coalition, and an interaction history rather than as an automatic consequence of geography or ethnicity.[1][2][3][4][6]

## Core Concepts

### Borders Are Institutions, Not Merely Lines

A boundary allocates more than land. It determines which state may tax, police, regulate, build infrastructure, control access, represent residents internationally, and claim resources. Because people and organizations adapt to these allocations, a durable border coordinates expectations even when its historical origin was arbitrary or coercive. Abramson and Carter show that old boundaries can retain these coordination effects and later provide focal lines for claims.[6] A border's persistence can therefore create its own practical legitimacy without erasing competing memories of how it was made.

This institutional view explains why map revision can produce consequences disproportionate to the territory's size. Moving a line may change control of a road, port, ridge, water source, settlement, offshore zone, or approach to a capital. It may also revise the rule by which other boundaries are understood. The author's synthesis is that a territorial concession has a local distributional effect and a systemic signaling effect: leaders ask both what is lost at this location and what the concession implies for other claims, allies, minorities, and adversaries.[1][2][6]

### A Claim Is Different from a Dispute, a Boundary, and Control

Analysts should separate four objects. A boundary is the recognized or administered dividing line. A territorial claim is an official demand for sovereignty over specified territory. A dispute is the interaction created by incompatible positions. Effective control is the ability to administer or occupy the place. These can diverge: a state may control territory whose sovereignty another state contests, recognize a boundary it cannot fully administer, or maintain a claim without attempting to change control.[1][14]

The distinction matters for escalation analysis. Diplomatic recognition, legal title, administrative presence, and military occupation do not necessarily point to the same actor. A settlement must specify which problem it resolves: title, delimitation, demarcation, access, administration, resource use, security, or final renunciation. Ambiguity can be useful for a temporary ceasefire, but it becomes hazardous when each side treats the arrangement as confirmation of incompatible sovereign rights. The author's assessment is that durable management requires deliberate clarity about which dimensions remain contested and which conduct is permitted while they remain open.[8][10][12]

### Territorial Salience Has Tangible and Intangible Components

ICOW measures six features that can make a territorial claim salient. Tangible indicators are valuable resources, strategic location, and permanent population. Intangible indicators are treatment as homeland, an ethnic, linguistic, religious, or related identity connection, and relatively recent historical sovereignty.[1] These components can reinforce one another, but they should not be reduced to a single slogan such as resources or nationalism.

The distinction explains variation among superficially similar claims. An uninhabited area can matter because it controls an approach route, offshore rights, or a precedent. A populated region can matter because residents are treated as co-nationals, because their presence strengthens a legal narrative, or because administration proves effective control. A former imperial possession may be framed as a historic homeland by one government and as an inherited sovereign territory by another. Higher salience raises the political price of compromise, but it also attracts more diplomatic effort because governments have stronger incentives to resolve or stabilize the issue.[1][2]

### Indivisibility Is Often Political Rather Than Physical

Territory is physically divisible in many cases, but political narratives can make division appear illegitimate. Sacred sites, national origin stories, constitutional descriptions of territory, war cemeteries, and claims of historical injustice can transform a bargaining range into a moral test. Leaders who define a place as an inseparable part of the nation may later be unable to trade it without appearing to deny the nation's identity.[1][2][6]

Maps and legal narratives are instruments in this process. A map selects a date, projection, label, and border; a legal brief selects treaties, administrative acts, occupation, recognition, or succession rules. These materials can clarify evidence, but they can also create an illusion that one historical snapshot eliminates every later political fact. Huth, Croco, and Appel find that law can facilitate settlement when relevant principles are clear and one side has a stronger legal claim, because the legal result supplies a focal point for bargaining.[10] The inverse follows as an author's inference: when rival precedents are plausible and no focal rule is accepted, competing maps and doctrines may harden rather than narrow the disagreement.[6][10]

### Irredentism Links External Territory to Domestic Coalition Politics

Irredentism converts a cross-border ethnic connection into a state project of territorial incorporation. Siroky and Hale's global analysis finds greater risk where the dominant kin group experiences status inconsistency, where states are more ethnically homogeneous, and where winner-take-all majoritarian institutions reduce the need to attract support from other groups.[3] Hale and Siroky's institutional model further predicts higher propensities in majoritarian electoral systems and military or personalist dictatorships than in proportional systems and single-party dictatorships; their observed and predicted comparisons support that ordering.[4]

Their mechanism is not that one regime label mechanically causes annexation. Political institutions change which supporters leaders must satisfy, which public or private goods matter to that coalition, and whether incorporating new citizens strengthens or threatens incumbents. Their triad-year evidence also shows that most potential opportunities do not become conflicts.[3][4] The geopolitical implication is that analysts should examine the domestic coalition that benefits from a kin claim, not assume that ethnic affinity automatically generates a coherent national interest.

The target population's preferences require equal attention. Coethnics may prefer existing citizenship, autonomy, independence, or unification; they may also be internally divided. A state can invoke their protection without a reliable mandate from them. Hale and Siroky explicitly note cases in which external kin did not seek retrieval.[4] The author's assessment is that an irredentist claim has at least three principals -- the claimant state, the host state, and the affected population -- and that treating the first as the authentic voice of the third erases a central source of uncertainty and coercion.[3][4]

### Land and Maritime Claims Share Mechanisms but Differ in Typical Salience

Land claims often combine strategic and economic value with population, homeland, identity, and prior sovereignty. Maritime claims more often concern jurisdiction over fisheries, hydrocarbons, navigation, strategic position, and the zones projected from land features. Hensel and colleagues therefore classify territorial issues as generally high in both tangible and intangible salience, while maritime issues are generally lower in intangible salience.[2]

The difference is probabilistic, not categorical. Islands can be populated or identity-laden, and maritime features can affect national narratives or strategic access. Hensel and colleagues find that territorial issues were more likely overall than maritime issues to produce militarized conflict in their covered data, but the issue-type difference lost significance after recent conflict management was considered. Within each issue type, higher salience and a history of recent militarization raised future conflict risk.[2] The same escalation framework can therefore compare land and maritime claims, but it must code what each place represents rather than infer danger from geography alone.

### Faits Accomplis Seek Advantage Without Inviting Full War

A fait accompli is a limited unilateral change imposed before an opponent can prevent it: occupying a small feature, establishing an outpost, changing patrol patterns, building administrative infrastructure, or otherwise altering control. The initiator seeks a completed fact whose reversal would force the opponent to bear the visible burden of escalation. This logic is closely connected to gray-zone coercion because the move is designed to remain below, or create uncertainty about, a threshold for large-scale response.[5]

Altman and Lee analyze conquest attempts since 1945 and argue that the material or ethnic value of the seized area often cannot by itself explain escalation over remarkably small pieces of territory. Using new geospatial and conquest data for 1965-2000 and process analysis of post-1945 attempts, they find evidence consistent with career incentives among military officers, especially where the military has greater political power.[5] Their finding broadens the unit of analysis: an action attributed to the state may also serve organizational or individual advancement within the coercive apparatus.

Limited seizures create a ratchet. If unopposed, the new position can become the baseline for later negotiation. If reversed, the challenger may portray the response as aggression. If met by a symmetrical move, both sides may accumulate forces in a space where local commanders have incomplete information. The author's synthesis is that the worst feature of a fait accompli is not its initial scale but the asymmetry it creates between easy entry and costly reversal.[2][5]

### Escalation Is Path-Dependent

Territorial conflict is not a single jump from disagreement to war. A stylized sequence may move through official claims, diplomatic protest, legal or historical mobilization, economic pressure, patrols, infrastructure, demonstrations of force, limited occupation, armed clashes, and wider war. The sequence is not universal, and steps can be skipped or reversed. Its analytical value is to identify how each action changes expectations, positions, and domestic commitments.[1][2]

Hensel and colleagues find that recent militarized conflict strongly predicts additional militarized conflict across territorial, maritime, and river claims. In their aggregated model, a change corresponding to one militarized dispute in the previous year, or several less recent disputes, raised the estimated annual conflict probability from .021 to .050. Failed peaceful efforts also raised later conflict risk, although by less in their model.[2] This is path dependence: coercion creates deployments, doctrines, grievances, and expectations that shape the next encounter.

The same study also finds more peaceful activity after militarized or failed diplomatic episodes.[2] This is not contradictory. Dangerous claims attract both coercion and diplomacy, and a stalemate may make renewed talks more likely. The correct inference is not that negotiation failed because conflict followed or that conflict succeeded because negotiation followed. Analysts must compare sequences and outcomes while accounting for the fact that the hardest disputes receive the most attention.[2][12]

### Bargaining Failure Has Several Mechanisms

A state may prefer an agreement to war and still reject the available settlement. Information problems arise when each side doubts the other's capability, resolve, or minimum terms. Commitment problems arise when a concession today would give the recipient strategic or economic resources that improve its ability to demand more tomorrow. Rider and Owsiak find that unsettled borders are especially associated with rivalry onset when the disputed territory contains strategic or economic power endowments, consistent with this commitment mechanism.[11]

Domestic audience and coalition problems narrow the range further. A leader may believe a compromise is materially beneficial but fear removal, military opposition, electoral punishment, or accusations of betraying the nation. Indivisibility narratives make side payments or partitions harder to legitimate. External patrons can change expected costs and benefits by deterring attack, subsidizing intransigence, or creating uncertainty about intervention. The author's synthesis is that escalation risk is highest when salience, future power shifts, and domestic punishment reinforce one another; none alone is a universal explanation.[1][3][4][11]

### Settlement and Management Are Different Outcomes

Final settlement reallocates or confirms sovereign rights and terminates the claim. Management reduces immediate danger without necessarily resolving title. Management tools include ceasefires, disengagement, hotlines, incident rules, monitoring, force limitations, reciprocal notification, access arrangements, and joint resource use. Settlement tools include negotiated delimitation, reciprocal renunciation, arbitration, adjudication, agreed partition, plebiscite under accepted conditions, or recognition of an existing line.[1][8][12][13]

The distinction prevents two errors. A stable management regime is not a final peace if each government preserves incompatible claims. Conversely, absence of final settlement is not proof that management has failed if it has prevented coercive revision and protected residents. The United Nations Charter leaves parties a broad choice of peaceful means, and the PRTD dataset records preferences for negotiation, non-binding third-party help, and binding methods rather than counting only completed attempts.[8][12] The practical question is which institutional arrangement can reduce the particular uncertainty, commitment problem, or legitimacy barrier in the case.

### External Guarantees Can Deter and Distort

The author's synthesis is that an outside guarantee can reassure the weaker party that negotiation will not expose it to attack and can deter unilateral revision. Monitoring or peacekeeping can reduce uncertainty about deployments. A guarantor can also provide political cover for a compromise by certifying compliance and spreading implementation costs. These functions are strongest when commitments, triggers, and limits are credible and understood.

Guarantees can also produce moral hazard or entrapment. A protected state may take greater risks if it expects rescue, while an ambiguous patron may encourage both sides to gamble on support. A guarantee tied to an undefined line can import the territorial ambiguity into the alliance itself. The author's assessment is that external involvement should be evaluated by inversion: does it reduce the benefit of a unilateral move and the fear of negotiated compliance, or does it reward maximal claims while obscuring who bears escalation risk?

## Evidence

### The ICOW Record: Salience and Prior Conflict Matter

Frederick, Hensel, and Macaulay's complete ICOW territorial-claims release identifies 843 claims from 1816 through 2001 using explicit coding rules for claimant, target, onset, termination, salience, and militarization.[1][14] The claim-year design records changing features rather than treating every dispute as constant. Its salience index combines three tangible and three intangible indicators for each claimant, while militarization is linked to the Correlates of War Militarized Interstate Dispute data.[1]

The descriptive results show substantial but non-universal violence. Of the 843 claims, 353, or 41.9 percent, produced at least one militarized interstate dispute, and 232, or 27.5 percent, produced a fatal one. Among claims with high salience scores, 53.2 percent were militarized and 39.6 percent produced fatalities; among low-salience claims, the corresponding figures were 17.7 percent and 7.1 percent.[1] These are historical associations, not estimates that salience alone causes conflict, but the gradient is large and consistent with the mechanism that leaders invest more in places carrying several forms of value.[1][2]

Claim endings also show that neither conquest nor adjudication is the modal story. In the original coding, bilateral negotiations ended 34.4 percent of completed claims, dropped or renounced claims accounted for 16.2 percent, binding third-party activity for 8.0 percent, non-binding third-party activity for 5.8 percent, and direct conquest for 9.2 percent. When agreements and peace conferences that codified battlefield outcomes are reclassified as militarized settlements, military outcomes account for 25.1 percent and bilateral negotiation for 28.9 percent.[1] The reclassification demonstrates why settlement labels must be read with their causal history: a treaty can formalize either bargaining or force.

### Cross-Issue Models: Conflict and Diplomacy Respond to the Same Danger

Hensel, Mitchell, Sowers, and Thyne compare territorial, maritime, and river claims in the Americas and Europe with logistic models of annual militarized conflict and peaceful settlement attempts.[2] They find that within-issue salience significantly raises the likelihood of both types of activity. Across the modeled range, estimated annual militarized-conflict probability rises from roughly .01 at minimum salience to about five times that level at maximum salience. Recent militarization and failed peaceful attempts also predict renewed conflict.[2]

Their models place the evidence in needed context. Peaceful techniques were more common than militarized ones, with baseline annual probabilities ranging from .055 to .228 depending on issue type and salience, compared with about .01 to .05 for militarized conflict. Well under half of the issues produced even one militarized dispute, and more than 90 percent of peaceful attempts did not begin during an ongoing militarized dispute.[2] Research restricted to crises would therefore select the hardest cases and understate routine peaceful management.

The land-maritime comparison is similarly conditional. Territorial claims showed greater overall conflict propensity than maritime claims before recent issue management was added, but after a first confrontation the difference across issue types diminished. Salience and recent conflict remained important within each type.[2] The evidence rejects two simple rules: maritime disputes are not inherently harmless, and every territorial claim is not equally dangerous.

### Irredentism: Opportunity Is Common, Activation Is Rare

Siroky and Hale construct a global dataset of actual and potential irredentist triads from 1946 to 2014, allowing restraint to be compared with action rather than examining only wars.[3] Their results associate irredentism with a kin group's economic status inconsistency, greater ethnic homogeneity in the potential claimant state, and majoritarian institutions that permit a dominant group to pursue policy without assembling a broader coalition.[3] The design is observational, so the results identify systematic relationships rather than proving that changing one institution would automatically change a state's behavior.

Hale and Siroky extend the analysis with a computational model and institutional data matched to the same universe of potential and observed cases.[4] In descriptive frequencies, 5.83 percent of opportunities under majoritarian systems and 4.47 percent under their military category produced irredentism, compared with 1.16 percent under proportional systems and 3.07 percent under single-party systems. Their logistic model estimates predicted probabilities of .050 for majoritarian systems and .052 for military systems, versus .019 for proportional systems and .025 for single-party systems; anocratic dyads also show elevated risk.[4] The evidence supports attention to institutional subtype rather than a blanket democracy-versus-autocracy claim.

The study's limits are also instructive. The event is rare, fixed-effects approaches are constrained by limited within-triad variation, and the authors model institutions as exogenous while acknowledging possible unmeasured connections between ethnicity, institutional choice, and irredentism.[4] The defensible conclusion is that domestic incentives help distinguish action from restraint, not that constitutional engineering supplies a universal prevention formula.

### Historical Boundaries and Legal Focal Points

Abramson and Carter assemble spatial data on European boundaries from the Peace of Westphalia to the French Revolution and test whether those precedents predict territorial claims after the Congress of Vienna.[6] Their analysis supports two mechanisms: competing historical precedents create rival bases for entitlement, and old borders retain coordination effects that make them attractive claim lines. History matters here not merely because leaders tell stories about it, but because former institutions leave settlements, jurisdictions, and expectations in space.[6]

Huth, Croco, and Appel study territorial disputes from 1945 to 2000 and analyze negotiation, final settlement, and selection into legal dispute resolution.[10] They find that clear legal principles which favor one side can create a focal point that helps leaders overcome distributional bargaining problems. In a related analysis of negotiated settlements, they report that states with a strong legal advantage are more likely to obtain favorable terms.[10] Law does not mechanically enforce itself; its geopolitical contribution is to narrow the range of defensible outcomes and provide political justification for movement toward one of them.

The 2025 study by Gerolymatou, Magliveras, and Naldi examines equity in international arbitration of violent territorial disputes.[13] The authors identify only sixteen violent territorial conflicts submitted to arbitration and therefore reject broad generalization from that small universe. Their case analysis nevertheless shows a design problem: strict legal determination can clarify title, while equity may be invoked to account for geography, relationships, and peace, but ambiguity over equity can itself generate contestation.[13] The evidence supports treating arbitration as an institution requiring consent, mandate design, and implementation rather than as a neutral machine that transforms law into peace.

### Limited Conquest and Organizational Incentives

Altman and Lee combine statistical analysis of conquest attempts in territorial disputes from 1965 to 2000 with process analysis of post-1945 attempts.[5] Their dependent phenomenon is not wholesale conquest of states, which became rare after 1945, but attempts to seize small disputed areas. They argue that conventional material and ethnic value often does not explain why actors accept substantial risk for tiny gains, and find evidence that officers' pursuit of promotion or political office contributes to these attempts where militaries possess greater political power.[5]

This finding does not negate salience research. It separates the value of the disputed issue from the incentives of the actor who chooses the move. A small seizure can carry organizational prestige even when the captured land has little direct value, while high-value territory can remain peaceful where command structures and political incentives restrain freelancing. The author's synthesis is that escalation assessment needs two ledgers: what the state gains from the territory and what decision-makers gain from taking action.[1][2][5]

### Border Settlement and the Territorial Peace

Rider and Owsiak link unsettled power-endowed borders to commitment problems and rivalry. Using border-settlement data and three rivalry measures, they find that unsettled borders raise rivalry-onset risk particularly where disputed territory contains strategic or economic value that could alter future bargaining power.[11] Gibler and Owsiak's analysis of borders from 1919 to 2001 reports a temporal sequence in which border settlement precedes peace in the dyad rather than peace merely making settlement possible.[15]

These findings should not be read as proof that signing any document causes peace. Settlement may be difficult precisely because underlying power and identity stakes are high, and implementation quality varies. They do support a mechanism: a mutually accepted border removes a recurring object of bargaining, reduces uncertainty about jurisdiction, and limits the locations at which coercive testing can occur.[11][15] The causal target is not the cartographic line alone but the credible closure of competing claims.

## Implications

### For Early Warning: Monitor Interaction Histories, Not Maps Alone

A map of unsettled claims is a starting inventory, not a risk forecast. ICOW evidence shows that salience and recent militarization distinguish more dangerous claims, while cross-issue models show that failed settlement attempts can also signal renewed action.[1][2] An early-warning system should therefore track changes in official claim language, force posture, patrol and infrastructure patterns, legal narratives, domestic coalition incentives, and the sequence of prior incidents.

The highest-value warning indicator may be a change in process rather than a new claim. Routine protests can become riskier when one side establishes permanent presence, delegates discretion to local commanders, shortens decision time, or frames reversal as a test of national survival. The author's assessment is that analysts should record each move's effect on control, commitment, and reversibility. A reversible patrol is different from a fortified outpost; a private nationalist map is different from an official sovereignty claim; a legal filing is different from a seizure.[1][5][14]

Repeated restraint also contains information. If governments preserve a claim but maintain incident rules and avoid changing control, the unresolved title may coexist with effective management. Analysts should not mistake continued rhetoric for imminent war or, conversely, assume that long calm makes a sudden fait accompli impossible. Path dependence cuts both ways: stable routines can institutionalize restraint, while accumulated coercive precedents can normalize escalation.[2][12]

### For Diplomats: Decompose the Dispute Before Trading Sovereignty

Negotiators should identify which values make the claim salient. Strategic access, resources, resident protection, historical recognition, symbolic ownership, administrative convenience, and domestic prestige require different instruments. A joint-development zone can address resource access without deciding sovereignty; transit guarantees can address connectivity; minority protections can address communal security; demilitarization can address threat; and a legal process can clarify title.[2][8][10]

This decomposition does not make every issue divisible. A state may reject functional cooperation because it fears that cooperation concedes title, or it may use joint administration to entrench control. Agreements must therefore state whether practical arrangements are without prejudice to sovereignty claims and how conduct will be interpreted. The author's synthesis is that successful management creates a protected layer of shared function beneath unresolved status, whereas failed ambiguity allows each practical act to become evidence in the sovereignty contest.

Negotiation design should also account for domestic punishment. A legal or third-party focal point can provide leaders with a justification that bilateral concession cannot, especially when the relevant principle is clear.[10] Sequencing can matter: incident controls and verified disengagement may build confidence before final delimitation, while premature insistence on a maximal final-status bargain may collapse cooperation that could first reduce risk. PRTD's proposal-level design is useful because it reveals changing preferences for negotiation, non-binding assistance, and binding procedures even when the counterpart refuses the proposed method.[12]

### For Irredentist Claims: Separate Kin Protection from Annexation

Policy toward cross-border kin should not assume that protection requires territorial transfer. Language rights, political participation, local autonomy, monitoring, cross-border movement, education, and cultural exchange can address specific concerns without changing sovereignty. Whether these instruments work depends on consent and enforcement, but they preserve more options than a claim that defines annexation as the only authentic expression of group welfare.

The affected population must be treated as an actor rather than evidence. External kin may have heterogeneous preferences, and annexation may expose minorities within the claimed territory to a new hierarchy. Hale and Siroky's research emphasizes that potential targets do not always seek incorporation and that institutional incentives in the claimant state shape the decision.[4] The author's assessment is that any plebiscitary or consultative mechanism must define the electorate, protect dissent, exclude coercive demographic change, and specify what happens to those who lose. Without these conditions, an invocation of self-determination can become a method for one state to select another state's borders.

Outside actors should distinguish humanitarian concern from territorial endorsement. Monitoring abuse or supporting minority rights need not validate an annexation claim. Conversely, dismissing all kin concerns as propaganda may leave real discrimination available for exploitation. The policy objective is to reduce the grievance that mobilizers can weaponize while raising the cost of converting that grievance into interstate revision.[3][4]

### For Military and Alliance Planning: Make Limited Moves Unprofitable

Faits accomplis exploit uncertainty about whether a small loss warrants a large response. Deterrence should therefore emphasize denial, observation, rapid consultation, and proportionate response options before a seizure occurs. Clear incident protocols reduce the chance that local actions surprise political leaders; persistent monitoring makes attribution faster; and resilient access prevents a tiny occupation from becoming irreversible by default. These are strategic design principles, not prescriptions for a particular force package.[5]

Alliance commitments require geographic precision. A general guarantee attached to a disputed line may be interpreted differently by the protected state, adversary, and guarantor. The worst case is simultaneous optimism: one party expects automatic rescue, another expects non-intervention, and the guarantor has not decided. The author's assessment is that an effective assurance identifies covered territory, consultation procedures, prohibited unilateral changes, and the consequences of provocations by the protected party as well as attacks by the adversary.

Military organizations also require internal controls. Altman and Lee's evidence on career incentives implies that escalation can originate below the level assumed by unitary-state models.[5] Civilian oversight, command accountability, rules for patrol changes, and reliable reporting are therefore part of territorial deterrence. A government cannot credibly promise restraint if officers can manufacture facts on the ground and expect organizational reward.

### For Legal and Third-Party Processes: Design the Focal Point and the Exit

Adjudication and arbitration are strongest when parties accept the forum, the mandate covers the actual disagreement, evidence can be evaluated, and implementation is politically feasible. Clear law can narrow bargaining and give leaders cover, as Huth, Croco, and Appel show.[10] Yet the small number of violent disputes submitted to arbitration and the contested role of equity identified by Gerolymatou and colleagues show that legal settlement is selected, designed, and embedded in politics.[13]

A complete process must answer what follows the award. Delimitation on paper may require demarcation, withdrawal, property arrangements, access rights, citizenship choices, resource allocation, and monitoring. If the decision produces an enclave, divides infrastructure, or reallocates a populated area without transition rules, legal clarity over title can coexist with new security problems. The author's synthesis is that the best third-party design joins a principled focal point to an implementation architecture; either part alone can fail.

Mediation has a different advantage. It can create packages that a court could not order, including compensation, joint institutions, phased withdrawal, or reciprocal concessions in other issue areas. Its weakness is that non-binding flexibility may not solve a commitment problem. The choice between bilateral negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and adjudication should therefore follow the obstacle: information, distribution, domestic cover, legal uncertainty, or future enforcement.[8][10][12][13]

### For Maritime Disputes: Avoid Importing Land Assumptions

Maritime claims often involve fisheries, hydrocarbons, strategic position, navigation, and the zones generated by land features rather than a resident population.[2] This can expand the menu of functional bargains, including joint development or coordinated resource management, but it does not eliminate escalation risk. Offshore infrastructure and patrols can create faits accomplis, while ambiguous feature status can magnify the legal consequences of a small physical presence.

Analysts should therefore separate control of a feature, sovereignty over it, the maritime entitlements it may generate, and access to resources. Treating these as one prize makes compromise harder and can exaggerate what follows from control of a rock or island. At the same time, assuming that uninhabited space lacks national meaning ignores cases in which maps, prior administration, or strategic narratives supply intangible salience. Hensel and colleagues' findings support measuring each claim rather than assigning risk by issue label.[2]

### For Investors and Infrastructure Planners: Price Jurisdictional Process, Not Headlines

Territorial disputes affect assets through control, access, sanctions, insurance, shipping, licenses, and the enforceability of contracts. The relevant exposure is not simply whether two states issue rival claims, but whether the dispute can change who regulates the asset, whether coercive incidents interrupt access, and whether an agreement would preserve existing rights. A resource concession in contested space contains political title risk even when production is technically viable.

Scenario analysis should distinguish managed dispute, limited seizure, legal settlement, negotiated joint use, and wider conflict. Each has different consequences for duration, reversibility, and counterparties. The author's assessment is that the most dangerous assumption is binary: either the status quo persists or full war begins. Faits accomplis, patrol restrictions, selective sanctions, and administrative changes can impair an asset without crossing the threshold that conventional country-risk models label war.[2][5]

Historical claims also complicate time horizons. A dispute can remain quiet for decades and still constrain infrastructure routing or financing because no actor wants investment to prejudice sovereignty. Conversely, a settled border can reduce a recurring source of rivalry and enable long-lived cross-border assets.[11][15] Investment analysis should therefore treat settlement institutions and incident-management quality as observable risk variables, not background diplomacy.

### For Comparative Analysis: Do Not Let One Famous Conflict Become the Model

Territorial disputes vary by population, terrain, legal history, material value, identity connection, regime institutions, relative power, external patrons, and prior interaction. ICOW's global and cross-issue evidence shows large variation within categories, while irredentism studies show that most potential kin configurations remain peaceful.[1][2][3][4] A theory built from one highly salient conflict will overpredict violence elsewhere and may mistake features of that case for general mechanisms.

Comparison should proceed by mechanism. Ask whether the claim rests on competing historical precedents, whether territory carries power endowments, whether domestic coalitions benefit from revision, whether local actors can create faits accomplis, whether outside guarantees are credible, and whether legal principles provide a focal point. Cases can then be compared across regions without claiming that identical geography or identity labels produce identical outcomes.[5][6][10][11]

This method also disciplines analogy. Calling a situation another Munich, Crimea, Kashmir, or South China Sea imports assumptions about aggressor intent, local preference, alliance credibility, and settlement feasibility before those variables are tested. The author's assessment is that analogy is useful only after the mechanism is specified. The question is not which famous case the map resembles, but which bargaining failure and escalation pathway the evidence supports.

### A Practical Escalation Audit

The topic can be reduced to a seven-part audit. First, define the claimed territory and distinguish title, boundary, administration, and access. Second, identify tangible and intangible salience for each actor. Third, map affected populations and their actual preferences rather than inferred ethnic alignment. Fourth, reconstruct the interaction history, especially militarized episodes, failed settlements, and unilateral changes. Fifth, identify decision-makers and their domestic or organizational incentives. Sixth, test whether legal rules, monitoring, guarantees, or functional bargains can reduce uncertainty and commitment problems. Seventh, invert the proposed policy by asking how it could reward a limited seizure, trap a guarantor, or make compromise politically impossible.[1][2][3][5][10][11]

The audit does not produce certainty. It prevents category errors that repeatedly distort territorial analysis: equating rhetoric with an official claim, ethnicity with support for annexation, a signed agreement with an uncoerced settlement, law with automatic enforcement, and the absence of total war with stability. The author's synthesis is that territorial peace is built when institutions make unilateral revision hard, peaceful adjustment legitimate, and everyday cooperation possible without requiring communities to erase their histories.
