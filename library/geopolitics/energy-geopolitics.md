# Energy Geopolitics -- How Oil, Gas, and Critical Minerals Shape Global Power

Energy is not just a commodity; it is the lifeblood of modern economies
and the most powerful lever in international relations. Control over
energy resources -- who has them, who needs them, and who controls the
routes between them -- has been a central driver of alliance formation,
military conflict, and economic leverage since the 20th century. The
energy map is now being redrawn by the shale revolution, the clean
energy transition, and the rise of critical minerals as the new oil,
making energy geopolitics the single most important structural force
shaping the 21st-century international order.

## Background

The geopolitical importance of energy first became visible to the
world on October 17, 1973, when the Arab members of OPEC, led by Saudi
King Faisal, imposed an oil embargo on the United States and its
allies for supporting Israel during the Yom Kippur War. Oil prices
quadrupled nearly overnight, triggering a global recession and
demonstrating that energy could be weaponized with devastating effect.
The embargo's lesson was clear: asymmetrical interdependence in energy
is a source of geopolitical power (Keohane and Nye, 1977).

The embargo catalyzed structural changes that still shape energy
markets today. Western nations created the International Energy Agency
(IEA) in 1974 to coordinate strategic petroleum reserves. Oil
companies, expelled from Middle Eastern concessions as producer states
nationalized their resources, pivoted to the North Sea, the Gulf of
Mexico, and Alaska. The petrodollar system, formalized through a
US-Saudi agreement, ensured that oil continued to be priced in
dollars, cementing the dollar's role as the global reserve currency
and giving the United States what Valery Giscard d'Estaing called an
"exorbitant privilege."

For the next four decades, OPEC's control over the marginal barrel of
oil gave its members -- particularly Saudi Arabia -- outsized
geopolitical influence. But two developments have since reshaped the
energy landscape: the American shale revolution, which transformed the
United States from the world's largest oil importer into its largest
producer and a net exporter; and the accelerating clean energy
transition, which is shifting strategic competition from hydrocarbons
to the critical minerals -- lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earth
elements -- that underpin batteries, solar panels, wind turbines, and
defense systems.

## Core Concepts

### Energy as a Weapon: The OPEC Model and Its Successors

The concept of the "energy weapon" rests on asymmetrical
interdependence: when one party depends more on a resource than the
supplier depends on the revenue, the supplier holds leverage. The 1973
Arab oil embargo was the prototype. OPEC's Arab members cut production
by 25 percent and selectively embargoed the United States and the
Netherlands. The weapon worked because oil was irreplaceable in the
short term, global spare capacity was minimal, and OPEC controlled 53
percent of global production.

Russia reprised this playbook with natural gas. Unlike oil, which
trades in fungible global markets where substitute supplies can be
procured relatively easily, natural gas historically moved through
fixed pipeline infrastructure, creating bilateral dependencies that
are far harder to break. By 2021, Russia supplied roughly 40 percent
of Europe's natural gas, and Gazprom had spent decades cultivating
dependency relationships with individual European states -- offering
Germany cheap gas through Nord Stream while charging higher prices to
Eastern European countries with fewer alternatives (Baker Institute,
2017). In 2021, Russia restricted gas flows to Europe, driving prices
to record levels and demonstrating that pipeline gas is a far more
effective weapon than oil ever was.

### The Shale Revolution: How Technology Rewrote the Energy Map

The combination of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing
("fracking") unlocked vast hydrocarbon reserves from shale rock
formations previously considered uneconomic. US crude oil production,
which had peaked at 9.6 million barrels per day in 1970 and declined
to roughly 5 million by 2008, rebounded to over 13 million by 2019,
making the United States the world's largest oil producer. US natural
gas production similarly surged, transforming the country from a
projected LNG importer to one of the world's largest LNG exporters.

The geopolitical consequences were profound. OPEC's ability to
manipulate global oil prices diminished as US shale producers, with
shorter investment cycles and greater price responsiveness, acted as a
swing producer. The 2014-2016 oil price collapse was partly a Saudi
attempt to bankrupt high-cost US shale producers -- a strategy that
failed as shale operators cut costs and survived. Russia's gas leverage
over Europe weakened as US LNG cargoes provided an alternative supply
source, a dynamic that proved critical after Russia's 2022 invasion of
Ukraine, when European LNG imports from the United States surged to
replace Russian pipeline gas.

However, the shale revolution also created new geopolitical
dependencies. The United States' status as an energy superpower has
enabled more assertive foreign policy but has also made global energy
markets more sensitive to US domestic political cycles and regulatory
changes.

### Petrodollar Recycling and the Resource Curse

The petrodollar system created a self-reinforcing cycle. Oil-exporting
countries accumulated vast dollar reserves, which they reinvested in
US Treasury bonds and Western financial assets -- "petrodollar
recycling." This gave the United States cheap financing for its trade
deficits and reinforced dollar hegemony. In return, the US provided
security guarantees to Gulf states, creating a strategic compact that
has anchored Middle Eastern geopolitics for half a century.

For oil-rich states, however, hydrocarbon wealth often proved a curse
rather than a blessing. The "resource curse" describes the paradox
that countries with abundant natural resource wealth tend to have
worse development outcomes than resource-poor peers. The mechanisms
include Dutch disease (resource exports appreciate the currency,
making other sectors uncompetitive), governance degradation (resource
rents reduce the need for taxation, weakening the social contract
between state and citizens), and conflict (control over resource
revenues becomes the primary prize of political competition). Nigeria,
Venezuela, Angola, and Libya are canonical examples. The handful of
countries that escaped the resource curse -- Norway being the
exemplar -- did so through strong institutions established before the
oil was discovered, sovereign wealth funds that ring-fenced revenues
from political interference, and deliberate industrial policy to
maintain non-oil sectors.

### Critical Minerals: The New Geography of Power

The clean energy transition is creating an entirely new resource
geography. Electric vehicle batteries require lithium, cobalt, nickel,
and graphite. Wind turbines and electric motors require rare earth
elements for permanent magnets. Solar panels require silver, copper,
and polysilicon. The IEA projects that under a net-zero scenario,
lithium demand could increase by over 40 times by 2040, cobalt demand
could triple, and rare earth demand could increase sevenfold (IEA,
2021).

The problem is not geological scarcity -- these minerals exist in
sufficient quantities -- but extreme processing concentration. China
controls approximately 60 percent of global lithium chemical
processing, 70 percent of cobalt refining, over 80 percent of
battery-grade graphite, and roughly 90 percent of rare earth element
processing (Goldman Sachs, 2023). This dominance is not accidental; it
is the product of decades of strategic state investment, lax
environmental enforcement that lowered costs, and deliberate industrial
policy. China's 2010 rare earth export embargo against Japan
demonstrated willingness to weaponize this position.

The West is scrambling to diversify. The US launched the Minerals
Security Partnership (MSP) in 2022, upgraded to FORGE (Forum on
Resource Geostrategic Engagement) in 2026, and committed over $30
billion in financing to build alternative processing capacity. Project
Vault, a $10 billion US Export-Import Bank initiative, aims to
establish a Strategic Critical Minerals Reserve. But building
processing capacity takes a decade or more, requires skills that have
atrophied in the West, and faces local environmental opposition.
Indonesia, which holds the world's largest nickel reserves, has
banned raw nickel ore exports to force downstream processing
investment into the country -- a model other resource-rich nations are
watching closely.

### Chokepoints and Transit Routes

Energy geopolitics is not just about who has resources but about who
controls the routes between producer and consumer. The Strait of
Hormuz, through which roughly 21 percent of global petroleum
consumption passes, is patrolled by the US Navy's Fifth Fleet and
guarded by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps on the opposite
shore. The Strait of Malacca, through which roughly 16 million barrels
per day of oil transit en route from the Middle East to East Asia, is
a chokepoint that China calls its "Malacca dilemma" -- the
vulnerability of having its energy lifeline pass through waters
controlled by the US Navy and its allies. This vulnerability is a
primary driver of China's Belt and Road Initiative overland energy
corridors, its naval expansion, and its strategic petroleum reserve
build-up.

Similarly, Russia's gas leverage over Europe was not just about
possession of reserves but about pipeline geography. The
Druzhba ("Friendship") pipeline network, built during the Soviet era,
locked Eastern Europe into dependence on Russian supply. Nord Stream 1
and 2, running directly from Russia to Germany under the Baltic Sea,
were designed to bypass transit states like Ukraine and Poland,
allowing Russia to maintain gas deliveries to its most important
customer while cutting off countries it wished to pressure.

### The Energy Transition as Geopolitical Disruption

The shift from hydrocarbons to renewables fundamentally alters the
structure of energy geopolitics. Fossil fuel geopolitics is about
concentrated resources in a limited number of states, creating
oligopolistic markets where a handful of producers can influence
global prices. Renewable energy geopolitics is about widespread,
abundant resources (sun, wind) combined with concentrated
manufacturing and mineral supply chains.

Solar panels and wind turbines can be built anywhere, reducing the
importance of physical chokepoints and producer cartels. But their
manufacture is concentrated: China produces roughly 80 percent of the
world's solar panels and dominates every step of the supply chain from
polysilicon to finished modules. The geopolitics of energy is shifting
from "who has the oil" to "who controls the supply chain" -- a shift
that favors manufacturing powers over resource-extraction states.

## Evidence

The empirical record strongly supports the claim that energy resources
drive geopolitical outcomes.

The 1973 oil embargo provides the foundational case study. A 2023
analysis by Krane and Finley documented that within six months of the
embargo, oil prices quadrupled, US GDP contracted by 3.2 percent, and
the S&P 500 lost nearly half its value. The political effects were
equally dramatic: the embargo fractured NATO unity as European allies,
desperate for oil, broke with Washington on Middle East policy. The
embargo also triggered the most significant energy policy response in
US history: the creation of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve (capacity:
714 million barrels), Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards,
and a permanent naval presence in the Persian Gulf.

LaBelle (2023), in a comparative study of energy weaponization
published in Global Policy, identified four enabling conditions:
interdependence, energy security concerns, neoliberal economic
structures, and sovereignty threats. Applying this framework to both
1973 and the 2021-2022 Russia-Europe gas crisis, LaBelle found that
gas proved a more effective weapon than oil because pipeline
infrastructure creates bilateral lock-in that fungible oil markets do
not. Europe was unable to quickly replace Russian gas because LNG
terminals take years to build; by contrast, the global oil market
rebalanced within months of the 1973 embargo as non-OPEC production
ramped up.

The Goldman Sachs Global Institute (2023) quantified the critical
minerals concentration risk. China accounts for 85-90 percent of
global rare earth mine-to-metal refining, refines 68 percent of
cobalt, 65 percent of nickel, and 60 percent of battery-grade lithium.
Goldman Sachs estimated that duplicating China's rare earth processing
capacity would require $30-50 billion and 5-10 years, assuming
permitting and workforce availability that do not currently exist in
the West. The 2026 Critical Minerals Ministerial, which launched FORGE
with $30 billion in commitments, implicitly acknowledged that the West
is starting from a position of severe disadvantage.

Atari (2024) documented how the shale revolution reshaped global
energy trade. US LNG exports to Europe rose from near zero in 2015 to
over 70 billion cubic meters by 2023, directly displacing Russian
pipeline gas. The share of global oil production controlled by OPEC
fell from 53 percent in 1973 to approximately 36 percent today, a
decline largely attributable to non-OPEC production growth led by the
United States, Canada, and Brazil. The geopolitical effect was to
reduce OPEC's pricing power and give consuming nations greater
security of supply -- though not immunity from price shocks, as the
2022 energy crisis demonstrated.

## Implications

For investors, energy geopolitics is not a peripheral risk factor but
a core structural force. Every energy company operates within a
geopolitical framework that determines access to resources, exposure
to sanctions, and vulnerability to supply disruption. The clean energy
transition does not reduce geopolitical risk; it transforms it. A
portfolio that replaces oil majors with lithium miners has traded
Middle East political risk for supply chain concentration risk -- and
the latter may prove more acute, given the extreme processing
concentration in a single country.

For policymakers, the central lesson of the past 50 years is that
energy security is bought, not granted. The US achieved energy
independence through a confluence of technological innovation (shale),
market incentives (high prices post-2008), and infrastructure (LNG
terminals) -- not through policy declarations. Europe's vulnerability
to Russian gas was a choice: decades of underinvestment in
alternatives, regulatory barriers to LNG terminal construction, and
the politically convenient belief that trade interdependence
guarantees peaceful relations. The 2022 shock forced a reckoning, and
Europe's pivot to LNG and renewables is the most rapid energy
transition in history, but the cost -- deindustrialization risk,
inflation, fiscal strain -- has been immense.

For the international order, the energy transition introduces new
fault lines. The US-China competition, already the defining
geopolitical dynamic of the era, now has an energy dimension: China's
critical mineral dominance gives it leverage over the pace of the
Western energy transition. Countries that were marginal in the fossil
fuel era -- Chile and Argentina (lithium), the Democratic Republic of
Congo (cobalt), Indonesia (nickel) -- are becoming strategically
significant. The resource nationalism of these states, following
Indonesia's successful model of banning raw ore exports to force
domestic processing investment, will reshape supply chains and create
new alliance patterns.

The Gulf states exemplify this complexity. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and
Qatar are simultaneously maximizing hydrocarbon revenues while the
window remains open and deploying those returns into the assets that
will matter when it closes -- a dual bet that has seen an estimated
$2.5 trillion in Gulf commitments to US technology and infrastructure.
This is not divestment from oil but geopolitical hedging: using fossil
fuel wealth to buy structural positions in the post-oil economy while
maintaining the strategic relationships that fossil fuels built.

The author's assessment is that we are entering a period of energy
geopolitics more complex and multi-dimensional than the relatively
simple OPEC-vs-consumers dynamic of the 1970s-2000s. There are now
three overlapping energy systems -- hydrocarbons, renewables, and
nuclear -- each with its own geographic logic, supply chain
vulnerabilities, and geopolitical alliances. The countries and
companies that navigate this complexity will shape the global order
for the next half-century.
