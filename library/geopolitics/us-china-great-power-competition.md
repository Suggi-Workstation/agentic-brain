# US-China Great Power Competition -- How the Defining Rivalry of the 21st Century Is Reshaping Global Order

The US-China great power competition is the most consequential
geopolitical dynamic of the 21st century, a multi-domain struggle
spanning trade, technology, military posture, and ideological
influence that is reshaping global supply chains, alliance systems,
and the rules-based international order. Unlike the Cold War, this
competition unfolds within deep economic interdependence -- the two
largest economies are simultaneously rivals and trading partners --
which creates a unique dynamic of "competitive coexistence" where
neither side can afford total decoupling. Understanding this rivalry
is essential for anyone navigating global markets, supply chain
strategy, or geopolitical risk, because it is the gravitational
force pulling every other regional dynamic into its orbit.

## Background

The US-China competition did not begin with the Trump administration's
tariffs in 2018, though that marked a visible inflection point. Its
roots trace back to China's economic reforms under Deng Xiaoping
(1978), its accession to the WTO (2001), and the subsequent growth
that elevated China from a developing economy to the world's
second-largest. For two decades after China joined the WTO, the
prevailing US assumption was that economic integration would lead to
political liberalization -- the "engagement thesis." That thesis
collapsed as China's economy grew without democratization and as its
assertiveness under Xi Jinping increased on multiple fronts:
territorial claims in the South China Sea, the Belt and Road
Initiative (2013), military modernization, and technology ambitions
under the "Made in China 2025" industrial policy.

The turning point in US policy came with the 2017 National Security
Strategy, which formally designated China as a "strategic competitor"
and identified great power competition as the central challenge to US
interests. This bipartisan consensus hardened through the Biden
administration (2021-2025) and into the second Trump administration
(2025-present), producing an increasingly structured but volatile
rivalry conducted through tariffs, technology export controls,
military deterrence, and competing alliance-building in the
Indo-Pacific.

## Core Concepts

### Competitive Coexistence

Unlike the Cold War binary of two ideologically opposed blocs largely
separated from each other economically, the US and China are deeply
interwoven. Bilateral trade in goods exceeded $575 billion in 2024
despite years of tariffs. China holds approximately $770 billion in
US Treasury securities (as of 2025). Thousands of US companies maintain
operations in China, and Chinese demand is critical for US technology,
agricultural, and energy exports. This interdependence means neither
side can pursue "decoupling" as an absolute goal -- the real dynamic
is "de-risking": selective decoupling in strategic sectors like
semiconductors while preserving economic ties in other areas.

### Technology as the Central Battleground

While the Cold War was defined by nuclear competition, the US-China
rivalry is defined by competition for technological supremacy.
Semiconductors, artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and
biotechnology are the high ground both sides seek to control. US
export controls (October 2022 onward) have systematically restricted
China's access to advanced chips and chip-making equipment. China has
responded with massive state investment -- over $150 billion in
subsidies through its National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment
Fund -- and achieved approximately 28% semiconductor self-sufficiency
by Q4 2025, up from 16% in 2024. The technology war has created two
emerging "chip blocs": a US-led alliance (Chip 4: US, Japan, South
Korea, Taiwan) and a Chinese domestic ecosystem.

### The Indo-Pacific as the Primary Theater

China's core strategic ambition is regional hegemony in the
Indo-Pacific, which includes dominating the "first island chain" (the
maritime boundary running from Japan through Taiwan to the
Philippines), controlling access to the South China Sea (through which
roughly one-third of global maritime trade passes), and securing sea
lanes to the Middle East and Africa. The US strategy of "integrated
deterrence" seeks to contain China within this first island chain by
strengthening alliance networks -- the Quad (US, India, Japan,
Australia), AUKUS (US, UK, Australia), and bilateral alliances with
Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines.

### Taiwan as the Critical Flashpoint

Taiwan is the single most dangerous point of potential conflict. China
claims sovereignty over Taiwan and has stated its intent to achieve
"national reunification" by 2049. Taiwan manufactures over 90% of the
world's most advanced semiconductors through TSMC -- its "Silicon
Shield." China conducted military exercises simulating an encirclement
of Taiwan in 2023 and again in late 2025, while US intelligence
assessments warned of a potential invasion window by 2027. The 2026
University of Washington report "Averting War: Taiwan and US-China
Relations in 2026" identified the Taiwan Strait as "one of the world's
most volatile flashpoints."

### Trade War as Economic Statecraft

Since 2018, the US has imposed escalating tariffs on Chinese goods --
reaching an average tariff rate of 55% by 2025 under Section 301,
Section 232, fentanyl-related duties, and reciprocal tariff measures.
China has retaliated with tariffs on US goods and export controls on
critical minerals (gallium, germanium, antimony) essential for
semiconductor and defense manufacturing. A 90-day tariff truce
implemented in May 2025 reduced some duties temporarily but risked
renewal of full hostilities upon expiration. The trade war has reduced
US-China bilateral trade by roughly 30% from its peak, with the US
shifting approximately two-thirds of this gap to imports from other
partners, notably Vietnam, Mexico, and India.

### China's Military Modernization and Civil-Military Fusion

China's People's Liberation Army (PLA) has been systematically
modernizing across all domains -- land, sea, air, space, and cyber.
The 2026 UPenn Perry World House analysis documented how the PLA is
integrating AI across military operations: autonomous combat vehicles,
computer vision for target identification, drone swarms for coordinated
attack, and AI systems for decision advantage in wartime. China's
"civil-military fusion" strategy blurs the boundary between commercial
and military technology, complicating export control efforts. Chinese
AI companies like DeepSeek have trained models on US-designed hardware
despite export controls, demonstrating the limits of technology denial
strategies.

## Evidence

The FIIA report on great-power competition (2021) established the
theoretical framework for understanding the rivalry as a structural
shift in the international system, not a transient policy dispute. It
documented how geostrategic and geo-economic dynamics interact in the
US-China case differently from the US-Soviet rivalry, particularly
the role of supply chains, technology competition, and financial
interdependence.

The Strategy Central comprehensive report (August 2024) provided a
detailed snapshot of the competition across economic, military, and
diplomatic domains. It documented continued escalation in export
controls and trade disputes during the first half of 2024, while
noting the paradox that both sides continue diplomatic engagement
despite deepening rivalry. The report highlighted that China's
military capabilities have steadily narrowed the gap with the US,
though economic slowdown and demographic challenges present structural
headwinds.

The RAND Corporation (February 2025) identified specific strategic
imperatives where the US faces capability gaps: long-distance power
transmission infrastructure, critical infrastructure protection, and
engineering expertise. China's investments in these areas represent a
deliberate strategy to build leverage across multiple domains
simultaneously.

The 2026 Perry World House analysis at the University of Pennsylvania
documented the PLA's systematic integration of AI across military
domains through procurement document analysis. It revealed that the
PLA is pursuing AI-enabled capabilities in autonomy, decision support,
cyber operations, and space targeting through rapid prototyping and
short-timeline development cycles. The analysis also found evidence
that third-party distributors are circumventing US export controls
to supply chips to the Chinese military.

The semiconductor decoupling analysis (March 2026) documented the
emergence of two incompatible chip ecosystems. An ITIF report estimated
that full decoupling would cost US firms $77 billion in semiconductor
sales in the first year and reduce US R&D investment by 24% ($14
billion), while eliminating over 80,000 semiconductor industry jobs
plus nearly 500,000 downstream jobs. This reveals the paradox of the
technology war: measures intended to protect US technological
leadership may ultimately weaken it by denying US firms access to the
Chinese market and fragmenting global innovation ecosystems.

## Implications

For investors: The US-China competition introduces a structural
geopolitical risk premium that did not exist during the 1990-2016 era
of globalization. Companies with concentrated China exposure -- whether
in supply chains or revenue -- face regulatory risk from both sides.
Semiconductor supply chain diversification creates opportunities in
Japanese, South Korean, and emerging manufacturing hubs. The
"friend-shoring" trend benefits countries like Vietnam, India, and
Mexico. Taiwan risk -- the potential disruption of TSMC production
through conflict -- is a low-probability, catastrophic-consequence
scenario that most portfolios do not adequately price.

For supply chain strategy: The era of single-country sourcing is
ending. Firms must build redundant supply chains capable of functioning
if either the US or Chinese technological ecosystem becomes
inaccessible. The bifurcation of semiconductor standards means
technology companies face a costly choice: develop products for one
market or maintain parallel product lines.

For global governance: The competition fragments international
institutions. The WTO's dispute resolution mechanism has been
effectively paralyzed since 2019, when the US blocked appellate body
appointments. Competing infrastructure initiatives -- China's Belt
and Road versus the G7's Partnership for Global Infrastructure and
Investment -- create parallel development architectures. Climate
cooperation, one of the few areas of sustained US-China collaboration,
is increasingly threatened by broader tensions.

For the international order: The competition is producing a "multiplex"
world rather than a simple bipolar order. Middle powers -- India,
Brazil, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Indonesia -- gain leverage by playing
both sides. The Global South increasingly resists being forced to
choose between US- and China-led blocs, as evidenced by the expansion
of BRICS and the non-aligned responses to the Ukraine war. The
competition may accelerate the transformation of the post-1945
US-led order, but the shape of what replaces it remains uncertain.
