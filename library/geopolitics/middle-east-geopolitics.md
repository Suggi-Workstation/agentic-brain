# Middle East Geopolitics -- How Oil, Religion, and Colonial Borders Created the World's Most Volatile Region

The Middle East is the world's most geopolitically contested region
not because of ancient hatreds or religious inevitability, but because
three structural forces -- hydrocarbon wealth, sectarian identity
politics, and borders drawn by departing empires -- interact to
produce persistent instability that draws in every major global power.
Understanding the Middle East requires seeing it not as a collection
of discrete conflicts but as a single geopolitical system in which oil
revenues fund proxy wars, colonial borders trap rival communities
inside artificial states, and external powers alternately exploit and
are trapped by the region's fault lines. The region is less a
chessboard than a pressure cooker: remove one lid and another blows
off.

## Background

The modern Middle East was born from the ruins of the Ottoman Empire
at the end of World War I. For four centuries, the Ottoman system had
managed the region's diversity -- Sunni and Shia, Arab and Turk, Kurd
and Persian, Muslim, Christian, and Jew -- through a flexible imperial
structure organized around religious communities (the millet system)
rather than national identities. When that empire collapsed, the
victorious European powers did not create nation-states that reflected
the region's organic communities. They drew lines on a map.

The 1916 Sykes-Picot Agreement, a secret pact between British
diplomat Mark Sykes and French diplomat Francois Georges-Picot,
partitioned the Ottoman Arab territories into British and French
spheres of influence. The agreement contradicted promises made to Arab
leaders (the McMahon-Hussein Correspondence of 1915-1916 had promised
an independent Arab kingdom in exchange for revolt against the
Ottomans) and to the Zionist movement (the 1917 Balfour Declaration
promised a Jewish national home in Palestine). When the Bolsheviks
leaked the secret treaty in 1917, the betrayal became public
knowledge, and Arab trust in Western promises has never fully
recovered.

The 1920 San Remo Conference converted the Sykes-Picot lines into
League of Nations Mandates: France received Syria and Lebanon; Britain
received Palestine, Transjordan, and Iraq. At the 1921 Cairo
Conference, Winston Churchill stitched three disparate Ottoman
provinces -- the Sunni center around Baghdad, the Shia south around
Basra, and the Kurdish north around Mosul -- into the modern state of
Iraq, installed a Hashemite king, and secured British access to
Mesopotamian oil. The borders created by this process were not drawn
to reflect ethnic, sectarian, or tribal realities but to serve
imperial interests: secure the Suez Canal, protect the route to India,
and control the oil fields discovered in Persia (1908) and Iraq
(1927).

The discovery of oil transformed these colonial arrangements into
objects of permanent great-power interest. Before oil, the Middle East
was strategically important as a transit corridor. After oil, it
became the global economy's single point of failure. In 1945,
President Roosevelt met King Abdulaziz Ibn Saud aboard the USS Quincy
on the Great Bitter Lake, establishing the foundational bargain of the
postwar Middle East: American security guarantees for the Saudi
monarchy in exchange for stable oil supplies and pricing in dollars.
This petrodollar arrangement, formalized in the 1970s, anchored the
dollar's role as the global reserve currency and gave the United
States a structural interest in Gulf stability that has shaped every
American administration since.

The Cold War layered superpower competition onto the region's internal
dynamics. The United States backed Israel, Saudi Arabia, and the Shah
of Iran as pillars of its regional strategy. The Soviet Union
cultivated relationships with Arab nationalist regimes in Egypt,
Syria, and Iraq. After the Soviet collapse, the United States emerged
as the region's dominant external power, a role that reached its apex
with the 1991 Gulf War and its nadir with the 2003 invasion of Iraq,
which removed the Sunni-dominated regime that had contained Iran and
unintentionally opened the door to Iranian expansion across the
region.

## Core Concepts

### Oil: The Geopolitical Resource Par Excellence

Oil transforms the Middle East from a region of modest population and
economic output into the central nervous system of the global economy.
The Persian Gulf contains approximately 48 percent of the world's
proven oil reserves and 40 percent of its natural gas reserves. The
Strait of Hormuz, a 21-nautical-mile-wide chokepoint between Iran and
Oman, carries roughly 20 percent of global oil consumption daily. Any
disruption to this flow sends shockwaves through energy markets,
financial systems, and the political stability of every importing
nation.

Oil wealth creates a distinctive set of political pathologies. The
"resource curse" means that states funded by resource rents rather
than taxation need not develop the extractive capacity, bureaucratic
competence, or political legitimacy that tax-based states require.
Gulf monarchies can fund generous welfare states, buy off dissent, and
maintain security apparatuses without the accountability that taxation
creates. This has preserved political systems that would otherwise
have been transformed by the economic development oil made possible.

Oil revenue also funds regional ambitions. Saudi Arabia's ability to
export Wahhabi Islam through mosques, madrasas, and charitable
foundations -- a multi-billion-dollar effort that shaped religious
discourse from Pakistan to Nigeria -- was made possible by oil wealth.
Iran's ability to fund Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas in Gaza, the Houthi
movement in Yemen, and Shia militias in Iraq and Syria depends on oil
revenues that survive even stringent sanctions. The proxy wars that
define the modern Middle East are, at their economic foundation,
resource-funded conflicts.

The strategic geography of oil creates its own geopolitics. Iran's
position on the eastern shore of the Persian Gulf gives it the ability
to threaten the Strait of Hormuz, a capability it has exercised
periodically as a deterrent against external pressure. The concentration
of Saudi Arabia's oil infrastructure in its Eastern Province, home to
its marginalized Shia minority, creates a structural vulnerability that
the Kingdom's security policy has been organized around for decades.
The American naval presence in Bahrain (the Fifth Fleet) exists
primarily to ensure freedom of navigation through Hormuz, a mission
that has made the US Navy the de facto guarantor of global energy
security.

### Religion: The Sectarian Divide as Geopolitical Weapon

The Sunni-Shia schism dates to the seventh century and the succession
dispute following the Prophet Muhammad's death: Sunnis believed the
leader should be chosen by consensus (leading to the Caliphate), while
Shias believed leadership should remain within the Prophet's family
(leading to the Imamate under Ali and his descendants). For most of
Islamic history, this theological difference did not produce the kind
of sectarian violence that characterizes the modern Middle East.
Communities coexisted, intermarried, and shared mosques in many parts
of the region.

What changed was the weaponization of sectarian identity by states
seeking regional dominance. The 1979 Iranian Revolution was the
catalyst. When Ayatollah Khomeini overthrew the Shah and established
an Islamic Republic built on Shia clerical rule, he explicitly
exported revolution -- calling for the overthrow of "corrupt" Sunni
monarchies and positioning Iran as the leader of the Muslim world's
oppressed. Saudi Arabia, as the custodian of Islam's two holiest sites
and the world's leading Sunni power, saw this as an existential
challenge. The sectarian Cold War that followed was not primarily
about theology; it was about which country would dominate the Islamic
world and, by extension, the geopolitics of the entire region.

This rivalry plays out through proxies. Iran backs Hezbollah in
Lebanon (a Shia militia-turned-political-party that is now the
strongest military force in the country), the Assad regime in Syria
(Alawite, a Shia offshoot), Hamas in Gaza (Sunni but aligned with
Iran's anti-Israel agenda), the Houthi movement in Yemen (Zaidi Shia),
and Shia militias in Iraq that effectively control the state's
security apparatus. Saudi Arabia counters through support for Sunni
governments and movements across the region: the Lebanese government,
Syrian opposition groups (until their defeat), the internationally
recognized Yemeni government, and Sunni political forces in Iraq.

The sectarian framing of these conflicts serves both sides. For Iran,
presenting itself as the defender of Shia communities justifies
intervention in Arab states and provides a transnational constituency.
For Saudi Arabia, framing Iranian expansion as a Shia threat mobilizes
Sunni solidarity and provides cover for its own interventions.
Academic research increasingly confirms what regional analysts have
long observed: sectarianism is a tool, not the cause, of the
Saudi-Iranian rivalry. Both states intervene where their strategic
interests demand, and deploy sectarian rhetoric only when it serves
those interests. The Syrian Civil War is perhaps the clearest
illustration: a popular uprising against a repressive regime became a
sectarian conflict because external patrons on both sides poured in
arms, money, and fighters along sectarian lines.

### The Legacy of Empire: Artificial States, Real Conflicts

The Sykes-Picot borders did not cause all of the Middle East's
problems, but they created the structural conditions that make
problems intractable. By forcing disparate communities into unitary
states ruled by minorities (Sunnis in Iraq, Alawites in Syria,
Hashemites in Jordan), the colonial powers built instability into the
DNA of every state they created.

Iraq is the archetypal example. Churchill's 1921 creation combined
three groups -- Kurds in the north, Sunni Arabs in the center, Shia
Arabs in the south -- that had no shared national identity and no
desire to share a state. The British installed a Sunni monarchy to
rule over a Shia majority and a Kurdish minority, establishing a
pattern of minority rule that persisted through Saddam Hussein's
regime and created the conditions for the sectarian civil war that
followed the 2003 American invasion. When ISIS swept across northern
Iraq in 2014, it explicitly framed its campaign as erasing the
"Sykes-Picot border" between Iraq and Syria, a propaganda point that
resonated precisely because those borders were experienced as
illegitimate impositions.

The Kurdish question is perhaps the most direct consequence of
colonial border-making. Roughly 40 million Kurds, concentrated in a
contiguous territory spanning Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, were
promised a state in the 1920 Treaty of Sevres but were left stateless
by the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne, which made no mention of Kurdistan.
Kurds today live as minorities in four states, each of which has
alternately repressed them and used them as leverage against the
others. The Kurdish experience encapsulates the Sykes-Picot legacy: a
people divided by lines drawn without their consent, their aspirations
for self-determination perpetually subordinated to the interests of
the states that contain them.

Lebanon illustrates how sectarian power-sharing embedded in colonial
borders creates durable instability. The French carved Lebanon out of
Greater Syria in 1920 to create a state with a Christian majority
friendly to French interests. That demographic reality has since
shifted -- Muslims now constitute a majority -- but the 1943 National
Pact that allocated political power by confessional quotas remains
largely intact. The result is a political system in which every issue
becomes sectarian, every census becomes a threat to the existing
distribution of power, and governance grinds to a halt when
confessional interests conflict. The 1975-1990 civil war, the 2005
Cedar Revolution, and the current economic collapse are all
manifestations of a state structure that was designed to manage
sectarian diversity but instead institutionalizes it.

## Evidence and Case Studies

The Iran-Saudi proxy war in Yemen provides a controlled experiment in
how oil wealth, sectarian identity, and state failure interact. In
2014, the Houthi movement -- a Zaidi Shia group from northern Yemen
with Iranian backing -- seized the capital Sanaa and overthrew the
internationally recognized government. Saudi Arabia responded in March
2015 with a military coalition, viewing a Houthi-controlled Yemen as
an Iranian foothold on its southern border. Eight years of war
produced what the UN describes as the world's worst humanitarian
crisis: over 377,000 deaths, roughly 60 percent from indirect causes
including hunger and disease. The Houthis, far from being defeated,
emerged with enhanced military capabilities including drones and
missiles that can reach deep into Saudi territory.

The Yemen conflict demonstrates the analytical limits of viewing the
Middle East through a purely sectarian lens. The Houthis are Zaidi
Shia, a branch distinct from the Twelver Shia of Iran, and their
grievances are primarily local: marginalization by the central
government, economic underdevelopment, and the corruption of the
Saudi-backed regime. Iranian support for the Houthis was initially
limited and grew only after Saudi intervention created a shared enemy.
The sectarian narrative -- Iran backing Shia proxies against a Sunni
coalition -- obscures more than it reveals, but it serves the
political purposes of all parties.

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict illustrates a different dimension of
Middle Eastern geopolitics: how an externally imposed settlement --
the 1917 Balfour Declaration and the 1947 UN Partition Plan -- created
a conflict that has drawn in every major power for over seven decades.
The 1948 war that followed Israel's declaration of independence
produced roughly 700,000 Palestinian refugees, a number that has grown
through generations to over 5 million registered refugees today. The
1967 Six-Day War left Israel occupying the West Bank, Gaza, the Golan
Heights, and the Sinai Peninsula, creating a permanent occupation that
has defined the conflict's modern phase.

The two-state solution -- an independent Palestine alongside Israel on
the pre-1967 borders with mutually agreed land swaps -- has been the
framework for every peace negotiation since the 1993 Oslo Accords. The
core issues remain unresolved: borders, the status of Jerusalem,
Israeli settlements in the West Bank (now housing over 700,000
settlers and rendering a contiguous Palestinian state increasingly
implausible), Palestinian refugees' right of return, and security
arrangements. The settlement expansion has been described by the UN
Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights as systematic,
documenting in March 2026 that it "renders the contiguous territorial
basis for a future Palestinian state increasingly implausible."

The 2003 Iraq War and its aftermath provide the clearest illustration
of how external intervention interacts with the region's structural
dynamics. The American invasion removed Saddam Hussein's Sunni regime,
but in doing so it dismantled the state that had contained both
Iranian influence and sectarian tensions. The Coalition Provisional
Authority's decision to disband the Iraqi army and purge Baathists
from the civil service created a security vacuum and a class of
disenfranchised Sunni men who would later form the core of the ISIS
insurgency. The resulting Shia-dominated government aligned with Iran,
transforming Iraq from an Iranian adversary into an Iranian ally -- a
geopolitical transformation that cost the United States approximately
$2 trillion and over 4,400 American lives to achieve the opposite of
its strategic objective.

## Implications

For global energy security, Middle Eastern instability is not a
regional problem with global consequences -- it is a global problem
that happens to be located in one region. The Strait of Hormuz
chokepoint means that any Gulf conflict immediately becomes an
economic crisis for every oil-importing nation. The 2026 Iran crisis,
which closed Hormuz to commercial traffic for months, demonstrated
that this vulnerability is not theoretical. Energy markets, global
supply chains, and the political stability of governments from Tokyo
to Berlin are structurally dependent on a waterway that Iran can
credibly threaten to close. This is not a bug in the international
system; it is a feature that every great power must factor into its
strategic planning.

For great power competition, the Middle East remains a theater where
American, Chinese, and Russian interests intersect and conflict. The
United States has been the region's dominant external power since the
British withdrawal east of Suez in 1971, but that dominance is
increasingly contested. China's Belt and Road Initiative treats the
Middle East as a transit corridor and energy source rather than a
security commitment, buying oil from everyone and taking no sides in
regional conflicts -- a posture that the Gulf states increasingly
prefer to American entanglement. Russia's intervention in Syria (2015)
demonstrated its willingness to use military force to protect clients
and project power in the region, while its growing relationship with
Iran and its role as an OPEC+ partner with Saudi Arabia give it
multiple levers of influence.

For investors, Middle Eastern geopolitics is not an exotic risk to be
ignored but a structural factor that shapes energy prices, currency
markets, and global economic stability. The oil price shocks of 1973,
1979, 1990, 2003, and 2022 were all triggered by Middle Eastern
events. The lesson is not that the region is uniquely unstable but
that its instability is priced into the global economy in ways that
periodically erupt. A value investor who understands the geopolitical
structure of the Gulf can anticipate energy price movements that pure
financial analysis would miss. A portfolio manager who understands the
Iran-Saudi rivalry can contextualize risk premiums that others treat
as random noise. Geopolitics is not separate from markets; in the
Middle East, geopolitics is the market.

For international order, the Middle East poses a fundamental challenge
to the Westphalian system of sovereign states. The Sykes-Picot borders
have proven surprisingly durable -- no UN member state in the region
has been formally dissolved despite decades of conflict -- but they
have not produced stable nation-states with the loyalty of their
populations. The result is a region of hollow states: borders that are
internationally recognized but internally contested, governments that
control territory but not legitimacy, and populations whose primary
identities are subnational (tribe, sect, ethnicity) or supranational
(religion, ideology) rather than national. This condition is not
temporary; it is the structural legacy of how the modern Middle East
was built. Until the region's populations develop the national
identities that the colonial borders presupposed, the gap between the
map of states and the map of peoples will continue to produce the
conflicts that make the Middle East the world's most geopolitically
consequential region.
