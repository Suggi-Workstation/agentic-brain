# International Institutions -- Why the UN, WTO, and IMF Were Built to Prevent Another World War, and Why They Are Now Failing at Their Founding Mission

The United Nations, the World Trade Organization, and the International
Monetary Fund are the three pillars of the post-World War II
international order -- an order deliberately designed to prevent the
return of great-power war by embedding sovereign states in a web of
rules, norms, and mutual obligations. Yet all three institutions are
now in crisis: the UN Security Council is paralyzed by great-power veto
competition, the WTO's dispute settlement system has been nonfunctional
since 2019, and the IMF faces mounting challenges to its legitimacy as
rising powers demand voting shares that reflect twenty-first-century
economic realities rather than the 1944 balance of power. Understanding
how these institutions were designed, how they distribute power, and
why they are straining under contemporary pressures is not merely an
exercise in institutional history -- it is the key to understanding
whether the rules-based international order can survive the return of
great-power competition.

## Background

The architecture of the postwar international order was not built on
abstract ideals. It was built on a very specific diagnosis of what had
gone wrong between 1914 and 1945. The architects -- principally American
and British officials working between 1941 and 1945 -- believed that
the Second World War had been caused by three interrelated failures:
the collapse of collective security after the League of Nations proved
toothless in the face of Axis aggression, the descent into protectionism
and competitive devaluation that followed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of
1930, and the absence of any mechanism to stabilize the international
monetary system after the gold standard disintegrated. Each of the three
Bretton Woods institutions plus the UN was designed to address one of
these specific failures.

The United Nations was conceived first. The Atlantic Charter of August
1941, signed by Roosevelt and Churchill before the United States had
even entered the war, called for "a wider and permanent system of
general security." This became the UN, formally established in San
Francisco in June 1945 with 51 founding members. Unlike the League,
which required unanimous consent for action, the UN concentrated
enforcement power in a Security Council where five permanent members --
the victorious Allied powers -- held veto authority. The logic was
brutally realist: the League had failed because great powers could
simply ignore it. The UN would work because the great powers were baked
into its enforcement mechanism and given every incentive to stay. The
trade-off, which was well understood at the time, was that any one of
those five powers could paralyze the system when its own interests were
at stake.

The IMF and the World Bank were designed the previous summer, at a
conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in July 1944 -- before the
war was even over. Forty-four Allied nations sent delegates. The
conference was dominated by two men: John Maynard Keynes, representing
a desperately indebted Britain, and Harry Dexter White, representing
an ascendant United States. Keynes wanted a genuinely international
currency ("bancor") and a clearing union with substantial lending
capacity. White wanted a dollar-based system that would lock in American
financial dominance. White won. The IMF was given three missions:
promote international monetary cooperation, facilitate the expansion of
trade, and provide short-term loans to countries facing balance of
payments crises -- but only on condition that they implement policy
reforms. This conditionality principle would become the IMF's most
controversial feature.

The third pillar -- trade -- was supposed to be an International Trade
Organization, but the US Congress refused to ratify the Havana Charter
in 1950. What emerged instead was the General Agreement on Tariffs and
Trade (GATT), a provisional treaty that lacked an institutional
framework. The GATT functioned through successive "rounds" of
negotiation for nearly five decades, progressively reducing tariffs and
extending trade rules to new sectors. Only in 1995, with the conclusion
of the Uruguay Round, did the World Trade Organization finally come into
existence as a permanent institution with a binding dispute settlement
mechanism and a mandate covering services, intellectual property, and
agriculture alongside manufactured goods.

The original architecture reflected the power realities of 1945. The
UN Security Council's permanent five were the victors of World War II.
The IMF's voting shares were based on quota contributions, giving the
United States an effective veto with 17 percent of the vote (major
decisions require 85 percent approval). The GATT/WTO negotiations were
dominated by the "Quad" -- the US, the European Community, Japan, and
Canada. These arrangements were stable for as long as the underlying
distribution of power remained stable. It no longer does.

## Core Concepts

### The UN Security Council: The Paradox of Great-Power Peacekeeping

The Security Council is the most concentrated institutional expression
of great-power privilege in the international system, and it works
precisely because of -- not despite -- that concentration. The Council
has 15 members: five permanent (the United States, the United Kingdom,
France, Russia, and China) with veto power, and ten non-permanent
members elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms on a
geographic rotation. The veto is the Council's defining feature. It
means that no substantive resolution can pass without the affirmative
vote -- or at least the abstention -- of all five permanent members.

The veto was designed to solve a specific problem: the League of Nations
had collapsed in the 1930s because it could not enforce collective
security against great powers. Japan invaded Manchuria and simply walked
out of the League. Italy invaded Ethiopia and faced only half-hearted
sanctions. The UN's founders reasoned that enforcement against a great
power was impossible anyway -- if a permanent member was determined to
act, no resolution would stop it. The veto institutionalized this
reality. It gave the great powers a guarantee that the UN would never
be used against their vital interests, which was the necessary condition
for them to join at all.

The result is a system where the Security Council can act decisively
when the permanent members agree -- as it did in authorizing the use
of force to reverse Iraq's invasion of Kuwait in 1990, or in
establishing peacekeeping missions in dozens of post-conflict states --
but is paralyzed whenever a permanent member's interests are directly
implicated. Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine illustrated the limits
dramatically: Russia vetoed every Security Council resolution
condemning its own actions, reducing the Council's role to a forum for
rhetorical condemnation rather than enforcement. Similarly, the United
States has repeatedly vetoed resolutions critical of Israel, and China
has blocked action on human rights situations it considers internal
affairs.

The veto has been used over 300 times since 1945. The Soviet
Union/Russia leads with roughly 160 vetoes, followed by the United
States with approximately 90. The pattern reveals the veto's evolution
from a Cold War instrument of bloc discipline into a tool for
protecting narrow national interests. The French-Mexican initiative
and the ACT (Accountability, Coherence and Transparency) group have
proposed voluntary veto restraint in cases of mass atrocities, but
these remain non-binding political commitments with no enforcement
mechanism. As of 2025, the Security Council's paralysis has become,
in the words of General Assembly President Annalena Baerbock, the
"poster child" for wider global gridlock.

### The WTO Dispute Settlement System: How Binding Arbitration Worked -- and Why It Broke

The WTO's dispute settlement mechanism was the most ambitious
experiment in binding international adjudication ever attempted. Under
the GATT, dispute resolution was essentially diplomatic: a panel would
issue a report, but any contracting party -- including the losing party
-- could block its adoption. This meant that powerful states could
effectively ignore rulings they disliked. The 1995 WTO Agreement
radically changed this by making panel rulings binding unless there
was a consensus to reject them -- a "negative consensus" rule that
effectively made adoption automatic. A losing party could appeal to a
standing Appellate Body of seven members, whose rulings were also
adopted automatically. If the losing party failed to comply, the
winning party could impose retaliatory tariffs authorized by the
Dispute Settlement Body.

The system worked remarkably well for two decades. Between 1995 and
2019, the WTO handled over 590 disputes -- far more than any other
international tribunal. Major powers regularly complied with rulings
that went against them. The United States lost cases on steel tariffs,
cotton subsidies, and tax provisions for foreign sales corporations
and -- however grudgingly -- brought its policies into compliance. The
European Union lost cases on banana imports and genetically modified
organisms. Even China, which joined the WTO in 2001, became an active
and generally compliant participant.

The system broke because of a specific American grievance: the charge
of judicial overreach. From the Obama administration onward, US trade
officials argued that the Appellate Body had exceeded its mandate by
"filling gaps" in WTO agreements through expansive interpretations,
creating new obligations that member states had never negotiated. They
cited cases where the Appellate Body had ruled on issues the US
considered deliberately left ambiguous. The Trump administration
operationalized this grievance by blocking all new appointments to the
Appellate Body starting in 2017. By December 2019, the Body's terms had
expired to the point where it lacked the quorum of three members
necessary to hear appeals. It has been nonfunctional ever since.

The practical consequence is that any WTO member can now escape an
adverse panel ruling by simply filing an appeal into the void --
"appealing into the void" has become the standard tactic of states
that lose. A Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement
(MPIA), led by the EU and including about 50 WTO members, provides an
alternative appeals mechanism, but the United States, China, and India
are not participants, which limits its coverage. WTO members at the
2022 and 2024 Ministerial Conferences committed to restoring a fully
functioning dispute settlement system, but progress remains blocked by
fundamental disagreements over the Appellate Body's proper role.

### The IMF: Conditionality, Quotas, and the Politics of Crisis Lending

The IMF's core function is to provide short-term financing to countries
facing balance of payments crises -- situations where a country cannot
pay for its imports or service its foreign debts. But IMF lending comes
with strings attached: the country must agree to a program of policy
reforms, a principle known as conditionality. The IMF's lending is not
aid; it is a bridge loan that comes with a policy playbook.

Conditionality has evolved substantially since the IMF's founding. In
the 1980s and 1990s, during the era of "structural adjustment," IMF
programs typically required borrowing countries to liberalize trade,
privatize state enterprises, cut government spending, and deregulate
markets. This "Washington Consensus" approach was deeply controversial
and generated a vast critical literature. Critics argued that
conditionality undermined national sovereignty, imposed one-size-fits-all
policies that ignored local conditions, and often exacerbated poverty
and inequality in the short term while the promised growth failed to
materialize. The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis was a watershed: the IMF's
insistence on fiscal austerity and interest rate hikes in countries like
Indonesia and Thailand was widely blamed for deepening the crisis. Joseph
Stiglitz, the Nobel laureate and former World Bank chief economist,
became a prominent critic, arguing that IMF programs often made things
worse.

In response to these criticisms, the IMF undertook significant reforms
in the 2000s and 2010s. It streamlined conditionality, focusing on
fewer, more targeted conditions. It introduced the Flexible Credit Line
for countries with strong policy track records, providing access to
funds without ex-post conditions. It expanded its focus to include
social safety nets and poverty reduction. A 2025 study by Apeti,
published in Economics of Transition and Institutional Change, found
that IMF conditionality programs do promote structural reforms in
developing countries on average, though the effects vary significantly
depending on the type of condition, the domestic political context, and
the initial level of development.

The more fundamental governance problem is voting shares. IMF voting
power is based on quota contributions, which are supposed to reflect a
country's economic weight. But quota reforms lag behind economic
reality. China's economy is now larger than Japan's and roughly 70
percent of the US economy, but its IMF voting share remains far below
America's. The 2010 quota reforms, which modestly increased
representation for emerging economies, were blocked by the US Congress
until 2015. The 2023 General Quota Review concluded without agreement
on further realignment. As Woods (2024) argues in the Oxford Review of
Economic Policy, the larger the divergence between the values and
purposes of the IMF's principal shareholders, the harder it becomes for
the institution to fulfill its mandate.

### Bretton Woods and the Dollar System

The IMF was designed as part of the Bretton Woods system of fixed
exchange rates, in which currencies were pegged to the US dollar and
the dollar was convertible to gold at $35 per ounce. This system
collapsed in 1971 when Richard Nixon ended dollar-gold convertibility,
but the IMF survived by reinventing itself. In the post-Bretton Woods
era of floating exchange rates, the IMF shifted its focus from
exchange-rate surveillance to crisis lending and macroeconomic policy
advice. The dollar retained its central role -- it remains the dominant
reserve currency, the primary invoicing currency for international
trade, and the medium for most IMF lending. This "dollar dominance"
gives the United States structural power in the international financial
system that exceeds what its GDP share would suggest, and it is a major
source of frustration for rising powers like China that seek to
internationalize their own currencies.

## Evidence

The record of these institutions is mixed, and the evidence for both
their effectiveness and their failures is substantial.

On the effectiveness side, the post-1945 period has seen no great-power
war -- a historically unprecedented 80-year peace among major states.
Whether this is attributable to institutions, nuclear deterrence, or
economic interdependence is vigorously debated among IR scholars, but
the correlation is striking. The UN has deployed over 70 peacekeeping
missions and, according to a 2008 RAND Corporation study, UN
peacekeeping operations are both less expensive and more effective at
maintaining post-conflict peace than unilateral interventions. Notably,
a meta-analysis by Fortna (2008) in "Does Peacekeeping Work?" found
that peacekeeping missions significantly reduce the risk of war
recurrence, with robust mandates producing the strongest effects. The
WTO dispute settlement system resolved over 350 disputes between 1995
and 2019 before its paralysis, and research by Bown and Keynes (2020)
for the World Bank found that compliance with WTO rulings was high,
even among major powers -- the US and EU complied with adverse rulings
in over 80 percent of cases. The sheer volume of cases itself is
informative: countries filed disputes because they expected rulings to
be enforced, which suggests systemic credibility. The IMF provided over
$650 billion in lending to nearly 100 countries since its founding, and
its crisis response during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and the
COVID-19 pandemic was widely regarded as swift and substantial. During
COVID-19, the IMF approved emergency financing for over 80 countries
within months and issued a $650 billion Special Drawing Rights
allocation in 2021 -- the largest in its history -- which provided
liquidity to the global financial system without traditional
conditionality.

On the failure side, the evidence is equally clear. The UN Security
Council's veto has been used more frequently in the last decade than in
previous ones, and it has blocked action on Syria, Ukraine, Gaza, and
Myanmar -- exactly the kind of mass-atrocity situations the UN was
supposed to address. The WTO's collapse of binding dispute resolution
has been accompanied by a return to unilateral tariff actions: US
tariffs on Chinese goods, Chinese retaliatory tariffs, EU carbon border
adjustment mechanisms, and a general fraying of the most-favored-nation
principle. The IMF's structural adjustment programs in the 1980s and
1990s were associated with increased poverty and inequality in multiple
cases, most notably in sub-Saharan Africa, where per-capita income
stagnated during two decades of IMF-guided reforms. A 2016 study by
Stubbs and King in the Review of International Political Economy found
that IMF conditionality reduced developing countries' policy space
without consistently improving economic outcomes.

The author's assessment is that these institutions succeeded precisely
where power was aligned -- the Bretton Woods conference itself, the
1990 Gulf War coalition, the 2008 financial crisis response -- and
failed where power was contested. Institutions do not overcome power
politics; they channel it. When the underlying consensus among major
powers breaks down, the institutional superstructure cracks.

## Implications

For investors, the erosion of multilateral institutions has direct
consequences. A world without binding trade dispute resolution is a
world of higher and more unpredictable tariffs, which raises costs,
disrupts supply chains, and compresses margins for companies dependent
on cross-border trade. The weaponization of the dollar-based financial
system -- through IMF conditionality and US sanctions enforced via the
SWIFT payment system -- creates an incentive for countries like China,
Russia, and India to build alternative financial infrastructure, which
could over time reduce demand for US Treasury securities and weaken the
dollar's reserve status. The paralysis of the UN Security Council
increases the probability that regional conflicts escalate without a
credible multilateral mechanism for de-escalation, which raises the
geopolitical risk premium embedded in asset prices.

For policymakers, the choice is increasingly between reforming these
institutions or accepting their gradual irrelevance. Security Council
reform proposals -- expanding permanent membership to include Japan,
Germany, India, Brazil, and African representation -- have been debated
for decades but face a nearly insurmountable procedural obstacle: any
charter amendment requires approval by two-thirds of the General
Assembly AND all five permanent members. The IMF's quota reform has
proven only slightly less difficult, with the US Congress acting as a
persistent bottleneck. WTO reform faces a different problem -- the
fundamental disagreement between the United States and China over the
role of the state in the economy, which makes new rules impossible to
negotiate and old ones impossible to enforce.

For citizens of the international order, the stakes are existential in
ways that were not apparent during the stable Cold War and immediate
post-Cold War decades. The institutions built between 1944 and 1945
were designed for a world of sovereign states trading physical goods
under American hegemony. They were not designed for a world of digital
services, cross-border data flows, climate-linked trade measures, cyber
conflict, private military companies, and a multipolar power
distribution. The question is whether they can adapt faster than they
are being abandoned.

The deeper implication is about the relationship between power and
rules. The postwar institutions worked because American hegemony
provided a stable enforcement backbone: the US Navy guaranteed freedom
of navigation, the US Treasury underpinned the dollar system, and US
diplomats led the negotiation of every major multilateral agreement.
When the hegemon begins to see the rules as constraints on its own
power rather than as tools for exercising it -- which is the shift that
occurred under the Trump administration and has continued in various
forms since -- the entire institutional architecture loses its
foundation. This is the most dangerous moment for multilateralism since
the 1930s. The difference is that in the 1930s the institutions did not
yet exist; today they exist but are being hollowed out from within.
Whether they can survive the passing of unipolarity is the defining
question of the next decade.
