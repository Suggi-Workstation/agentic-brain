# International Institutions -- The UN, WTO, and IMF Channel Power but Cannot Replace Political Consent

The United Nations, World Trade Organization, and International Monetary Fund reduce the cost of cooperation by supplying rules, forums, information, finance, and procedures for collective action, but none can make major powers cooperate against their perceived vital interests. They were created at different times for different mandates, and their current strains are specific rather than evidence that one unified postwar system has simply stopped working [1][10][15][24].

## Background

The UN, IMF, and WTO are often grouped as pillars of a post-1945 order, but that shorthand can obscure their distinct origins. The UN Charter was signed in San Francisco on 26 June 1945, entered into force on 24 October 1945, and made the maintenance of international peace and security one of the organization's purposes [1][2]. The IMF was negotiated by 44 Allied countries at Bretton Woods in July 1944 to supervise a system of exchange rates linked to the dollar, promote monetary cooperation, support trade and growth, and discourage destructive economic policies; it began financial operations in 1947 [15]. The trade institution planned in the 1940s was an International Trade Organization, not the WTO. The Havana Charter failed to enter into force after the United States declined to seek ratification, so the provisional General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade governed much of world trade from 1948 until the WTO began on 1 January 1995 [9].

These institutions nevertheless shared a broad political premise. The governments designing the postwar order connected security with economic stability: collective security was intended to deter aggression, while monetary cooperation and less discriminatory trade were intended to reduce the economic conflict that had accompanied depression and war [1][9][15]. That does not mean the IMF or WTO was given a mandate to prevent war directly. The UN Charter gives the Security Council primary responsibility for international peace and security; the IMF's Articles concern monetary cooperation and balance-of-payments adjustment; and WTO agreements regulate trade relations and dispute settlement [1][10][15]. Their contribution to peace is therefore different in kind.

The UN design embedded rather than abolished great-power privilege. The Security Council has five permanent members and ten elected members. Article 27 requires nine affirmative votes for substantive decisions and includes the concurring votes of the permanent members, while post-1945 practice treats a permanent-member abstention as compatible with adoption. A negative vote by a permanent member blocks a substantive draft [1][3]. This arrangement was a political bargain: the organization could take binding decisions under the Charter, but the states expected to bear the greatest enforcement burdens would not be subjected to such decisions over their veto. The bargain keeps major powers inside the institution while making paralysis predictable when their core interests collide.

Bretton Woods embedded power differently. Quotas determine IMF financial contributions, access, and voting strength, so governance is weighted rather than one-country-one-vote [17]. The original monetary system fixed currencies to the dollar and made the dollar convertible into gold for official holders. The system did not survive unchanged: the United States suspended gold convertibility in August 1971, fixed parities gave way to more flexible exchange arrangements, and the IMF shifted toward surveillance, crisis lending, and policy advice [15]. Institutional continuity therefore came through adaptation, not preservation of the 1944 operating model.

Trade governance followed another path. GATT was a treaty framework with limited institutional machinery. The Uruguay Round created the WTO, expanded rules beyond goods into services and intellectual property, and established a dispute system in which panel and appellate reports were adopted unless members reached consensus not to adopt them [9][10]. This reversal of the old GATT consensus rule reduced the ability of a losing party to block a ruling. It also created a judicialized system whose authority depended on members continuing to appoint adjudicators and accept the boundary between interpretation and negotiation.

By 2026, the principal strains differ across the three bodies. The Security Council remains capable of action but is blocked on conflicts implicating a permanent member or its close partners [3][8]. WTO panels and consultations continue, but appellate review through the standing Appellate Body remains unavailable because all seats are vacant [10][11]. The IMF continues surveillance and lending, but its quota distribution and conditionality remain contested as economic weight, policy preferences, and creditor-borrower relations change [16][17][25]. The common issue is not a single institutional collapse. It is the widening gap between rules designed around one distribution of power and cooperation that must occur under another [24][25][26].

## Core Concepts

### The Security Council: authority constrained by the veto

The Security Council can adopt decisions that UN members agree to carry out, authorize sanctions, establish peace operations, and under Chapter VII authorize force [1]. Its authority is therefore qualitatively different from the recommendation-making role of most General Assembly resolutions. Yet substantive Council action requires nine votes without a veto by China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, or the United States [3]. The institution is strongest when those states see a common threat or accept a negotiated compromise, and weakest when a permanent member is a belligerent, patron, or direct target of the proposed measure.

This is a design constraint, not proof that the entire UN is inactive. In 1990, Security Council resolution 678 authorized states cooperating with Kuwait to use all necessary means to implement prior resolutions after Iraq's invasion; the vote was 12 in favor, two against, and one abstention [8]. In contrast, Russia vetoed a February 2022 draft that would have demanded an end to its attack on Ukraine, despite 11 affirmative votes. Two days later the Council used a procedural resolution, which is not subject to the veto, to convene an emergency special session of the General Assembly [8]. The comparison shows both sides of the design: concentrated authority permits strong action when permanent-member consent exists, while procedural and Assembly channels can preserve debate and political accountability when enforcement consent fails.

The General Assembly cannot convert a recommendation into a binding Security Council decision, but it can raise the political cost of a veto and document wider membership preferences. Resolution 76/262, adopted in 2022, created a standing mandate for an Assembly meeting within ten working days after a Security Council veto, unless an emergency special session is already meeting on the matter [4]. This mechanism increases transparency; it does not repeal Article 27. Formal Charter reform is still exceptionally difficult because Article 108 requires adoption by two-thirds of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of UN members, including every permanent Security Council member [1].

Peacekeeping supplies a second channel between inaction and war-fighting. Since 1948, the UN has deployed more than 70 peacekeeping operations, ranging from observer missions to multidimensional operations supporting ceasefires, civilian protection, elections, and state institutions [5]. Peacekeeping normally depends on Security Council authorization, host-state consent, resources from member states, and a political process that troops can support. It is not an automatic enforcement arm. The failures in Rwanda and Srebrenica, acknowledged in UN reviews, demonstrate that an institutional label cannot compensate for an inadequate mandate, insufficient forces, or absent political backing [5].

### WTO dispute settlement: binding rules with a missing appellate tier

The WTO Dispute Settlement Understanding treats adjudication as a means of providing security and predictability to the trading system. A case begins with consultations. If consultations fail, a panel examines the challenged measure; a report is adopted unless all members agree not to adopt it or a party appeals. Where a violation is found, the preferred remedy is conformity with the agreement, with compensation or authorized suspension of concessions available under specified conditions [10]. This structure does not create a global trade police. Compliance still comes from domestic policy change, negotiated settlement, reciprocal pressure, and the value members attach to the system.

The system remains heavily used. As of late September 2026, 646 disputes had been brought since 1995 and more than 350 rulings had been issued [10]. The number should not be confused with 646 completed judgments: many cases settle in consultations, some share panels, and others remain inactive. WTO statistics through the end of 2024 recorded panels in 376 disputes, panel reports in 297, and appeals in 195 original proceedings [10]. These distinctions matter because filing volume measures use, not compliance or institutional health by itself.

The appellate failure is narrower than saying that all WTO dispute settlement is nonfunctional, but it is serious. The standing Appellate Body was designed to have seven members; it can uphold, modify, or reverse panel legal findings. Vacancies prevented new divisions from being formed in December 2019, and the last sitting member's term expired on 30 November 2020 [11]. The United States had raised objections across administrations concerning missed deadlines, review of facts and domestic law, treatment of prior reports, and interpretations it viewed as adding to negotiated obligations. The Trump administration's appointment blockage converted those objections into institutional paralysis, and subsequent US administrations had not restored appointments by September 2026 [11][12]. A losing party can consequently appeal a panel report to a body unable to hear it, preventing adoption unless the parties agree on another path.

Some members created the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement under Article 25 of the dispute rules. Its participants include China, the European Union, Japan, Pakistan, the United Kingdom, and numerous other members; the United States and India are not participants [11]. The arrangement matters because it supplies binding review among participants and commits them not to appeal into the void. It remains partial and explicitly temporary. Bilateral no-appeal agreements, Article 25 arbitration, settlements, and unappealed panel reports also keep parts of the system operating, but they do not recreate a universal appellate tier.

The WTO's negotiating function is strained but not extinguished. Members adopted the Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies by consensus in 2022, and it entered into force in September 2025 after the required acceptances [14]. At the March 2026 Ministerial Conference, members adopted some decisions but left major items for further work in Geneva and continued negotiations on additional fisheries disciplines [14]. The coexistence of a functioning new agreement, incomplete negotiations, and appellate paralysis is evidence against a binary verdict of either success or failure.

### The IMF: finance, conditionality, and unequal voice

The IMF's contemporary work has three main components: surveillance of member economies and the international monetary system, financing for actual or potential balance-of-payments needs, and technical assistance or training [15][18]. Lending provides time for adjustment, but most arrangements release funds in stages and link later disbursements to reviews of agreed policies. The borrowing government formally selects and implements its program, while the IMF conditions continued access on quantitative performance criteria, structural benchmarks, prior actions, and other review findings [16]. This structure combines liquidity support with creditor safeguards and inevitably raises questions about sovereignty, burden distribution, and policy ownership.

Conditionality is not uniform across facilities. Standard arrangements may require fiscal, monetary, financial, or structural measures judged critical to program objectives. The Flexible Credit Line instead uses demanding ex-ante qualification for countries with very strong policies and institutions and imposes no ex-post program conditionality [16]. Emergency instruments and concessional facilities also differ in purpose and terms. Treating every IMF loan as the same austerity package therefore obscures the design actually used, while treating formal country ownership as proof of unconstrained choice ignores the bargaining power created by a financing crisis.

Quota governance converts economic size and financial contribution into formal influence. In the IMF table current in 2026, the United States held 16.49 percent of total votes, China 6.08 percent, and Japan 6.14 percent [17]. Major decisions requiring 85 percent of total voting power cannot pass over United States opposition, although ordinary program decisions generally use lower thresholds [17]. The asymmetry is material: World Bank data put 2025 nominal GDP at about $30.8 trillion for the United States and $19.5 trillion for China, so China's economy was about 63.3 percent of the US economy at market exchange rates while its IMF vote share was about 36.9 percent of the US share [17][22]. Different quota formulas and basic votes mean GDP alone does not determine voting power, but the comparison explains persistent demands for realignment.

The Sixteenth General Review illustrates the difficulty. Governors approved a 50 percent equiproportional quota increase in 2023, which enlarged permanent resources without changing relative quota shares. As of 29 April 2026, 149 members representing 76.66 percent of quotas had consented, below the 85 percent effectiveness threshold, and the consent deadline had been extended to 15 November 2026 [17]. Work on the Seventeenth Review and quota formula continued. The episode separates two questions often conflated in debate: whether the Fund has enough durable resources and whether voting shares represent current economic weight.

The dollar system also requires distinction. The fixed dollar-gold regime ended in 1971, but the dollar remained the largest reserve currency. IMF COFER data for the first quarter of 2026 put its share of global foreign-exchange reserves at 57.13 percent, compared with 20.03 percent for the euro and 1.99 percent for the renminbi; the IMF cautions that quarterly shares also move with exchange rates and asset valuations [23]. Dollar clearing, correspondent banking, and financial messaging networks can give states coercive leverage, as the weaponized-interdependence literature explains [27]. IMF conditionality is not the same mechanism as sanctions or exclusion from SWIFT. Combining them under one label conceals different legal authorities, decision makers, and transmission channels.

### Institutions as constrained bargains

The author's synthesis is that all three institutions are incomplete contracts among sovereign states. They specify procedures, delegate limited authority, create information, and make some commitments more credible, but they cannot pre-negotiate every future conflict or eliminate changes in power. Their apparent strength therefore depends on four linked conditions: the mandate must fit the problem, decisive members must prefer institutional action to unilateral action, the institution must possess usable procedures and resources, and affected states must see the distribution of voice and burdens as legitimate enough to continue participating [24][25][26].

This model explains why the same institution can be effective in one setting and blocked in another. The Security Council can authorize peace operations where permanent members accept the bargain but cannot bind a vetoing belligerent. WTO rules can settle ordinary trade disputes while appointment politics disable appellate review. The IMF can mobilize large-scale liquidity while quota reform lags behind changes in the world economy. Institutions channel power; they neither erase it nor merely reflect it passively.

## Evidence

### Peacekeeping changes some conflict outcomes

Virginia Page Fortna's 2008 study compared post-Cold War civil-war settlements with and without peacekeepers and supplemented quantitative analysis with interviews and case studies in Bangladesh, Mozambique, and Sierra Leone. Her analysis addressed the selection problem that missions may be sent to unusually difficult conflicts, rather than assuming that observed success reflected easy cases. The study found that peacekeeping substantially reduced the risk of renewed war and identified mechanisms including monitoring, reassurance, reduced uncertainty, prevention of accidental escalation, and support for political processes [6]. The finding supports a bounded claim: appropriately designed peacekeeping can improve the durability of peace after civil war. It does not show that the UN prevents every war or can impose settlement where the necessary consent and capacity are absent.

Hultman, Kathman, and Shannon tested a different outcome. Their 2014 American Political Science Review article used data on all civil wars in Africa from 1992 through 2011 and examined how the number and type of UN personnel related to battlefield deaths. Increasing numbers of armed troops were associated with fewer battlefield deaths, while police and observers did not produce the same result in that model [7]. The method and result matter because they connect effectiveness to mission composition rather than the mere presence of a blue flag. They also leave limits: observational conflict data require assumptions about deployment selection, and results for African civil wars do not establish identical effects in every theater.

The institutional record also contains severe counterexamples. UN history records reassessment after failures in Rwanda and Srebrenica, where mandates and member-state support did not protect populations [5]. The combined evidence rejects both extremes. Peacekeeping is neither reliably futile nor self-executing; effects depend on mandate, force composition, consent, logistics, and political strategy.

### Security Council outcomes track permanent-member alignment

The 1990 Kuwait case and the 2022 Ukraine case provide a controlled institutional contrast. Resolution 678 authorized cooperating states to use all necessary means after a sequence of Council demands concerning Iraq's invasion of Kuwait; no permanent member vetoed it [8]. In February 2022, a draft demanding that Russia stop its attack on Ukraine received 11 votes but failed because Russia voted against it [8]. The voting rule was the same. The position of a permanent member changed the available outcome.

The Ukraine response also shows that vetoes do not end all institutional action. The Council adopted procedural resolution 2623 by 11 votes to one, with three abstentions, to convene an emergency special session of the General Assembly; a permanent-member negative vote cannot veto a procedural decision [3][8]. The Assembly then adopted resolutions expressing the wider membership's position. Resolution 76/262 separately institutionalized Assembly debate after future vetoes [4]. These steps cannot authorize coercion equivalent to a Chapter VII Council decision, but they preserve agenda setting, public justification, and coalition signaling.

### WTO adjudication produced compliance but not automatic enforcement

WTO activity data show sustained demand: 646 disputes and more than 350 rulings since 1995 by September 2026 [10]. For the system's first decade, William Davey's review reported an 83 percent overall compliance rate with adopted decisions but only about 60 percent timely compliance; trade remedies, subsidies, agriculture, and sanitary measures were recurrent problem areas [13]. Those figures are not a timeless score. They show that a rules-based system elicited substantial implementation while delays and partial compliance remained material even before the appellate crisis.

The post-2019 evidence is institutionally different. Panels still form and reports still issue, but an appeal can prevent adoption because the Appellate Body cannot sit [10][11]. Participants in the MPIA have used Article 25 arbitration to preserve review among themselves, and China is a participant rather than an outsider to that arrangement [11]. This directly corrects the claim that China is excluded from the interim mechanism. The current configuration is fragmented: some disputes receive binding two-stage review, some end with an unappealed panel report or settlement, and some enter an appellate void.

Negotiation outcomes are similarly mixed. The Fisheries Subsidies Agreement was adopted in 2022 and entered into force in 2025, proving that members can still conclude and ratify a multilateral rule [14]. The 2026 Ministerial Conference left important draft texts and further subsidy disciplines for later negotiations [14]. A precise diagnosis is therefore selective cooperation under strain, not total negotiating impossibility.

### IMF programs show benefits, costs, and identification problems

Evidence on IMF conditionality is heterogeneous because countries seek programs during crises and because facilities, conditions, and domestic institutions differ. A 2019 European Central Bank review concluded that external and fiscal balances generally improve under IMF-supported programs, while evidence on growth and net capital inflows is mixed. It emphasized measurement limits and sensitivity to how studies correct for non-random program participation [19]. That finding cautions against attributing every post-program outcome either to the IMF or to pre-existing crisis conditions.

Disaggregated studies identify distributional risks. Forster and coauthors studied a panel of 135 countries from 1980 to 2014, used multivariate methods intended to correct for non-random selection into IMF programs and conditions, and found that mandated policy reforms increased income inequality on average; the study traced different pathways across condition types [21]. Apeti's 2025 study reached a different but compatible result for another outcome: IMF conditions promoted structural reforms in developing countries on average, with stronger effects when conditions were met and when domestic institutional quality was higher [20]. Reform adoption is not the same as growth, poverty reduction, or fair burden sharing. Together the studies show why condition content and institutional context must be examined rather than collapsing all programs into one verdict.

The IMF also supplies liquidity at a scale that conditionality-only accounts miss. In 2021, governors approved an SDR 456 billion general allocation, then equivalent to about $650 billion, distributed according to quotas to supplement members' reserves [18]. The IMF's 2024 annual report recorded $357 billion of lending to 97 countries since the start of the pandemic, including both standard arrangements and emergency support [18]. Distribution by quotas meant that the largest economies received the largest SDR allocations, which generated a separate debate about voluntary channeling to more vulnerable countries. The episode demonstrates both capacity and the distributive consequences of the governance formula.

### Representation has not kept pace with economic change

World Bank data for 2025 put nominal GDP at approximately $30.8 trillion in the United States and $19.5 trillion in China [22]. The IMF voting table still assigned 16.49 percent to the United States and 6.08 percent to China [17]. The derived ratios are approximately 63.3 percent for China's GDP relative to US GDP and 36.9 percent for its voting share relative to the US share. This does not prove a unique correct quota: formulas also consider openness, variability, reserves, and basic votes. It does verify the direction and magnitude of the representation gap that quota-reform debates seek to address.

## Implications

### Diagnose the broken function, not the entire institution

Policy should begin by identifying which function has failed. In the UN, the primary blockage may be a veto on one conflict while humanitarian agencies, peacekeeping missions, technical bodies, and the General Assembly continue to operate. In the WTO, the acute legal gap is universal appellate review, not the disappearance of consultations, panels, committees, or all negotiation. In the IMF, the dispute may concern quota legitimacy or the design of a particular program, not an absence of financial capacity. This functional diagnosis prevents reforms aimed at the wrong mechanism [10][17][24].

For the Security Council, proposals should be judged against the Charter's political constraints. Voluntary veto restraint in mass-atrocity cases, stronger use of General Assembly debate, and clearer public explanations can increase accountability without pretending to remove the veto [3][4]. Expansion of permanent or elected membership may improve representation, but any Charter amendment still requires ratification by all five permanent members [1]. Peacekeeping reform should focus on mandates matched to resources, force composition, political strategy, and measurable civilian-protection or recurrence outcomes, because the empirical evidence indicates that mission design affects results [6][7].

For the WTO, restoring a broadly accepted appellate function remains the central repair. A durable settlement must address members' stated concerns about deadlines, mandate, precedent, and the boundary between adjudication and negotiation while preventing a losing party from nullifying a panel result by filing an unhearable appeal [11][12]. Until then, MPIA participation, case-specific Article 25 arbitration, and pre-agreed no-appeal arrangements are reversible ways to preserve adjudication. They are bridges, not substitutes for a membership-wide bargain. The Fisheries Subsidies Agreement also suggests that narrower negotiations with clear shared interests can succeed even when a comprehensive package cannot [14].

For the IMF, resource adequacy and representation should be treated separately. The equiproportional Sixteenth Review would strengthen quota resources but leave relative shares unchanged; a later realignment requires agreement on who gains and loses formal influence [17]. Any formula must balance economic weight, member equality through basic votes, the protection of poorer members, and the willingness of large shareholders to supply resources. Because an 85 percent threshold gives the United States blocking power over major decisions, US domestic authorization is part of the global governance constraint rather than an external detail [17].

Conditionality should be evaluated condition by condition and outcome by outcome. Program baselines need realistic downside scenarios, conditions should be limited to measures critical to the program, and distributional effects should be assessed explicitly [16][19][21]. The evidence that some conditions promote reform does not establish that every reform improves welfare; the evidence of inequality costs does not establish that liquidity support is unnecessary [19][20][21]. A rigorous review asks who bears adjustment, whether financing is sufficient, whether domestic institutions can implement the plan, and whether social protections are operational rather than rhetorical.

### Separate monetary governance from financial coercion

Investors and policymakers should not use "the dollar system," "the IMF," "SWIFT," and "sanctions" as synonyms. Dollar reserve dominance is an observed portfolio fact; IMF lending is governed by its Articles, Board votes, and program agreements; SWIFT is a financial-messaging network; and sanctions arise from national or coalition legal authority. These systems interact, but they transmit power through different nodes [17][23][27]. The distinction changes risk analysis. A quota dispute affects IMF governance, an asset freeze affects legal access to reserves, a SWIFT exclusion affects messaging connectivity, and a reserve-diversification decision affects portfolio demand.

The 57.13 percent dollar share in first-quarter 2026 COFER data shows continued predominance, not immutability [23]. Quarter-to-quarter changes partly reflect exchange rates and asset prices, so a small share movement is not by itself evidence of strategic abandonment. The author's assessment is that repeated coercive use of central networks can create incentives for redundancy and alternative rails, but actual substitution depends on liquidity, convertibility, legal confidence, market depth, and network effects [27]. Claims of imminent dollar collapse or permanent immunity from diversification both exceed the evidence.

### Translate institutional strain into bounded scenarios

For investors, institutional risk should enter analysis as scenarios rather than slogans. WTO appellate paralysis can extend uncertainty over tariffs and remedies, but the relevant exposure depends on the product, jurisdiction, dispute path, and availability of negotiated settlement. Security Council paralysis can limit collective enforcement, but regional coalitions and General Assembly diplomacy may still alter costs. IMF governance disputes can slow resource reforms, while the institution can continue existing programs and deploy current facilities. Each scenario requires a transmission channel to revenue, cost, financing, asset access, or discount rates before it becomes an investment conclusion [10][17][27].

For governments, institutional hedging should preserve cooperation rather than accelerate fragmentation. Redundant payment links, diversified reserves, regional financing arrangements, and plurilateral trade accords may improve resilience. They can also split standards, reduce transparency, and weaken the common forums needed to manage spillovers. The worst outcome is not simply institutional stagnation; it is a sequence in which states respond to stagnation by building incompatible blocs that make later cooperation more costly [24][26]. Reversible interim arrangements are preferable where they keep a route back to universal rules.

For citizens, legitimacy depends on outputs and voice. A Council that cannot act on a permanent member's war, a trade system whose losing parties can block finality, or a Fund whose quota shares visibly lag economic change will face justified questions about representation. Yet abolishing procedure does not abolish conflict; it removes venues for evidence, negotiation, justification, and coordinated response. The practical choice is usually between imperfect institutions and more fragmented bargaining, not between imperfect institutions and apolitical rule enforcement [24][25][26].

The durable conclusion is conditional. International institutions work when their mandate fits the task, relevant powers accept the bargain, procedures remain usable, and participants view burdens and voice as sufficiently legitimate. Reform should therefore target the specific point where those conditions fail: veto accountability and peacekeeping capacity at the UN, final and accepted dispute review at the WTO, and quota legitimacy plus program design at the IMF. Institutions can make cooperation more credible and less costly, but political consent remains the scarce input they cannot manufacture.
