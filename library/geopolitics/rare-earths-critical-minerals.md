# Rare Earths and Critical Minerals -- The New Cartography of Power

Rare earth elements and critical minerals have become the strategic
chokepoints of the 21st century, transforming from obscure industrial
inputs into instruments of geopolitical leverage. China's near-monopoly
on processing and refining -- approximately 90 percent of global
capacity -- gives Beijing coercive power over the defense, clean energy,
and semiconductor supply chains of virtually every industrialized
nation. The 2025-2026 export control cycle demonstrated that this
dominance is not an accident of geology but the product of decades of
deliberate state policy, and that breaking it requires a whole-of-supply-
chain response that no single country can achieve alone.

## Background

The geopolitical significance of rare earth elements (REEs) was not
immediately recognized. The 17 elements -- scandium, yttrium, and the 15
lanthanides -- were discovered and first put to industrial use in the
United States. For most of the 20th century, the Mountain Pass mine in
California was the world's leading producer. But lower labor costs, less
strict environmental regulations, and a deliberate state strategy
enabled China to capture the industry beginning in the 1980s. Chinese
leader Deng Xiaoping's early-1990s declaration -- "The Middle East has
oil, China has rare-earth elements" -- signaled that Beijing understood
the strategic potential of these materials long before the rest of the
world did (Foreign Affairs, 2026).

The turning point for global awareness came in September 2010. A Chinese
fishing trawler collided with Japanese Coast Guard vessels near the
contested Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands. Japan detained the Chinese captain,
and China reportedly halted rare earth exports to Japan in retaliation.
At the time, 89.1 percent of Japan's rare earth imports came from China
(USGS, 2011). Although China officially denied imposing an embargo, and
subsequent academic analysis using the synthetic control method found
that the price spike was global rather than Japan-specific, the incident
exposed the vulnerability of concentrated supply chains (Kang, Lee, and
Oh, 2025). Japan accelerated diversification efforts, reducing its
dependence on Chinese rare earths from 90 percent in 2010 to
approximately 48 percent by 2017, with Vietnam emerging as a major
alternative supplier.

The 2010 episode also triggered the first major WTO case on rare earths.
The United States, the European Union, and Japan filed a joint complaint
arguing that China's export quotas and restrictions violated
international trade rules. In 2014, the WTO ruled against China, which
led Beijing to formally drop its export quotas in 2015. But the quota
removal did not end Chinese dominance -- it simply shifted the
instruments of control from quotas to licensing, environmental
regulations, and ultimately the export control law passed in late 2020.

The architecture of Chinese control was built methodically over
decades. Since 2008, Beijing prohibited the export of several
technologies used for rare earth extraction, refining, and magnet
production. In December 2021, it directed the formation of the China
Rare Earth Group, consolidating the industry under State Council
oversight. The industry was reorganized into two state-owned enterprises
with strict production quotas, cracking down on what Beijing called
"excessive exploitation" (RUSI, 2026). This was not a resource play --
it was an industrial strategy designed to capture the entire value chain
from mine to magnet.

The critical minerals category extends beyond rare earths to include
materials such as gallium, germanium, lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite,
tungsten, and antimony. The International Energy Agency's Global
Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 found that for 19 of 20 important
strategic minerals, China is the leading refiner, with an average market
share of 70 percent (IEA, 2025). China controls roughly 80 percent of
tungsten and 60 percent of antimony processing. This broader dominance
means that rare earths are not an isolated chokepoint but part of a
systematic pattern of supply chain concentration.

The 2025-2026 period marked a qualitative escalation. On April 4, 2025,
China's Ministry of Commerce introduced export controls on seven heavy
rare earth elements -- samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium,
lutetium, scandium, and yttrium -- plus all related compounds, alloys,
and magnets, in retaliation for US tariffs. On October 9, 2025, China
expanded controls to include five additional elements and, for the first
time, applied the foreign direct product rule -- a mechanism long used
by Washington to restrict semiconductor exports -- to rare earth
products. Foreign firms were required to obtain Chinese government
approval to export magnets containing even trace amounts of
Chinese-origin rare earth materials. By June 2026, China formalized a
reporting mechanism for violations, turning export controls from
paperwork into something closer to criminal enforcement (CSIS, 2026;
Modern Diplomacy, 2026).

## Core Concepts

### The Four-Stage Supply Chain

The rare earth industry consists of four stages: reserves, mining,
refining and separation, and downstream manufacturing. China dominates
all four but its dominance intensifies downstream. According to the IEA
(2025), China accounts for approximately 60 percent of global mining
output for magnet rare earths, 91 percent of separation and refining,
and 94 percent of permanent magnet manufacturing. The Diplomat (2026)
reports slightly different figures -- 50 percent of reserves, 65 percent
of mining, 85 percent of refining, and 90 percent of magnet
manufacturing -- but all sources agree that China's dominance increases
at each successive stage of the value chain. This structural feature
means that even countries with significant rare earth deposits --
Australia, the United States, Brazil, Vietnam -- cannot independently
produce finished magnets without Chinese processing or Chinese
technology.

### Light vs Heavy Rare Earths

The 17 rare earth elements are divided into light rare earth elements
(LREEs) and heavy rare earth elements (HREEs). The LREEs --
neodymium, praseodymium, lanthanum, cerium -- are more geologically
prevalent and form the backbone of most permanent magnets. The HREEs --
dysprosium, terbium, and others -- are scarcer and have essential
applications in defense, nuclear energy, and high-temperature permanent
magnets for EV motors and offshore wind turbines. China's chokehold is
even stronger for HREEs: it accounts for 84 percent of global dysprosium
mining (with neighboring Myanmar at 9 percent), and close to 100 percent
of refining of all heavy rare earths (EUISS, 2026). Without stable
access to dysprosium and terbium, neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet
performance collapses at high temperatures, risking both clean energy
deployment and defense readiness.

### Export Controls as Geoeconomic Weaponry

China's export control strategy has evolved from blunt instruments to
precision tools. The April 2025 controls on seven HREEs were not a total
ban -- Beijing maintained and even grew exports of finished rare earth
permanent magnets while restricting the raw materials that foreign
magnet makers need as inputs (RUSI, 2026). This distinction is critical:
China retains both the ability to influence through restriction and the
ability to export through dominance. The October 2025 expansion
introduced the foreign direct product rule, asserting Chinese
jurisdiction over any foreign-made product containing even 0.1 percent
heavy rare earth elements sourced from China, or produced using Chinese
rare earth technologies (CSIS, 2026). The IEA estimated that if China
fully implemented these restrictions, industries outside China would
lose approximately $6.5 trillion (Foreign Affairs, 2026).

### The Dual-Use Framework

China frames its rare earth controls through dual-use legislation --
materials valuable to both military and commercial sectors. The Ministry
of Commerce made clear that any requests to use rare earths for military
purposes will be automatically rejected. Starting December 1, 2025,
companies with any affiliation to foreign militaries are largely denied
export licenses (CSIS, 2026). This creates a structural asymmetry:
Chinese defense industries enjoy unrestricted access to domestic rare
earth supplies, while foreign defense manufacturers face bureaucratic
delays, denials, or outright bans. The F-35 fighter jet, Virginia-class
submarines, Tomahawk missiles, and precision-guided munitions all depend
on rare earth permanent magnets. The United States maintains strategic
reserves -- approximately 1,100 tons of lanthanum, 300 tons of oxide,
and 450 tons of magnets -- sufficient for roughly six months to one year
of military production (The Diplomat, 2026).

### The Green Transition Paradox

The clean energy transition intensifies rare earth demand precisely as
geopolitical competition constricts supply. A battery electric vehicle
requires 2 to 3 kilograms of rare earth permanent magnets. A large
3-megawatt direct-drive wind turbine may consume roughly 715 to 1,100
pounds of rare earth materials (The Diplomat, 2026). The IEA projects
that rare earth demand will grow significantly through 2030 and beyond,
driven by EVs, wind power, robotics, and AI data centers. This creates
a paradox: the very technologies needed to reduce dependence on
hydrocarbon geopolitics create new dependencies on mineral geopolitics.
Nations seeking energy independence through renewables find themselves
trading dependence on oil-producing states for dependence on
mineral-processing states.

### Diversification and Its Limits

Western diversification efforts face structural barriers that the
existing literature consistently identifies. New mining projects have
long lead times, averaging approximately eight years. Refining
development is even more nascent, with only a few industrial-scale
facilities operating outside China -- in Malaysia, the United States,
and Estonia (IEA, 2025). The IEA projects that based on all current
capacity and planned expansions outside China, rare earth diversification
will only meet 50 percent of non-Chinese demand for mining, 25 percent
for refining, and 20 percent for permanent magnets by 2035 (EUISS,
2026). The technical complexity of separation and refining -- many rare
earth ores contain radioactive elements such as uranium and thorium --
and the environmental costs of processing (one ton of rare earths
generates 13 kilograms of hazardous dust, 9,600 to 12,000 cubic meters
of hazardous gas, 75 cubic meters of wastewater, and approximately one
ton of radioactive residue) create political resistance in democracies
that China's state-directed model does not face (The Diplomat, 2026).

### The Price-Dominance Feedback Loop

China's market power operates through a self-reinforcing price dynamic.
When Western firms invest in alternative processing capacity, China can
increase production and flood the market, driving prices below the cost
of production for non-Chinese operators. The Mountain Pass mine in
California "sputtered" for years despite US government interest because
scarce profits, price volatility, and Chinese overproduction made
private-sector operations commercially unviable (Foreign Affairs, 2026).
This creates a predator-prey dynamic: diversification investments raise
costs, Chinese overproduction lowers prices, and the resulting losses
discourage further investment. Breaking this cycle requires sustained
state support -- price floors, offtake guarantees, equity investments --
that insulates non-Chinese producers from Chinese pricing power. The
2025 MP Materials deal, with its $110 per kilogram NdPr price floor
guaranteed for 10 years, represents the first systematic Western attempt
to neutralize this feedback loop through government-backed price
insurance (CSIS, 2026).

### The Technology Export Ban Layer

China's control strategy extends beyond materials to the technology and
expertise required to process them. Since 2008, Beijing prohibited the
export of rare earth extraction, refining, and magnet manufacturing
technologies (EUISS, 2026). A December 2023 ban on rare earth processing
technology exports further restricted the transfer of know-how. The
October 2025 controls barred Chinese nationals from engaging in overseas
rare earth projects without explicit authorization from Chinese
authorities, preventing the outflow of proprietary expertise (CSIS,
2026). This technology layer compounds the material chokepoint: even if
a country secures rare earth deposits and builds refining facilities, it
cannot operate them without Chinese-trained expertise or Chinese-
licensed technology. The technology ban transforms a resource problem
into a knowledge problem, extending the timeline and cost of
diversification.

### Deep-Sea Mining as Frontier

The seabed contains billions of tonnes of polymetallic nodules rich in
cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements, lying four to six kilometers
below the ocean surface. The Clarion-Clipperton Zone in the Pacific,
between Hawaii and Mexico, holds over 21 billion tonnes of nodules by
conservative estimates (Carbon Brief). The International Seabed
Authority (ISA) has issued 30 exploration contracts to 21 contractors
sponsored by 20 countries. China holds five ISA exploration contracts --
the most of any country -- including three for polymetallic nodules
(Congressional Research Service). In April 2025, the United States
signed an executive order aiming to fast-track seabed mining permits
outside the ISA framework, creating a potential regulatory race between
unilateral action and multilateral governance. Deep-sea mining
represents both a path to diversification and a new arena for
geopolitical competition over resource access. The environmental risks
-- disruption of deep-sea ecosystems that scientists barely understand --
create a tension between mineral security and ecological precaution
that the ISA regulatory process has not yet resolved.

### The Fish Trap Effect

The EUISS (2026) identifies a "fish trap" mechanism in China's export
control architecture. Beijing denies licenses to traders and importers
suspected of building stockpiles. Even companies whose China-based
suppliers receive "general licenses" face constraints: MOFCOM strictly
limits annual exports to no more than the average volumes shipped over
the previous three years, ostensibly to prevent stockpiling and re-
exports. Additionally, foreign companies are obligated to limit imports
to no more than 25 percent of the permitted annual volume per quarter.
A European company holding a general license that imported 9,000
kilograms annually in 2023-2025 could import no more than 3,000
kilograms in 2026, capped at 750 kilograms per quarter. This mechanism
prevents high-growth industries from scaling production using China-
controlled materials anywhere other than inside China. The fish trap
does not merely restrict access -- it structurally penalizes growth,
channeling expansion toward domestic Chinese manufacturing.

## Evidence

### The 2010 Senkaku Incident and Its Aftermath

The September 2010 collision between a Chinese fishing trawler and
Japanese Coast Guard vessels near the Senkaku/Diaoyu Islands is the
foundational case study of rare earth weaponization. China reportedly
halted rare earth exports to Japan, though Beijing officially denied
imposing an embargo and suggested that entrepreneurs had acted on their
own (New York Times, 2010). Academic analysis by Kang, Lee, and Oh
(2025), published in Resources Policy, employed the synthetic control
method to test whether the dispute caused a targeted price spike for
Japan. Their finding was nuanced: Japan's import prices of rare earths
from China did increase relative to a counterfactual, but in-space
placebo tests showed that some other countries experienced even greater
price surges, undermining the notion of a Japan-specific embargo. The
price spike was global, not targeted. Nevertheless, the political effect
was decisive: Japan cut its reliance on Chinese rare earths from 90
percent to 48 percent by 2017, with Vietnam emerging as a major
alternative supplier at 42 percent of Japanese imports at its peak
(Kang et al., 2025). This case demonstrates that even ambiguous
weaponization -- whether real or perceived -- generates diversification
responses that erode the coercer's leverage over time.

### The 2025 Export Control Cycle

The April 4, 2025 controls marked China's most systematic use of rare
earth export restrictions as a geoeconomic instrument. The Ministry of
Commerce required license applications for shipments of seven medium
and heavy rare earth elements, their compounds, alloys, and mixtures.
The immediate impact was severe: rare earth magnet exports plummeted 74
percent in May 2025 compared to the previous year. Carmakers in the
United States, Europe, and Japan struggled to obtain permanent magnets,
with some forced to cut utilization rates or temporarily shut down
factories (IEA, 2025). An EUCCC survey in October 2025 found that 60
percent of member companies expected "moderate" or "significant"
disruptions to their supply chains, and 13 percent expected production
stoppages. By early September -- five months after controls took effect
-- Beijing had approved only 19 of 141 rare earth license requests
(EUISS, 2026). European prices of rare earths reached up to six times
those inside China, creating a structural cost disadvantage for
manufacturers outside China (IEA, 2025).

The cycle also revealed the action-reaction dynamic of geoeconomic
warfare. After Washington imposed restrictions on jet engines and
semiconductor design software, China retaliated with the October 2025
expansion. When the United States retracted its measures and agreed to
further talks in June, China agreed to resume exports (Foreign Affairs,
2026). But stability proved temporary. The SUERF policy brief (2026)
modeled a scenario of persistent but partial restrictions lasting 18
months -- long enough to deplete inventories but short enough to limit
full-scale substitution -- and estimated US output losses of 0.3 to 0.6
percent, with automotive and electronics sectors most affected. China
experienced short-term GDP gains from bottleneck rents, but the models
suggest these gains are unlikely to be permanent as trade re-routing,
substitution, and investment in alternative supply chains erode the
benefits.

### Western Response: FORGE, Project Vault, and the MP Materials Model

The Western response has accelerated but remains fragmented. In February
2026, the United States hosted the inaugural Critical Minerals
Ministerial, convening 54 nations and the European Commission. Secretary
of State Marco Rubio launched the Forum on Resource Geostrategic
Engagement (FORGE), the successor to the Minerals Security Partnership,
with over $30 billion in mobilized financing and 11 new bilateral
frameworks. The EU's Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA) established
diversification targets and supply chain monitoring requirements. The
US Project Vault created a strategic minerals reserve (Informed Clearly,
2026).

The MP Materials case illustrates the state-capital model emerging in
response. In July 2025, the US Department of War invested $400 million
in equity into MP Materials, making the US government the company's
largest shareholder. The deal included a 10-year price floor commitment
of $110 per kilogram for NdPr products, designed to protect commercial
viability against Chinese overproduction that sinks prices globally. The
Office of Strategic Capital extended a $150 million loan to expand the
Mountain Pass, California facility with heavy rare earth separation
capabilities. The Department of War entered a 10-year offtake agreement
for 100 percent of the new magnet facility's output (CSIS, 2026). Japan
pursued a different model: JOGMEC, a state-owned corporation, supports
resource development through subsidies, debt guarantees, and public-
private partnerships. Japan partnered with India for joint rare earth
mining and with France on the Caremag project in Lacq, designed to
process recycled magnets and raw ore into individual rare earth oxides
(The Diplomat, 2026).

### Structural Barriers to Diversification

The Diplomat (2026) identifies a fundamental tension undermining
diversification: the conflict between friendshoring and strategic
autonomy. Countries with the technical expertise to process rare
earths -- Japan and South Korea -- possess almost no deposits. Countries
with credible reserves -- Australia, Canada, India -- possess little
technical expertise. Simultaneously, every nation wants to build
domestic processing and manufacturing for local value addition rather
than serving as a resource extraction location. The United States wants
diversification but simultaneously pursues homeshoring through
industrial policy. Europe talks about international partnerships while
pursuing strategic autonomy. These competing ambitions slow the
diversification that all parties agree is necessary. Independent
diversification efforts could paradoxically lead to further reliance on
China, as individual states undercut each other through competition for
the same limited expertise and capital (The Diplomat, 2026).

## Implications

### For National Security and Defense Planning

Rare earth dependency creates an asymmetric vulnerability in defense
industrial bases. China's dual-use export control framework structurally
denies foreign militaries access to materials that Chinese defense
industries enjoy unrestricted. The CSIS (2026) analysis notes that China
is scaling up munitions manufacturing at a rate estimated to be five to
six times faster than the United States. The F-35, Virginia-class
submarines, Tomahawk missiles, and precision-guided munitions all depend
on rare earth permanent magnets for motors, actuators, sensors, and
radar components. US strategic reserves can sustain military production
for approximately six months to one year (The Diplomat, 2026). If
diversification cannot fill the gap within that window, a sustained
export cutoff would degrade the ability to replenish precision munitions
-- a vulnerability with direct implications for Indo-Pacific contingency
planning. The defense implication is not that rare earth access will
determine the outcome of a conflict, but that it constrains the
sustainability of high-intensity operations that consume precision
munitions at rates exceeding peacetime production capacity.

### For Industrial Strategy and Supply Chain Resilience

The 2025 disruption demonstrated that rare earth dependency is not a
hypothetical risk but an active constraint on industrial production.
European and US carmakers drew down inventories and halted production
lines during the summer of 2025. One firm expected China's controls to
cost it 20 percent of gross global revenue; another projected losses
exceeding 250 million euros (EUISS, 2026). The SUERF (2026) analysis
identifies four policy lessons: policymakers should attend to supply
chain bottlenecks even when underlying markets are small; inventories
and strategic stockpiles can absorb temporary disruptions; investment in
recycling, substitution technologies, and alternative suppliers reduces
dependence; and international cooperation remains essential because no
single country can rapidly replicate the entire rare earth value chain.
The objective is not complete self-sufficiency but greater resilience --
reducing single points of failure to limit economic costs and lessen
the scope for strategic leverage. Less than 5 percent of global rare
earths are currently recycled despite technical potential for over 90
percent recovery of neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium
from magnet scrap (Rare Earth Exchanges, 2026). Closing this gap
represents the most actionable near-term diversification pathway.

### For Energy Transition Policy

The green transition creates a structural demand surge for critical
minerals that intersects directly with geopolitical competition. The IEA
(2025) projects that rare earth demand will grow significantly through
2030, driven by EVs, wind power, and AI data center infrastructure. The
policy challenge is that the technologies needed to reduce dependence on
hydrocarbon geopolitics create new dependencies on mineral geopolitics.
Governments pursuing net-zero targets must now integrate mineral supply
chain security into energy policy -- not as an afterthought but as a
structural constraint on the pace of transition. The IEA's finding that
planned capacity outside China will meet only 20 percent of non-Chinese
permanent magnet demand by 2035 means that the energy transition itself
is partially hostage to Chinese export control decisions. This argues
for parallel investment in mineral diversification and renewable
deployment, rather than sequential planning that treats supply chains as
a solved problem.

### For International Cooperation Architecture

The FORGE initiative, the EU CRMA, Japan's JOGMEC model, and bilateral
partnerships between France and Japan, Japan and India, and the US and
Saudi Arabia represent an emerging architecture of mineral security
cooperation. But this architecture faces the coordination problem
identified by The Diplomat (2026): everyone agrees that dependence on
China is dangerous, but they do not agree on who should replace China at
each stage of the supply chain. The friendshoring-versus-strategic-
autonomy tension means that allied nations simultaneously compete for
the same processing capacity and technical expertise. Breaking China's
chokepoint requires competitors to decide whether securing the supply
chain together matters more than owning it individually. The success of
diversification will depend less on whether competitor nations possess
the necessary resources than on whether they can overcome their
competing industrial ambitions. This is fundamentally a governance
problem, not a geological one.

### For Investors and Market Participants

For investors, the rare earth sector presents a paradox of strategic
necessity and commercial difficulty. The industry is small by market
value -- global trade in rare earths reached $4.2 billion in 2011 and
fell to $1.2 billion by 2014 -- yet it is strategically critical
(Foreign Affairs, 2026). Private-sector-led diversification has
repeatedly failed because of scarce profits, price volatility, and
Chinese overproduction that sinks prices below the cost of production
for non-Chinese operators. The Mountain Pass mine in California
"sputtered" for years despite US government interest. The solution
emerging in 2025-2026 is the state-backed model: government equity
investments, price floors, offtake guarantees, and strategic stockpiling
that de-risk private capital. Investors evaluating rare earth
opportunities must assess not just geology and processing economics but
the political durability of government support frameworks, which remain
the primary determinant of commercial viability in this sector.

### For the Global Trading System

The rare earth export control cycle exposes a structural gap in the
international trade architecture. The WTO ruled against China's export
quotas in 2014, but Beijing adapted by shifting from quotas to licensing
regimes, environmental regulations, and dual-use export controls that
are harder to challenge under existing trade law. The foreign direct
product rule -- long used by the United States to restrict semiconductor
exports -- was adopted by China for rare earths in October 2025,
creating a symmetry of extraterritorial assertion that the WTO system
was not designed to mediate (CSIS, 2026). The trade system's inability
to distinguish between legitimate national security controls and
geoeconomic coercion leaves importing nations with limited recourse
beyond bilateral negotiation and strategic investment. The CEPR analysis
of the 2010 dispute (Evenett and Fritz, 2023) found that even the
factual record of whether China imposed a targeted embargo remains
contested, illustrating how opacity in export licensing creates
plausible deniability that complicates WTO dispute proceedings. This
governance gap means that rare earth controls are likely to persist as
an instrument of statecraft regardless of WTO rulings, because the
enforcement lag between restriction and adjudication exceeds the
disruption window.

### For Middle Powers and the Global South

The rare earth chokepoint creates a strategic choice for middle powers
and Global South nations that possess mineral resources but lack
processing capacity. Countries like Australia, Canada, India, Brazil,
Vietnam, and African nations with rare earth or related mineral deposits
face a decision: partner with Western diversification initiatives like
FORGE and accept the conditionalities of strategic alignment, or
maintain commercial relationships with China's processing infrastructure
and risk becoming pawns in great power competition. The Diplomat (2026)
notes that Vietnam's refining future depends on an Australian junior
miner and a Korean conglomerate, while Morocco's critical minerals
partnership with Washington is, in practice, co-signed by the United
States. Indonesia, despite years of speculation, has no verified
commercial rare earth mining as of mid-2026, and its most plausible path
-- recovering monazite from tin waste -- depends on an industry where
approximately 80 percent of production operates outside the law (Modern
Diplomacy, 2026). These nations are not passive bystanders but active
hedges, seeking to extract maximum value from their geological
endowments while avoiding exclusive dependency on either bloc. Their
alignment decisions will shape the future geography of mineral
processing.
