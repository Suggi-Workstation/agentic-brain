# Trade Wars and Economic Sanctions -- How Interdependence Became a Weapon and Why the Global Economic Order Is Fragmenting

Trade wars and economic sanctions are not new instruments of statecraft,
but their scale, sophistication, and systemic consequences have
transformed dramatically in the twenty-first century. The post-Cold War
assumption that deepening economic interdependence would produce peace
has given way to a world where the very networks of trade, finance, and
technology that bind nations together are being repurposed as weapons
of coercion. Tariffs on semiconductors, export controls on advanced
chips, the exclusion of Russian banks from SWIFT, and the freezing of
nearly $300 billion in central bank reserves are not isolated tactics --
they are expressions of a coherent strategy of weaponized
interdependence that is actively fragmenting the global economic order
and accelerating de-dollarization. Understanding how these tools work,
when they succeed, and what their unintended consequences are is
essential to navigating a world where economic integration no longer
guarantees mutual restraint.

## Background

The intellectual foundations of weaponized interdependence were laid in
a 2019 paper by political scientists Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman,
published in International Security. They argued that globalization had
created a world of asymmetric network structures -- hubs, spokes, and
chokepoints -- that dominant states could exploit for coercive purposes.
The key insight was that interdependence is not symmetric: some nodes in
global networks (the dollar clearing system, the SWIFT messaging
platform, semiconductor supply chains, the internet's backbone) are
structural chokepoints that give the states controlling them enormous
leverage over others. Farrell and Newman identified two mechanisms:
the "panopticon effect," where controlling network hubs allows states to
monitor and gather information on adversaries, and the "chokepoint
effect," where controlling essential nodes allows states to deny
adversaries access to the network entirely.

This framework helped explain what had already been happening for
decades. The United States had used its control of the dollar-based
financial system to impose extraterritorial sanctions since at least the
1990s, when it fined foreign banks for violating Cuba and Iran sanctions
that had no legal force outside the United States. The 2012 expulsion of
Iranian banks from SWIFT, achieved through EU sanctions legislation
rather than direct US action over the Belgium-based cooperative,
demonstrated that even nominally neutral financial infrastructure could
be enlisted in geopolitical campaigns. But the real inflection point
came in February 2022, when Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine
triggered what one Financial Times analyst called "economic shock and
awe" -- an unprecedented campaign of coordinated financial, trade, and
technology sanctions that went far beyond anything attempted against
Iran or North Korea.

The sanctions on Russia included the freezing of roughly $300 billion
in Russian central bank reserves held in G7 jurisdictions, the
exclusion of major Russian banks from SWIFT, export controls on
semiconductors and dual-use technologies, and coordinated efforts to cap
the price of Russian oil exports. These measures were designed not
merely to impose costs on Russia but to degrade its military-industrial
capacity. In parallel, the Trump administration's trade war with China
(2018-2020, renewed and intensified in 2025) demonstrated that tariffs
could be wielded unilaterally as instruments of coercion rather than as
negotiating tools within a rules-based system. The December 2024 US
semiconductor export controls, which expanded restrictions to include
high-bandwidth memory chips and tightened the Foreign Direct Product
Rule, represented the most comprehensive technology containment
strategy since the Cold War.

## Core Concepts

### Weaponized Interdependence

Farrell and Newman's framework rests on the observation that global
economic networks are not flat or evenly distributed. They are
structured around hubs -- central nodes through which most transactions
pass. The dollar clearing system is a hub because nearly all
international trade is settled in dollars through US correspondent
banks. SWIFT is a hub because it carries roughly 42 million financial
messages per day between over 11,000 institutions. Taiwan Semiconductor
Manufacturing Company (TSMC) is a hub because it fabricates
approximately 90 percent of the world's most advanced logic chips.
States that control these hubs can use them as weapons.

The panopticon effect refers to the surveillance capability that comes
from controlling network hubs. When all dollar-denominated transactions
pass through US banks or their correspondents, the United States gains
visibility into global financial flows that no other state possesses.
This information asymmetry enables targeted enforcement of sanctions and
provides strategic intelligence. The chokepoint effect is more direct:
controlling an essential node allows the controlling state to deny
access. The United States can bar a foreign bank from the dollar system
entirely, effectively cutting it off from most international trade. The
Netherlands and Japan can restrict ASML and Tokyo Electron from
exporting lithography equipment to China, throttling its semiconductor
ambitions at a manufacturing bottleneck that no other supplier can fill.

### Tariffs as Coercive Instruments

Tariffs -- taxes on imported goods -- are among the oldest instruments of
economic statecraft, but their modern use differs in scale and intent.
Traditional trade policy used tariffs as bargaining chips within a
reciprocal framework: country A lowers tariffs in exchange for country B
lowering theirs, governed by WTO most-favored-nation rules. The Trump
administration's approach, in both its first and second terms, abandoned
this reciprocal logic. Tariffs were imposed unilaterally without WTO
authorization, at levels not seen since the 1930s, and with the explicit
goal of compelling trading partners to accept unequal terms -- reduced
access to the US consumer market in exchange for concessions on other
fronts.

The Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods, initially imposed in 2018-2019
and expanded under both the Biden and second Trump administrations,
covered hundreds of billions of dollars in imports. By late 2025, the
USTR had initiated a new Section 301 investigation specifically
targeting China's semiconductor industry practices, with tariffs set to
escalate on a predetermined schedule. The goal was no longer
reciprocity; it was strategic decoupling -- reducing US dependence on
Chinese supply chains and denying China access to the US market as a
source of revenue for its technological ambitions.

### Export Controls and Technology Denial

Export controls are restrictions on what goods, software, and
technology can be sold to specific countries, entities, or individuals.
Unlike tariffs, which raise prices but generally permit trade to
continue, export controls aim to stop particular transactions entirely.
The modern export control regime targeting China began in earnest with
the October 2022 BIS rules that restricted the export of advanced logic
and memory chips, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and related
software and technology. The December 2024 updates expanded these
restrictions by incorporating high-bandwidth memory (HBM) -- chips
critical for AI training -- and widening the Foreign Direct Product Rule,
which asserts US jurisdiction over foreign-made items that contain US
technology.

The logic chains are deliberately structured. Advanced semiconductors
enable AI systems; AI systems have military applications including
autonomous weapons, intelligence analysis, and cyber operations. By
controlling the semiconductor supply chain at multiple points -- design
software (US-based EDA tools from Synopsys and Cadence), manufacturing
equipment (Dutch ASML lithography machines, Japanese and US deposition
and etching tools), and the chips themselves -- the United States and its
allies aim to impose a permanent technology gap that China cannot close
through indigenous innovation alone.

### Financial Sanctions and Dollar Weaponization

Financial sanctions exploit the structural centrality of the US dollar
and the US financial system. Because the dollar is the dominant reserve
currency, the primary medium for international trade settlement, and the
denominating currency for most commodities including oil, every
significant international bank must maintain correspondent relationships
with US institutions to clear dollar transactions. This gives the United
States the power to designate foreign entities as Specially Designated
Nationals (SDNs), effectively locking them out of the global financial
system. Any bank anywhere in the world that processes a dollar
transaction involving an SDN risks losing its own US correspondent
access -- a penalty so severe that most banks comply preemptively.

The 2022 sanctions on Russia marked a qualitative escalation. For the
first time, the G7 froze the central bank reserves of a G20 economy --
roughly $300 billion -- denying Russia access to its own money.
Simultaneously, selected Russian banks were removed from SWIFT, the
Belgium-based messaging system that does not move money but is
essential for banks to communicate about transfers. While Russia was
not entirely cut off from SWIFT (carve-outs preserved energy payment
channels), the message was unmistakable: the global financial
infrastructure was not neutral, and countries that depended on it were
vulnerable.

The author's assessment is that the 2022 sanctions were simultaneously a
demonstration of American financial power and the catalyst for its
eventual erosion. China, India, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, and other major
economies watched the freezing of Russian reserves and concluded that
dollar-denominated assets were no longer unconditionally safe. This
realization has accelerated efforts to build alternative payment
systems, increase non-dollar trade settlement, and diversify reserve
holdings -- not because these countries support Russia, but because they
recognize they could be next.

### Sanctions Effectiveness and the Russia Paradox

The most uncomfortable fact about modern sanctions is that Russia's war
machine has continued to function despite the most comprehensive
sanctions regime ever assembled. As the CFR analysis notes, Russia did
not need access to its frozen reserves or new Western financing to
prosecute its war against Ukraine. So long as there were buyers for
Russian oil exports -- and China and India proved to be eager buyers at
discounted prices -- the Kremlin could fund its military. The oil price
cap, while reducing Russian revenues on the margin, did not cut them
off. Russia's current account surplus actually increased in 2022 as
imports collapsed faster than exports.

This exposes a fundamental limitation of financial sanctions: they are
most effective against countries that are deeply integrated into the
global financial system and lack alternative patrons. They are least
effective against countries that produce commodities the world needs,
have large domestic economies, or can turn to a network of
non-sanctioning powers for trade and finance. The Iran model -- where
comprehensive sanctions helped bring Tehran to the negotiating table in
2015 -- assumed the target country had no alternative great-power
sponsor. Russia has China. This changes the calculus entirely.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

The theoretical foundation of weaponized interdependence is well
established. Farrell and Newman's 2019 paper in International Security
provided a rigorous framework for understanding how network topology
creates coercive opportunities. Their subsequent 2023 book,
"Underground Empire: How America Weaponized the World Economy,"
documented the historical evolution of these capabilities from the 9/11
attacks through the Russia sanctions, showing how financial surveillance
tools developed for counterterrorism were progressively repurposed for
great-power competition.

The empirical track record on sanctions effectiveness is mixed and
highly context-dependent. The Peterson Institute for International
Economics, which maintains the most comprehensive sanctions database,
has found that sanctions achieve their stated foreign policy objectives
in roughly 34 percent of cases -- but this average obscures enormous
variation depending on the target, the goal, and the breadth of the
sanctioning coalition. Unilateral sanctions by a single country almost
never succeed. Multilateral sanctions with broad international support
succeed roughly 40-50 percent of the time. Sanctions aimed at modest
policy changes (e.g., releasing political prisoners) succeed more often
than those aimed at regime change or military capitulation, which rarely
succeed.

The Russia sanctions case provides the most important natural experiment
of the twenty-first century. A 2024 study by the Yale School of
Management found that while the sanctions and corporate exodus damaged
Russia's medium-term growth potential by denying it access to Western
technology and investment, Russia's economy proved remarkably resilient
in the short term. GDP contracted by only 2.1 percent in 2022 (far less
than the 10-15 percent initially forecast), and by 2024 had recovered to
pre-war levels, driven by massive fiscal stimulus, military production,
and continued energy exports to non-Western buyers. The Congressional
Research Service reported in September 2025 that China's semiconductor
advancements, while still trailing TSMC and Samsung by approximately
two to three generations, had progressed faster than many analysts
expected -- Huawei's Ascend 910D AI chip, fabricated using domestic
packaging technology, demonstrated that export controls had not
prevented Chinese firms from developing competitive AI hardware, though
at higher cost and lower yields.

On the de-dollarization front, IMF data shows that the dollar's share
of global allocated foreign exchange reserves has declined from roughly
71 percent in 2000 to approximately 58 percent by early 2026 -- though
it is important to note that much of this shift predates the 2022
sanctions and reflects the long-term rise of the euro, yen, and
renminbi as alternative reserve assets. More telling is the increase in
bilateral trade settled in non-dollar currencies. Russia and China now
settle roughly 70 percent of their bilateral trade in rubles and
renminbi. Brazil and China have established a renminbi clearing
arrangement. Saudi Arabia has signaled openness to pricing oil exports
in currencies other than the dollar. Each of these developments is
incremental on its own, but taken together they represent a structural
shift in the architecture of global payments.

## Implications

The weaponization of interdependence carries profound implications
across multiple domains.

For the global economic order, the most significant consequence is
fragmentation. The systematic use of sanctions, export controls, and
tariffs as instruments of coercion is producing not a single global
economy governed by shared rules but a bifurcating system: one bloc
centered on the United States, the dollar, and Western financial
infrastructure; another centered on China, the renminbi, and
alternative payment systems like CIPS (Cross-Border Interbank Payment
System). Countries in the "global south" are increasingly unwilling to
be forced to choose sides, seeking instead to diversify their economic
relationships to reduce vulnerability to any single hegemonic power.
The author's assessment is that this fragmentation is likely
irreversible: even if the United States and China were to reach a grand
bargain tomorrow, countries that have invested in alternative
infrastructure will not dismantle it.

For investors and businesses, the fragmentation of the global economic
order imposes new costs and risks. Supply chains that were optimized
for efficiency under the assumption of open trade must now be
restructured for resilience under the assumption of potential
disruption. The CHIPS and Science Act of 2022, which provided $52.7
billion in direct grants and attracted over $450 billion in private
investment for domestic semiconductor manufacturing, represents a
deliberate state-led reconfiguration of a critical supply chain.
Companies operating across the US-China divide face compliance
challenges that did not exist a decade ago: an export that is legal
today may be prohibited tomorrow, and the extraterritorial reach of
US sanctions means that non-US companies can be penalized for
transactions that are legal under their own country's laws.

For international relations theory, the era of weaponized
interdependence challenges core assumptions of both realism and
liberalism. Realism's expectation that economic interdependence reduces
conflict by raising its costs has been turned on its head:
interdependence creates vulnerabilities that aggressors can exploit,
and the very networks that liberals hoped would bind nations together
have become vectors of coercion. Constructivism offers a partial
resolution by emphasizing that the meaning of interdependence is
socially constructed -- the same financial networks can be instruments of
cooperation or weapons, depending on the intentions of the states that
control them.

For policymakers, the central dilemma is this: economic sanctions and
tariffs are simultaneously the most powerful non-military instruments
of statecraft available and the surest way to erode the system that
gives those instruments their power. Every time the United States uses
its financial dominance to coerce an adversary, it provides that
adversary and every other country with a motive to reduce their
dependence on the dollar. The weaponization of interdependence contains
the seeds of its own obsolescence. The question is not whether
de-dollarization will happen -- the evidence suggests it already is --
but how quickly, how chaotically, and whether the United States can
manage the transition to a multipolar financial system without losing
the strategic advantages that dollar dominance has historically
provided.
