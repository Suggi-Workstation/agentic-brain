# International Relations Theory -- How the Three Great Paradigms Explain Why Nations Cooperate and Why They Fight

International relations theory is the intellectual toolkit for making
sense of world politics. Three grand paradigms -- realism, liberalism,
and constructivism -- compete to explain why states go to war, when
they cooperate, and how the international system changes over time.
Realism sees a world of power-seeking states trapped in anarchy;
liberalism sees institutions and interdependence creating space for
cooperation; constructivism sees a social world where ideas, norms,
and identities shape what states want and who they are. No single
paradigm fully captures the complexity of global politics, but together
they provide the essential lenses through which every geopolitical
event can be interpreted.

## Background

The academic study of international relations (IR) emerged as a
distinct field after World War I, driven by the question of why
nations fight and whether war could be prevented. The first wave of
IR scholarship was idealist -- it assumed that rational diplomacy,
international law, and collective security could make war obsolete.
The League of Nations embodied this vision.

The idealism was shattered by the 1930s and World War II. In 1939,
E. H. Carr published The Twenty Years' Crisis, a devastating critique
of the "utopian" approach that mistook aspirations for reality. Carr
argued that power, not morality, drove international politics -- and
that ignoring power was more dangerous than acknowledging it. This
marked the birth of classical realism as the dominant paradigm.

The 1970s and 1980s saw the rise of two challengers. Neoliberal
institutionalism, pioneered by Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye, argued
that realism could not explain the persistence of international
cooperation even under anarchy. Constructivism, articulated most
forcefully by Alexander Wendt in the 1990s, argued that both realism
and liberalism missed the deeper question: where do state interests
come from in the first place?

Today, the three paradigms coexist in what most scholars describe as
an "eclectic" or "pluralistic" IR field. The practical imperative is
not to pick one winner but to understand what each lens reveals and
what each obscures.

## Realism: Power and Survival in an Anarchic World

### Core Assumptions

Realism rests on four core propositions:

1. **States are the primary actors.** Non-state actors (corporations,
   NGOs, international organizations) matter, but the state remains
   the unit that matters most for war and peace.
2. **The international system is anarchic.** There is no world
   government to enforce rules or protect the weak. States must
   ultimately rely on themselves -- the "self-help" principle.
3. **States are rational, unitary actors.** Realists treat the state
   as a black box pursuing coherent national interests, setting aside
   domestic politics and individual psychology.
4. **Power is the currency of international politics.** States seek
   power -- military, economic, and diplomatic -- because power is the
   ultimate guarantor of survival in an anarchic system.

The realist world is a tragic world. No state wants war for its own
sake, but the structure of anarchy makes conflict inevitable, even
when all actors are rational and well-intentioned. This is the
security dilemma: actions taken by one state to increase its security
(e.g., building up arms) make other states feel less secure, prompting
them to respond in kind, creating a spiral that can lead to war even
though no one wanted it.

### Classical Realism (Morgenthau)

Hans Morgenthau's Politics Among Nations (1948) codified classical
realism. His six principles center on the idea that politics is
governed by objective laws rooted in human nature -- specifically, the
"animus dominandi," the lust for power. For Morgenthau, the struggle
for power is not a feature of the international system but of human
beings. States seek power because the people running them seek power.

Morgenthau's key insight: interest defined as power provides a
universal framework for analyzing foreign policy. It allows the
analyst to step into the shoes of a statesman and ask: what would a
rational actor do, given these constraints? It also provides a
cautionary principle -- the national interest, prudently defined,
should guide policy rather than ideology, moral crusading, or
domestic political calculation.

### Neorealism / Structural Realism (Waltz)

Kenneth Waltz's Theory of International Politics (1979) shifted the
locus of explanation from human nature to system structure. States
do not seek power because of human nature; they seek it because the
anarchic structure of the international system compels them to.
Waltz's theory has three key structural elements:

1. **Ordering principle:** Anarchy (as opposed to hierarchy in
   domestic politics).
2. **Character of units:** States are functionally similar -- they
   all perform the same essential tasks (defense, taxation, etc.).
3. **Distribution of capabilities:** States differ in power, and
   the distribution of power among them determines system polarity
   -- unipolar, bipolar, or multipolar.

Waltz argued that bipolar systems (two great powers) are more stable
than multipolar ones because miscalculation is less likely. His theory
also distinguished between defensive realism (states seek enough power
to be secure) and offensive realism, later developed by John
Mearsheimer, which holds that states seek to maximize power because
only hegemony truly guarantees security.

## Liberalism: Institutions, Interdependence, and the Possibility of Progress

### Core Assumptions

Liberalism challenges realism on nearly every front:

1. **States are not the only important actors.** International
   institutions, multinational corporations, NGOs, and transnational
   networks all shape outcomes.
2. **Anarchy does not preclude cooperation.** Even without a world
   government, states can build institutions and regimes that create
   predictable expectations, reduce transaction costs, and make
   cooperation rational.
3. **Domestic politics matters.** Democracies behave differently
   from autocracies. Public opinion, interest groups, and electoral
   incentives shape foreign policy.
4. **Economic interdependence reduces conflict.** When states are
   deeply connected through trade and investment, the costs of war
   become prohibitively high.

The liberal vision is essentially optimistic: the arc of history bends
toward cooperation, institutions, and peace -- but progress is not
automatic and can reverse.

### Democratic Peace Theory

The most famous liberal empirical claim: democracies do not fight
wars against each other. The evidence is strong. While democracies
fight non-democracies at roughly the same rate as other regime types,
there is virtually no historical case of two consolidated democracies
going to war. Proposed mechanisms include: democratic norms of
peaceful conflict resolution, institutional constraints (legislatures,
elections), and the transparency of democratic decision-making, which
reduces miscalculation.

The democratic peace theory has been enormously influential in foreign
policy, underpinning arguments for democracy promotion and NATO
enlargement. Critics note that the transition to democracy can be a
dangerous period (democratizing states are actually more war-prone
than stable autocracies) and that the theory says nothing about wars
between democracies and non-democracies.

### Neoliberal Institutionalism (Keohane and Nye)

Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye's Power and Interdependence (1977)
introduced "complex interdependence" as a description of the modern
international system. In a world of complex interdependence:

1. **Multiple channels** connect societies -- formal state-to-state
   diplomacy, informal government ties, and transnational
   relationships between corporations and civil society.
2. **There is no hierarchy of issues.** Military security does not
   consistently dominate the agenda; economic, environmental, and
   social issues compete for attention.
3. **Military force is less usable.** Among states in a region of
   complex interdependence (e.g., Western Europe, North America),
   military force is largely irrelevant to resolving disagreements.

Keohane later developed institutional liberalism or "regime theory":
international regimes (sets of principles, norms, rules, and
decision-making procedures) help states overcome collective action
problems. They provide information, reduce transaction costs, and
create reputational incentives to comply. The institutions persist
even when the power distribution that created them shifts -- which
explains why NATO survived the end of the Cold War.

### Commercial Liberalism

The idea that trade promotes peace dates back to Montesquieu and Kant.
Modern commercial liberals argue that economic interdependence creates
vested interests in peace: businesses, workers, and consumers whose
welfare depends on cross-border exchange lobby against war. The
"Golden Arches" theory -- no two countries with McDonald's have ever
fought a war -- is a popular (if oversimplified) expression of this
logic.

Critics point out that interdependence can also create friction.
Asymmetric interdependence gives one state leverage over another
(the "weaponization of interdependence"), and close economic ties
did not prevent World War I.

## Constructivism: Ideas, Identity, and the Social Construction of Reality

### Core Assumptions

Constructivism emerged in the 1990s as a challenge to the materialism
of both realism and liberalism. Its core claim: the international
system is not a given, objective reality -- it is socially constructed
through the shared ideas, norms, and identities that states develop
through interaction.

1. **Ideational factors matter as much as material ones.** Nuclear
   weapons in British hands feel different to the United States than
   nuclear weapons in North Korean hands -- not because the material
   capability differs, but because the social relationship differs.
2. **State interests are not fixed.** What a state wants (security?
   prestige? ideological expansion?) depends on its identity, which
   is shaped through social interaction.
3. **Anarchy is what states make of it.** Anarchy does not inherently
   mean a self-help system of perpetual conflict. It depends on the
   intersubjective understandings states hold.

### Wendt's Three Cultures of Anarchy

Alexander Wendt's 1992 article "Anarchy Is What States Make of It"
and his 1999 book Social Theory of International Politics laid out
three possible "cultures of anarchy" -- sets of shared ideas about
who the enemy is and how states relate:

1. **Hobbesian culture:** States view each other as enemies. The
   logic is "kill or be killed." Violence is endemic, survival is
   the only goal. This describes the European wars of religion or
   genocidal conflicts.
2. **Lockean culture:** States view each other as rivals. They
   compete but recognize each other's right to exist. Violence is
   constrained by sovereignty norms. Much of modern international
   politics operates in this culture.
3. **Kantian culture:** States view each other as friends. Disputes
   are resolved without the threat of force, and collective security
   prevails. Wendt sees the NATO alliance or the European Union as
   approaching this culture.

The key insight: these cultures are not structural inevitabilities.
A collection of states can move from Hobbesian to Lockean to Kantian
culture through sustained interactions that create new shared
understandings. The transformation of Franco-German relations between
1870 and 1950 (from hereditary enemies to the core of European
integration) exemplifies this process.

### Norms and Identity

Constructivists study how international norms (standards of
appropriate behavior) emerge, spread, and shape state conduct. Martha
Finnemore and Kathryn Sikkink described a "norm life cycle": norms
emerge through advocacy by norm entrepreneurs, reach a tipping point
(cascade) as states adopt them for legitimacy reasons, and eventually
become internalized -- taken for granted as "the way things are done."

Examples: the prohibition on chemical weapons, the norm against
territorial conquest (which has made wars of annexation far rarer
since 1945), the emergence of the "responsibility to protect" (R2P)
doctrine, and the stigmatization of apartheid and colonialism. In
each case, what was once normal became unthinkable -- not because
material conditions changed, but because shared norms changed.

## How the Paradigms Interpret the Same World

The value of IR theory is not in picking the "correct" paradigm but
in using them as lenses that reveal different aspects of the same
event. Consider Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine:

- A **realist** sees NATO enlargement crossing a great power's red
  line, the security dilemma in action, and a revisionist power
  seeking to overturn a post-Cold War order it never accepted.
- A **liberal** sees the failure of economic interdependence to
  prevent war (Europe's gas dependency did not deter Russia), but
  also the success of international institutions in coordinating
  sanctions, arming Ukraine, and maintaining a coalition.
- A **constructivist** sees competing identities at work: Russia's
  self-conception as a great power and civilizational center entitled
  to a sphere of influence, versus Ukraine's post-2014 identity
  shift toward Europe and democracy, accelerated by the war itself.

None of these interpretations is "wrong." Each captures something
essential that the others miss. The skillful analyst deploys all
three, treating them as complementary rather than competing.

## Criticisms of Theoretical Pluralism

Some scholars resist the pluralist consensus. John Mearsheimer argues
that realism is simply correct and the alternatives are wishful
thinking -- when material interests are at stake, norms and
institutions bend to power. Post-structuralists and critical theorists
argue that all three mainstream paradigms are state-centric and
Western-centric, systematically excluding the perspectives of the
Global South, women, and marginalized populations.

There is also a practical tension: theoretical pluralism can become
an excuse for avoiding falsifiable claims. If every theory always
fits, none of them can be tested. The discipline continues to debate
whether IR should aspire to predictive social science or interpretive
understanding.

## Implications for Investors

International relations theory has direct implications for capital
allocation and risk assessment:

- **Realism warns that great power transitions are dangerous.** As
  China rises and the US-led order comes under strain, the probability
  of miscalculation and conflict rises -- not because anyone wants
  war, but because the structure of power is shifting. This affects
  long-term discount rates for assets exposed to geopolitical risk.
- **Liberalism suggests that deep interdependence creates powerful
  constituencies for de-escalation.** Even in US-China competition,
  the integration of supply chains, capital markets, and technology
  ecosystems creates costs to decoupling that both sides must weigh.
- **Constructivism reveals that narratives matter.** How a conflict
  is framed (trade war, cold war, clash of civilizations) shapes the
  range of outcomes markets must price in. The transition from "China
  as partner" to "China as systemic rival" was itself a constructivist
  shift with real economic consequences.

The author's assessment: a successful geopolitical investor uses all
three lenses. The realist lens identifies the structural pressures.
the liberal lens identifies the cooperative counterweights. The
constructivist lens identifies the narratives driving market sentiment
and policy shifts. None suffices alone.
