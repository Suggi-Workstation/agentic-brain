# Soft Power -- Why Attraction Works When Coercion Cannot

Soft power is the ability of a state to obtain preferred outcomes through
attraction and persuasion rather than coercion or payment. Coined by Joseph
Nye in 1990, the concept identifies a dimension of international influence
that military and economic power cannot reach: shaping what others want
through the appeal of one's culture, political values, and foreign policies.
Soft power rarely substitutes for hard power, but it shapes the terrain on
which competition unfolds, lowering the cost of coercion and widening the
space for cooperation. Its measurement is contested, its effects are
context-dependent, and the line between attraction and manipulation has
grown murkier as authoritarian states deploy influence operations that mimic
soft power but pierce rather than persuade.

## Background

The concept of soft power emerged from a specific debate in late-Cold War
American academia. In the 1980s, the prevailing view held that the United
States was in terminal decline. Paul Kennedy's "The Rise and Fall of the
Great Powers" (1987) argued that the US suffered from imperial overstretch
and would follow the trajectory of seventeenth-century Spain or Edwardian
Britain. Kenneth Waltz and his neorealist followers treated power as
tangible resources you could drop on a city, and measured it in military
and economic terms. Against this consensus, Joseph Nye dissented. After
examining American military and economic power resources, he concluded
that something was missing from the analysis: the ability to affect others
through attraction and persuasion rather than coercion and payment.

Nye first published the term "soft power" in a 1990 Foreign Policy article
titled "Soft Power" and in his book "Bound to Lead: The Changing Nature of
American Power" the same year. He defined it as co-optive power, in
contrast to command power. The term was deliberately slightly oxymoronic
in the discourse of international relations, where power meant force and
"soft" implied weakness. Nye chose it to make scholars think again about
their assumptions. He traced the underlying idea to ancient philosophy and
to classical realists like E. H. Carr, but argued that neorealism had
narrowed the concept of power to its coercive dimensions, missing the role
of ideas, culture, and institutional attraction.

The intellectual lineage extends further back. The concept that a state
could influence others through the attractiveness of its culture and
ideology was not new. The Soviet Union had exercised soft power in the
early postwar period through communist ideology, the myth of historical
inevitability, and transnational communist institutions. Susan Strange's
"States and Markets" (1988) identified transnational production as a source
of American co-optive power. Robert Keohane and Nye's "Power and
Interdependence" (1977) had already developed the concept of complex
interdependence, which highlighted how non-military channels of influence
operated in an interconnected world. Soft power was the distillation of
these threads into a single analytical category.

The concept took on political resonance gradually. In the United States,
Nye noted that political leaders were reluctant to use the term because
audiences wanted tough talk. The European Union, as it developed, adopted
the language of soft power more readily, with European leaders describing
the EU's enlargement policy, norm-setting, and regulatory power as forms
of attraction rather than coercion. The concept's most dramatic reception
came in China. After President Hu Jintao told the 17th Congress of the
Chinese Communist Party in 2007 that China needed to invest more in its
soft power, and as Xi Jinping continued this emphasis, billions of dollars
were invested in soft power promotion and thousands of academic articles
were published on the subject. Nye himself was invited to address the
School of Marxism at Peking University, where he experienced firsthand the
Chinese preference to restrict the concept to cultural issues rather than
political ones.

After the 2003 invasion of Iraq proved disastrous for American
attractiveness, Nye expanded the concept in his 2004 book "Soft Power: The
Means to Success in World Politics" and introduced the complementary
concept of "smart power" -- the ability to combine hard and soft power
into strategies where they reinforce each other. Smart power was later
adopted by Hillary Clinton as Secretary of State, who used the term in her
confirmation hearing. The concept also spawned a critical offspring:
"sharp power," coined by the National Endowment for Democracy in 2017 to
describe authoritarian influence operations that pierce, penetrate, or
perforate the information environments of democracies, operating as a dark
mirror of soft power.

## Core Concepts

### The Three Sources of Soft Power

Nye identified three primary resources from which a country's soft power
is generated. First, culture: the set of values and practices that create
meaning for a society, including literature, art, film, music, education,
and cuisine. Culture produces soft power where it is attractive to others.
Second, political values: the principles a state upholds at home and
abroad, including democracy, human rights, rule of law, and transparency.
Political values generate soft power when a state actually lives up to
them rather than merely proclaiming them. Third, foreign policies: the
policies a state pursues internationally, which generate soft power when
they are seen as legitimate and as having moral authority. These three
sources are interdependent. A culture that appeals globally can be
undermined by foreign policies that repel the same audiences. Conversely,
policies perceived as legitimate can amplify cultural attraction.

The distinction between resources and behavior is critical. Nye repeatedly
emphasized that soft power is not the same as cultural resources. A
foreigner drinking Coca-Cola or wearing a Michael Jordan shirt does not in
itself mean that America has power over that person. This view confuses
resources with behavior. Whether power resources produce a favorable
outcome depends on the context, just as a larger tank army may produce
military victory in desert terrain but not in swampy jungle. Soft power
is the behavioral outcome -- obtaining preferred outcomes through
attraction -- not merely possessing attractive resources. This distinction
is the source of much confusion in both academic and policy discourse.

### Hard Power, Soft Power, and Smart Power

Hard power is the use of coercion (sticks) and payment (carrots) to alter
the behavior of others. Soft power is the use of attraction and persuasion
to shape what others want, so that they voluntarily align their
preferences with yours. The two are not opposites on a single spectrum but
distinct behavioral mechanisms that can reinforce or undermine each other.
A state that is militarily dominant but diplomatically isolated may find
that its hard power resources are less effective because other states form
balancing coalitions against it. A state that is culturally attractive but
militarily weak may find that its soft power cannot prevent coercion by
others.

Smart power, a term Nye developed in 2003 and elaborated in a 2007 CSIS
commission co-chaired with Richard Armitage, refers to the skillful
combination of hard and soft power into integrated strategies. The
commission defined it as "neither hard nor soft -- it is the skillful
combination of both." Smart power requires what Nye called "contextual
intelligence": the ability to read the environment and combine resources
appropriately. The concept is prescriptive rather than analytical; it
answers the question of how to deploy power effectively, not merely how to
describe it. Hillary Clinton's adoption of the term during her tenure as
Secretary of State reflected the recognition that the Bush administration's
overreliance on hard power had damaged American influence and that a
rebalancing was needed.

### Public Diplomacy and Cultural Diplomacy

Public diplomacy is the mechanism through which governments attempt to
communicate with and influence foreign publics, as distinct from
traditional diplomacy, which targets foreign governments. It includes
broadcasting (Voice of America, BBC World Service, Radio Free Europe),
exchange programs (Fulbright, Chevening, DAAD, Erasmus+), cultural
centers (Alliance Francaise, British Council, Goethe-Institut, Confucius
Institutes), and digital engagement through social media. Public
diplomacy is the instrument; soft power is the outcome it seeks to
produce.

Cultural diplomacy is a subset of public diplomacy that specifically
involves the exchange of ideas, art, language, and other cultural elements
among nations. It operates on the principle of mutual engagement, promoting
reciprocal exchanges that build interpersonal ties. Unlike unilateral
broadcasting, it prioritizes authenticity and voluntarism. Historical
examples include the US Jazz Ambassadors program, which sent Louis
Armstrong and Duke Ellington on international tours during the Cold War,
and the Soviet Union's global ballet performances. The Fulbright Program,
established in 1946, has facilitated hundreds of thousands of scholarly
visits, creating long-term diplomatic networks. Cultural diplomacy's
effectiveness depends on credible execution: when it is perceived as covert
propaganda or cultural imperialism, it can produce backlash and resistance
from recipient nations.

### The Agency Problem: Who Controls Soft Power?

One of the structural features of soft power is that its most potent
resources are often outside government control. Governments can control
foreign policy and spend money on public diplomacy, broadcasting, and
exchange programs. But they cannot control popular culture, which is
produced by private actors -- Hollywood studios, music labels, technology
companies, universities, foundations. The Council on Foreign Relations
suggested the formation of a Corporation for Public Diplomacy to engage
wider participation among private groups and individuals who are often
unwilling to be part of official government productions. This agency
problem means that a state's soft power is partly the product of its
civil society, not just its government. The United States, with its
vast private cultural industries and open society, has a structural
advantage in soft power generation that authoritarian states with
state-controlled media cannot easily replicate. However, the same
openness means the government cannot direct its cultural outputs toward
specific diplomatic goals.

### Soft Power vs. Sharp Power

The concept of sharp power, introduced by the National Endowment for
Democracy in 2017, distinguishes a category of influence operations that
superficially resemble soft power but operate through fundamentally
different mechanisms. Sharp power pierces, penetrates, or perforates the
information environments of target countries through disinformation,
covert influence, censorship, and manipulation of media and academic
institutions. It is the dark mirror of soft power. Where soft power
works through attraction and voluntary alignment, sharp power works
through deception and manipulation of the information space. The
distinction matters because authoritarian regimes (particularly Russia
and China) have adopted practices that mimic cultural diplomacy --
Confucius Institutes, media partnerships, academic exchanges -- but
whose purpose and effect are to distort information rather than to
foster genuine mutual understanding. The confusion between soft and
sharp power is itself an instrument: if democratic publics cannot
distinguish attraction from manipulation, the manipulator gains an
advantage. The NED report emphasized that authoritarian sharp power
exploits the openness of democratic societies -- free press, academic
autonomy, civil society organizations -- turning these strengths into
vulnerabilities. The response is not to close open societies but to
build resilience through transparency, media literacy, and institutional
safeguards that expose manipulation without suppressing legitimate
cultural exchange.

### The Measurement Problem

Soft power is notoriously difficult to measure, and its critics have
argued that it is too elastic a concept to be useful. Nye countered that
it is possible to quantify sources of soft power -- cultural,
communications, and diplomatic resources -- and that public opinion polls
can track changes in a country's attractiveness over time. Several
indexes have been developed to operationalize the concept. The Soft
Power 30 index, created by Portland Communications, ranked countries
across multiple metrics including culture, education, digital
engagement, enterprise, and government. The Brand Finance Global Soft
Power Index, launched in 2019, scores all 193 UN member states across
55 metrics in eight pillars: Familiarity, Reputation, Influence,
Business and Trade, Governance, International Relations, Culture and
Heritage, and Media and Communication. In the 2026 edition, the United
States ranked first with 74.9 points, China second with 73.5, Japan
third, the UK fourth, and Germany fifth. These indexes measure
perceptions and resources rather than behavioral outcomes, which
remains the fundamental limitation of soft power measurement: they
quantify inputs and proxies, not the actual influence produced.

## Evidence

### The Cold War: Soft Power as a Weapon System

The Cold War provides the most extensively documented case of soft power
as a deliberate strategic instrument. The United States combined military
deterrence with cultural and ideological attraction. The Voice of
America broadcast American music, news, and values into the Soviet bloc.
The Jazz Ambassadors program sent prominent musicians to Africa, Asia,
and the Middle East to demonstrate American cultural vitality. The
Fulbright Program built academic networks that outlasted the Cold War
itself. Hollywood films and American popular culture penetrated the Iron
Curtade despite Soviet efforts to block them. Soviet teenagers wore blue
jeans and sought American recordings. Chinese students used a symbol
modeled on the Statue of Liberty during the 1989 uprisings. Nye argued
that when the Berlin Wall fell, it was destroyed not by artillery but by
hammers and bulldozers wielded by people who had lost faith in communism --
an outcome shaped in part by decades of Western cultural and ideological
attraction. However, the causal attribution remains debated: whether
cultural attraction produced the collapse of Soviet legitimacy or merely
accelerated a process driven by economic failure and political
dysfunction.

### American Soft Power: Hollywood and the Market Empire

The United States has been the most extensively studied case of soft
power in practice. American cultural exports -- film, television, music,
technology brands, universities -- have global reach unmatched by any
other nation. In the 1990 original article, Nye cited data showing that
the United States exported about seven times as many television shows as
the next largest exporter (Britain) and that American films occupied
about 50 percent of world screentime despite accounting for only 6-7
percent of all films made. The English language became the lingua franca
of the global economy. The 2026 Brand Finance Index confirmed continued
American dominance, with the US ranking first in arts and entertainment,
global brands, space exploration, and influence in media and
communications.

However, research has complicated the straightforward link between
Hollywood exports and American soft power. A study published in the
journal "Media, Culture and Society" examined the relationship between
Hollywood box office and global public opinion of the United States,
finding no discernible empirical relationship between expanding box
office and favorable opinion. The author argued that the globalization
of Hollywood's audience has paradoxically diluted American soft power:
as films are produced with global audiences in mind, they become
de-Americanized, with international co-productions, multinational casts,
and content adjusted to avoid offending foreign markets. The study of
"Captain America: Civil War" illustrated the point: despite "America"
in the title, the film was a co-production involving German, Dutch,
Slovenian, Chilean, and Kazakh companies, with special effects from
over 20 companies across six countries. The globalization of cultural
production complicates the assumption that cultural exports translate
directly into national soft power.

### Chinese Soft Power: Ambition and Contradiction

China represents the most ambitious state-directed soft power campaign
of the twenty-first century. After Hu Jintao's 2007 directive, China
invested billions in soft power promotion. The Confucius Institute
program, established in 2004, grew to 1,086 affiliates (440 institutes
and 646 classrooms) in 120 countries by 2014, rivaling the Alliance
Francaise and British Council in scale, though those organizations were
80-120 years older. The Belt and Road Initiative, announced in 2013,
projected Chinese infrastructure investment as a form of attractiveness.
China's record of economic growth, which raised hundreds of millions
from poverty, was itself a source of attraction for developing countries.

Results have been mixed. The 2026 Brand Finance Index ranked China
second globally, narrowing the gap with the US to 1.4 points. China was
the only country in the index to record an increase in its soft power
score that year. However, the Portland/Soft Power 30 index had
previously ranked the US first and China 28th of the top 30 countries,
suggesting that the indexes measure different things or that Chinese
soft power has risen rapidly. The core contradiction is that China's
soft power strategy is undermined by its hard power behavior. As Nye
observed, China's aggressive territorial claims in the South China Sea
frighten its neighbors into balancing coalitions, undermining the
attraction its culture and economic model might otherwise generate.
The Confucius Institutes have faced closures and restrictions in
numerous countries due to concerns about political interference and
academic freedom, leading to a 2020 rebranding of the parent
organization from Hanban to the Ministry of Education Center for
Language Education and Cooperation. The case illustrates the agency
problem in reverse: state-directed cultural diplomacy can generate
suspicion that undermines the attraction it seeks to produce.

### The Korean Wave: State Investment in Cultural Salience

South Korea provides the most successful recent case of deliberate soft
power construction through cultural industry investment. Following the
1997 Asian Financial Crisis, South Korea identified cultural exports as
a new economic and strategic engine. President Kim Dae-jung's
administration launched the Hallyu Industry Support Development Plan,
boosting the cultural budget from $14 million in 1998 to $84 million in
2001. Over subsequent decades, the government consistently spent between
1.4 and 2 percent of its budget on culture. The Korean Wave (Hallyu) --
K-pop, K-dramas, K-film, K-beauty, K-food -- became a global phenomenon.
BTS, Blackpink, the film "Parasite" (2019), and the Netflix series
"Squid Game" (2021) achieved worldwide cultural penetration. The 2026
Brand Finance Index ranked South Korea 11th, with gains driven largely
by its content industries. KOCCA's annual budget exceeds $400 million,
supplemented by a 2023 $620 million support package and a 2024 K-Content
Fund of $420 million.

The Korean case demonstrates that sustained state investment in cultural
industries can translate into international influence, economic returns,
and political capital, especially among younger global audiences. It
also demonstrates the defensive function of cultural salience: South
Korea offset declines in governance perception by leveraging gains in
familiarity and influence driven by entertainment content. However, the
Hallyu model's effectiveness as soft power (shaping what others want)
versus its effectiveness as nation-branding (improving perception)
remains a distinction that the indexes do not fully capture.

### The European Union: Normative Power and Conditionality

The European Union represents a distinct model of soft power based on
norms and institutional attraction rather than cultural exports. The
EU's soft power derives from its political values -- democracy, human
rights, rule of law, market economy -- and from the attractiveness of
its institutional model. Enlargement policy has been the EU's most
powerful soft power instrument: the prospect of membership incentivized
candidate countries to adopt EU norms, laws, and standards, a process
termed "normative power Europe" by Ian Manners. The EU's regulatory
power (the "Brussels effect") extends its influence through standards
that trading partners adopt to access the single market. However, the
EU's soft power faces a "capability-expectations gap": the Union is
perceived as a soft power superpower but often lacks the
decision-making coherence to translate its attractiveness into
concerted foreign policy action. The EU's response to the migration
crisis, Brexit, and internal democratic backsliding in member states
has at times damaged the very image of normative consistency that
underpins its soft power.

## Implications

### For Great Power Competition

Soft power has become a central arena of great power competition, not a
supplement to it. The 2026 Brand Finance Index, showing the US and
China separated by 1.4 points, frames the competition in measurable
terms. For the United States, the challenge is that its soft power
resources -- Hollywood, universities, technology, civil society -- are
increasingly globalized and less under national control, while its
foreign policies (tariffs, withdrawal from international agreements,
unilateral action) can repel the audiences its culture attracts. For
China, the challenge is that state-directed soft power generates
suspicion in democratic societies while its coercive behavior in the
South China Sea and elsewhere undercuts its cultural appeal. The
structural asymmetry favors the United States: open societies generate
soft power organically through civil society, while authoritarian
states must manufacture it through state institutions, which are
inherently less credible as agents of attraction.

For policymakers, the implication is that soft power cannot be treated
as a tool to be deployed in isolation. Nye's smart power framework
argues that the effective question is not "hard or soft?" but "how to
combine?" The United States spends approximately 500 times as much on
the military as on broadcasting and exchange programs, raising the
question of whether this ratio serves strategic objectives. An
overmilitarized foreign policy, as the CSIS commission warned, leads
to turning to the Pentagon because it can get things done, while
neglecting the instruments that shape preferences and reduce the need
for coercion.

### For Smaller and Middle Powers

For states that cannot compete in hard power, soft power offers a
strategic alternative. South Korea's investment in Hallyu demonstrates
that a middle power can achieve global cultural salience through
sustained investment, creating economic returns and diplomatic capital
that exceed what its military and economic weight alone would produce.
Japan has similarly leveraged anime, manga, and cuisine as soft power
assets. The Gulf states (UAE, Qatar) have invested in cultural
institutions, sports events, and media to build international presence.
For smaller powers, the implication is that cultural industries are
not merely commercial sectors but strategic assets whose development
deserves state investment. The Atlantic Council's analysis of Taiwan's
limited cultural footprint identified this as a strategic vulnerability:
without cultural salience among younger generations in partner
countries, sustained foreign support and international legitimacy are
at risk.

However, the Hallyu model also carries lessons about the limits of
state direction. South Korea's success depended on a genuine creative
industry that the government supported but did not control. Attempts by
authoritarian states to manufacture cultural appeal through
state-controlled institutions face the credibility problem: audiences
recognize propaganda, and recognition converts attraction into
resistance.

### For the Information Environment and Democratic Resilience

The sharp power concept has significant implications for how democracies
understand the contemporary information environment. If authoritarian
influence operations mimic soft power but operate through manipulation
and deception, then democratic resilience requires the ability to
distinguish attraction from manipulation. This has practical
consequences for policy: Confucius Institutes, media partnerships, and
academic exchanges that may serve genuine cultural exchange in some
contexts may serve sharp power in others. The response is not blanket
rejection of engagement but calibrated scrutiny of the mechanisms
through which influence operates.

The information age has altered the conditions under which soft power
operates. Nye argued that in today's information age, success is the
result not merely of whose army wins but of whose story wins. Social
media has decentralized the production and distribution of cultural
content, reducing the control that any state -- democratic or
authoritarian -- can exercise over its cultural narrative. The
proliferation of information sources paradoxically makes attraction
harder to achieve: in a fragmented media environment, cultural messages
are more easily drowned out, distorted, or discredited. The competition
for attention has become a prerequisite for the competition for
attraction.

### For Measurement and Strategy

The measurement problem has strategic consequences. If soft power
cannot be measured precisely, it cannot be managed precisely. The
indexes that exist (Brand Finance, Soft Power 30) measure perceptions
and resources, not behavioral outcomes. A country can rank high on
familiarity and influence while failing to convert that recognition
into preferred policy outcomes from other states. Conversely, a
country can rank lower while achieving its objectives through quiet
diplomacy and institutional influence. The implication for analysts is
that soft power indexes should be treated as diagnostic instruments,
not as scorecards. They identify areas of strength and weakness but do
not establish the causal link between attraction and outcomes that the
concept of soft power requires.

For researchers, the implication is that soft power studies need better
behavioral measures. The gap between resource measurement and outcome
measurement is the central methodological challenge. Studies that
examine the relationship between cultural exports and foreign public
opinion, like the Hollywood box office analysis, suggest that the
causal links are weaker and more conditional than the theory predicts.
Future research needs to specify the conditions under which attraction
produces alignment, and the conditions under which it does not. The
Contextual Contingency Framework proposed in studies of Confucius
Institutes -- which conceptualizes soft power outcomes as a
multiplicative function of cultural efforts and contextual receptivity
-- offers one approach, but it remains to be tested at scale.

### For the Digital Era and Platform Power

The rise of digital platforms has introduced a new dimension to soft power
that Nye's original framework did not fully anticipate. Social media
platforms (X, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram), streaming services (Netflix,
Spotify), and technology ecosystems (Apple, Google, Meta) are themselves
instruments of cultural projection, but they operate across national
boundaries in ways that complicate the state-centric model of soft power.
A K-pop video on YouTube or a Korean drama on Netflix reaches global
audiences without passing through any government's diplomatic apparatus.
The algorithmic curation of content means that cultural attraction is
mediated by platform logic rather than by state strategy. This creates
opportunities -- a small country's cultural output can achieve global
reach if it resonates with platform algorithms -- and risks -- the same
platforms can amplify disinformation and sharp power operations with
equal efficiency. The implication for strategists is that soft power in
the digital era is partly a function of platform governance, not merely
of cultural production or diplomatic effort.
