# Space Geopolitics -- Strategic Dependence Makes Orbits Contested but Indiscriminate Destruction Self-Defeating

Satellites convert orbital position, launch access, ground infrastructure, data links, and commercial capacity into geopolitical power because modern military and civilian systems depend on services delivered through space [2][4][7]. The same dependence creates vulnerability: states can disrupt a rival through physical attack, electronic interference, cyber operations, or coercion of commercial providers, but debris and service spillovers can also impose costs on neutral users and on the attacker itself [1][2][3]. The author's synthesis is that durable space power comes less from possessing a single exquisite satellite or anti-satellite weapon than from sustaining a distributed network, understanding activity in orbit, coordinating allies and firms, and controlling escalation.

## Background

The geopolitical use of space began before any state described space as a warfighting domain. Early reconnaissance and missile-warning satellites gave governments information that aircraft could not collect as safely or continuously, while communications satellites connected forces and leaders across long distances. Navigation and timing constellations later enabled precision operations and supported civilian transport, telecommunications, finance, energy, and emergency services. These systems made space an enabling layer beneath terrestrial power rather than a distant theater separated from events on Earth [2][4][8].

Cold War competition established several enduring features. First, strategic satellites were closely connected to nuclear stability because early warning, communications, and technical intelligence helped leaders assess attack and maintain command. Second, both superpowers explored anti-satellite systems, demonstrating that satellites could be treated as military targets even while most public space activity was framed as peaceful [1][2]. Third, arms-control practice focused more clearly on weapons of mass destruction than on conventional interference: the 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits placing nuclear weapons or other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, but it does not prohibit every conventional weapon or every military use of Earth orbit [10].

The physical geography of orbit distributes strategic value unevenly. Low Earth orbit, approximately 160 to 2,000 kilometers above Earth in the NASA classification, supports rapid revisit, lower-latency communications, Earth observation, and large constellations. Medium Earth orbit extends roughly from 2,000 to 35,500 kilometers and hosts major navigation systems. Geosynchronous orbit at about 35,786 kilometers permits a satellite to match Earth's rotation; an equatorial geostationary satellite can remain over one region, which is valuable for persistent communications, weather observation, and warning [15]. These regimes are not interchangeable. Distance affects launch energy, latency, coverage, sensor resolution, maneuver cost, replacement time, tracking, and the persistence of debris [3][15].

A space system is also larger than the object in orbit. It includes launch vehicles and ranges, satellite buses and payloads, control stations, user terminals, spectrum, software, terrestrial networks, data processing, trained operators, suppliers, and the organizational authority to use them. An adversary therefore does not have to destroy a satellite to deny its service. It may jam an uplink or downlink, spoof a navigation signal, compromise a terminal, attack a control network, disable a ground station, disrupt a launch site, or pressure the company that supplies the service [2][4][8]. This system view explains why some low-cost and reversible attacks can be operationally significant even when no object is damaged in orbit.

Commercial expansion changed the distribution of power. Governments once designed and owned most strategically important satellites, but commercial firms now provide broadband communications, remote sensing, weather data, launch, space-domain awareness, and other services that armed forces can purchase. The war in Ukraine demonstrated that a commercial constellation can support civilian connectivity and military operations during conflict, while public threats against commercial systems showed that private ownership does not remove strategic exposure [2][14]. NATO's 2025 Commercial Space Strategy consequently seeks continuous access to commercial services in peace, crisis, and conflict while warning against overreliance on one provider or solution [7].

The increase in actors and spacecraft also produces congestion independent of conflict. ESA reported that more than 300 launches placed more than 4,000 payloads into orbit in 2025, while launch traffic, fragmentation, and insufficient post-mission disposal continued to raise long-term collision risk [3]. Congestion and competition interact: more satellites create more redundancy and innovation, but they also increase conjunctions, complicate attribution of unusual maneuvers, and expand the set of systems that military operations may depend on. Deliberate debris creation is therefore not only an environmental problem; it can alter access to a strategic domain for every operator [1][3].

Institutions have adapted unevenly. NATO recognized space as an operational domain in 2019 and is integrating national and commercial capabilities [7]. The United States Space Force published doctrine and a warfighting framework that explicitly describe offensive and defensive counterspace operations across orbital, link, cyber, and terrestrial segments [8]. France, Germany, the United Kingdom, China, Russia, India, Japan, and other states have also expanded military space organizations, policies, or counterspace research [1][14]. At the same time, United Nations negotiations remain divided over whether to regulate particular weapons, prohibit certain placements, or focus on responsible behavior and harmful effects [11][14].

The existing legal foundation is important but incomplete for current competition. The Outer Space Treaty establishes freedom of exploration and use, applies international law and the UN Charter, prohibits national appropriation, assigns states responsibility for national activities, and restricts weapons of mass destruction in orbit [10]. The United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space adopted 21 voluntary long-term sustainability guidelines covering regulation, operational safety, cooperation, capacity, and technical research [12]. The General Assembly also adopted Resolution 77/41 in 2022, calling on states not to conduct destructive direct-ascent anti-satellite missile tests; the recorded vote was 155 in favor, 9 against, and 9 abstaining [11]. These measures establish principles and a behavioral focal point, but they do not remove ambiguity over rendezvous operations, non-destructive interference, conventional weapons, verification, or crisis response.

Cislunar space and the Moon extend the geopolitical horizon beyond Earth orbit. Planned missions include scientific exploration, long-duration presence, communications, navigation, logistics, and possible resource use. The term "cislunar space" itself lacks an agreed international definition, and current monitoring capabilities focus mainly on Earth orbit [13]. The Outer Space Treaty subjects the Moon to a higher demilitarization standard than Earth orbit by requiring exclusively peaceful use and prohibiting military bases, weapons testing, and military maneuvers, while still allowing military personnel and equipment for peaceful purposes [10][13]. Competition over scarce operational locations, uncertain resource rules, and incomplete situational awareness may nevertheless create friction unless actors coordinate before activity becomes dense [13].

## Core Concepts

### Space Power Is Network Power

A satellite creates influence only when its service reaches a decision or user. The complete chain includes the spacecraft, links, ground infrastructure, user equipment, data systems, operators, and terrestrial institutions that act on the output. A highly capable imaging satellite has little operational value if its tasking network is compromised, its downlink is jammed, or its data cannot be processed and distributed in time. A navigation constellation may remain intact while local receivers are deceived by spoofed signals. A broadband constellation may survive attacks in orbit but fail if gateways, terminals, identity systems, or software are compromised [2][4][6].

Network power has two geopolitical consequences. First, states with central positions in launch, manufacturing, spectrum coordination, cloud processing, insurance, finance, or data distribution can exert influence without owning every satellite. Second, the attack surface is distributed across jurisdictions and owners. Protection therefore requires agreements among military organizations, civilian agencies, allies, regulators, and firms. The author's synthesis is that the relevant unit of power is the service architecture, not the satellite count alone.

### Orbital Regimes Create Strategic Geography

Orbital mechanics constrains strategy more strongly than a flat map suggests. Satellites cannot stop or maneuver freely like aircraft; changing orbital altitude, plane, or phase consumes time and propellant. Low Earth orbit provides rapid revisit and lower link delay but requires many satellites for persistent global coverage. Medium Earth orbit permits broad navigation coverage with fewer spacecraft. Geostationary orbit provides persistent regional service and uses a narrow belt in which neighboring systems must coordinate position and spectrum [3][15]. Highly elliptical orbits can provide long dwell over high latitudes that geostationary systems serve poorly.

These differences shape both defense and attack. A small number of high-value satellites can present concentrated targets, but they may be hardened, maneuverable, and closely monitored. A proliferated low Earth orbit constellation presents many targets and can re-route service, but it depends on large-scale production, frequent launch, automated control, gateways, and user terminals. Debris at lower altitudes may decay faster than debris at higher altitudes, while destructive activity in long-lived orbits can create persistent hazards [1][3]. The best architecture depends on the mission, threat, and required recovery time rather than on one universally superior orbit.

### Counterspace Is a Spectrum of Effects

Counterspace operations seek to deceive, disrupt, deny, degrade, or destroy a space system. CRS separates offensive operations from defensive operations and groups weapons into kinetic, non-kinetic, electronic, and cyber forms [4]. Secure World Foundation uses five capability categories: co-orbital, direct-ascent, electronic warfare, directed energy, and cyber [1]. These taxonomies overlap because one classification emphasizes physical mechanism while the other emphasizes delivery and operational form.

Kinetic attacks use physical force. A direct-ascent interceptor launches from Earth toward a satellite; a co-orbital system first enters orbit and later approaches or strikes a target. Kinetic attacks can create permanent loss and, when fragmentation occurs, long-lived debris. Terrestrial strike against a ground station, factory, launch facility, or control center is also a physical counterspace action because it can deny the service without touching the satellite [2][4][8].

Non-kinetic attacks use energy without a physical collision. Lasers can temporarily dazzle optical sensors or damage them at higher power. High-powered microwave effects and radiation from a nuclear detonation can affect electronics over a wider area. Electronic warfare jams or spoofs communications and navigation links. Cyber operations can compromise ground networks, terminals, software supply chains, command systems, or spacecraft interfaces [1][2][4]. Effects vary in duration, visibility, scale, and reversibility, so the label "attack" is insufficient without specifying the target, mechanism, geographic area, duration, and consequence.

### Reversibility Does Not Guarantee Stability

Jamming, dazzling, spoofing, or a reversible cyber action may appear less escalatory than destruction because service can return when the action stops. These tools offer governments options below permanent attack and can permit signaling or battlefield denial without creating debris. Public evidence summarized by Secure World Foundation indicates that non-destructive counterspace capabilities, rather than destructive ones, are the capabilities currently used against space systems in active conflicts [1].

Reversibility does not make intent clear. A temporary loss of warning, communications, or navigation during a crisis may be interpreted as preparation for a larger attack. Interference may spill into civil aviation, emergency communications, or neutral territory. A cyber intrusion intended for espionage may be discovered and interpreted as pre-positioning for sabotage. RAND found that even reversible counterspace capabilities may escalate if they cause accidental damage or if the adversary misinterprets their purpose [5]. The strategic value of reversibility therefore depends on bounded effects, communication, attribution, and the protected mission.

### Dual Use Creates Ambiguity

Many technologies required for peaceful operations also enable attack. Rendezvous and proximity operations support inspection, docking, refueling, repair, assembly, and debris removal. The same navigation, propulsion, robotic arm, and tracking capabilities can approach, manipulate, disable, or relocate another satellite. Missile defense interceptors may have latent ability against low-orbit satellites. Space-domain awareness supports collision avoidance and verification, but it also supplies targeting and battle-damage assessment [1][2][4][14].

Capability alone does not prove hostile intent. An unusual maneuver may be a servicing test, an intelligence collection activity, a malfunction response, or preparation for attack. Conversely, a benign label does not remove military utility. SIPRI reported that uncoordinated or non-consensual proximity operations generate friction because international law does not set a general notification duty or minimum distance for all such operations [14]. The author's synthesis is that behavior, context, disclosure, pattern of life, and effects provide stronger evidence than a platform label.

### Dependence Can Create First-Move Incentives

A state that believes its military advantage depends on vulnerable satellites may fear losing them at the start of conflict. It may disperse capabilities and improve defense, but it may also acquire weapons to disable the adversary's sensors and communications first. If both sides believe early attack provides a decisive advantage, crisis stability weakens even when neither wants a war in space. Concealed capabilities, uncertain attribution, rapid cyber effects, and the difficulty of observing intent can reinforce this problem [5][14].

The relationship with nuclear forces raises the stakes. Satellites may support missile warning, nuclear command and communications, or intelligence used by both conventional and nuclear missions. An attacker may intend to blind conventional forces while the target believes its retaliatory capability is threatened. SIPRI describes this space-nuclear nexus as a source of misperception and escalation risk, while RAND cautions that traditional communication channels may be absent or ineffective in a crisis [5][14]. Resilience can reduce incentives for preemption by making a first strike less decisive, but it cannot by itself explain which attacks a state will treat as strategically intolerable.

### Commercial Systems Redistribute Sovereignty and Risk

Commercial constellations can increase capacity, refresh technology rapidly, and spread service across many satellites. They also blur the line between civilian and military infrastructure when the same network serves households, firms, governments, and armed forces. CSIS reported that Russia publicly described commercial systems supporting military space activity as possible targets [2]. NATO and the United States have responded by seeking contractual access, interoperability, threat information sharing, and integration in exercises [7][9].

Reliance on firms creates governance questions. A provider may control coverage, software updates, terminal authorization, or service conditions during a crisis. Governments may lack replacement capacity if one company dominates a service. Commercial systems may not be hardened to the same standard as purpose-built military systems, particularly against cyber threats. NATO and U.S. strategy therefore emphasize pools of providers, diversified supply chains, security standards, and avoidance of single-provider dependence [7][9]. The author's synthesis is that commercial integration strengthens sovereignty only when governments retain informed alternatives and crisis access arrangements.

### Resilience Is Deterrence by Denial

Resilience is the ability to continue, adapt, recover, or reconstitute a service under attack or failure. Measures include distributing payloads across more satellites; using several orbital regimes; diversifying suppliers, launch sites, and ground stations; hardening links and software; enabling terminals to switch networks; maintaining spares; sharing allied capacity; and rehearsing degraded operations. DOD's planned enterprise satellite communications architecture seeks multiple paths and access points so that failure of one element does not stop communication [6].

Resilience is not synonymous with more satellites. A large constellation can still have common software, shared gateways, one terminal ecosystem, one launch provider, or a concentrated supply chain. Redundancy that shares a common failure is not independent. Resilience also carries coordination costs: multi-orbit terminals, data permissions, interoperability, cyber accreditation, and acquisition schedules must function before a crisis [6][7]. A credible architecture states which failure it must survive and demonstrates recovery under that condition.

### Alliances Turn National Assets into Collective Capacity

Few allies possess complete launch, sensing, communications, navigation, and warning architectures. Collective capacity can pool geography, sensors, industrial bases, spectrum expertise, and commercial contracts. Distributed ground stations improve observation and contact opportunities; common standards let forces use several providers; shared data improves attribution; and multinational use raises the political cost of attacking a system [7].

Alliance dependence also creates burdens and escalation questions. Members may disagree over whether interference constitutes an attack, whether a commercial provider is covered, what evidence is sufficient for attribution, or how to respond proportionally. NATO's strategy seeks better interoperability, space-domain awareness, continuous access, and Council-approved options across the conflict spectrum [7]. The author's synthesis is that an alliance deters most effectively when consultation rules, service priorities, evidence sharing, and replacement arrangements are agreed before disruption.

### Governance Competes Through Rules and Practice

Space governance is not one treaty or institution. It combines binding treaties, national law, spectrum regulation, registration, licensing, export controls, voluntary sustainability guidelines, military doctrine, commercial standards, and repeated state behavior. States disagree over whether the priority should be banning placement of weapons, regulating effects, defining responsible behavior, or negotiating comprehensive arms control with verification [10][11][12][14].

Behavioral measures can advance even when weapon definitions stall. Commitments not to conduct destructive direct-ascent tests, notification of risky maneuvers, exchange of orbital information, protection of critical civilian services, incident communication, and post-mission disposal address observable conduct. Their weakness is that voluntary commitments may be ignored and may not cover concealed capabilities. Their strength is that they can reduce immediate debris and misperception without first resolving every disagreement over what counts as a weapon [11][12][14].

## Evidence

### Open-Source Capability Assessments Show Broad Proliferation but Limited Wartime Use

Secure World Foundation's 2026 assessment compiles public evidence for 13 countries across co-orbital, direct-ascent, electronic warfare, directed energy, and cyber capabilities, while also assessing space situational awareness and military utility. Its country-by-country method distinguishes research, testing, operational capability, and use in conflict rather than inferring deployment from technical possibility. The report finds significant research and development across destructive and non-destructive categories, but states that only non-destructive capabilities are actively being used against satellites in current military operations [1].

The same assessment recorded 6,904 catalogued debris pieces from counterspace testing by the United States, Russia, China, and India, with 2,773 still in orbit at its cutoff [1]. This finding measures one persistent externality of historical tests rather than the complete debris environment. It supports a bounded conclusion: destructive testing can impose risks long after the political demonstration ends, while the current operational pattern favors interference that is temporary, deniable, or locally targeted.

CSIS's 2025 Space Threat Assessment draws on eight years of open-source collection and separates kinetic, non-kinetic, electronic, and cyber mechanisms. It reported no known kinetic anti-satellite missile use during its review period, but documented continuing GPS jamming and spoofing near conflict zones, increasingly sophisticated Chinese and Russian maneuvering in low and geostationary orbit, cyber activity, and threats toward commercial systems used by armed forces [2]. The method also highlights uncertainty: a maneuver can demonstrate skill applicable to warfighting without proving an attack plan.

### Ukraine Demonstrates the Strategic Importance of Links, Terminals, and Firms

The Ukraine war supplies several cases in which the service architecture mattered more than a physical strike on a satellite. Secure World Foundation identifies the February 2022 cyberattack on the user segment of Viasat's commercial broadband service as the largest publicly disclosed cyberattack directly targeting a space system; the attack coincided with Russia's entry into Ukraine and affected terrestrial modems rather than destroying spacecraft [1]. CSIS also documents persistent interference with navigation and broadcast signals and Russian efforts to identify or defeat commercial terminals [2].

These observations show that ground and user segments are operational targets. They also show commercial adaptability: distributed terminals and alternative satellite connectivity can restore service faster than replacement of a large destroyed spacecraft. The case does not establish that commercial systems are invulnerable or politically neutral. It demonstrates that ownership, technical design, government contracts, provider decisions, and cyber defense all shape whether a satellite service remains available in war [1][2][7].

### Rendezvous Operations Demonstrate Useful Capability and Attribution Difficulty

CSIS used commercial space-domain-awareness observations to document maneuvering by Chinese and Russian satellites in both low and geostationary orbit, including close approaches and complex formation behavior [2]. SIPRI's 2026 review compared these concerns with cooperative U.S.-UK and U.S.-French proximity operations conducted in 2025. SIPRI notes that docking, inspection, and refueling are legitimate uses, while a robotic system can also impair another spacecraft; absent shared terminology and notification rules, rhetoric can overstate what a maneuver proves [14].

The cross-case finding is not that all proximity operations are hostile or benign. It is that technical dual use makes intent an evidentiary problem. Observers need accurate tracking, prior orbital patterns, mission disclosure, ownership, maneuver geometry, and communication between operators. A policy that treats every close approach as attack may escalate routine servicing; a policy that treats every declared inspection mission as harmless may ignore preparation for coercion [2][14].

### ESA Data Show That Destructive Effects Can Outlive the Conflict

ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report uses catalogued and asserted object data, physical estimates, compliance metrics, and long-term simulations to assess orbital sustainability. It reports steadily increasing object count, mass, and area; more than 300 launches and 4,000 new payloads in 2025; and significant conjunction risk in heavily used altitude bands [3]. ESA also estimates that current global compliance with debris mitigation remains insufficient for long-term sustainability.

The long-term model is especially relevant to counterspace policy. ESA finds that debris collisions in low Earth orbit would continue to increase the debris population even if no further launches occurred. Under a business-as-usual extrapolation, its environment risk index is projected to reach four times the first target threshold for long-term sustainability [3]. The exact projection depends on assumptions and ESA labels several metrics as estimates, but the mechanism is robust: fragments can collide with other objects and create additional fragments. A debris-generating attack therefore alters a shared environment rather than producing only a bilateral military effect.

### Resilient Communications Require Integration, Not Only Proliferation

GAO examined DOD plans, budgets, operational needs, and stakeholder interviews for an enterprise satellite communications architecture. Traditional systems connected a user terminal to one type of satellite through a relatively linear chain, so failure of one part could interrupt transmission. The planned architecture would integrate military and commercial constellations, ground systems, and multi-network terminals to create alternative paths [6].

GAO found initial progress but identified access permissions, data sharing, terminal acquisition, interoperability, and historically slow acquisition as unresolved challenges. It also found that the hardest work -- integrating hybrid systems and networks -- remained ahead [6]. This is evidence against a simple numerical model of resilience. More satellites can expand options, but the user must be able to discover, authorize, connect to, and operate through those options under contested conditions.

### Strategic-Stability Research Finds No Automatic Safe Form of Counterspace Competition

RAND examined six propositions in U.S. thinking about space escalation: first-use incentives, commercial and allied systems, crisis communications, norms, selective revelation, and reversible capabilities. The study compared conceptual claims with U.S., Chinese, and Russian perspectives and identified conditions under which each stabilizing measure could fail [5]. It found, among other limits, that selective disclosure may be misinterpreted and that reversible effects may still escalate through accidental damage or mistaken intent.

This method matters because deterrence proposals often assume the adversary receives the intended signal. RAND instead asks how different actors understand vulnerability, balance, and hostile intent. Its findings support communications and resilience but reject them as guarantees. The author's synthesis is that strategic stability must be tested against the adversary's beliefs and the protected mission, especially where space systems also support nuclear warning or command [5][14].

### Governance Has Produced Narrow Progress but Not a Comprehensive Settlement

The Outer Space Treaty supplies the basic legal architecture, including peaceful use under international law, freedom of exploration, state responsibility, and the prohibition on placing weapons of mass destruction in orbit [10]. COPUOS's 21 long-term sustainability guidelines add voluntary practices on operational safety, regulation, information, cooperation, and technical capacity [12]. General Assembly Resolution 77/41 established a broad political focal point against destructive direct-ascent anti-satellite missile testing [11].

SIPRI's review of 2025 negotiations finds that comprehensive multilateral progress remained slow because states disagree over procedure, weapon definitions, placement, behavior, and verification. The 2025 open-ended working group initially stalled on procedure before later adopting an agenda and allowing future civil-society participation subject to a no-objection process [14]. The evidence therefore supports a layered governance assessment: narrow behavioral norms can reduce particular risks, but they coexist with expanding national counterspace programs and unresolved arms-control disputes.

### Lunar Planning Shows the Value of Governing Before Congestion

CIGI's 2024 policy brief reviews planned lunar missions, the Outer Space Treaty, the Moon Agreement, Artemis Accords, International Lunar Research Station plans, and transparency gaps. It finds that cislunar space lacks an agreed definition, lunar resource rules remain contested, and current situational awareness concentrates on Earth orbit [13]. It also identifies possible friction around scarce operational locations, resource activity, safety zones, military signaling, and fragmented coalitions.

Unlike the evidence from congested Earth orbit, this is prospective analysis rather than a record of repeated conflict. Its value is preventive. The Moon is subject to stricter peaceful-use rules, and current activity remains limited enough that states can establish information exchange, emergency assistance, location reporting, and coordination before incompatible practices harden [10][13].

## Implications

### States Should Protect Services, Not Merely Satellites

National planning should begin with the service that must survive. For each mission -- warning, communications, navigation, weather, intelligence, or command -- planners should map the spacecraft, links, gateways, terminals, software, data, operators, suppliers, launch access, and legal authority required to deliver an outcome. The worst failure is an architecture that appears redundant in orbit but shares one ground station, terminal type, identity service, software dependency, supplier, or decision authority [6][9].

This service map should identify acceptable degradation and recovery time. Nuclear warning and command demand different assurance from commercial imagery or routine weather data. A short local navigation outage may be manageable for one user and catastrophic for civil aviation or precision operations. The map should therefore distinguish mission criticality, geographic spillover, civilian dependence, and whether the function can be replaced by terrestrial, airborne, allied, or commercial means [2][4][5].

Resilience investments should follow the failure map. Proliferated satellites can reduce target concentration; diversified orbits can complicate attack; optical, radio, and terrestrial paths can provide alternatives; multi-network terminals can redirect traffic; spares and launch contracts can support reconstitution; and manual procedures can preserve essential functions during cyber failure. Each measure should be tested against common-mode failure rather than counted as independent because it has a different owner or label [3][6][9].

### Counterspace Policy Should Separate Effect, Duration, and Escalation Risk

Policymakers should not organize response solely around whether an action is kinetic. A cyberattack that disables thousands of terminals can be more operationally consequential than a reversible laser dazzle against one sensor. A direct-ascent test that destroys an obsolete national satellite can create a larger international externality than an attack designed to produce no debris. A useful classification records target segment, mechanism, duration, reversibility, geographic extent, civilian spillover, debris, attribution confidence, and connection to nuclear or other strategic missions [1][2][4].

Reversible options may provide a rung below destruction, but their employment needs limits and communication. Operators should define when interference will stop, which frequencies or services are excluded, how accidental effects will be detected, and how an adversary can distinguish a bounded response from preparation for wider attack. Where public communication is unsafe, established military or diplomatic channels can still reduce misreading. RAND's findings imply that reversibility is a design property, not an assurance that the opponent will perceive restraint [5].

Destructive attacks should be assessed through the debris and precedent they create. The responsible question is not only whether the target is militarily valuable, but whether fragments persist, which orbital bands they cross, how many neutral systems are exposed, and whether the attacker also depends on that environment. ESA's long-term projections and SWF's test-debris record show why a successful intercept can be a strategic loss if it degrades shared access [1][3].

### Space-Domain Awareness Is a Strategic Public Good

Tracking supports collision avoidance, anomaly diagnosis, warning, attribution, verification, and assessment of whether an attack succeeded. No single sensor network observes every object with equal accuracy, particularly across low orbit, geostationary orbit, and cislunar space. Sharing selected observations among allies, civil agencies, firms, and neutral operators can improve accuracy and expose dangerous behavior without requiring disclosure of every classified capability [1][2][7].

Data sharing requires common definitions and confidence labels. Reports should distinguish a detected maneuver from an inferred mission, a close approach from a hostile act, a communications outage from confirmed jamming, and a technical attribution from a political or legal judgment. Premature certainty can provoke escalation; impossible proof standards can permit repeated coercion. The practical objective is a chain of evidence strong enough for the action under consideration, from operator warning through public attribution and collective response [2][5][14].

Cislunar awareness should be built before traffic rises. Shared ephemerides, mission timelines, emergency contacts, and observation standards can reduce uncertainty around Lagrange points, lunar orbit, transfer trajectories, and surface operations. Because current tracking is concentrated on Earth orbit and the term cislunar lacks a uniform definition, states should first agree on the operational areas and information needed for coordination rather than importing terrestrial chokepoint language without analysis [13].

### Commercial Integration Needs Reciprocal Obligations

Governments benefit from commercial scale, rapid refresh, and diverse innovation, while firms benefit from predictable demand, threat information, and public support during crises. Contracts should specify availability across peace, crisis, and conflict; cyber and supply-chain standards; data rights; priority among civilian and military users; conditions for service restriction; incident reporting; reconstitution; and compensation for extraordinary risk. NATO and DOD both identify continuous access and risk allocation as central to commercial integration [7][9].

Governments should avoid converting one commercial monopoly into a national single point of failure. Pools of providers, interoperable terminals, open interfaces where feasible, and pre-negotiated cross-network access preserve alternatives. Supplier diversity must include launch, components, software, ground infrastructure, and skilled operations, not merely several brands that depend on one underlying chain [6][7][9].

Commercial operators also need clarity about military association. A service used by armed forces may become a target even when it simultaneously supports civilian users. Governments should not conceal this exposure from providers, employees, insurers, or customers. They should identify applicable protection, consultation, threat sharing, and continuity arrangements without promising that civilian ownership creates immunity. CSIS's record of public targeting threats demonstrates that ambiguity already affects commercial risk [2].

### Alliances Should Pre-Agree Consultation and Recovery

Alliance planning should specify which disruptions trigger consultation, what evidence members will share, and which services receive priority. The plan should include attacks on national assets, commercial services contracted by members, terrestrial infrastructure, and interference whose source is uncertain. It should also address cumulative low-level activity, since repeated jamming or cyber probing may remain below a dramatic threshold while steadily reducing operational freedom [2][7].

Exercises should test degraded space support rather than assume normal service. Forces should practice switching communications providers, navigating with reduced satellite input, operating with delayed imagery, sharing commercial data under emergency permissions, and restoring ground systems after compromise. These tests expose interoperability and authority gaps that satellite procurement alone cannot solve [6][7].

Collective resilience can strengthen deterrence by denial. If an attacker cannot remove a function by striking one national satellite or pressuring one provider, the expected military benefit falls. At the same time, alliance integration can widen escalation if the attacker cannot distinguish national from collective systems. Clear signaling about protected services, consultation, and proportional options should accompany technical integration [5][7].

### Governance Should Advance Through Verifiable Behaviors

A comprehensive arms-control settlement remains difficult because technologies are dual use, weapons can be based on Earth or in orbit, and verification of intent is weak. States should continue treaty discussions, but near-term risk reduction can focus on observable behavior: no debris-generating direct-ascent tests; timely registration; disposal and passivation; notification of potentially hazardous proximity operations; exchange of contact data; protection of critical civilian services; and communication during anomalies [11][12][14].

Rules for proximity operations should distinguish consensual servicing, cooperative inspection, routine formation flight, unannounced observation, and coercive approach. Minimum-distance rules alone may be too rigid because safe distance depends on orbit, maneuverability, navigation accuracy, and mission. A more practical framework would require risk assessment, notification when another operator may be affected, safe-separation capability, and explanation of anomalous behavior. Shared terminology would reduce the incentive to label every maneuver "dogfighting" or every servicing vehicle a weapon [13][14].

Debris policy should connect security and sustainability. Destructive testing and use should be judged by their international externality, not only by national ownership of the target. The General Assembly test resolution supplies a focal norm, while ESA data show that disposal compliance and mitigation remain insufficient to stabilize the environment [3][11]. States can strengthen the norm through national moratoria, licensing conditions, allied statements, procurement rules, and transparent reporting of tests that do not create long-lived debris.

### Cislunar Competition Should Be Governed Before Positions Harden

Lunar governance should preserve the Outer Space Treaty's stricter peaceful-use standard and avoid treating scientific or commercial presence as territorial control. Planned missions should publish objectives, trajectories, operating areas, communication frequencies, emergency contacts, and relevant safety needs. Safety arrangements should be limited to preventing harmful interference, proportionate to the actual operation, and open to coordination rather than becoming indefinite exclusion zones [10][13].

Artemis and International Lunar Research Station participants need channels that cross political blocs. Emergency assistance, collision avoidance, location reporting, and scientific data exchange are narrower than agreement over resource ownership and may therefore be achievable first. Emerging space nations can also mediate through COPUOS because they have an interest in preventing early movers from converting technical advantage into rules that others did not help shape [13].

The worst-case cislunar outcome is not necessarily a battle over one location. It is an action-reaction cycle in which vague military signaling, incomplete tracking, and incompatible rules cause each coalition to treat ordinary infrastructure as strategic encirclement. Preventing that outcome requires transparency before assets become numerous, expensive, and politically symbolic [13].

### Analysts Should Use a Dependency-Escalation Audit

A disciplined assessment can use seven questions. First, which service is at stake and who depends on it? Second, which orbital, link, ground, user, software, and supply-chain elements deliver it? Third, what attack mechanisms can affect each element, with what duration and spillover? Fourth, which alternatives and recovery paths have been demonstrated rather than merely planned? Fifth, what evidence would support attribution? Sixth, could interference be interpreted as threatening nuclear forces, alliance commitments, or civilian safety? Seventh, which response denies the attacker's objective with the least irreversible escalation [3][5][6].

This audit prevents three common errors. Satellite counts do not prove resilience when dependencies are concentrated. Dual-use capability does not prove hostile intent without behavior and context. Non-destructive effects are not automatically safe when they create strategic blindness or civilian spillover. The author's synthesis is that space competition remains manageable when actors can absorb failure, identify conduct, communicate limits, and preserve shared orbital access; it becomes unstable when vulnerability, secrecy, and first-move incentives reinforce one another [1][2][5][14].
