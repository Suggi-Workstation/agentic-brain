# Shared Water Creates Leverage, but Institutions Determine Whether Dependence Becomes Cooperation or Coercion

Transboundary water connects states through one physical system, but upstream position, dams, pumping, data, markets, and political power determine how that connection is used. Scarcity can intensify bargaining, yet the historical record shows more international cooperation than violent conflict; the decisive risk is often rapid change without institutions capable of managing its distributional effects.[1][2][3]

## Background

Rivers, lakes, and aquifers became objects of interstate politics long before they became a distinct field of study. Navigation, irrigation, flood control, drinking water, fisheries, and hydropower tied political authority to moving water, while the creation and breakup of empires converted many connected watersheds into systems divided by sovereign borders. Modern hydropolitics emerged from the resulting mismatch: states control territory, but water crosses, accumulates beneath, or drains through that territory according to physical rather than political boundaries.[2][4]

The scale of that mismatch is global. The 2024 UN-Water assessment identifies an estimated 313 transboundary rivers and lakes and 468 transboundary aquifers shared by two or more countries. A total of 153 United Nations Member States depend on water that flows from or to another country, transboundary rivers account for about 60 percent of world freshwater flows, and more than three billion people live in transboundary river and lake basins.[1] These figures describe exposure, not predetermined conflict. They establish that decisions about storage, withdrawal, pollution, data, and ecosystem protection in one jurisdiction can alter costs and options in another.

Early security commentary often framed this interdependence through a simple scarcity-to-war sequence. The empirical literature complicated that claim. Petersen-Perlman, Veilleux, and Wolf reviewed a database of more than 1,800 interstate water events from 1948 through 2000 and reported that cooperative events outnumbered conflictive events by more than two to one. Their review also distinguished water as a cause of disagreement from water infrastructure as a target, tool, or casualty of a wider conflict.[2] The implication is not that water is peaceful. It is that scarcity alone does not specify who acts, which instrument is used, how institutions respond, or whether a dispute remains diplomatic, becomes economically coercive, or escalates into violence.

A second intellectual development shifted attention from conflict counts to power. Zeitoun and Warner's hydro-hegemony framework argues that basin outcomes depend on power asymmetries as well as hydrology. A powerful riparian can combine control of infrastructure, finance, knowledge, legal narratives, alliances, and agenda setting to shape which uses appear legitimate and which alternatives are negotiable. Formal cooperation can therefore coexist with an unequal distribution of water, benefits, voice, or risk.[3] This framework corrected the opposite simplification that any treaty or joint commission proves equitable cooperation.

A third development treated institutions as adaptive systems rather than signed documents. Research summarized by Petersen-Perlman and colleagues identifies agreements, informal working relationships, monitoring, allocation rules, joint fact-finding, and dispute procedures as forms of institutional capacity. Such capacity can stabilize expectations, but a nominal institution may be ineffective if it lacks data, finance, authority, participation, or procedures for drought and new infrastructure.[2] The 2024 UN-Water monitoring method reflects that distinction: an arrangement is counted as operational only when a joint body exists, countries meet and exchange information at least annually, and joint or coordinated management plans or objectives have been established.[1]

International law supplied a framework without mechanically allocating every basin. The 1997 United Nations Watercourses Convention requires equitable and reasonable utilization, participation in protection and development, appropriate measures to prevent significant harm, cooperation in good faith, regular exchange of hydrological and related data, prior notification of planned measures with possible adverse effects, ecosystem protection, and peaceful dispute settlement.[4] These are procedural and substantive standards rather than a universal volumetric formula. Article 6 directs states to consider geography, hydrology, climate, ecology, social and economic needs, dependent populations, effects on other states, existing and potential uses, conservation, costs, and alternatives together.[4]

Groundwater widened the problem. Surface flows are visible at dams and gauging stations, whereas an aquifer's boundaries, recharge zones, storage, flow paths, quality, and depletion may remain uncertain for years. The International Law Commission's 2008 Draft Articles on the Law of Transboundary Aquifers apply related principles to shared groundwater, including equitable and reasonable utilization, cooperation, protection of recharge and discharge zones, monitoring, and regular exchange of geological, hydrogeological, hydrological, meteorological, ecological, and hydrochemical information.[10] UNESCO's GGRETA program demonstrates the practical sequence required in several shared aquifers: build a common scientific model, train institutions, create dialogue, and then develop governance tools.[9]

Climate change adds variability rather than one uniform shortage. Drought, flood, snow and glacier change, altered seasonality, higher evaporative demand, and extreme rainfall can shift both average supply and the frequency of operating emergencies. Population, irrigation, hydropower, urban demand, and pollution can change at the same time. Petersen-Perlman and colleagues identify the hazardous combination as physical or economic change coupled with inadequate institutional capacity, while the UNEP transboundary assessment finds high or very high environmental, human, or agricultural water-stress risk in 41 of 286 assessed basins.[2][11]

The author's synthesis is that transboundary water geopolitics is not a contest over a fixed volume in a pipe. It is bargaining over a variable system that produces water, energy, food, transport, ecosystems, public health, territorial access, and political legitimacy at different places and times. Geography creates mutual dependence; infrastructure and power distribute options; institutions determine whether changes are observed, negotiated, compensated, resisted, or imposed.

## Core Concepts

### Upstream and Downstream Are Positions, Not Complete Power Rankings

Gravity gives upstream actors an important option: they may store, divert, release, pollute, or collect information before water reaches downstream users. A dam can change timing even when annual volume changes little, and irrigation or evaporation can reduce the volume that returns. Pollution and sediment management can also transmit downstream. International law consequently treats planned measures, data exchange, ecosystem protection, and significant harm as connected duties rather than reducing the problem to ownership of flow.[4]

Upstream position is not absolute control. A reservoir needs finance, engineering, transmission lines, markets, operating capacity, and political security. A downstream state may possess a larger economy, stronger military, established infrastructure, treaty rights, trade leverage, or alliances. It may also control access to a market for upstream electricity. Zeitoun and Warner describe basin hegemony as a combination of riparian position, resource-control strategies, and broader material and ideational power, not as a synonym for being upstream.[3]

The author's synthesis is that a useful power map has at least five layers: physical position, infrastructure control, economic alternatives, institutional rights, and narrative legitimacy. A state can be strong in one layer and weak in another. Bargaining changes when a new dam, power interconnector, irrigation scheme, legal coalition, satellite dataset, or alternative food supplier shifts one of those layers.

### Scarcity Is a Stressor, Not a Deterministic Cause of War

Scarcity can be physical, seasonal, qualitative, economic, or institutional. A basin may have enough annual runoff but inadequate dry-season storage; abundant water may be unusable because of pollution; households may lack access despite nearby flow; or governments may lack the information and administrative capacity to allocate supply. Treating all these conditions as one scarcity variable hides the mechanism that produces conflict.[2][11]

The international event record shows why the distinction matters. Cooperative events outnumbered conflictive ones in the historical database reviewed by Petersen-Perlman and colleagues, but conflict and cooperation can coexist in the same basin.[2] A state may exchange flood data while contesting a dam, participate in a commission while withholding information, or sign an agreement that preserves an unequal allocation. The absence of war is therefore not evidence of justice, and a diplomatic dispute is not evidence that institutions have failed. Conflict can reveal an excluded interest and force renegotiation; cooperation can stabilize domination.[2][3]

Risk rises when the rate of change exceeds the rate of institutional adaptation. New storage, rapid irrigation expansion, drought, state fragmentation, population growth, or a shift in relative power can make yesterday's rules inadequate. The UNEP assessment specifically identifies planned dams and diversions in basins where international cooperation instruments are absent or limited, and it recommends updating agreements to include cooperation, information exchange, and prevention of significant harm.[11]

### Dams Convert Flow into Time, Options, and Bargaining Power

A dam does more than hold a quantity of water. It changes when water is available, how quickly floods move, how much electricity can be generated, where sediment settles, which fish migrations remain possible, and how operators respond during drought. Storage converts a variable natural flow into an operating decision. That decision can provide mutual benefits through flood reduction, firm power, irrigation reliability, and drought buffering, or it can impose risk if rules, data, and emergency procedures are unilateral.[11][12]

Filling and operation are different bargaining problems. Initial filling transfers part of the river's flow into a new stock over a bounded period. Long-term operation repeatedly determines releases, reservoir levels, power production, evaporation, drought response, and coordination with downstream reservoirs. An agreement that addresses filling but not multi-year drought, or normal operation but not emergency drawdown, leaves the most consequential choices undefined.[4][12]

Dams also make expectations strategic. An upstream operator may value rapid filling and reliable generation, while downstream irrigators value minimum releases and advance notice. Downstream hydropower may benefit from regulated flows even when political leaders contest the upstream project. The Nature Communications model of coordinated GERD operation found scenarios in which coordination reduced Egyptian water deficits, sustained Sudanese use, and improved economic outcomes relative to a less coordinated operating approach.[12] The result is model-based and conditional, not proof that interests are identical. It shows that operating rules can create a bargaining range that physical ownership alone does not reveal.

### Aquifers Create Hidden Interdependence

Shared groundwater is politically difficult because cause and effect are delayed and difficult to observe. Pumping in one state can lower pressure or storage elsewhere, but the result depends on geology, transmissivity, recharge, well depth, and the time horizon. Pollution can persist underground after its source stops. Some aquifers are hydraulically connected to rivers, wetlands, springs, or coastal discharge zones, so pumping can transfer harm from an apparently separate groundwater system to surface ecosystems.[9][10]

Data can therefore become a source of power. A state with well logs, pumping records, isotope studies, models, and monitoring networks can define the apparent size and behavior of the resource. Another state may distrust that model or lack the capacity to challenge it. The Draft Articles call for regular exchange of available data, best efforts to collect missing information, compatible monitoring methods, and protection of dependent ecosystems.[10] UNESCO's GGRETA work pairs scientific products with training, stakeholder participation, policy instruments, and cross-border dialogue because a shared model is both a technical artifact and a negotiating foundation.[9]

The author's synthesis is that aquifer diplomacy should begin before severe depletion. Once users have invested in farms, cities, and industries based on contested pumping, every adjustment creates visible losers. Early joint monitoring preserves more reversible options than late allocation under crisis.

### Information Is an Operating Resource

River gauges, reservoir levels, rainfall forecasts, snowpack, groundwater heads, water quality, withdrawal records, crop demand, and power schedules shape every other decision. Without common data, states may disagree about both facts and intentions. A release can appear coercive when it follows unexpected rainfall; a legitimate safety operation can appear as manipulation; and a drought-sharing rule cannot function if parties use incompatible measurements.[2][4]

The Watercourses Convention requires regular exchange of readily available hydrological, meteorological, hydrogeological, ecological, water-quality, and forecast information. It also requires technical data and environmental impact information when planned measures may have significant adverse effects.[4] The UN-Water definition of an operational arrangement similarly includes at least annual information exchange.[1] Data exchange is thus not diplomatic decoration. It is part of the control system for shared infrastructure.

Trust does not require blind acceptance. Joint monitoring, agreed stations, published methods, independent technical review, uncertainty ranges, audit rights, and real-time emergency protocols can make claims testable. The author's synthesis is that the objective is not one politically neutral dataset, which may be impossible, but a procedure under which differences in measurements are visible, reproducible, and correctable before they become accusations of bad faith.

### Treaty Design Must Combine Principles with Procedures

Equitable and reasonable utilization does not mean equal volumes. The Watercourses Convention directs states to weigh natural conditions, social and economic needs, dependent populations, existing and potential uses, cross-border effects, conservation, costs, and alternatives as a whole.[4] This flexibility allows basin-specific bargains, but it also creates room for incompatible interpretations. A durable arrangement therefore needs procedures that turn principles into repeated decisions.

Core procedures include a defined geographic scope, a joint body, regular meetings, data exchange, notification of planned measures, consultation periods, joint monitoring, public participation, financing, implementation review, amendment, drought and flood rules, emergency communication, and graduated dispute settlement. The UNECE Practical Guide presents these as building blocks that states can tailor rather than as one institutional blueprint.[5] The World Bank's shared-basin toolkit likewise distinguishes declarations, memoranda, treaties, management plans, financing, guarantees, mediation, and technical support as instruments for different stages and obstacles.[6]

Allocation rules need both specificity and adaptability. Fixed volumes are easy to observe but can become unrealistic under prolonged drought or changed demand. Vague fairness clauses preserve flexibility but can leave each party uncertain about minimum performance. Percentage shares, reservoir-trigger rules, priority bands for vital needs, periodic review, and agreed drought stages can combine precision with variability. The author's synthesis is that a rule is credible when parties can determine what it requires before a crisis, yet revise it through an agreed process when the physical system changes.

### Benefit Sharing Expands the Bargaining Space

Volumetric division asks who receives how much water. Benefit sharing asks which combination of water, power, flood protection, irrigation reliability, navigation, fisheries, ecosystem services, finance, and trade produces gains that can be distributed. The 1995 Mekong Agreement expressly frames cooperation around multiple uses and benefits, including irrigation, hydropower, navigation, flood control, fisheries, recreation, and conservation.[7] UNESCO's 2024 water report similarly argues that cooperation can create economic, social, environmental, and political gains exceeding those available through unilateral action.[14]

Benefit sharing does not eliminate distributional conflict. A hydropower project may create national export revenue while imposing local fisheries or resettlement costs. Downstream flood control may benefit cities but alter floodplain agriculture. Regional power trade can compensate a state treasury without compensating affected communities. Benefits must therefore be identified by recipient, place, timing, and risk, while costs and uncertainty are allocated just as explicitly.[11][14]

The author's synthesis is that benefit sharing is strongest when it changes the feasible set rather than relabeling a loss. Examples include coordinated reservoir operation that produces more reliable power and fewer downstream deficits, a transmission agreement that gives an upstream generator a market while giving downstream users energy, or watershed restoration financed by beneficiaries of reduced sediment and flood risk.

### Hydro-Hegemony Explains Coercion Below the Threshold of War

Hydro-hegemony directs attention to control strategies that do not require open violence. Resource capture uses infrastructure or territorial control to secure a preferred allocation. Integration can bring weaker actors into an order designed by the hegemon. Containment can narrow the issues, parties, or evidence admitted to negotiation. These strategies can be supported by finance, technical expertise, diplomatic recognition, treaty interpretation, and a narrative that presents one use as natural or historically settled.[3]

This perspective changes how cooperation is evaluated. A commission may meet regularly yet exclude a riparian, affected community, or aquifer. Data may be exchanged after key design decisions are irreversible. A treaty may protect existing uses while making future development difficult for late-developing states. Conversely, a powerful state can supply leadership, finance, or guarantees that make basin-wide projects possible. Power is not inherently destructive; unaccountable power makes distributional outcomes difficult to contest.[3]

A complete analysis therefore asks who controls infrastructure, data, finance, agenda setting, legal forums, and external alliances; who can delay an agreement; and who bears the cost of non-agreement. The author's synthesis is that leverage is the ability to change another actor's options at tolerable cost to oneself. Water is one channel through which that broader ability operates.

### Water Links Food, Energy, Ecosystems, and Migration

Irrigation converts water into food and rural income, hydropower converts elevation and regulated flow into electricity, and ecosystems convert flow, sediment, flood pulses, and water quality into fisheries, wetlands, deltas, and livelihoods. These outputs are connected: storing water for dry-season power can conflict with irrigation timing; irrigation can reduce downstream power and environmental flow; sediment trapping can protect a reservoir while weakening a delta; and energy shortages can constrain pumping or treatment.[7][11][14]

Migration belongs in this system but should not be treated as an automatic result of basin scarcity. The World Bank's Ebb and Flow study links water deficits to part of the historical rise in internal migration and finds that dry rainfall shocks have a larger effect on out-migration than wet shocks. It also finds that war and unemployment have been more influential migration drivers than water events in the Middle East and North Africa.[13] Water insecurity can therefore alter livelihoods, destinations, and urban pressure without serving as a single sufficient cause of displacement or interstate conflict.

The author's synthesis is that water geopolitics becomes regional order when basin decisions change food prices, electricity trade, fiscal revenue, rural livelihoods, migration pressure, and confidence in state institutions. A river agreement is narrow if it allocates flow while ignoring the economic systems that make each state value that flow differently.

## Evidence

### Global Event Data Reject a Simple Water-War Rule

Petersen-Perlman, Veilleux, and Wolf synthesize historical event data, treaty records, and case literature. The Basins at Risk dataset they discuss catalogued more than 1,800 interstate water events from 1948 through 2000; cooperative events outnumbered conflictive events by more than two to one, and more than 650 water-related treaties had been signed since 1820 in the database available to them.[2] Their method captures reported interactions across an intensity scale, which makes it more informative than counting wars alone but still dependent on coding, source coverage, and event definitions.

The same review warns against complacency. It identifies 38 acute water disputes involving violence from 1948 through 2008 under a specified definition, while noting that many involved water as a target, tool, or victim of wider conflict rather than the sole cause.[2] It also argues that formal cooperation may reproduce power inequalities. The defensible finding is therefore conditional: interstate interaction over water has historically been more cooperative than conflictive, but the distribution and quality of cooperation require separate evaluation.[2][3]

### Global Monitoring Shows an Institutional Coverage Gap

UN-Water's 2024 indicator report measures whether transboundary basin area is covered by operational arrangements. For the 117 countries where indicator 6.5.2 could be calculated, the average coverage was 59 percent, with no significant change from the 2017 and 2020 reporting rounds. Only 43 of the 153 UN Member States sharing transboundary waters reported operational arrangements covering at least 90 percent of their shared basin area.[1] Because the indicator requires a joint body, meetings, annual information exchange, and coordinated plans or objectives, it measures more than treaty signature.

The metric still has limits. Basin-area coverage does not directly measure equitable outcomes, enforcement quality, community participation, ecological condition, or crisis performance. A large basin can dominate a country's percentage, and national reporting quality varies. Its evidentiary value lies in showing that operational cooperation remains incomplete even under a bounded institutional definition, particularly for aquifers.[1][9]

### The Indus Treaty Demonstrates Resilience and Design Trade-offs

The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty followed nine years of India-Pakistan negotiations assisted by the World Bank. It allocated the Western Rivers -- the Indus, Jhelum, and Chenab -- primarily to Pakistan and the Eastern Rivers -- the Ravi, Beas, and Sutlej -- to India, while permitting specified uses across that division.[8] The treaty created a Permanent Indus Commission with one commissioner from each country and established graduated procedures: questions go to the Commission, differences to a Neutral Expert, and disputes to a Court of Arbitration.[8]

The World Bank describes the treaty as having survived repeated political tension and conflict while providing a framework for irrigation and hydropower development.[8] That record supports the value of clear allocations, a standing commission, information exchange, and differentiated dispute procedures. It does not prove that the treaty resolves every contemporary concern. The allocation structure separates rivers more than it integrates basin-wide environmental management, and later hydropower-design disputes have tested how parallel procedures interact.[8]

The author's assessment is that the Indus case demonstrates institutional endurance rather than permanent harmony. A resilient treaty can preserve communication and channel technical disagreement even while the wider bilateral relationship remains hostile. Its design also shows the cost of stability through partition: clear control can reduce one class of dispute while leaving water quality, ecosystem, climate, and integrated-operation questions less developed.

### The Mekong Shows Cooperation Without Full Consensus

Cambodia, Lao PDR, Thailand, and Viet Nam established the Mekong River Commission through the 1995 Mekong Agreement. The agreement commits the members to cooperation in sustainable development, use, management, and conservation and recognizes multiple uses including irrigation, hydropower, navigation, flood control, fisheries, recreation, and tourism.[7] Its procedures support notification, prior consultation, basin planning, technical review, and information exchange.[6][7]

This framework provides a standing regional forum and common technical processes for projects whose effects cross borders. It does not give every member a simple veto over every project, and China and Myanmar are dialogue partners rather than members of the Commission.[7][15] Mainstream hydropower therefore remains both an object of institutional consultation and a source of distributional conflict over power revenue, fisheries, sediment, and downstream livelihoods.[6][7][11]

The evidence illustrates the difference between process and outcome. Prior consultation can reveal impacts, improve design, and record objections without producing full agreement. The author's assessment is that this is still institutionally valuable, but its quality depends on when information arrives, whether alternatives remain open, how cumulative impacts are assessed, and what happens after consultation ends.

### Coordinated Nile Operation Reveals a Negotiable Technical Frontier

The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam changed the Eastern Nile bargaining structure by creating major upstream storage and hydropower capacity. Egypt, Ethiopia, and Sudan signed a Declaration of Principles in 2015 that incorporated cooperation, equitable and reasonable use, prevention of significant harm, information exchange, dam safety, and peaceful dispute settlement, yet disagreements over filling and long-term operation persisted.[4][12]

Basheer and colleagues linked a Nile system model to an Egyptian macroeconomic model and compared operating approaches across hydrological traces. Their coordinated strategy reduced Egypt's total water deficits relative to the comparison approach in 77 percent of simulated traces, sustained Sudanese Nile water use under the study assumptions, and improved modeled economic outcomes in Egypt and Ethiopia.[12] The model does not settle legal claims or guarantee political agreement; results depend on inflows, demand, reservoir constraints, economic structure, and the selected objectives.

Its contribution is causal and practical. It shows that coordinated operation can move water storage between reservoirs, alter drought exposure, and produce combinations that are not visible in a zero-sum argument about dam ownership. The author's synthesis is that joint modeling is most valuable when it exposes trade-offs and robustness, not when one model is treated as an authority that eliminates political choice.

### Aquifer Programs Show That Common Knowledge Must Be Built

UNESCO's GGRETA program worked in the Stampriet aquifer system in southern Africa, the Pretashkent aquifer in Central Asia, and the Ocotepeque-Citala aquifer in Central America. Its reported outputs include shared knowledge products and groundwater models, training for roughly 1,400 experts and policymakers, stakeholder-participation materials, policy instruments, and working sessions intended to support cooperative governance.[9] The sequence reflects the special difficulty of negotiating a resource whose extent and response to pumping may not be commonly understood.

The International Law Commission's Draft Articles provide a normative counterpart. They call for equitable and reasonable use, protection and preservation of ecosystems, regular data exchange, joint or compatible monitoring, and cooperation over recharge and discharge zones.[10] The Draft Articles are not the same as a basin-specific operating agreement, but they identify the information and ecological relationships such an agreement must address.

The evidence supports a modest conclusion. Training and models do not themselves allocate groundwater or enforce pumping limits. They reduce the factual uncertainty and capacity asymmetry that otherwise make negotiation more vulnerable to suspicion and unilateral development.[9][10]

### Environmental Assessment Shows Why Flow Alone Is Incomplete

The 2016 UNEP-DHI assessment compared 286 transboundary river basins across water quantity, water quality, ecosystems, governance, and socioeconomic exposure. It found high or very high risk for at least one of environmental, human, or agricultural water stress in 41 basins, high or very high nutrient or wastewater pollution risk in 218 basins, and moderate to very high extinction risk across 70 percent of transboundary basin area.[11] These are indicator-based relative-risk classifications, not forecasts that conflict will occur in a specified basin.

The assessment also identifies dams, diversions, sediment trapping, altered downstream flow, pollution, and delta vulnerability as connected transboundary pressures. It recommends integrated cooperation, updated legal instruments, improved dam siting and operation, and attention to upstream effects on deltas.[11] This evidence broadens geopolitical analysis from volumetric allocation to the condition of the system that produces shared benefits.

### Migration Evidence Supports a Multicausal Model

The World Bank's Ebb and Flow project analyzed census and other data covering nearly half a billion people in 64 countries. It reports that water deficits were linked to 10 percent of the historical rise in global internal migration and that dry rainfall shocks had a larger migration effect than wet shocks. It also finds that extreme poverty can trap people rather than enable movement and that war and unemployment outweighed water events as migration drivers in the Middle East and North Africa.[13]

This evidence rejects both denial and determinism. Water conditions can change mobility and the vulnerability of receiving cities, but the result depends on income, employment, governance, conflict, and the cost of moving. The author's synthesis is that transboundary water policy affects migration indirectly through livelihoods, food, energy, and urban services; it should not be presented as a switch that turns drought into international movement or war.

## Implications

### For States and Diplomats: Negotiate the Operating System, Not Only the Allocation

A durable negotiation should define how decisions will be made during normal years, droughts, floods, project construction, equipment failure, and political crisis. Volumetric rights matter, but so do measurement points, timing, water quality, reservoir rules, minimum needs, sediment, ecosystems, notification, review, and amendment. The Watercourses Convention and UNECE Practical Guide provide principles and building blocks, while the Indus and Mekong cases show different ways to institutionalize continuing interaction.[4][5][7][8]

Diplomats should separate issues that can be jointly optimized from issues that require distributional bargaining. Flood forecasting, dam safety, emergency communication, and compatible monitoring can often produce mutual gains. Irrigation allocation, power revenue, reservoir control, and recognition of prior uses may create winners and losers. Combining every issue into one final settlement can allow the hardest question to block useful cooperation; separating every issue can conceal cumulative effects. The author's assessment is that agreements need modularity with a basin-wide accounting layer.

Negotiators should also map the cost of non-agreement. A state may prefer unilateral action if delay preserves bargaining power or if domestic audiences punish compromise. Third parties can provide mediation, finance, guarantees, technical review, or a neutral appointment process, as the World Bank's role under the Indus treaty illustrates.[8] Third-party involvement should have a defined mandate; otherwise it can shift rather than reduce power asymmetry.

### For Treaty Designers: Build Adaptive Specificity

The worst treaty-design failure is a rule that is precise under yesterday's hydrology and unusable under tomorrow's. The opposite failure is a broad fairness principle that provides no observable standard during crisis. Designers should combine explicit baselines with adjustable mechanisms: percentage or trigger-based drought sharing, reservoir bands, priority for vital human needs, ecological thresholds, periodic review, and a procedure for new science or infrastructure.[4][5]

An operational arrangement should specify who collects which data, where and how measurements occur, how quickly they are shared, how uncertainty is reported, and who can audit or challenge the record. Joint bodies need a budget, technical staff, decision rules, public records, and escalation procedures. The UN-Water operational criteria are a minimum; effective performance also requires implementation capacity and legitimacy.[1][2]

Dispute settlement should be graduated. Technical questions may need a commission or neutral expert; legal interpretation may need arbitration; urgent safety issues need rapid consultation; and political deadlock may need mediation or heads-of-government engagement. The Indus treaty's separate tracks show the value of matching forum to issue, while its later procedural tensions show why the relationship among tracks must be explicit.[8]

### For Dam Owners and System Operators: Treat Coordination as Infrastructure

Concrete and turbines are only part of a transboundary project. Forecast exchange, coordinated release schedules, drought protocols, emergency notification, joint simulations, dam-safety review, and post-project monitoring are operating infrastructure. They require staffing, communications, finance, exercises, and maintenance. A written rule that cannot be implemented during a communications failure or political rupture is not an operating capability.[4][5]

Project assessment should compare system-wide alternatives rather than one site's generation. Questions include whether existing reservoirs can be re-operated, whether power trade can reduce the need for additional storage, how sediment affects downstream deltas and reservoir life, which communities lose fisheries or land, and whether benefits can be contractually transferred. UNEP's assessment shows that dam impacts extend through ecosystems and deltas, while the GERD modeling study shows that coordinated reservoir operation can change water and economic outcomes.[11][12]

Operators should publish assumptions and performance against agreed rules. Real-time transparency can reduce false attribution, but security-sensitive details may require protected channels. The Watercourses Convention recognizes both regular data exchange and a limited national-security exception accompanied by a duty to provide as much information as possible in good faith.[4]

### For Aquifer States: Invest Before the Crisis Becomes Visible

Shared-aquifer governance should begin with a jointly accepted conceptual model: boundaries, recharge, discharge, groundwater-surface-water connection, quality, pumping, uncertainty, and monitoring gaps. States should harmonize well identifiers, units, sampling methods, and reference levels so that exchanged numbers are comparable. Where science remains uncertain, phased pumping rules and review triggers preserve reversibility.[9][10]

Local users must be included because most groundwater control is exercised through wells, permits, electricity, land use, and agricultural incentives inside each state. An interstate agreement without domestic implementation can produce formal cooperation and continued depletion. The author's assessment is that national well governance and international aquifer diplomacy are one system operating at different scales.

Recharge areas and dependent ecosystems may lie outside the zone where most pumping occurs. Protecting only the well field can therefore fail. The Draft Articles' attention to recharge, discharge, monitoring, and dependent ecosystems provides a broader unit of management than a line drawn around current extraction.[10]

### For Food, Energy, Climate, and Migration Planners: Use a Basin Balance Sheet

Sector ministries often optimize different outputs from the same water. Agriculture seeks seasonal reliability, energy agencies seek storage head and dispatchable generation, cities seek quality and continuity, disaster agencies seek flood space, and environmental authorities seek flow, sediment, and habitat. A basin balance sheet should record how each proposed action changes water quantity, timing, quality, energy, food production, ecosystem services, fiscal transfers, and household risk in every affected jurisdiction.[7][11][14]

Climate scenarios should test sequences, not only averages. Multi-year drought, consecutive floods, hot years with high power demand, and simultaneous crop failure can create different bargaining pressures from a smooth change in mean flow. Agreements should specify which indicators trigger emergency rules and how normal rules resume. The author's synthesis is that uncertainty is not a reason to postpone cooperation; it is a reason to agree on monitoring and revision before parties know which scenario will favor them.

Migration planning should remain multicausal. Water deficits can weaken livelihoods and increase movement, but poverty can trap people, and conflict or employment may dominate the decision.[13] Basin investments should therefore be linked to rural income, urban service capacity, and social protection rather than justified by unsupported predictions of mass cross-border migration.

### For Analysts: Audit Power, Performance, and Distribution Separately

Analysts should not infer peace from a treaty, conflict from a dam, or coercion from upstream geography. First, measure physical exposure: flow, storage, variability, quality, sediment, recharge, and alternatives. Second, measure institutional performance: meetings, data exchange, plans, compliance, amendment, and dispute handling. Third, map power: finance, infrastructure, expertise, alliances, agenda control, and the cost of delay. Fourth, trace distribution: who receives water and benefits, who bears local cost, and whose claims are represented.[1][3][5]

Case comparison should preserve basin differences. The Indus largely partitions river use through a durable bilateral treaty; the Mekong organizes multi-use consultation among four lower-basin members; the Eastern Nile combines a new upstream dam with contested system operation; and shared aquifers require joint knowledge before many impacts can be attributed.[7][8][9][12] These are mechanisms to compare, not templates to copy whole.

Quantitative models should be treated as structured arguments. Inputs, objectives, constraints, uncertainty, and excluded effects should be visible. A model can identify a feasible operating range or expose a hidden trade-off, but it cannot decide whose risk is legitimate. The GERD study is valuable because it compares coordinated system outcomes under many hydrological traces; its conclusions remain conditional on model design and political implementation.[12]

### A Practical Basin Bargaining Audit

A disciplined assessment can use nine questions. First, which surface and groundwater units are physically connected? Second, who controls storage, diversion, pumping, data, and finance? Third, which uses are consumptive, seasonal, or quality-dependent? Fourth, what changes under drought, flood, and infrastructure failure? Fifth, which communities and ecosystems bear costs outside national averages? Sixth, what information is jointly observable? Seventh, which benefits can be enlarged or transferred? Eighth, which institution can make, review, and enforce each decision? Ninth, how could the proposed arrangement entrench a power imbalance or fail under political rupture?[2][3][4][5]

The author's final assessment is that the central geopolitical variable is not absolute scarcity but controllable dependence. Shared water becomes coercive when one actor can alter another's essential options faster than institutions and alternatives can respond. It becomes cooperative when states can observe the system, negotiate distribution, share or compensate benefits and costs, and revise rules without treating every hydrological change as a test of sovereignty.[1][2][3][5]
