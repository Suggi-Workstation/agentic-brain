# Nuclear Proliferation and Nonproliferation -- Weapons Spread When Security and Institutions Fail Together

Nuclear proliferation is not the automatic result of technical capability: states seek weapons when leaders judge them valuable and feasible, while many capable states stop short because alliances, norms, verification, domestic coalitions, and expected costs make restraint preferable [6][7][8]. The nonproliferation regime works by reinforcing those reasons for restraint, but it becomes fragile when security guarantees lose credibility, violations appear to pay, safeguards cannot establish peaceful intent, or nuclear-armed states modernize while disarmament commitments stagnate [8][10][13][15].

## Background

The first nuclear weapons created both a military technology and a political category. The United States used atomic bombs in 1945; the Soviet Union tested a device in 1949, followed by the United Kingdom, France, and China. Early forecasts often expected a much larger nuclear club because the scientific principles were public and civilian nuclear programs could diffuse relevant knowledge. Yet the historical outcome was selective proliferation rather than a simple technological cascade. Scott Sagan's review of the proliferation literature emphasizes that analysts still face uncertainty in identifying when programs began, how far they advanced, and why some were abandoned, which cautions against treating any single motive as a universal explanation [6].

The 1968 Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, or NPT, converted an emerging power distribution into a legal bargain. Articles I and II prohibit transfer or acquisition of nuclear weapons; Article III requires non-nuclear-weapon parties to accept International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards; Article IV recognizes peaceful nuclear cooperation consistent with nonproliferation obligations; Article VI commits all parties to pursue negotiations on ending the nuclear arms race and on nuclear disarmament; and Article X permits withdrawal after notice when extraordinary events jeopardize a party's supreme interests [1]. The treaty entered into force on 5 March 1970 and was extended indefinitely in 1995. The IAEA reports 191 states parties, making it the most widely adhered-to treaty in its field [2].

The bargain is unequal by design but not one-sided in text. The NPT recognizes as nuclear-weapon states only the five states that manufactured and exploded a nuclear device before 1 January 1967, while other parties renounce acquisition [2]. Article IV recognizes an inalienable right to peaceful nuclear research, production, and use in conformity with Articles I and II; Article VI legally obligates each party to pursue in good faith negotiations on ending the nuclear arms race and on nuclear disarmament [1]. This structure helped separate legitimate civilian programs from prohibited weapon acquisition, but it also embedded a hierarchy between recognized possessors and permanent non-possessors. Nina Tannenwald argues that this tension has become a source of contestation because a regime intended to transform the nuclear order can appear to preserve the status of nuclear-armed states when disarmament progress stalls [13].

Safeguards evolved because accounting for declared material was insufficient to detect every undeclared activity. Comprehensive safeguards agreements authorize the IAEA to verify that nuclear material is not diverted from peaceful uses [2]. The discovery of Iraq's undeclared program after the 1991 Gulf War exposed the limits of a system centered on declared facilities [3]. Programme 93+2 combined measures available under existing comprehensive-safeguards authority, including environmental sampling and satellite imagery, with measures requiring additional legal authority. In 1997 the IAEA Board approved the Model Additional Protocol, which provides broader declarations and access across the nuclear fuel cycle and improves the Agency's ability to assess undeclared material and activities [3]. As of 31 December 2025, Additional Protocols were in force with 144 states and Euratom, leaving substantial variation in verification authority across countries [3].

The regime also grew beyond the NPT and IAEA. The Nuclear Suppliers Group coordinates guidelines for transfers of nuclear items and nuclear-related dual-use equipment, software, materials, and technology [5]. United Nations Security Council Resolution 1540, adopted in 2004, requires states to establish and enforce measures against non-state acquisition of nuclear, chemical, and biological weapons and their means of delivery [4]. These measures address different failure points: treaty commitments define prohibited conduct, safeguards generate information, export controls restrict supply, national implementation reduces illicit access, and diplomacy or sanctions attempt to change incentives. No layer substitutes fully for the others [2][3][4][5].

The number of nuclear-armed states remains small relative to early fears, but nonproliferation has not ended nuclear competition. SIPRI estimated that nine states possessed approximately 12,187 nuclear weapons at the start of 2026, with 9,745 in military stockpiles and 4,012 deployed with operational forces. It also reported that all nine nuclear-armed states strengthened their arsenals during 2025 and that some deployed new nuclear-armed or nuclear-capable systems [9]. This is vertical proliferation or qualitative and quantitative improvement within existing arsenals, distinct from horizontal proliferation to additional states. The distinction matters because arsenal modernization can affect threat perceptions and the legitimacy of demands that non-nuclear states remain permanently restrained [9][13].

Nonproliferation therefore developed as a continuing political process, not a solved technical problem. The IAEA applied safeguards in 190 states during 2025, but its conclusions varied with each state's legal arrangements and the information available [10]. The 2025 report on the Democratic People's Republic of Korea stated that the Agency could not verify the correctness and completeness of the DPRK's declarations and remained unable to conduct verification activities there, illustrating how withdrawal, expulsion of inspectors, or denial of access can degrade institutional knowledge [12]. The 2026 NPT Review Conference then ended without consensus on a final declaration, continuing a sixteen-year period without a consensus review outcome [15]. These facts do not establish regime collapse, but they show why compliance, verification, security, and legitimacy must be analyzed together.

## Core Concepts

### Capability, intention, and decision

A state needs relevant capability to build nuclear weapons, but capability does not determine intention. Weapon acquisition requires fissile material, specialized facilities and knowledge, delivery options, organizational coordination, resources, secrecy or political protection, and a leadership decision to proceed. Civilian nuclear activity can produce expertise and infrastructure without proving a weapon decision, and Sagan concludes that the empirical relationship between civilian nuclear power and weapon acquisition is not sufficiently robust to support a simple deterministic rule [6]. The analytical task is therefore to distinguish technical latency -- the ability to move toward a weapon -- from active pursuit and completed acquisition.

Latency has contradictory effects. It can provide an option value that reassures leaders without crossing the political threshold to weaponization. It can also alarm rivals, invite coercion, and shorten warning time if enrichment or reprocessing capacity can produce weapon-usable material. Safeguards seek to reduce this ambiguity by verifying declared material and, where an Additional Protocol applies, by increasing confidence that undeclared activities are absent [2][3]. The author's synthesis is that latency is neither proliferation nor innocence by itself; its strategic meaning depends on declared purpose, legal commitments, transparency, facility design, regional threats, and the time required to convert capacity into a usable arsenal.

### Security demand and strategic opportunity

Security explanations begin with the possibility that leaders view nuclear weapons as protection against conquest, coercion, or abandonment. Monteiro and Debs argue that acquisition requires both willingness and opportunity: very powerful states may resist preventive attack, while weaker states may succeed when they have sufficiently strong protection or when adversaries cannot credibly stop them. A powerful ally can lower demand by providing security guarantees, yet it can also protect a client from preventive attack and thereby increase opportunity if the client still wants the bomb [7]. This two-sided effect explains why alliance labels alone do not predict restraint.

Extended deterrence can substitute for a national arsenal when an ally believes the patron has the capability and resolve to defend it. A powerful ally can reduce proliferation demand when it credibly covers the protege's significant security threats, while a consequential threat of abandonment can constrain a relatively weak ally's opportunity to proliferate [7]. The relevant question is not merely whether a treaty alliance exists, but whether decision-makers expect protection to persist under the specific dangers they fear.

Adversaries shape opportunity as well as demand. Monteiro and Debs argue that a preventive-war threat is less credible when a potential proliferator's relative power or powerful ally makes attack costly; otherwise preventive pressure can deny the opportunity to acquire weapons [7]. Counterproliferation can therefore produce opposite effects: credible pressure may stop a vulnerable program, while threats that increase insecurity without removing capability may strengthen the perceived need for a deterrent. This is why military threats cannot be evaluated separately from the target's security incentives and the credibility of alternatives to weapon acquisition.

### Domestic organizations, prestige, and norms

Security is not the only source of demand. Sagan organizes major explanations around external security, domestic politics, and norms or identity [6]. Military organizations, nuclear laboratories, political leaders, and industrial agencies may favor programs for different reasons, including budgets, authority, technological ambition, or bureaucratic survival. Other ministries, exporters, fiscal authorities, and social movements may oppose weaponization because it threatens economic integration, alliance benefits, democratic legitimacy, or alternative spending priorities. A state's decision is thus an outcome of institutional competition as well as a response to international conditions.

Nuclear weapons can also signify modernity, sovereignty, status, or membership in an exclusive power category. Norms work in both directions: possession may be framed domestically as proof of national achievement, while nonpossession may be embedded in legal identity, anti-nuclear principles, regional institutions, or expectations of responsible statehood [6][13]. These meanings are not decorative rhetoric. They affect which costs leaders accept, how publics interpret external pressure, and whether compromise appears prudent or humiliating.

The NPT attempts to strengthen restraint as a normal and reciprocal behavior. Kaplow argues that members infer the regime's reliability from its compliance record: long periods with few violations encourage compliance, while visible violations increase the probability that other members pursue weapons. His analysis of nuclear programs from 1968 to 2010 found that NPT members were more likely to pursue nuclear weapons following a larger number of recent regime violations [8]. In this model, enforcement matters not only for the immediate violator but also for the information that the response sends to every state assessing whether others will keep the bargain.

### The NPT bargain and its institutional layers

The NPT performs several functions at once. It creates legal commitments against transfer and acquisition, authorizes safeguards, protects peaceful use, preserves a disarmament obligation, and supplies recurring conferences where parties review implementation [1][2]. The treaty does not itself inspect facilities, control every export, impose automatic sanctions, or guarantee allies. Its effectiveness depends on institutions and state policies that translate broad obligations into information and consequences.

IAEA safeguards are an information system rather than a world police force. Inspectors use records, measurements, containment and surveillance, on-site access, and analysis to evaluate whether nuclear material and activities remain peaceful. A comprehensive safeguards agreement focuses on verifying nuclear material declared by a non-nuclear-weapon state, while an Additional Protocol expands information and access needed to assess possible undeclared activity [2][3]. The 2025 Safeguards Implementation Report shows that conclusions are calibrated: for some states the Agency can conclude that all nuclear material remained in peaceful activities, while for others its conclusion is limited to declared material [10]. A safeguards conclusion is therefore evidence bounded by legal authority, access, and available information, not a blanket certificate about every possible activity.

Export controls address supply before material or equipment reaches a program. The Nuclear Suppliers Group maintains one set of guidelines for nuclear transfers and another for nuclear-related dual-use items, software, materials, and technology [5]. Monteiro and Debs note that alternative suppliers and indigenous development can limit supply-side restrictions [7]. The author's assessment is that export control creates friction but cannot substitute for diplomacy or enforcement.

Resolution 1540 extends the architecture to non-state actors and domestic implementation. It obligates states to prevent support for non-state acquisition and to establish controls over related materials, including accounting, physical protection, border measures, and export controls [4]. This layer recognizes that proliferation pathways can run through brokers, companies, criminal networks, or weak regulatory systems rather than only direct state-to-state transfers. Implementation capacity differs widely, so international assistance and national institutions remain part of the security outcome.

Monteiro and Debs argue that the effects of inspections, supply restrictions, and sanctions depend on credible threats and assurances, while stronger allied security commitments can reduce a protege's willingness to proliferate [7]. The author's synthesis is that durable restraint often requires instruments aimed separately at security demand, technical opportunity, and verification uncertainty.

### Restraint, reversal, rollback, and disarmament

Nonproliferation outcomes form a spectrum. A state may never explore weapons, may study an option without an organized program, may begin and later reverse a program, may achieve a device but not a durable operational arsenal, or may dismantle weapons it already possesses. These outcomes have different evidentiary value. Egeland warns that no historical case perfectly represents a deeply entrenched nuclear power abandoning a large, long-standing deterrent complex, so lessons from rollback and partial reductions should not be generalized casually to complete disarmament by established nuclear powers [14].

Restraint can result from several mechanisms acting together: a threat recedes; an alliance becomes more credible; leaders change; a program becomes fiscally or technically unattractive; international access and legitimacy become more valuable; domestic coalitions shift; or norms against possession gain force [6][7][14]. Reversal does not prove that security was irrelevant, because a different security arrangement may have replaced a national arsenal. Nor does it prove that treaties alone caused the outcome, because states often join or deepen commitments after a domestic decision to restrain. Causal analysis must establish timing and compare plausible alternatives.

South Africa is the clearest case of a state dismantling nuclear devices it had built and then accepting international verification. It joined the NPT in July 1991 and concluded a comprehensive safeguards agreement in September 1991. IAEA teams examined records, material inventories, former facilities, and the terminated weapon program, supported by extensive South African cooperation and access [11]. The case demonstrates that verified rollback is possible, but it also depended on exceptional political change and transparency [11][14]. It is evidence for verification after disarmament, not proof that the same pathway applies automatically to every nuclear-armed state.

Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan inherited Soviet nuclear weapons on their territory but did not inherit the same independent operational posture as a mature national arsenal. Their denuclearization remains important, yet it differs from dismantling an indigenous program controlled through long-standing national institutions. Egeland uses such variation to argue that disarmament theory must distinguish cases carefully and remain explicit about inferential limits [14]. The broader lesson is methodological: apparent examples of "giving up the bomb" may involve different control, ownership, threat, and organizational conditions.

### Vertical proliferation, horizontal proliferation, and regime legitimacy

Horizontal proliferation adds nuclear-armed states; vertical proliferation expands or modernizes arsenals within states that already possess them. The NPT focuses strongly on preventing horizontal spread but includes Article VI commitments relevant to the arms race and disarmament [1]. SIPRI's 2026 assessment that all nine nuclear-armed states strengthened their arsenals in 2025 indicates that restraint by non-nuclear states coexists with continuing modernization by possessors [9]. This interaction matters politically even when the legal categories differ.

Tannenwald argues that uneven obligations and stalled disarmament can make the regime appear as a failed promise to non-nuclear states, stimulating contestation and support for institutions outside the NPT framework [13]. This does not mean that modernization mechanically causes a new state to proliferate. It means that legitimacy influences cooperation, diplomatic coalitions, willingness to accept stronger verification, and confidence that restraint is reciprocal. The author's synthesis is that horizontal and vertical proliferation are linked through threat perceptions and institutional legitimacy even though their technologies, actors, and legal rules are not identical.

## Evidence

### The broad record: fewer proliferators than capability alone predicts

The central empirical puzzle is the gap between widespread technical capacity and the small number of nuclear-armed states. Sagan's review documents both states that developed weapons and states that began but abandoned programs, while emphasizing uncertainty in the underlying historical data [6]. Monteiro and Debs test a strategic account against the overall proliferation pattern and detailed cases involving the Soviet Union, Iraq, Pakistan, South Korea, and West Germany; they conclude that willingness and opportunity, including adversary and ally behavior, help explain why threat alone does not produce uniform outcomes [7]. The evidence rejects a one-variable model in which technology automatically becomes a weapon.

The NPT's membership and compliance record provide a second broad observation. The IAEA reports 191 parties, and by the end of 2025, 183 non-nuclear-weapon parties had comprehensive safeguards agreements in force while three had not yet brought the required agreements into force [2]. The author's methodological caution is that near-universal membership alone does not identify the treaty's causal effect because states with low nuclear demand may be especially willing to join. Kaplow tests a narrower track-record mechanism: analysis of nuclear weapons programs from 1968 to 2010 finds that regime members were more likely to pursue weapons after a larger number of recent violations [8].

Kaplow's compliance study supplies a more specific mechanism. Using data on nuclear weapon programs from 1968 through 2010, it finds that regime members were more likely to pursue weapons when recent violations were more numerous [8]. The method treats the institution's recent track record as information about whether other members will honor their commitments. The finding supports an interdependent model of restraint: one state's violation can alter another state's expectations even when the two are not immediate adversaries. It also implies that impunity and selective enforcement create systemic costs beyond the original case.

### Safeguards: broad reach with calibrated conclusions

The IAEA's 2025 implementation report offers direct evidence about verification scale and limits. Safeguards applied in 190 states under agreements in force, and the Secretariat drew conclusions according to the type of agreement, implementation results, and all safeguards-relevant information available [10]. For states where the Agency had both a comprehensive safeguards agreement and an Additional Protocol and could complete the necessary evaluation, it could provide broader assurance; in other cases, conclusions related only to declared nuclear material [3][10]. The evidence shows that verification is not binary. Stronger legal authority and access can materially increase what the Agency is able to conclude.

The Additional Protocol's institutional history also illustrates policy learning. The IAEA describes how measures available under existing comprehensive agreements were supplemented by measures requiring new legal authority, including broader information about and access to the nuclear fuel cycle [3]. This change followed recognition that traditional material accountancy at declared sites did not fully address undeclared activities. As of the end of 2025, 144 states and Euratom had protocols in force [3]. That coverage is substantial but incomplete, leaving unequal assurance across states with otherwise similar NPT status.

The DPRK demonstrates the opposite condition: verification cannot substitute for access. The IAEA reported in August 2025 that it had not been able to verify the correctness and completeness of the DPRK's declarations and remained unable to carry out verification activities in the country. The Agency therefore relied on limited knowledge while maintaining readiness to return if requested [12]. This case is not evidence that safeguards are useless; it shows the boundary condition that an inspectorate requires legal authority, physical access, and continuing cooperation to produce robust findings.

### South Africa: rollback plus intrusive verification

South Africa provides evidence that a completed national weapon program can be dismantled and subjected to international scrutiny. After accession to the NPT and conclusion of a safeguards agreement in 1991, the IAEA verified a large declared inventory across an extensive fuel cycle. Following the 1993 public disclosure of the former weapon program, the verification team added weapon experts, examined historical records, visited connected facilities, and assessed whether nuclear material from the program had been recovered and placed under safeguards [11].

The method mattered as much as the political declaration. South Africa supplied extensive access to records and locations, while the IAEA compared declared inventories, operating histories, waste streams, facilities, and information provided by member states [11]. This enabled the Agency to investigate apparent discrepancies rather than accepting a single aggregate declaration. The case supports three bounded findings: rollback requires a domestic decision, credible verification requires cooperation and access, and material accountancy must be integrated with program history. It does not establish that external pressure alone causes disarmament or that verification can compel a state that refuses access.

Egeland's comparative analysis places the South African case alongside nuclear reversals, post-Soviet denuclearization, stockpile reductions, and non-nuclear abolition analogies [14]. His method asks whether cases resemble the hard problem of an established state abandoning a large arsenal and a durable deterrence identity. He finds that commonly invoked cases vary greatly and that some offer limited evidence for general theories of complete disarmament. This cautions policymakers against importing a successful verification technique while ignoring the political conditions that made consent possible.

### Strategic protection and proliferation opportunity

Monteiro and Debs compare cases to examine the joint behavior of potential proliferators, adversaries, and allies [7]. Their account explains why a severe threat may not lead to acquisition when a program is vulnerable to preventive action, and why an ally can either restrain proliferation through credible protection or facilitate opportunity by shielding a client. South Korea and West Germany illustrate how security guarantees and patron pressure can support non-nuclear outcomes, while Pakistan illustrates how strategic conditions can permit acquisition despite opposition [7]. The case comparisons support conditional rather than universal claims about alliances.

This approach also clarifies why extended deterrence debates matter before any technical decision. If a protected state believes the patron's guarantee remains credible, a national arsenal imposes costs without adding enough security to justify them. If leaders expect abandonment, those same alliance ties may no longer offset demand. Yet an ally may use economic, military, and diplomatic leverage to make pursuit costly [7]. The observable outcome depends on the balance among assurance, coercion, domestic politics, and opportunity, which is why public calls for an arsenal do not by themselves establish an active program.

### Contemporary stress: modernization and failed consensus

SIPRI's 2026 inventory documents a system with nine nuclear-armed states and approximately 12,187 weapons at the start of the year. Although the total continued to decline because the United States and Russia dismantled retired weapons, SIPRI warned that dismantlement was slowing and could soon be outpaced by additions to global stockpiles. It also observed modernization across all nine arsenals [9]. The method combines open-source force assessments with estimates where official transparency is incomplete, so exact numbers carry uncertainty; the direction of broad modernization is the stronger inference.

Institutional diplomacy shows parallel strain. The 2026 NPT Review Conference ended without a consensus final declaration, and the United Nations reported that no review conference had reaffirmed or strengthened commitments by consensus for sixteen years [15]. Failure to agree does not repeal treaty obligations or prove noncompliance, because the NPT remains in force and safeguards continue. It does demonstrate persistent disagreement over how to balance nonproliferation, peaceful use, risk reduction, and disarmament. Tannenwald's institutional analysis connects such contestation to the perception that a legally strong regime remains unequal and insufficiently transformative [13].

Taken together, the evidence supports a layered conclusion rather than a single success-or-failure verdict. The regime has near-universal membership, extensive verification, and a small nuclear club relative to the diffusion of technical knowledge [2][6][8][10]. It also faces incomplete Additional Protocol coverage, cases where inspectors lack access, inconsistent enforcement, continuing arsenal modernization, and repeated diplomatic deadlock [3][8][9][12][15]. The author's synthesis is that nonproliferation is successful as an ongoing restraint system but fragile as a reciprocal political bargain.

## Implications

### For policymakers: match instruments to causal mechanisms

Policy should begin by diagnosing why a particular state is considering weapons. If the primary driver is fear of attack or abandonment, export controls alone cannot remove demand; credible defense commitments, conventional resilience, regional diplomacy, or threat reduction may be necessary [7]. If the primary constraint is procurement, coordinated controls and intelligence can delay progress [4][5]. If domestic organizations benefit from a program, sanctions or inducements must be evaluated for their distributional effects rather than only their aggregate economic size. The author's synthesis is that policy packages outperform symbolic reliance on a single tool because acquisition itself requires multiple conditions.

Security assurances require both reassurance and leverage. A patron that only threatens punishment may intensify its ally's fear of abandonment, while a patron that offers unconditional protection may reduce leverage over risky behavior. Alliance consultation, visible capabilities, and clear conditions can make protection credible without treating proliferation as costless [7]. For adversaries, coercive threats should be judged against the possibility that they increase nuclear demand or accelerate a vulnerable program. Preventive action may delay capability but can also remove confidence in diplomatic restraint; this tradeoff must be stated rather than assumed away.

Enforcement should be assessed for system-wide signaling. Kaplow's evidence suggests that violations change other states' beliefs about whether the regime works [8]. A response that appears selective, delayed, or reversible may therefore have consequences beyond the target. Consistency does not require identical penalties in unlike cases, but it does require explainable standards and visible consequences tied to conduct. Conversely, verified compliance should produce credible benefits, because a system that delivers penalties reliably but relief unreliably weakens incentives to negotiate.

### For verification institutions: communicate the boundary of each conclusion

Safeguards reporting should preserve the distinction between declared material remaining peaceful and broader assurance about the absence of undeclared material and activities [3][10]. Public debate often collapses these into a binary label of compliant or noncompliant. That simplification can generate false confidence or unwarranted alarm. Policymakers and analysts should identify the agreement in force, the access available, the period covered, unresolved questions, and the exact IAEA conclusion before using safeguards evidence.

Universalizing the Additional Protocol would narrow an important assurance gap, but legal authority alone is not sufficient. The IAEA also needs resources, technical competence, timely declarations, analytical integration, and practical access [3][10]. States need domestic systems that account for and control nuclear material, regulate trade, secure facilities, and investigate illicit procurement [4]. Investment in these capacities is not administrative overhead; it determines whether international commitments generate usable evidence.

Verification plans for rollback should be designed around program history, not only current inventory. South Africa shows the value of reconstructing production records, examining waste and material balances, visiting former facilities, and comparing declarations with external information [11]. Future agreements may also need procedures for weapon-related knowledge, equipment disposition, environmental sampling, and long-term monitoring. The exact measures will be case-specific, but the principle is general: confidence comes from testing a declaration against independent evidence and resolving discrepancies.

### For analysts: separate warning indicators from proof

Nuclear latency, enrichment capability, missile development, procurement anomalies, leadership rhetoric, and alliance debates are indicators of potential risk, not interchangeable proof of a weapon decision. Intelligence analysis should combine technical observations with organizational behavior, political authorization, strategic context, and alternative explanations [6][7]. Overstating certainty can distort diplomacy or create pressure for preventive action; understating uncertainty can allow a clandestine program to mature. Calibrated language is therefore part of sound nonproliferation policy.

Analysts should also separate horizontal spread from vertical modernization while tracing their interaction. New weapon states change the number of actors and regional relationships; modernization by existing possessors changes force quality, perceived threats, and regime legitimacy [9][13]. Combining both under one undifferentiated label obscures causal mechanisms. Ignoring their interaction is equally misleading because arsenal expansion and disarmament stagnation affect how non-nuclear parties judge the fairness and durability of restraint.

Case selection requires similar discipline. South Africa is strong evidence that dismantlement can be verified under conditions of transparency, but weaker evidence that established nuclear powers will choose abolition [11][14]. The post-Soviet cases illuminate inherited weapons, bargaining, and security assurances but differ from indigenous arsenals. Failed programs may reveal technical barriers, external pressure, domestic reversal, or all three. Analysts should specify what each case can test and avoid treating every non-nuclear outcome as evidence for the same theory.

### For diplomacy: protect both effectiveness and legitimacy

The NPT bargain depends on nonproliferation, peaceful use, and disarmament being treated as connected commitments rather than competing slogans [1][2]. Parties may prioritize them differently, but durable cooperation requires confidence that restraint is reciprocal and that legal categories do not excuse unlimited arms competition. Tannenwald's analysis implies that institutional inequality becomes destabilizing when reform through existing channels appears blocked [13]. The repeated failure of review conferences to reach consensus strengthens the case for practical progress between conferences rather than waiting for a comprehensive final document [15].

Practical progress can include stronger safeguards coverage, transparent export-control implementation, risk-reduction measures, crisis communication, fissile-material security, and verified limits where political conditions permit [3][4][5]. These measures do not replace Article VI disarmament obligations, and risk reduction should not be presented as equivalent to elimination. They can nevertheless reduce uncertainty and demonstrate that institutions still produce reciprocal restraint. The author's assessment is that modest verified steps are more stabilizing than ambitious declarations without implementation, provided they do not redefine the final obligation downward.

Peaceful nuclear cooperation also requires careful design. Article IV protects peaceful use in conformity with nonproliferation obligations [1]. The author's assessment is that supplier conditions, fuel assurances, safeguards-by-design, and transparent fuel-cycle choices may preserve peaceful access while reducing verification ambiguity. A blanket presumption that civilian programs are weapon programs would undermine cooperation and incentives for openness; an assumption that declared peaceful purpose eliminates risk would ignore the dual-use character of key technologies. Governance must preserve both rights and verifiability.

### For strategic and economic decision-makers: treat proliferation as a dynamic risk

Governments, firms, and investors should not model nuclear proliferation as a sudden binary event. Risk accumulates through changes in threat perception, alliance credibility, technical lead time, domestic authorization, procurement, verification access, and the regime's compliance record [6][7][8]. A structured indicator set can identify movement across these dimensions without claiming a precise probability unsupported by evidence. Scenario analysis should distinguish a latent capability, an active but reversible program, a tested device, and a survivable operational arsenal because each creates different coercive and escalation risks.

Resolution 1540 and the NSG guidelines cover controls involving exports, trans-shipment, financing, transport, end use, and dual-use technology [4][5]. Firms operating in dual-use sectors therefore need effective screening and end-use controls under applicable law. These are compliance and resilience questions, not a license to infer illicit intent from nationality or sector alone. Evidence-based due diligence should follow documented risk indicators and applicable law.

The deepest implication is that nonproliferation is a coordination achievement that must be reproduced. Weapons spread when security demand, technical opportunity, domestic support, and weak external constraints align; restraint persists when enough of those conditions are reversed [6][7]. Treaties and inspections cannot abolish geopolitical rivalry, but they can change information, expectations, access, and costs. Alliances cannot verify material, and sanctions cannot by themselves create legitimate institutions. The author's synthesis is that the worst failure is to let each layer assume another will carry the burden: the regime works only when security policy, verification, supply controls, enforcement, and reciprocal legitimacy reinforce one another.
