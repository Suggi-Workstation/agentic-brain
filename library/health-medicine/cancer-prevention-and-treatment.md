# Cancer Prevention and Treatment -- Progress Comes From Matching Prevention, Detection, and Multimodal Care to Each Cancer's Biology, Stage, and the Patient's Goals

Cancer is not one disease but a large group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow beyond their usual boundaries, invade nearby tissue, and can spread to distant organs; widespread metastasis is the main cause of cancer death [1][4]. Outcomes have improved where prevention removes causes such as tobacco and cancer-causing infections, where disease is found while still local, and where surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy are combined according to cancer type, stage, biomarkers, and the patient's goals [1][3][8]. The same evidence marks the limits: absolute benefits are often modest, more intensive treatment can do more harm than good in slowly progressing disease, and access to effective care remains highly unequal across and within countries [3][11][12][14].

## Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) describes cancer as a generic term for a large group of diseases that can affect any part of the body. Its defining feature is the rapid creation of abnormal cells that grow beyond their usual boundaries, invade adjoining parts of the body, and spread to other organs; widespread metastases are the primary cause of death from cancer [1]. WHO describes the origin as a multi-stage process that generally progresses from a pre-cancerous lesion to a malignant tumour. The changes result from interaction between a person's genetic factors and three categories of external agents: physical carcinogens such as ultraviolet and ionizing radiation, chemical carcinogens such as components of tobacco smoke, asbestos, alcohol, aflatoxin, and arsenic, and biological carcinogens such as certain viruses, bacteria, and parasites [1]. Incidence rises steeply with age, which WHO attributes most likely to cumulative exposure to risk factors, combined with the tendency of cellular repair mechanisms to become less effective in older people [1].

The burden is large and unevenly distributed by site. WHO reports nearly 10 million cancer deaths in 2024, nearly one in six deaths worldwide. The most common new cancers in 2024 were lung (2.6 million cases), breast (2.4 million), colorectal (2.0 million), and prostate (1.5 million); the leading causes of cancer death were lung (1.86 million), colorectal (918,000), liver (732,000), breast (694,000), and stomach (642,000) cancers [1]. Approximately 400,000 children and adolescents aged 0 to 19 develop cancer each year [1]. In the United States, the American Cancer Society projects 2,114,850 new cases and 626,140 cancer deaths in 2026, and estimates the lifetime probability of an invasive cancer diagnosis at about one in three for both men (39.2 percent) and women (38.7 percent) [3].

The biological understanding of cancer changed with genome sequencing. Vogelstein and colleagues wrote in 2013 that, ten years earlier, identifying every gene altered in a cancer at base-pair resolution would have seemed like science fiction; by then exome and genome sequencing was routine and its cost had fallen about 100-fold [4]. Those studies identified about 140 genes that can drive tumor growth when mutated, found that a typical tumor carries two to eight driver mutations, and grouped driver genes into 12 signaling pathways that regulate cell fate, cell survival, and genome maintenance [4]. The same analysis treated cancer as a somatic evolutionary process: mutations confer small selective growth advantages, clones expand over years, and a fully metastatic cancer takes decades to develop [4].

Treatment evidence developed through randomized trials and their pooled analysis. The Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) was established in 1985 to coordinate individual-patient meta-analyses of all randomized trials of adjuvant treatment for early breast cancer; its 2012 chemotherapy report covered about 100,000 women in 123 trials [9]. Molecular findings produced drugs aimed at specific altered proteins, such as inhibitors of mutant EGFR, ALK, and BRAF kinases [4]. Immune checkpoint inhibition followed: KEYNOTE-024 was, according to its investigators, the first phase III study to show superiority of immunotherapy over platinum-based chemotherapy as first-line treatment for a defined group with metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer [10]. Targeted therapy also transformed some blood cancers; five-year relative survival for chronic myeloid leukemia in the United States rose from 22 percent in the mid-1970s to 70 percent for diagnoses in 2015 to 2021 after tyrosine kinase inhibitors were developed [3].

Population statistics record both progress and its causes. United States cancer mortality rose during most of the 20th century, largely because of lung cancer from the tobacco epidemic, and then fell 34 percent from 1991 through 2023, an estimated 4.8 million deaths averted; the American Cancer Society attributes the decline to reduced smoking, earlier detection, and improved treatment [3]. Five-year relative survival for all cancers combined rose from 49 percent for diagnoses in the mid-1970s to 70 percent for diagnoses in 2015 to 2021 [3]. Internationally, the CONCORD programme established global surveillance of cancer survival in 2015 as a metric of health-system effectiveness. Its third cycle analysed individual records for 37.5 million patients from 322 population-based registries in 71 countries and territories, covering 18 cancers that represent about 75 percent of global diagnoses [14]. The previous cycle had attributed wide international differences in survival to differences in access to early diagnosis and optimal treatment [14]. In 2017 the World Health Assembly adopted resolution WHA70.12 on cancer prevention and control, urging governments to reduce premature cancer mortality [1][14].

## Core Concepts

### Cancer is a family of evolving clonal diseases

Tumors evolve from benign to malignant lesions by accumulating mutations over time. In the colorectal model described by Vogelstein and colleagues, a first gatekeeping mutation, most often in APC, lets an epithelial cell outgrow its neighbors; a second mutation such as KRAS triggers another round of clonal expansion; later mutations in genes such as PIK3CA, SMAD4, and TP53 produce a malignant tumor that can invade and metastasize [4]. Each driver mutation gives only a small advantage, estimated at about a 0.4 percent increase in the difference between cell birth and cell death, but compounded over years this produces a mass of billions of cells [4]. Most other mutations are passengers with no selective effect [4].

The number and type of mutations differ by tissue and exposure. Melanomas and lung tumors contain about 200 nonsynonymous mutations per tumor, reflecting ultraviolet light and cigarette smoke, and lung cancers in smokers carry about ten times as many somatic mutations as those in nonsmokers, whereas pediatric tumors and leukemias average about 9.6 [4]. Different genes can disrupt the same pathway: one lung cancer may carry an activating EGFR mutation, another KRAS, another an inactivating NF1 mutation, another BRAF, and such alterations within one pathway tend to be mutually exclusive [4]. Context also matters: BRAF inhibitors induce responses in most melanomas with BRAF V600E mutations but had no therapeutic effect in colorectal cancers carrying the identical mutation [4]. The organ of origin, the histology, and the molecular profile therefore each carry information, and none is sufficient alone; the precision-medicine topic in this library develops the validation requirements for molecular tests.

### Heterogeneity explains resistance and recurrence

Vogelstein and colleagues distinguish four kinds of genetic heterogeneity: among cells within one tumor, among different metastases in one patient, among cells within one metastasis, and among tumors of different patients [4]. A single metastasis can carry 20 clonal alterations not shared by other metastases in the same patient, although driver mutations usually sit in the trunk of the evolutionary tree, which is consistent with responses to targeted drugs that appear across all lesions [4]. Heterogeneity inside each metastasis is the seed of drug resistance. The authors calculate that a metastasis large enough to see on imaging already contains thousands of cells resistant to virtually any single drug, so patients with complete responses to targeted therapy invariably relapse; they argue that combinations acting on independent targets are the principled response [4]. Interpatient heterogeneity extends to individual codons: a KRAS G12D mutation does not carry the same clinical implications as a G13D mutation [4]. The author's synthesis is that recurrence after an initial response is frequently a predictable consequence of selection within a heterogeneous tumor rather than evidence that the original treatment had no effect.

### Risk accumulates from exposures, infections, inheritance, and age

WHO lists tobacco use, alcohol, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and air pollution as cancer risk factors, and attributes about 10 percent of cancers diagnosed in 2022 to infections, including Helicobacter pylori, human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B and C viruses, and Epstein-Barr virus; HIV infection raises cervical cancer risk six-fold [1]. A 2026 WHO and International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) analysis attributed 37 percent of new cancers in 2022, around 7.1 million cases, to 30 preventable causes; tobacco accounted for 15 percent of all new cases, infections for 10 percent, and alcohol for 3 percent, and lung, stomach, and cervical cancers made up nearly half of preventable cases [2]. The preventable share was 45 percent in men and 30 percent in women and ranged across regions from 24 to 38 percent in women and from 28 to 57 percent in men, reflecting differences in exposure, development, prevention policy, and health-system capacity [2]. In the United States, about 87 percent of lung cancers in men and 84 percent in women are attributable to cigarette smoking, and more than 90 percent of melanomas to ultraviolet radiation [3]. Inherited variants in some genes predispose to cancer, often indirectly by increasing genetic instability [4]. The author's synthesis is that an attributable fraction describes a population, not an individual: it estimates how many cases would not occur without the exposure, but it cannot say whether a given person's cancer was caused by it.

### Prevention acts on causes and precursor stages

WHO lists the main risk-reduction measures: not using tobacco, maintaining a healthy body weight, eating a healthy diet, being physically active, avoiding or reducing alcohol, vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B for eligible groups, avoiding ultraviolet exposure, safe medical use of radiation, minimizing occupational radiation, and reducing outdoor and indoor air pollution including radon [1]. The WHO and IARC analysis calls for context-specific strategies because the leading causes differ by country and sex [2]. Prevention works on long timescales. Persistent HPV infection usually precedes cervical cancer by 5 to 20 years, and the vaccine has no therapeutic effect on infection that already exists, so the largest benefit requires vaccination before exposure [13].

### Early diagnosis and screening are different interventions

WHO divides early detection into early diagnosis and screening [1]. Early diagnosis serves people who already have symptoms and has three components: awareness of symptoms and the importance of seeking advice, access to clinical evaluation and diagnostic services, and timely referral to treatment; WHO considers it relevant in all settings and for most cancers [1]. Screening seeks findings suggestive of cancer or pre-cancer in people without symptoms; it is effective for some but not all cancer types and is more complex and resource-intensive, and early diagnosis remains necessary for people outside screening eligibility [1]. Earlier diagnosis does not automatically mean longer life. The American Cancer Society notes that earlier detection raised survival statistics for breast, prostate, and kidney cancer partly through lead time and overdiagnosis, and that mortality rates are a better indicator of progress than incidence or survival because they are less affected by such detection biases [3]. The screening topic in this library treats these trade-offs in detail.

### Diagnosis, staging, and biomarkers define the treatment question

A correct diagnosis is essential because every cancer type requires a specific treatment regimen [1]. Stage describes the extent of disease and helps estimate seriousness and survival, plan treatment, and identify clinical trials [5]. The TNM system, the most widely used, records the size and extent of the primary tumor (T), involvement of nearby lymph nodes (N), and distant metastasis (M); combinations are often grouped into stages 0 to IV, and registries use the summary categories in situ, localized, regional, and distant [5]. Stage at diagnosis is kept as the reference even if the cancer later changes [5]. Stage carries large prognostic differences: in the United States, five-year relative survival for all cancers combined, for diagnoses in 2015 to 2021, was 69 percent for regional-stage and 35 percent for distant-stage disease, against 70 percent for all stages together [3]. Surgical specimens refine staging: removed lymph nodes and surrounding tissue are examined microscopically to see whether the cancer has spread, which guides further treatment [6]. Biomarkers add a further axis. KEYNOTE-024 enrolled only patients whose tumors expressed PD-L1 on at least 50 percent of tumor cells and lacked sensitizing EGFR or ALK alterations [10]. Vogelstein and colleagues stress that before a targeted drug is used, the cancer must be shown to carry the targeted alteration, because patients without it receive the toxicity while their tumors progress [4].

### Local and systemic treatments answer different questions

WHO lists surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy, including chemotherapy, hormonal treatment, and targeted biological therapy, as the usual treatment components [1]. Surgery is a local treatment that works best for solid tumors contained in one area; the National Cancer Institute (NCI) states that it is not used for leukemia or for cancers that have spread, and that it can remove the whole tumor, debulk part of it to help other treatments, or relieve symptoms, with risks that include pain, infection, bleeding, damage to nearby tissue, and anesthesia reactions [6]. Radiotherapy damages DNA so that cancer cells stop dividing or die over days to weeks; it is delivered by external beam, by implanted sources (brachytherapy), or systemically by radioactive drugs, and it can cure, prevent recurrence, slow growth, or palliate symptoms [7]. Radiation is most often combined with other treatments and may be given before, during, or after surgery; tissues have lifetime dose limits, and radiation also affects nearby healthy cells [7].

Adjuvant treatment, given after the primary tumor is removed, addresses disease that cannot yet be seen. After breast-conserving surgery, microscopic tumor foci may remain in the breast and can cause local recurrence or distant metastasis [8]. More generally, residual metastatic lesions that are undetectable at surgery can enlarge after the primary tumor is removed [4].

### Proportional and absolute benefit must be separated

The EBCTCG meta-analyses show a pattern that recurs across oncology. Radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery roughly halved the recurrence rate and reduced the breast cancer death rate by about a sixth, and these proportional effects varied little between groups of women; the absolute benefit, by contrast, varied substantially with the patient's baseline risk [8]. Effective adjuvant chemotherapy regimens reduced breast cancer mortality by about a third, largely independent of age and tumor characteristics, but the investigators concluded that low absolute risk implies low absolute benefit [9]. The author's synthesis is that the expected gain from a treatment is approximately the patient's baseline risk multiplied by the proportional effect, which must then be weighed against that treatment's harms.

### Treatment goals and intensity are clinical decisions

WHO describes determining the goals of treatment as an important first step: the primary goal is generally cure or considerable prolongation of life, and quality of life is also an important goal [1]. Goals differ by setting. Some common cancers, such as breast, cervical, oral, and colorectal cancers, have high cure probabilities when detected early and treated according to best practice, and testicular seminoma and several childhood leukemias and lymphomas have high cure rates even after spread [1]. Elsewhere the best option may be less treatment: in ProtecT, 15-year prostate cancer mortality after active monitoring of localized prostate cancer did not differ significantly from that after surgery or radiotherapy [11]. For advanced cancer that no longer responds to therapy, a comfort-focused end of life is often preferred, yet many patients continue to receive life-extending treatment against their wishes or without survival benefit [3]. The palliative-care topic in this library covers serious-illness communication and symptom care.

### Survivorship includes late effects of cure

Treatment harms can persist long after cancer is controlled. Long-term survivors of childhood cancer face elevated risks of treatment-related disease: in one cohort cited by the American Cancer Society, nearly one in six survivors had a major cardiovascular event by age 50, compared with fewer than 1 percent of community controls, and survivors aged 50 or older had three-fold excess mortality, predominantly from subsequent cancers among those treated with radiation [3]. Adjuvant breast cancer chemotherapy caused some deaths from acute myeloid leukemia and anthracycline cardiotoxicity [9]. A 12-year follow-up study cited by the American Cancer Society associated prostatectomy with a seven-fold higher risk of urinary or sexual complications and radiotherapy with a three-fold higher risk of bladder cancer compared with same-aged men [3]. The American Cancer Society concludes that such findings underscore the need for survivorship care plans for children and adolescents and for attention to medical history in older adults, to guide surveillance, prevention, and early detection [3].

## Evidence

### HPV vaccination and invasive cervical cancer

Lei and colleagues used linked Swedish national registers to follow 1,672,983 girls and women aged 10 to 30 from 2006 through 2017, treating quadrivalent HPV vaccination as a time-varying exposure and adjusting for age, calendar year, county, and parental education, income, country of birth, and maternal disease history [13]. Cervical cancer was diagnosed in 19 vaccinated and 538 unvaccinated women; cumulative incidence by age 30 was 47 versus 94 per 100,000 [13]. The fully adjusted incidence rate ratio was 0.37 overall, 0.12 for women vaccinated before age 17, and 0.47 for those vaccinated at 17 to 30 [13]. Because the study was observational, residual confounding by lifestyle factors such as smoking and sexual behavior could not be excluded, although healthy-volunteer bias would be expected to be strongest among older, privately vaccinated women, in whom the reduction was smaller [13]. Randomized trials cannot readily measure invasive cancer as an outcome because of the long interval from infection to cancer, so this registry design supplied evidence on the endpoint that vaccination programs aim to prevent [13].

### Radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery

The EBCTCG analysed individual data for 10,801 women in 17 randomized trials of radiotherapy versus no radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery [8]. Radiotherapy reduced the 10-year risk of any first recurrence from 35.0 to 19.3 percent and the 15-year risk of breast cancer death from 25.2 to 21.4 percent; about one breast cancer death was avoided by year 15 for every four recurrences avoided by year 10 [8]. In node-negative women, predicted large, intermediate, and lower recurrence benefits corresponded to absolute reductions in 15-year breast cancer mortality of 7.8, 1.1, and 0.1 percentage points [8]. Among node-negative women, the absolute 10-year recurrence reduction was 24.6 percentage points in those younger than 40 and 8.9 points in those 70 or older [8]. The investigators noted that screening, surgery, pathology, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy have all changed since the trials began, so contemporary absolute benefits may differ, and suggested estimating them by assuming that radiotherapy roughly halves a modern recurrence risk [8].

### Adjuvant chemotherapy for early breast cancer

In meta-analyses of about 100,000 women in 123 trials, the EBCTCG compared polychemotherapy with no chemotherapy and different regimens with one another [9]. Adding four cycles of a taxane to a fixed anthracycline-based regimen reduced breast cancer mortality (rate ratio 0.86), whereas trials that counterbalanced the taxane with extra cycles of other drugs showed no significant difference (0.94) [9]. Regimens with higher cumulative anthracycline dosage outperformed standard CMF chemotherapy (0.78), and proportional reductions were little affected by age, nodal status, tumor size or grade, estrogen-receptor status, or tamoxifen use [9]. Ten-year overall mortality paralleled breast cancer mortality despite toxicity, but anthracycline regimens raised cardiac mortality, and the investigators warned that in low-risk estrogen-receptor-positive disease treated with endocrine therapy, the absolute gain from chemotherapy cannot be large while patients not helped are harmed by its toxicity [9]. The analysis lacked tumor gene-expression data and pointed to later trials designed to identify patients who gain little [9].

### Biomarker-selected immunotherapy in metastatic lung cancer

KEYNOTE-024 randomized 305 patients with previously untreated metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer, PD-L1 expression on at least 50 percent of tumor cells, and no sensitizing EGFR or ALK alteration to pembrolizumab or platinum-based chemotherapy in an open-label design [10]. At about five years, median overall survival was 26.3 versus 13.4 months (hazard ratio 0.62) and estimated five-year survival was 31.9 versus 16.3 percent, although 66 percent of the chemotherapy group later received anti-PD-1 or PD-L1 therapy [10]. Grade 3 to 5 treatment-related adverse events occurred in 31.2 percent of pembrolizumab patients and 53.3 percent of chemotherapy patients, while immune-mediated adverse events and infusion reactions occurred in 34.4 versus 5.3 percent [10]. Historically, five-year relative survival for distant-stage non-small-cell lung cancer in the United States was 6.9 percent in 2010 to 2016 [10]. The trial was supported by the drug's manufacturer and required an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1, and it excluded patients with untreated brain metastases or active autoimmune disease needing systemic treatment [10]. The author's assessment is that the result therefore applies to fit, biomarker-selected patients like those enrolled.

### Treatment intensity in localized prostate cancer

ProtecT tested 82,429 men aged 50 to 69 in the United Kingdom for prostate-specific antigen and randomized 1,643 with localized cancer to active monitoring, prostatectomy, or radiotherapy [11]. After a median of 15 years, 45 men (2.7 percent) had died of prostate cancer, 3.1 percent with monitoring, 2.2 percent with surgery, and 2.9 percent with radiotherapy, with no significant difference, and deaths from any cause were similar across groups [11]. Radical treatment roughly halved metastasis, long-term androgen deprivation, and clinical progression; metastases occurred in 9.4 percent of monitored men versus 4.7 and 5.0 percent, and 24.4 percent of the monitoring group remained alive without any treatment [11]. The investigators concluded that, depending on side effects, more aggressive early treatment can cause more harm than good, and that risk tools of the time misclassified many men: up to 34 percent had intermediate- or high-risk disease by contemporary methods, and half of the men who developed metastases had been classed as low grade at baseline [11].

### Evidence available when cancer drugs reach the market

Davis and colleagues examined all 68 indications for 48 cancer drugs approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2009 to 2013 [12]. At approval, 24 indications (35 percent) showed significant prolongation of survival, with gains of 1.0 to 5.8 months (median 2.7), and 7 (10 percent) showed improved quality of life; only 18 of the 68 were supported by a pivotal study powered to evaluate overall survival, while progression-free survival and response rate were common surrogate primary endpoints [12]. After a median 5.4 years on the market, 35 indications (51 percent) had shown a significant improvement in survival or quality of life and 33 (49 percent) remained uncertain; of 23 survival gains that could be scored with the European Society for Medical Oncology benefit scale, 11 were judged clinically meaningful [12]. The authors note that their generous handling of ambiguous reports may overstate benefit [12].

### Population outcomes and inequality

In the United States, gains in five-year relative survival between the mid-1990s and 2015 to 2021 were largest for advanced disease: from 17 to 35 percent for distant-stage cancers overall, from 2 to 10 percent for distant-stage lung cancer, and from 16 to 35 percent for metastatic melanoma [3]. Breast cancer mortality fell 44 percent from its 1989 peak; one study attributed three-fourths of that decline to treatment and the remainder to screening [3]. Gains are not shared equally. Black patients have lower survival than White patients for nearly every cancer even after accounting for stage and socioeconomic status, and the American Cancer Society identifies unequal access to high-quality care as the largest contributor [3]. Internationally, CONCORD-3 found five-year breast cancer survival close to 90 percent in the United States and Australia but as low as 40 percent in South Africa, and childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia survival from 66 percent in Thailand to 95 percent in Finland [14]. Its authors interpret population-based survival as a measure of the whole health system, not of individual clinicians' competence [14].

## Implications

### For people at risk and patients

The largest controllable share of cancer risk lies in a few exposures. Tobacco alone accounts for an estimated 15 percent of new cancers worldwide and for most lung cancers in the United States, and infections account for about 10 percent globally [2][3]. Avoiding tobacco, limiting alcohol, and completing recommended HPV and hepatitis B vaccination therefore address causes rather than consequences [1][2]. HPV vaccination is most effective before exposure, consistent with school-based programs such as Sweden's, which vaccinates girls aged 10 to 12 [13]. An attributable fraction does not promise that any individual will avoid cancer, and cancer in a person who avoided all known risks is not evidence that prevention fails; the author's assessment is that prevention changes probabilities in populations, not certainties in individuals.

After a diagnosis, a person can ask questions that follow the structure of the evidence. What exact type and stage is this, and was it confirmed by pathology [1][5]? Is the goal cure, longer life, or symptom control [1]? What biomarkers were tested, and do they change the options [4][10]? For each option, what is my absolute chance of benefit over a defined period, and what are the short-term and lasting harms [8][9][11]? ProtecT shows that for PSA-detected localized prostate cancer, monitoring is a legitimate option rather than neglect, and the EBCTCG analyses show that the same proportional benefit can mean a large or a negligible absolute gain depending on baseline risk [8][11]. Survivors should expect follow-up that addresses late effects as well as recurrence, especially after childhood treatment [3].

### For clinicians and multidisciplinary teams

Treatment planning starts from a complete diagnosis: histology, stage, biomarkers, the patient's health and preferences, and the goal of care [1][5]. Because surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy act on different parts of the problem, sequencing them is a joint decision; radiation may be given before, during, or after surgery, and adjuvant systemic therapy targets microscopic disease that surgery cannot remove [4][7][8]. Benefit is best presented in absolute terms tied to the individual's risk; the EBCTCG investigators suggest estimating a contemporary patient's absolute recurrence risk and assuming that radiotherapy will roughly halve it [8][9]. Targeted therapy requires confirmation that the tumor carries the target, because untargeted use exposes patients to toxicity without benefit [4]. Biomarker-selected trials such as KEYNOTE-024 apply to their eligible populations, and toxicity profiles differ by drug class: immunotherapy carried fewer severe treatment-related events than chemotherapy but more immune-mediated events [10].

Teams also need rules for stepping down. ProtecT found that radical treatment of PSA-detected localized prostate cancer did not reduce 15-year cancer mortality, while treatment complications are common [3][11]. The finding that many patients near death receive life-extending treatment they do not want indicates that goal-setting conversations must recur as disease changes [3]. Recurrence after an initial response should be anticipated in metastatic disease treated with a single targeted agent [4]. The author's assessment is that a team's quality shows as much in the treatments it declines to give as in those it gives.

### For health systems and policymakers

Cancer control is a system-level intervention spanning prevention, early diagnosis, treatment, palliative care, and survivorship [1]. WHO reports that most countries do not fully include essential cancer services in publicly financed health-benefit packages [1]. CONCORD-3 documents survival differences of more than two-fold for breast cancer and wide differences for curable childhood leukemia, and describes settings without trained medical oncologists where chemotherapy is limited and paid out of pocket [14]. Denmark's national cancer plans, which introduced accelerated diagnostic pathways and public monitoring of waiting times, were followed by survival gains that nearly closed the gap with other Nordic countries [14]. Population-based cancer registries cost about 51 euros per registered patient in Europe in 2013 and are the instrument for measuring whether such policies work [14].

Prevention policy should follow local causes: the WHO and IARC analysis calls for tobacco control, alcohol regulation, HPV and hepatitis B vaccination, cleaner air, safer workplaces, and healthier food and physical-activity environments, tailored to each country's exposure profile [2]. Unequal uptake can widen inequality; in 2023, up-to-date HPV vaccination among United States adolescents ranged from 38 percent in Mississippi to 84 percent in Rhode Island, and states with the highest cervical cancer incidence had the lowest coverage [3]. Lung cancer screening reached only 18 percent of eligible United States adults in 2022 [3]. The author's synthesis is that new interventions improve average outcomes but can increase disparities unless delivery is planned for the groups at highest risk.

### For researchers, regulators, and payers

Davis and colleagues found that about half of European cancer-drug indications approved in 2009 to 2013 still lacked evidence of improved survival or quality of life after a median 5.4 years, and that many survival gains were small [12]. The authors note that surrogate endpoints show that an agent has biological activity but do not reliably predict longer survival in all settings, and they call on regulators to reconsider when approval on surrogate endpoints is appropriate and to require robust evidence of clinical benefit at authorization, because outcomes that matter most to patients may never be measured once a drug is in wide use [12]. ProtecT also questions whether metastasis can serve as a surrogate for lethality in localized prostate cancer, because the interval from metastasis to death extended to 10 to 20 years in some men, particularly in the monitoring group [11]. Long follow-up matters: the EBCTCG mortality effects were measured at 10 and 15 years, and 7 of the 16 prostate cancer deaths in the ProtecT radiotherapy group occurred after 15 years [8][9][11]. The heterogeneity and resistance mechanisms described by Vogelstein and colleagues support trials of combinations aimed at independent targets and more research on prevention and early detection [4].

### For readers of cancer statistics and cure claims

Survival statistics can rise without any change in the time of death when diagnosis moves earlier or detects indolent tumors, so mortality trends are the more reliable measure of progress [3]. Relative survival also shifts when the mix of tumor subtypes changes; part of the rise in United States pancreatic cancer survival reflects more incidentally detected neuroendocrine tumors [3]. Claims of a general cure for cancer conflict with the evidence that cancer comprises many diseases with different drivers, heterogeneous cell populations, and predictable resistance [1][4]. A credible claim names the cancer type, stage, and biomarker group; the comparator; an endpoint such as overall survival or quality of life; and the absolute size of the effect [8][10][12]. The author's assessment is that a treatment claim lacking these elements is not yet evidence.
