# Preventive Screening Helps Only When Earlier Detection Improves Outcomes More Than It Causes Harm

Preventive screening applies tests to people without relevant symptoms in order to identify a higher-risk group early enough for an effective intervention to improve health outcomes. A screening result is not a diagnosis, and finding more abnormalities or improving survival measured from diagnosis does not by itself show that screening saves lives ([1] [3] [6]). Screening is beneficial only when the complete pathway from invitation through confirmation and treatment produces more health benefit than false positives, false negatives, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, procedural injury, anxiety, and opportunity cost ([1] [2] [5]).

## Background

### Screening begins before illness is clinically apparent

The defining feature of screening is not the technology used but the population in which it is used. The World Health Organization describes screening as identifying people at higher risk within an apparently healthy population so that an earlier intervention can reduce incidence or mortality ([1]). The same blood test, image, questionnaire, or physical examination can therefore be screening when offered to asymptomatic people, diagnostic testing when used to investigate symptoms, or surveillance when used to monitor a known condition. These settings have different disease prevalence, purposes, predictive values, and acceptable error rates. Evidence that a test performs well in symptomatic referral patients cannot be transferred automatically to a low-risk screening population ([1] [2] [17]).

Screening is presumptive. It sorts people into groups that do or do not require further assessment; it does not ordinarily establish the final diagnosis. A positive result may lead to repeat testing, imaging, endoscopy, biopsy, specialist consultation, or prolonged surveillance. A negative result reduces estimated risk but can be wrong, and false reassurance can delay attention to later symptoms. The program must therefore be evaluated as a linked pathway rather than as a stand-alone test ([1] [2] [17]).

The classical policy framework came from Wilson and Jungner's 1968 principles for screening. Their criteria asked whether the condition was important, its natural history was understood, a recognizable early stage existed, a suitable and acceptable test was available, treatment was effective, facilities for diagnosis and treatment existed, and case finding was economically balanced and continuous ([1] [2]). A 2018 systematic review and modified Delphi process examined 41 later sets containing 367 unique principles and consolidated them into 12 principles covering the disease, the test and intervention, and the program or system. Almost half of the later principles concerned program or system issues, showing that contemporary screening policy extends well beyond test accuracy ([2]).

### Earlier knowledge has conditional value

Earlier detection can create benefit when four conditions hold together. The target disease must have a detectable preclinical phase. The screen must identify clinically important disease with tolerable error. Treatment started in that phase must improve outcomes compared with treatment begun after symptoms. The health system must complete confirmation and treatment quickly and equitably. Failure of any link can erase the value of the others ([1] [2] [5]).

The intuitive claim that earlier detection is always better fails because biological progression is heterogeneous. Some disease progresses too rapidly for periodic screening to intercept it. Some progresses slowly enough to be found and successfully treated. Some never progresses to symptoms or death during the person's remaining lifetime. Screening preferentially finds lesions that remain detectable for longer, so the screen-detected group contains a disproportionate share of slower and less threatening disease. This is length-biased sampling; its limiting case is overdiagnosis ([3] [6]).

Overdiagnosis is not a false-positive test and not a pathology error. It is a true diagnosis under current criteria of a condition that would never have become clinically apparent during the person's lifetime without screening. Because the counterfactual cannot usually be observed in one individual, an overdiagnosed case generally cannot be identified with certainty at diagnosis. The patient may receive a disease label, surveillance, invasive procedures, or treatment without any possibility that earlier detection will improve that person's health. Overtreatment is the treatment consequence that often follows, but active surveillance can sometimes separate diagnosis from immediate treatment ([3] [4] [10]).

### Screening changed the meaning of evidence

Before organized screening, a disease was usually counted after symptoms led to clinical detection. Screening moves some diagnoses earlier, adds diagnoses that otherwise would have appeared later, and adds overdiagnosed cases that otherwise would never have appeared. These changes make several familiar statistics misleading. Five-year survival can rise because the diagnostic clock starts earlier even when the time of death does not change; this is lead-time bias. Survival can also rise because screening enriches the diagnosed group with slower disease; this is length bias. Incidence and the number of survivors can increase because screening creates additional diagnoses. None of these changes proves a reduction in mortality or severe morbidity ([3] [6]).

Randomized trials that assign eligible people to an invitation or control group and compare health outcomes are the strongest direct design for estimating a screening program's effect. Disease-specific mortality is often the primary outcome because it is more sensitive than all-cause mortality to a disease-specific intervention, while all-cause mortality can reveal large unexpected harms but usually requires much larger samples. Trials must follow participants long enough for benefits and delayed harms to emerge, account for screening outside the assigned program, measure downstream procedures and treatment, and analyze people according to assignment to preserve randomization ([3] [5]).

The relevant comparison is not screening versus doing nothing in an abstract sense. It is one defined program versus the actual alternative under specified conditions: ages, risk factors, test, threshold, interval, number of rounds, follow-up protocol, available treatment, and background opportunistic testing. Different choices can turn the same general technology into a beneficial, marginal, or harmful program. The author's synthesis is that screening is best understood as a time-bounded population intervention whose value must be re-established when its target group, threshold, treatment, or delivery system changes ([1] [2] [5]).

## Core Concepts

### A screening program is a causal chain

A complete screening pathway includes identifying the eligible population, issuing an invitation, supporting informed choice, performing the test, communicating the result, confirming abnormal findings, treating or monitoring confirmed disease, following outcomes, and evaluating program performance. Staffing, information systems, laboratory capacity, referral capacity, quality assurance, and treatment access are causal components, not administrative details. A program that generates positive results faster than it can confirm them may increase anxiety and delay without producing timely treatment. A program that detects disease but cannot provide effective treatment exposes people to knowledge and procedures without completing the route to benefit ([1] [2] [17]).

This chain distinguishes test efficacy from program effectiveness. A test can be analytically accurate under study conditions while a program performs poorly because invitations do not reach high-risk groups, samples are mishandled, thresholds vary, positive results are lost to follow-up, treatment is delayed, or quality differs across sites. Conversely, an organized program can improve net benefit through narrower eligibility, standardized interpretation, staged confirmation, longer intervals, active surveillance, and continuous audit even when the underlying test does not change ([1] [2] [17]).

The causal chain also fixes the denominator. The benefit question begins with everyone eligible or invited, not only people who attended, tested positive, received a diagnosis, or completed treatment. Restricting analysis to participants selects people who may differ in baseline risk, health behavior, and access. Restricting it to diagnosed cases guarantees distortions from lead time, length bias, and overdiagnosis. Population outcomes preserve the question that screening policy is meant to answer: what happens when this program is offered to this population? ([3] [5]).

### Prevalence governs what a positive result means

Every dichotomous screening result can be placed in a two-by-two table: true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with the target condition who test positive. Specificity is the proportion without the target condition who test negative. Positive predictive value is the proportion of positive results that are true positives, while negative predictive value is the proportion of negative results that are true negatives. Predictive values depend on prevalence in the tested population even when sensitivity and specificity are unchanged ([2] [3]).

Low prevalence is the normal condition of preventive screening because the target population is asymptomatic. Suppose a condition is present in 1 percent of 10,000 screened people, sensitivity is 90 percent, and specificity is 95 percent. The program would identify 90 true positives and miss 10 cases, while 5 percent of the 9,900 people without the condition would produce 495 false positives. Only 90 of 585 positive results, about 15 percent, would represent the target condition. This numerical example is the author's calculation from the stated assumptions and illustrates why a test described as "90 percent sensitive and 95 percent specific" can still produce many more false positives than true positives in a low-prevalence population.

Risk-based eligibility changes the prior probability. Limiting lung cancer screening to people with a substantial smoking history, for example, raises prevalence compared with screening all adults and therefore improves the expected ratio of true to false positive findings. It can also concentrate benefit among people with enough baseline risk to gain meaningfully from earlier detection. Risk restriction is not free: a model may omit people who later develop disease, reproduce inequities in exposure or access, or be poorly calibrated in groups underrepresented in its development data. Eligibility rules therefore require both discrimination and equity evaluation ([1] [2] [7] [8]).

### Thresholds exchange one error for another

Many screening tests produce a continuous measure rather than a natural positive or negative answer. Blood markers, nodule sizes, imaging scores, and risk models become positive only after a threshold is selected. Lowering the threshold generally increases sensitivity and detects more true disease, but it also reduces specificity and sends more people without consequential disease into confirmation. Raising the threshold generally improves specificity and reduces downstream work, but misses more potentially important disease. The threshold is therefore a policy choice about error costs, capacity, and acceptable uncertainty, not merely a laboratory fact ([2] [5]).

The appropriate threshold depends on what follows. A low-specificity first-stage test can be reasonable when it is inexpensive and safe and every positive result receives a highly specific, low-harm confirmation. The same first-stage performance can be unacceptable if a positive result leads directly to surgery or toxic treatment. Sequential testing can improve the pathway by requiring independent evidence before an invasive procedure. It can also introduce delay, attrition, and new false negatives. Evaluation must count the whole sequence rather than report the accuracy of its best component ([1] [5] [12]).

Screening interval creates a parallel trade-off. Shorter intervals may find rapidly progressing disease earlier but repeat false-positive exposure, radiation, procedures, and cost more often. Longer intervals reduce repeated harm and capacity burden but allow more interval disease to become symptomatic. Annual and biennial programs are not the same intervention, and evidence for one does not automatically justify the other. The 2024 USPSTF breast cancer recommendation notes that biennial mammography produced a lower ten-year cumulative false-positive biopsy rate than annual screening in observational Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium data ([15]).

### Lead time, length bias, and overdiagnosis answer different questions

Lead time is the interval by which screening advances the date of diagnosis. If a person would have developed symptoms at age 68 and died at age 70, but screening diagnoses the same disease at age 63 without changing death, measured survival rises from two years to seven years although life is not extended. Lead-time bias therefore invalidates comparisons of survival from diagnosis as evidence that screening reduces death ([3] [6]).

Length bias arises because a slowly progressing lesion remains in the screen-detectable preclinical phase longer than an aggressive lesion. Periodic screening samples conditions in proportion to how long they remain detectable, so indolent disease is more likely to be found and aggressive disease is more likely to arise between rounds. Better prognosis among screen-detected cases can therefore reflect the biology selected by the screen rather than a treatment effect ([3] [6]).

Overdiagnosis adds a counterfactual: the detected condition would not have caused symptoms or death before the person died of another cause. It can arise from nonprogressive disease, very slow progression, regression, or competing mortality. Its probability rises when testing becomes more sensitive to small abnormalities, when thresholds widen, when screening continues into ages with limited remaining life expectancy, or when the target disease has a large reservoir of indolent lesions ([3] [4] [10]).

False positives and overdiagnosis must not be combined. A false positive is resolved when diagnostic assessment shows that the target disease is absent. An overdiagnosed person has a confirmed diagnosis and may never learn that the disease was harmless. False positives mainly generate temporary uncertainty and diagnostic cascades, although their physical and psychological effects can be serious. Overdiagnosis can create a lifelong label and expose the person to all harms of disease management. Misdiagnosis is different again: the diagnostic conclusion itself is wrong ([3] [4]).

### The outcome must match the intended benefit

A valid endpoint sits downstream of the entire screening pathway and matters to patients. Appropriate endpoints can include disease-specific mortality, all-cause mortality, severe complications, incidence of invasive disease when a precursor is treated, quality of life, or disability. Which endpoint is primary depends on the natural history and purpose of the program. Cervical and colorectal screening can prevent some invasive cancers by finding and removing precursor lesions, so incidence is part of benefit. A program aimed only at detecting established cancer must show that stage shift translates into lower mortality or morbidity rather than simply more early-stage diagnoses ([3] [5] [13]).

Surrogate changes require a proven link to patient outcomes. More cancers detected, smaller average tumor size, higher five-year survival, and a greater share diagnosed at an early stage can all improve under screening even when mortality does not. A convincing stage-shift argument also requires a decline in late-stage disease, not merely growth in the early-stage denominator. Randomized mortality results remain the most direct safeguard against lead time and length bias when such trials are feasible ([3] [6]).

Absolute effects are necessary for decision-making. A relative mortality reduction can sound large while the absolute change is small because the baseline probability of disease-specific death is low. In the National Lung Screening Trial, the reported 20 percent relative reduction in lung cancer mortality corresponded to an approximate absolute reduction from 1.7 percent to 1.3 percent after about seven years. Both descriptions are correct, but only the pair communicates scale ([6] [7]). Benefits should be presented with a common denominator and time horizon, alongside false positives, invasive procedures, overdiagnoses, treatment complications, and false negatives ([5] [6] [16]).

### Net benefit depends on risk, time, and preferences

Net benefit is not test accuracy minus one generic harm score. It is the balance of heterogeneous outcomes that occur at different times and carry different importance for different people. Screening harms often occur immediately through anxiety, repeat testing, biopsy, bleeding, perforation, radiation, or treatment complications. Mortality benefits may take years to appear. A person with limited life expectancy can incur immediate harm while being unlikely to live long enough to receive delayed benefit. Competing mortality is therefore central to upper age limits and individualized stopping decisions ([3] [5] [10]).

Risk affects both sides. Higher disease risk raises the number of true cases available to benefit and usually improves positive predictive value. Comorbidity can simultaneously raise procedure risk and reduce the time available for benefit. Personal values matter most when average net benefit is small or closely balanced: one person may place high value on even a small reduction in disease-specific mortality, while another may place greater weight on avoiding biopsy, uncertainty, or treatment-related loss of function. The USPSTF consequently treats shared decision-making as essential when the preferred option depends on individual risk and preferences ([10] [16]).

Informed choice requires more than consent to a test. The person needs to understand baseline risk, the purpose and limits of the screen, the plausible absolute benefit, false-positive and false-negative results, confirmatory procedures, overdiagnosis, treatment choices, uncertainty, and the option not to screen. Decision aids should supplement rather than replace a clinical conversation. A 2024 Cochrane review of 209 studies involving 107,698 adults found that decision aids improved knowledge, accuracy of risk perceptions, participation, and informed values-congruent choices compared with usual care ([16]).

### Population programs require governance and learning

A screening program should specify who is invited, at what age, at what interval, with which test and threshold, through which confirmatory pathway, and under what treatment or surveillance policy. It should also specify who owns invitations, results, recalls, incident management, data quality, and follow-up. The consolidated screening principles identify infrastructure, coordination, ethics, benefit-harm assessment, and quality management as core decision criteria rather than optional implementation work ([2]).

Monitoring should cover every transition: invitation coverage, participation, invalid tests, positivity and recall rates, time to diagnostic resolution, positive predictive value, complications, treatment initiation, interval disease, stage distribution, disease-specific outcomes, and subgroup differences. High participation is not sufficient evidence of quality; a harmful program can be popular. Low participation can also obscure whether the program is ineffective or simply inaccessible. Program evaluation should therefore distinguish efficacy, reach, fidelity, timeliness, and equity ([1] [2] [17]).

Equity changes realized net benefit. People at higher disease risk can face greater barriers to invitation, transport, time off work, culturally appropriate information, confirmation, and treatment. Expanding invitations without ensuring diagnostic and treatment access can distribute false-positive burden broadly while concentrating benefit among people who can complete the pathway. The Australian population screening framework consequently places access and equity across testing, assessment, and treatment within the criteria for introducing and managing a program ([17]).

Screening policy must also include rules for revision and stopping. Changes in disease incidence, vaccination, treatment effectiveness, test technology, competing causes of death, and healthcare capacity can alter the balance. A once-beneficial interval may become too frequent; a risk threshold may become poorly calibrated; a new test may detect more disease without improving outcomes. The author's synthesis is that a screening program without prospective monitoring and a stopping rule is an uncontrolled intervention applied to healthy people ([1] [2] [17]).

## Evidence

### Lung screening shows why population selection and protocol matter

The National Lung Screening Trial randomized 53,454 people at high risk for lung cancer to three annual rounds of low-dose computed tomography or chest radiography. Positive screens occurred in 24.2 percent of CT examinations and 6.9 percent of radiographs across the rounds, and 96.4 percent of positive CT results and 94.5 percent of positive radiograph results were false positives under the trial definition. Lung cancer mortality was 247 versus 309 deaths per 100,000 person-years, a 20.0 percent relative reduction, and all-cause mortality was 6.7 percent lower in the CT group ([7]). The trial therefore demonstrated real mortality benefit and a large diagnostic burden in a deliberately high-risk population.

The NELSON trial tested a different CT protocol. It randomized 13,195 men and 2,594 women aged 50 to 74 to volume CT at baseline, one, three, and 5.5 years or no screening. Among men, average adherence was 90.0 percent, 9.2 percent underwent at least one additional CT for an initially indeterminate result, and 2.1 percent were referred for a suspicious nodule. At ten years, lung cancer mortality was 2.50 versus 3.30 deaths per 1,000 person-years, with a cumulative mortality rate ratio of 0.76 in the screening group ([8]). Together, NLST and NELSON show that benefit depends on high-risk eligibility and that nodule classification and staged follow-up can materially change the false-positive pathway ([7] [8]).

These trials do not justify CT screening of everyone. Their eligibility criteria raised baseline risk, their centers had specified imaging and follow-up protocols, and their mortality comparisons apply to those program designs. Extending CT to low-risk populations would reduce positive predictive value and expose more people per lung cancer death prevented. The evidence supports a bounded intervention, not a general rule that more imaging is better ([7] [8]).

### Prostate screening shows small benefit beside substantial excess diagnosis

The European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer assigned 182,000 men aged 50 to 74 to an offer of prostate-specific antigen screening or control, with a predefined core group aged 55 to 69. In the first major report, the rate ratio for prostate cancer death was 0.80, with an absolute difference of 0.71 death per 1,000 men. The investigators estimated that 1,410 men would need to be screened and 48 additional prostate cancers treated to prevent one prostate cancer death, and concluded that the mortality reduction came with a high risk of overdiagnosis ([9]).

The US PLCO trial produced a different estimate under a different comparison. It randomized 76,685 men aged 55 to 74 to organized annual PSA testing and digital rectal examination or usual care, which included substantial opportunistic screening. At 13 years, 4,250 prostate cancers had been diagnosed in the intervention group and 3,815 in control, while prostate cancer mortality rates were 3.7 and 3.4 per 10,000 person-years; the difference was not statistically significant ([11]). Control-group screening reduced the contrast between assigned strategies, so PLCO does not answer exactly the same question as a clean comparison with no screening. It does show that adding organized annual testing to a setting with widespread testing did not demonstrate mortality benefit over 13 years ([11]).

The USPSTF synthesis treats the average net benefit for men aged 55 to 69 as small and preference-sensitive. It reports that approximately one in six men screened at least once at five ERSPC sites experienced at least one false positive, that two-thirds of first-round positive results were false positives, and that biopsies can cause pain, fever, blood in semen, and hospitalization. It recommends against routine PSA screening in men aged 70 and older because the likelihood of delayed benefit is lower and harms from false positives, biopsy, overdiagnosis, and treatment are greater ([10]).

### A negative ovarian trial demonstrates direct harm without mortality benefit

The ovarian component of PLCO randomized 78,216 women aged 55 to 74 to annual CA-125 testing for six years plus transvaginal ultrasound for four years or usual care. Ovarian cancer mortality was not reduced. Among 3,285 women with false-positive results, 1,080 underwent surgical follow-up and 163 experienced at least one serious complication, a 15 percent complication proportion among those operated on after a false-positive screen ([12]). This trial is a direct example of the worst screening outcome: an intervention in healthy people generated invasive procedures and serious complications without showing its intended mortality benefit.

The result also shows why a biomarker and an imaging test cannot be evaluated separately from the escalation rule. The harm occurred after the positive result, through diagnostic surgery. Reporting only the sensitivity of CA-125 or ultrasound would omit the event that mattered to participants. Randomized pathway evaluation captured both the intended endpoint and the downstream cascade ([5] [12]).

### Colorectal screening demonstrates prevention as well as earlier detection

The PLCO flexible sigmoidoscopy trial randomized 154,900 adults aged 55 to 74 to screening, with a repeat at three or five years, or usual care. After a median 11.9 years, colorectal cancer incidence was 11.9 versus 15.2 cases per 10,000 person-years, a 21 percent reduction. Colorectal cancer mortality was 2.9 versus 3.9 deaths per 10,000 person-years, a 26 percent reduction; mortality from distal colorectal cancer fell by 50 percent, while proximal colorectal mortality was not significantly changed ([13]).

This pattern differs from screening that can only move diagnosis earlier. Sigmoidoscopy can identify and enable removal of precursor lesions, so a later reduction in incidence is a plausible intended benefit. Its anatomic reach also explains the stronger distal than proximal mortality result. The trial illustrates why program theory must name the mechanism: prevention through precursor removal and mortality reduction through earlier cancer detection can coexist, but they require different intermediate measures ([13]).

### Breast screening shows why absolute benefit and overdiagnosis must be reported together

The independent UK breast screening panel reviewed randomized and observational evidence. Its meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials estimated a breast cancer mortality rate ratio of 0.80 for women invited to screening, a 20 percent relative reduction. The panel also concluded that overdiagnosis occurs, while noting that estimates vary because studies use different denominators, follow-up periods, and methods ([14]). The 2024 USPSTF recommendation likewise identifies false positives, additional imaging and biopsy, psychological harm, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, and radiation exposure as screening harms, while concluding that biennial mammography has net benefit for women aged 40 to 74 ([15]).

The apparent conflict between mortality benefit and overdiagnosis is false. Both can occur in the same program: some screen-detected cancers are treated early enough to avert death, while others would never have caused harm. Policy must estimate both groups with a common population denominator rather than use the existence of one to deny the other ([3] [4] [14]).

### Overdiagnosis estimates remain method-dependent

Carter and colleagues systematically reviewed 52 studies that quantified cancer overdiagnosis and found wide methodological variation. Definitions differed in whether excess cases were divided by screen-detected cancers, all cancers during the screening period, or long-term cumulative cancers. Models depended on assumptions about lead time and natural history; observational comparisons faced confounding and differences in screening exposure. The review concluded that monitoring should include screening strategy, adherence, treatment, complications, morbidity, and mortality, not a single decontextualized overdiagnosis percentage ([4]).

This uncertainty does not make overdiagnosis imaginary. Persistent excess incidence in a screened group after sufficiently long follow-up, without a compensating decline after screening stops, is evidence that screening added diagnoses rather than merely advancing their timing. Rising early-stage incidence without a corresponding fall in late-stage disease is another warning pattern. The numerical magnitude remains sensitive to denominator, follow-up, contamination, competing mortality, and assumptions about lead time ([3] [4]).

### Decision support improves understanding, not the biology of the test

The Cochrane review of decision aids included 209 studies and 107,698 adults across 71 decisions, including breast, colorectal, and prostate screening. Compared with usual care, decision aids produced large improvements in knowledge and accurate risk expectations and increased active participation. Moderate-certainty evidence indicated better congruence between informed values and choices, with a risk ratio of 1.75 across 21 studies and 9,377 participants ([16]).

Decision aids do not convert a low-benefit screening program into a high-benefit one. Their role is to make close trade-offs legible and support choices that reflect informed preferences. Programs still require independent evidence that expected benefit exceeds harm in the eligible population. The evidence supports shared decision-making inside an evidence-based boundary, not as a substitute for that boundary ([1] [5] [16]).

## Implications

### For patients: an invitation is an offer, not a diagnosis or obligation

A screening invitation means that a program judges a person eligible for an assessment of risk; it does not mean that disease is present. A positive screen means that further evaluation is warranted under the program's protocol; it does not by itself establish disease. A negative screen reduces risk under stated test conditions but does not guarantee absence, so new symptoms still require diagnostic attention. Keeping these three statements separate prevents both panic after a positive result and false reassurance after a negative one ([1] [3] [17]).

The most useful questions are concrete. What is my baseline chance of the serious outcome over a stated period? How much does screening reduce that outcome in absolute terms? How many people like me receive a false positive, invasive follow-up, an overdiagnosis, or a major treatment complication? What happens after each possible result? How long does benefit take to appear, and is the evidence drawn from people with my risk and health status? What changes if I wait, choose another interval, or decline? These questions convert an abstract recommendation into a pathway that can be compared with personal values ([5] [10] [16]).

Risk communication should use the same denominator and time horizon for benefit and harm. Saying that a program reduces relative mortality by 20 percent while describing false positives as a percentage of all screening rounds makes comparison difficult. A natural-frequency display might state outcomes per 1,000 eligible people invited over ten years: disease deaths with and without screening, false-positive results, biopsies or operations, overdiagnosed cases, and serious complications. Where uncertainty is large, a range should be shown rather than hidden behind a single point estimate ([4] [6] [16]).

The option not to screen is part of informed choice when net benefit is small, evidence is insufficient, or preferences reasonably differ. Declining a preference-sensitive screen is not neglecting health. Conversely, accepting a recommended program is not an agreement to every later intervention: confirmatory testing, active surveillance, and treatment can require separate decisions. The author's assessment is that informed choice is strongest when each consequential branch is made visible before the first test ([10] [16]).

### For clinicians: estimate prior risk before ordering the test

Clinicians should first determine whether the person is asymptomatic and eligible under the evidence-based population. Symptoms change the task from screening to diagnosis and may require a different test or urgency. Family history, prior findings, exposures, age, sex, comorbidity, life expectancy, and previous screening can alter baseline risk or the expected time to benefit. Applying an average-risk program to a high-risk person can undertest; applying it to a very low-risk or short-life-expectancy person can create disproportionate harm ([1] [2] [10]).

Before testing, clinicians should map the likely positive-result cascade. What confirmatory test follows? How accurate and invasive is it? Is a watchful-waiting option available? What treatment would be offered, and has earlier treatment improved patient outcomes? A test is not clinically useful merely because it detects an abnormality. If no effective or acceptable action follows, the result may create surveillance and anxiety without benefit. If the only follow-up is hazardous, the threshold for initiating the pathway should reflect that downstream risk ([1] [5] [12]).

Shared decision-making is most valuable when the balance is close, not as a ritual applied after the decision has effectively been made. A concise process is to state the decision, establish individual risk, present absolute benefit and harm, explain uncertainty and alternatives, elicit which outcomes matter most, and record the preference. Decision aids can improve knowledge and risk perception, but the clinician remains responsible for recommending against screening when evidence shows no net benefit and for recommending indicated diagnostic evaluation when symptoms are present ([10] [16]).

After a positive result, language matters. Calling a screen "positive" rather than saying that the person "has" the disease preserves the distinction between risk classification and diagnosis. After confirmation, clinicians should distinguish disease that requires treatment from disease for which active surveillance is evidence-based. This separation can reduce overtreatment even when overdiagnosis cannot be eliminated at detection ([3] [10]).

### For program designers: optimize the pathway, not the detection count

The primary success measure should be the health outcome the program was created to improve. Detection rate is an operating measure, not the final goal. A new test that doubles detection but leaves late-stage disease, serious morbidity, and mortality unchanged may chiefly be expanding the reservoir of diagnosis. A program should therefore monitor interval disease and late-stage incidence alongside screen-detected cases, then connect those measures to treatment and outcomes ([3] [4] [6]).

Eligibility and interval should be treated as adjustable design variables. Higher-risk targeting can improve positive predictive value and absolute benefit, while stopping rules can protect people whose competing mortality makes benefit unlikely. Intervals should reflect the distribution of preclinical progression, treatment window, accumulated false-positive burden, radiation or procedural exposure, and system capacity. Changes should be evaluated as changes to the intervention, not described as minor operational refinements ([1] [2] [15]).

Confirmation should be staged where evidence supports it. Indeterminate categories, repeat noninvasive testing, risk calculators, and specialist review can reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, as long as delays do not erase benefit for aggressive disease. The contrast between the high positive and false-positive proportions in NLST and the lower referral proportion under NELSON's volume-based nodule protocol shows how classification and follow-up rules shape downstream burden even within low-dose CT screening ([7] [8]).

Quality assurance requires linked records from invitation to outcome. Programs need to know whether positive results received timely assessment, whether diagnoses received appropriate treatment or surveillance, whether complications occurred, and whether participants were lost at transitions. Aggregate participation without linkage can conceal a program that performs tests successfully but fails to deliver confirmation or care. Incident review and transparent reporting should include harms as well as cancers or conditions detected ([1] [2] [17]).

Capacity is part of safety. Launching a high-volume screen without laboratories, imaging, endoscopy, pathology, surgery, counseling, and treatment capacity can displace symptomatic patients or other higher-value care. The single worst operational outcome is a program that invites healthy people into a queue while delaying diagnosis or treatment for both screened and symptomatic patients. Prevention requires capacity modeling before launch, staged implementation, and stop or slow rules when follow-up times exceed safe limits ([1] [2] [17]).

### For policymakers: require evidence of net benefit and equitable delivery

Policy should separate three decisions: whether the intervention can work, whether the organized program can reproduce that benefit in the local system, and whether its opportunity cost is acceptable. Randomized trials can establish efficacy under defined conditions. Implementation evidence must show reach, fidelity, timeliness, and quality. Economic evaluation must include confirmation, surveillance, treatment of overdiagnosed disease, complications, data infrastructure, and the services displaced by the program, not only the price of the first test ([1] [2] [17]).

Equity should be measured at every step. Invitation coverage can be equal while completion of diagnostic assessment differs by income, geography, language, disability, or trust. Participation targets can also become coercive if staff are rewarded for uptake without equal attention to informed refusal. The objective is equitable access to informed choice and the full pathway, not maximal testing regardless of preference. Subgroup reporting should include baseline risk, participation, positivity, predictive value, time to resolution, complications, and outcomes ([2] [17]).

Coverage policy should preserve the distinction between an evidence-based population program and unsystematic testing. Opportunistic screening can use inconsistent eligibility, thresholds, intervals, and follow-up, weakening both net benefit and evaluation. Organized programs can standardize invitations, quality assurance, recall, data linkage, and review. Organization does not guarantee benefit, but it makes the intervention defined enough to govern and measure ([1] [2]).

Programs should have explicit reassessment dates and sunset conditions. A screening recommendation can become obsolete when vaccination lowers prevalence, treatment after symptomatic diagnosis improves, a trial fails to confirm benefit, or a new test increases overdiagnosis. Political difficulty in withdrawing a familiar program is not evidence of effectiveness. The author's assessment is that the ethical presumption should be reversible deployment: pilot, measure the complete pathway, expand only after predefined gates pass, and stop when net benefit no longer remains credible ([1] [2] [4]).

### For researchers: design studies around the counterfactual that matters

New screening technologies should be compared with current care using patient outcomes, not only retrospective accuracy. Case-control datasets enriched with disease can estimate discrimination but cannot show predictive value in the intended low-prevalence population, downstream procedures, behavior after results, or mortality benefit. Prospective studies should use representative eligibility, prespecified thresholds, complete follow-up, and subgroup calibration. When a randomized mortality trial is impractical, investigators should triangulate stage distribution, late-stage incidence, treatment, complications, and long-term excess incidence while stating the assumptions that connect them ([3] [4] [5]).

Overdiagnosis studies must report the numerator, denominator, screening exposure, follow-up after screening stops, contamination, competing mortality, and lead-time adjustment. A percentage without these elements is not portable across studies. Researchers should present multiple defensible denominators when audiences may interpret the same label differently. Models should expose how assumptions about progression, sojourn time, and treatment effect change the estimate ([4]).

Adaptive and risk-stratified programs need independent validation. A model can improve average positive predictive value while systematically missing disease in a subgroup or routing disadvantaged people through a higher-burden pathway. Evaluation should measure calibration, net benefit across plausible thresholds, subgroup performance, and the consequence of model drift. The same prospective and human-in-the-loop evidence requirements that govern clinical AI apply when AI selects people for screening or interprets their tests ([2] [5]).

### For evidence consumers: ask whether more diagnosis became better health

The shortest reliable audit is to trace five quantities. Did screening increase diagnoses? Did late-stage or clinically consequential disease decline? Did disease-specific mortality or severe morbidity decline? What harms followed testing, confirmation, and treatment? Did the comparison include everyone eligible rather than only diagnosed survivors? This sequence exposes lead-time bias, length bias, and overdiagnosis without requiring that any one overdiagnosed individual be identified ([3] [6]).

More detection can be beneficial, neutral, and harmful within the same program. The evidence from lung, prostate, ovarian, colorectal, and breast screening shows all three patterns: mortality reduction with substantial false positives, small benefit with excess diagnosis, no mortality benefit with surgical complications, prevention plus mortality reduction, and mortality reduction accompanied by overdiagnosis ([7] [9] [12] [13] [14]). The general rule is therefore not "screen" or "do not screen." It is to demand a defined population, a validated pathway, outcomes resistant to screening bias, explicit accounting of harm, and revision when the balance changes.
