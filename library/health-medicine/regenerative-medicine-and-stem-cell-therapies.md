# Regenerative Medicine Delivers Clinical Benefit Only When a Defined Cell Product Solves a Defined Disease Problem

Regenerative medicine uses cells or their derivatives to replace, restore, or support damaged human tissues, but a cell's ability to differentiate in a laboratory does not establish a therapeutic benefit in a patient. Hematopoietic transplantation and selected licensed products demonstrate that cell-based interventions can work for specific indications; most proposed uses still require controlled outcome evidence, manufacturing safeguards, and long-term monitoring. The relevant question is not whether a treatment contains stem cells, but what the product is, how it acts, and whether its benefits exceed its risks for the specified patient population ([1] [2] [3] [4]).

## Background

### From tissue renewal to clinical intervention

Many human tissues renew through resident cells with limited developmental potential. Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells replenish blood lineages, while limbal cells at the corneal edge help maintain the surface of the eye. Their normal roles suggested a clinical strategy: collect suitable cells, prepare them for a particular recipient and tissue, and assess whether function can be restored after injury or disease. This is different from assuming that any harvested cell can repair any organ. The NIH distinguishes tissue-restricted adult stem cells from pluripotent embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells; the latter can generate many specialized cell types but require differentiation and quality control before clinical use ([4] [13]).

Hematopoietic cell transplantation supplied the durable clinical precedent. Cells from a donor or the patient's own collection can reconstitute blood formation after conditioning, with uses in serious blood and immune disorders. This is a complex treatment pathway, not merely an injection of cells: the indication, conditioning regimen, donor compatibility, infection risk, and follow-up determine the clinical result. The NIH identifies transplantation for blood and immune disorders as an established class of stem-cell-based treatment, while FDA discussions of graft-versus-host disease illustrate that restoration of blood formation can come with major immune complications ([3] [4]).

The field subsequently widened from blood formation to cultivated tissues and differentiated descendants of pluripotent cells. The European Medicines Agency's Holoclar example takes a small limbal biopsy from a patient with ocular-burn-related limbal deficiency, expands corneal epithelial cells containing limbal stem cells, and implants a sheet to help restore a stable corneal surface. This is an anatomically specific use of cells with a relevant tissue-maintenance role, not a generalized regeneration claim. Holoclar first received conditional European authorization in 2015 and standard authorization in 2024 after additional evidence was supplied ([5]).

Another line of development uses a patient's hematopoietic cells as a vehicle for a genetic correction. The FDA's 2023 authorization of Casgevy and Lyfgenia for sickle cell disease involved collecting autologous blood stem cells, modifying them outside the body, giving myeloablative conditioning, and reinfusing the prepared cells. In July 2026 the FDA expanded Casgevy's indications to eligible children aged two years and older with recurrent sickle crises or transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia. These are cell-based gene therapies with their own indications and risks, not evidence that unmodified cells treat unrelated disorders ([6] [7]).

Mesenchymal stromal cells, often marketed as mesenchymal stem cells, created a different translational challenge. Their proposed effects can involve signaling and immune modulation rather than stable replacement of a lost tissue. The FDA's December 2024 approval of donor bone-marrow-derived Ryoncil for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease in certain children is an indication-specific achievement. It does not authorize stromal cells from any source for arthritis, stroke, or other advertised conditions. The approval also exposes a currency problem: an FDA consumer alert last current in April 2024 says only umbilical-cord-blood hematopoietic products were FDA-approved as stem cell products, whereas the FDA later approved Ryoncil. The older general statement cannot be used as a current exhaustive product list ([3] [8] [12]).

Patient demand grew alongside legitimate research. NIH warns that clinics can invoke laboratory plausibility or trial registration while selling interventions that have not passed appropriate regulatory and efficacy review. ClinicalTrials.gov registration records a study; it is not FDA authorization or proof that the study is scientifically sound. The ISSCR guidelines similarly separate responsible clinical translation, which requires adequate preclinical evidence and meaningful clinical assessment, from commercial offerings that borrow the vocabulary of scientific investigation without completing that process ([1] [4]).

The clinical boundary of this topic is deliberate. The mechanisms of differentiation are relevant insofar as they determine product identity and risks, but a survey of basic cell biology belongs to science. Engineering of delivery devices and the economics of biotechnology businesses are not substitutes for evidence that patients recover function. Regulation appears here as a clinical evidence and safety boundary; the detailed legal doctrine is outside the present scope, consistent with the health-medicine domain anchor (`library/health-medicine/anchor-health-medicine.md`) ([1] [11]).

## Core Concepts

### A cell source does not specify a clinical product

The word "stem cell" covers materially different starting materials. A patient's own cells are autologous; cells from another person are allogeneic. Adult tissue stem cells have restricted differentiation in their normal environment; embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells can be directed toward many mature types. A finished therapy may contain stem cells, lineage-committed progenitors, differentiated descendants, or mixed populations. The relevant clinical unit is the entire specified product -- source, processing, identity, dose, route, delivery procedure, and accompanying care -- not the appealing name of its starting material ([1] [13]).

An autologous source can limit donor-recipient mismatch but does not make the procedure harmless or automatically effective. Casgevy begins with the patient's own cells, yet the treatment also requires collection, manufacturing, myeloablative conditioning, engraftment, and extended safety assessment. Conversely, a donor-derived product can be useful when its intended function and patient population are defined, as Ryoncil's pediatric graft-versus-host indication demonstrates. The comparison is therefore not autologous good versus allogeneic bad; it is whether the chosen source can deliver a reproducible mechanism at an acceptable total treatment risk ([3] [6] [7]).

Pluripotency creates both opportunity and control problems. If a stem-cell line can be differentiated into cells that secrete insulin or dopamine, it offers an alternative to a scarce donor tissue. But the manufacturing process must separate the desired differentiated population from undesired cells, retain functional potency after processing and storage, and address genetic or epigenetic changes acquired during expansion. ISSCR describes identity, purity, potency, genomic stability, and storage effects as matters for independent manufacturing oversight. The author's synthesis is that the ability to generate a cell is only the first link in a chain; a safe, stable, precisely identified administered population is a distinct achievement ([1] [9] [10]).

### Repair, replacement, and immunomodulation are different hypotheses

A replacement intervention supplies a functional population missing from the target tissue. Blood-forming cells may repopulate marrow; cultivated limbal cells can help rebuild a corneal epithelial surface; differentiated islet cells are being investigated to secrete insulin. Each requires a plausible route to the correct anatomical site, survival or engraftment, and a clinical measure of restored function. The tissue-specific hypothesis tells investigators which outcomes to measure rather than letting a broad claim of "healing" absorb every possible result ([4] [5] [9]).

Some cell products aim instead to alter immune signaling or the local repair environment. Ryoncil treats an inflammatory transplant complication; its approved use is not equivalent to rebuilding an organ with donor stromal cells. A transient biological signal could in principle improve symptoms without permanently integrating, but it would require evidence against a relevant comparator and identification of the clinical effect that matters. A change in inflammatory markers alone would not show that a patient can walk better, avoid organ damage, or live longer. The author's synthesis is that a product should be judged against its claimed mechanism and endpoint, not against a generic label such as regenerative ([1] [3]).

Delivery changes the hypothesis. Cells infused into the bloodstream may distribute very differently from cells implanted in an ocular surface, deposited inside the brain, or delivered to the liver through the portal circulation. The prospective diabetes study administered differentiated islets into the portal vein with immunosuppression; a Parkinson trial grafted dopamine progenitors into both sides of the putamen under immunosuppression. Those choices introduce procedural and immune risks distinct from the cells' intrinsic characteristics. Results for one route cannot be transferred to another route by calling both procedures stem-cell treatment ([9] [10]).

### Manufacture determines whether one dose resembles the next

Cell-based medicine is sensitive to donor or patient variation, collection conditions, culture history, identity, viability, contamination, differentiation state, cryopreservation, and transport. ISSCR calls for screening relevant donors and banks for infectious risks, rigorous control of reagents and manufacturing, and release criteria suited to the final product. Identity answers what cells are present; purity asks what else is present; potency asks whether the product performs a function relevant to its proposed mechanism. A cell count alone is not an adequate surrogate for all three ([1]).

Autologous manufacturing introduces a further matching problem: a collected sample, intermediate preparation, and released dose must belong to the correct patient, while a failed preparation can delay treatment after clinical planning has begun. Off-the-shelf donor material offers repeatable batches but creates its own immune, donor-screening, and scale-control questions. Storage and thawing can change potency even if labels and counts remain identical. These are not peripheral factory questions; they affect whether evidence from a trial's administered product applies to the product a later patient receives ([1] [3] [10]).

The same discipline applies to combination treatment. In the licensed gene-edited blood-cell pathway, the cell preparation cannot be interpreted separately from marrow conditioning and supportive care. In the small islet study, immunosuppression accompanied every transplant. A clinician considering a benefit must therefore account for the harms, monitoring and resource demands of the entire regimen, not assign all observed benefit to the cells and all toxicity to something called an ancillary procedure. The author's assessment is that changing the cell product, conditioning or accompanying treatment produces a new clinical question unless comparability is demonstrated ([6] [7] [9]).

### The evidence ladder has indication-specific rungs

A laboratory differentiation assay shows biological capability; an animal study tests a model; a small first-in-human trial can establish feasibility and detect some early harms. None of these automatically establishes a comparative patient benefit. A controlled trial can test whether functional outcomes improve relative to standard care or placebo, while longer follow-up can capture delayed toxicities, loss of effect, graft failure, and the burden of accompanying immune suppression. ISSCR calls for compelling preclinical rationale, properly conducted trials, adverse-event reporting, publication of negative as well as positive findings, and post-approval surveillance ([1]).

The appropriate endpoint depends on the hypothesis. For limbal therapy, investigators examine durable corneal surface integrity and vision-related outcomes. For diabetes islet replacement, they can measure stimulated C-peptide, severe hypoglycemia, glycemic control, insulin use, and immunosuppression harms. For an osteoarthritis injection, pain and function matter more directly than a claimed ability to grow cartilage; imaging can be informative but cannot replace a meaningful treatment comparison. For Parkinson disease, graft imaging, motor function, independence, and delayed safety address different questions. The author's synthesis is to keep mechanistic, surrogate, symptomatic, and long-term endpoints explicitly separate ([5] [9] [10] [14] [15]).

Small uncontrolled studies can be informative for severe rare conditions when a validated comparator is difficult, but they cannot support the same causal interpretation as blinded randomization. Ryoncil's pivotal evidence came from a single-arm pediatric study, so a response rate describes treated patients rather than a randomized difference from a control. Holoclar's early evaluation used historical records rather than randomized concurrent controls. Conversely, a randomized knee study can measure a symptom difference at six months without proving durable tissue repair or benefit from every commercial formulation. The evidence claim must be as narrow as the observed design ([3] [5] [14]).

### Safety follows the mechanism and the procedure

Safety assessment includes immediate infusion reactions, infections introduced with a product, immune rejection or graft-versus-host complications, tissue growth in an unwanted location, and delayed effects of persistent or genetically modified cells. The FDA identifies infections, hemorrhage, edema and infusion-related concerns in Ryoncil's setting. The ISSCR emphasizes tumor formation, genetic alteration, delivery-site effects and long-term monitoring, particularly where cells remain or proliferate after administration. These risks differ among blood-cell replacement, stromal immunomodulation and pluripotent-cell-derived grafts; a reassuring result in one class cannot clear another ([1] [3]).

Risk can also arise because the patient forgoes care with established benefit. An unapproved clinic can charge for a procedure while bypassing adequate product testing, adequate controls and meaningful follow-up. NIH reports serious adverse events and warns that delay of effective treatment can itself harm patients. The FDA's consumer alert warns that broad marketing claims for orthopedic and neurological conditions are not evidence of approval; however, its exhaustive 2024 statement about the number of approved stem-cell products is superseded by the specific later Ryoncil approval. Clinical decisions should consult current product-specific information rather than a dated blanket statement ([3] [4] [12]).

### Evidence categories cannot be collapsed into a binary label

"Approved" names a product, indication, jurisdiction, date and conditions of use. European authorization of Holoclar does not itself authorize it in the United States; US approval of Ryoncil for specified pediatric transplant complications does not approve it for arthritis. Likewise, a registered study or an investigational new drug process permits research under defined conditions but does not establish that its intervention works. The author's synthesis is a four-part clinical record: exact product, exact indication, exact evidence design, and exact applicable jurisdiction and date. Without those fields, statements about a stem-cell treatment being "available" or "proven" are ambiguous ([3] [4] [5] [12]).

## Evidence

### Established blood-cell replacement and cell-based genetic correction

NIH describes bone marrow transplantation and treatments for blood and immune disorders as established stem-cell-based applications rather than hypothetical possibilities. FDA's description of allogeneic hematopoietic transplantation also makes the limiting context visible: restoring blood formation can be followed by graft-versus-host disease. The evidence is therefore for selected clinical uses of a structured transplant pathway, not for the proposition that all cell injections regenerate tissue. Patient selection and transplant complications belong in the benefit-risk assessment ([3] [4]).

The FDA's 2023 Casgevy and Lyfgenia decision provides a second form of clinically adopted blood-cell therapy: patients' own hematopoietic cells are collected, genetically modified and returned after high-intensity conditioning for defined sickle-cell indications. The FDA expanded Casgevy's indication in July 2026 to eligible patients as young as two with recurrent vaso-occlusive crises or transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia; its release describes a small younger thalassemia cohort and notes warnings for engraftment failure, delayed platelet engraftment, hypersensitivity and off-target editing risk. This is evidence of indication-specific clinical translation and continuing safety obligations, not a claim that gene editing eliminates the risks of transplantation ([6] [7]).

### Holoclar shows tissue-specific restoration with design limits

EMA reports that Holoclar, an autologous expanded corneal-cell sheet containing limbal stem cells, is used in adults with moderate to severe limbal stem-cell deficiency after ocular burns. In the retrospective study the agency summarized, 75 of 104 patients had a successful implant at one year, defined by a stable corneal surface without defects and with little or no abnormal vessel growth. The indication is anatomically narrow and the described assessment drew on past medical records rather than a randomized controlled comparison. EMA says conditional authorization was converted to standard authorization after additional data; this status does not turn the initial case-series comparison into a randomized trial ([5]).

A useful clinical lesson from this case is that the transplanted cells' normal physiological role and the target lesion align. This alignment does not make a corneal-cell product a plausible treatment for spinal injury or systemic inflammation without an independent program of product characterization and clinical evidence. The author's assessment is that Holoclar is a success precisely because its claim is restricted to a defined product, route, lesion and observable repair endpoint ([5] [1]).

### Ryoncil supports a narrow immunomodulatory indication

The FDA authorized allogeneic bone-marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells as Ryoncil in December 2024 for steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease in children aged two months or older. The prospective multicenter single-arm study enrolled 54 pediatric patients after allogeneic blood-cell transplantation. FDA reported day-28 overall response of 70 percent, comprising complete response in 30 percent and partial response in 41 percent; median response duration was 54 days. Because there was no randomized concurrent control, these percentages do not measure the benefit over another second-line treatment. They are nevertheless the product-specific evidence reported with the approval ([3] [8]).

FDA lists infections, fever, hemorrhage, edema, abdominal pain, hypertension and possible hypersensitivity or infusion reactions among the treatment considerations. The case demonstrates an approved cell intervention whose clinical aim is control of a severe immune complication, not visible rebuilding of cartilage or neurons. It is also an internal check against older blanket FDA consumer language that claimed only cord-blood-derived hematopoietic products were approved. The dated alert still provides useful warnings about unsupported consumer uses, but its product inventory was overtaken by the later approval ([3] [12]).

### Knee osteoarthritis trials show why symptomatic gain is not proof of regeneration

Kim and colleagues' multicenter phase III double-blind randomized trial studied 261 people with symptomatic Kellgren-Lawrence grade 3 knee osteoarthritis. At six months, the published abstract reported greater improvement after a single injection of culture-expanded autologous adipose-derived stromal cells than after placebo: 25.2 versus 15.5 points on a 100-mm pain visual-analog scale and 21.7 versus 14.3 points on the total WOMAC score. Those are differences in the studied product and population over a short observation window, not proof of an enduring restoration of joint cartilage or safety over years ([14]).

Tian and colleagues' 2024 systematic review searched randomized trials and included 18 articles. It reported pooled improvement in pain and function at 12 months relative to placebo but cautioned that the included studies were heterogeneous and follow-up was relatively short for predicting long-term efficacy and safety. Its conclusion and the individual trial should be read alongside the FDA's warning that no marketed regenerative product has been approved in the United States for an orthopedic condition in the categories the alert lists. The trial result, the synthesis of heterogeneous trials and a jurisdiction-specific approval claim address different questions; none should be used to erase the others ([12] [14] [15]).

### Islets and dopamine progenitors remain investigational in the cited trials

The 2025 phase 1-2 zimislecel report evaluated allogeneic stem-cell-derived islets for type 1 diabetes with immunosuppression. Fourteen participants had at least twelve months of follow-up in an interim analysis; all had detectable stimulated C-peptide after implantation when none had detectable C-peptide at baseline. Among twelve who received a full dose, ten were not using external insulin at day 365, while serious events included neutropenia in three and two deaths described by the investigators. The analyses were interim and not prespecified, with no randomized control. The findings support the hypothesis of restored islet function in the studied setting but cannot establish population-wide long-term net benefit or isolate cells from the effects and harms of the complete regimen ([9]).

A 2025 open-label phase I Parkinson study implanted embryonic-stem-cell-derived dopaminergic progenitors in twelve patients, with two dose groups and one year of immunosuppression. Its primary safety and tolerability objectives were met at one year; imaging at eighteen months showed increased putaminal fluorodopa uptake consistent with graft survival. Exploratory motor findings, including an improvement in an off-medication score in the high-dose group, were not a blinded comparison against a control. A separate 2026 open-label phase 1-2 study treated eight patients with another progenitor product, with seven completing a year of follow-up and one dying of pulmonary infection. These early trials support feasibility and structured follow-up; they do not establish an approved or reliably effective general Parkinson treatment ([10] [16]).

Taken together, the cases show an evidence gradient, not a single score for "stem cells." Hematopoietic transplantation has established clinical uses; Holoclar and Ryoncil are licensed for their respective narrow uses in different jurisdictions; cell-derived islets and dopamine progenitors have early human data; and osteoarthritis injections have controlled short-term symptom findings but no US approval for the marketed orthopedic use described by the FDA. The author's synthesis is that each rung depends on a product, indication, comparator, endpoint, jurisdiction and follow-up period ([3] [4] [5] [9] [10] [12] [14] [15]).

## Implications

### For patients and caregivers: ask for the full treatment identity

A patient offered "regenerative medicine" should ask which cells or derivative will be given, whose cells they are, how they were processed, what exact disease and disease stage the intervention targets, and whether the claimed use is authorized in the relevant country. Approval should be checked against the product and indication, not inferred from the clinician's qualifications, a business registration, trial listing or another product made from a similar tissue. NIH explicitly notes that ClinicalTrials.gov listing is not FDA study authorization or evidence of approval, and FDA's product-specific Ryoncil release shows how narrow a legitimate approval can be ([3] [4]).

A second question is what clinically important outcome improved compared with what would otherwise happen. For an ocular lesion, is the surface stable and can the patient see or function better? For diabetes, how much severe hypoglycemia and insulin dependence are prevented, for how long, at what immunosuppressive cost? For a painful knee, how do pain, mobility and need for other treatment compare after an adequate follow-up? A dramatic scan, cell count or laboratory measurement may support a mechanism but should not replace the primary patient outcome. The author's synthesis is to request the study design, denominator, comparator and duration together with any headline percentage ([5] [9] [14]).

Safety questions must cover the whole procedure: collection, surgery or infusion, conditioning or immunosuppression, infections, follow-up, and alternatives forgone. An apparent lack of immediate infusion complications in a small trial cannot exclude uncommon or late harm. The 2025 islet trial reported both promising one-year function and serious adverse events; the Parkinson programs also used prolonged immunosuppression. Patients should discuss those facts with a qualified treating team rather than interpret a stem-cell label as a guarantee of natural or risk-free treatment ([1] [9] [10] [16]).

For commercial offerings without convincing trials, the worst case is not just paying for an ineffective procedure. A patient might receive a contaminated or otherwise unsafe product, or delay care with established benefit. NIH documents serious harms associated with unapproved products and recommends asking about product approval or a properly authorized clinical investigation; ISSCR calls for limited, carefully governed use of unproven interventions. A clinician's willingness to administer a product is not equivalent to a demonstration that its benefits exceed those risks ([1] [4]).

### For clinicians: separate clinical plausibility from demonstrated effect

Clinical plausibility begins with the lesion and cell mechanism. Are cells intended to replace a depleted lineage, form a new functional graft, modulate immunity, or produce a temporary signal? The answer determines the relevant endpoint, required delivery site and foreseeable safety events. A hematopoietic-cell transplant that repopulates marrow and a stromal-cell product used for acute graft-versus-host disease have different success criteria. Adopting a study's conclusion in another tissue or disease without reconstructing that chain would substitute analogy for evidence ([1] [3] [4]).

Next, check that the tested product resembles what the patient would receive. Source tissue, manipulation, culture, cell mixture, potency assay, dose, storage, route and accompanying treatment all matter. A clinic using adipose tissue fragments cannot simply cite a trial of culture-expanded adipose-derived cells; a different preparation and treatment pathway require their own evidence. Equally, an autologous label does not erase manufacturing variability, surgical risks or the need to monitor graft outcomes. ISSCR's manufacturing and trial guidance makes product definition a condition of interpretable clinical science ([1] [14]).

Communicate evidence by grade rather than a binary promise. A regulated product for a specified indication, a controlled trial showing limited symptomatic benefit, an open-label feasibility study and a laboratory experiment are not interchangeable. If a new therapy is offered within research, informed consent should explain uncertainty, available standard care, costs and adverse-event response. If a licensed intervention is considered, the indication, patient-selection criteria, monitoring and country-specific authorization should be verified against current product information; the FDA's 2024 general consumer alert demonstrably lagged the 2024 Ryoncil approval ([1] [3] [12]).

### For trial designers: build an interpretable causal test

Trial designers should prespecify an exact product and a defensible clinical target, with a potency assay linked to the hypothesized mechanism. They should separate preliminary engraftment or biomarker outcomes from patient-important function, explicitly track procedural and immune-suppression harms, and state how long a graft is expected to work. Baseline disease severity, donor or cell line, dose, route, comparator and concomitant therapies should be recorded so that a result can be interpreted or reproduced. ISSCR calls for complete publication and adverse-event reporting, including neutral and negative results ([1]).

A compelling early-phase observation can justify the next study without settling efficacy. The islet and Parkinson reports demonstrate detectable cell function or graft survival in small cohorts, but their open-label or interim designs leave room for selection, background treatment and natural variation. Controlled designs become especially important for fluctuating symptoms, as in joint pain or Parkinson motor scores. A trial for a rare, severe condition may face a different comparator constraint; the constraint should be stated rather than hidden behind the word "breakthrough" ([3] [5] [9] [10] [14]).

Manufacturing comparability is another trial-design question. A lot's identity and potency need measurements that can be repeated at release; storage and delivery must preserve the intended dose and function. Post-market registries can complement pre-approval trials by finding uncommon harms and evaluating durability across more diverse recipients, but a registry cannot by itself supply the missing comparator for an unproven benefit claim. The author's synthesis is that evidence must follow the product through manufacture, administration and time rather than end at the first clinical response ([1]).

### For health systems: preserve evidence, access and follow-up together

Health systems deciding whether to introduce a cell intervention should evaluate the complete pathway: collection or procurement, quality-assured manufacturing, eligibility review, specialized delivery, immunosuppression when required, infection management, follow-up and outcome collection. A promising cell product that cannot be delivered consistently or monitored safely is not a complete clinical service. When an approved product has an anatomically narrow indication, expansion to another disease should trigger a fresh evidence assessment rather than routine service-line marketing ([1] [3] [5]).

Access should be evaluated without using urgency as a reason to discard evidence. People with serious disease may reasonably seek trials, but therapeutic misconception can arise when enrollment, compassionate innovation and established treatment are presented as equivalent. ISSCR recommends independent oversight, consent, transparency and plans to address harms. NIH's warning about clinic marketing suggests that referral pathways should help patients distinguish regulated research from direct-to-consumer claims. The author's assessment is that maintaining a trustworthy route into trials protects patients and also makes genuinely useful therapies easier to recognize ([1] [4]).

Finally, information systems must be versioned. The FDA alert dated April 2024 still says only certain cord-blood hematopoietic products are approved as stem cell products; the later FDA Ryoncil approval makes that inventory incomplete. The correct remedy is not to discard all consumer warnings but to attach dates and check the current product-specific source before repeating an absolute approval claim. This applies equally to emerging indications, revised safety labeling and international differences. A living clinical decision requires living evidence, even when the foundational rule -- defined product, defined indication, tested benefit and monitored harm -- stays constant ([3] [5] [7] [12]).
