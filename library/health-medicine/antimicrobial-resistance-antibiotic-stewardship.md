# Antibiotic Stewardship Preserves Effective Treatment by Reducing Selection Without Withholding Necessary Care

Antibiotic resistance turns treatable bacterial infections into harder, costlier, and sometimes untreatable disease, but antibiotics remain indispensable when bacterial infection is likely or proven. Antibiotic stewardship therefore means delivering effective therapy while continually narrowing the drug, dose, route, and duration to what the patient actually needs; it is not indiscriminate restriction. Preservation succeeds only when prescribing is joined to diagnostics, infection prevention, surveillance, reliable access, and One Health controls across human, animal, agricultural, and environmental systems ([1] [4] [6]).

## Background

### Resistance is an evolutionary consequence with clinical stakes

Antimicrobial resistance, or AMR, is the loss of susceptibility of bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites to medicines intended to control them. Antibiotic resistance is the bacterial subset of that larger problem. This distinction matters because stewardship tools, laboratory methods, and treatment decisions differ by organism and medicine; the present topic focuses on bacterial resistance and antibiotic use while retaining the wider AMR context. Resistance can arise through genetic change and can spread between organisms, people, animals, food systems, and environmental reservoirs. Antibiotic exposure does not purposefully teach a bacterium to resist. It changes the competitive environment so that organisms already carrying, or newly acquiring, a survival advantage are more likely to persist and reproduce ([1] [13] [14]).

The resulting damage extends beyond a failed prescription. Resistant infection can delay effective therapy, require more toxic or costly alternatives, prolong illness and hospital care, and increase the probability of disability or death. It also weakens procedures that depend on dependable infection treatment or prophylaxis, including surgery, intensive care, transplantation, neonatal care, and cancer chemotherapy. The World Health Organization identifies inappropriate antibiotic use, weak water and sanitation systems, inadequate infection prevention, poor access to vaccines and diagnostics, and unequal access to quality medicines as interacting drivers rather than as separate problems ([1]).

The scale is already measurable. The GBD 2021 Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators estimated that 4.71 million deaths in 2021 were associated with bacterial AMR and that 1.14 million were attributable to it. "Associated" compares deaths involving resistant infection with no infection, whereas "attributable" compares resistant infection with a counterfactual susceptible infection; the two measures answer different questions and should not be interchanged. The same analysis estimated substantial variation by age, pathogen, drug class, and location and forecast a rising future burden without stronger prevention and treatment systems ([3]).

WHO surveillance provides a complementary, laboratory-based view. Its 2025 global report analyzed more than 23 million bacteriologically confirmed infections reported by 104 countries and estimated that approximately one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections in 2023 involved resistance to antibiotic treatment. National data completeness was only 53.8 percent among reporting countries, and surveillance gaps were concentrated where laboratory capacity and access to alternative therapy were often weakest. The report therefore measures both the extent of resistance and the limits of what current systems can see ([2]).

### Stewardship emerged because individual treatment creates shared consequences

An antibiotic prescription has two effects at once. It can benefit the treated patient by suppressing a bacterial pathogen, and it can impose selection pressure on the pathogen and on susceptible organisms in the patient's microbiota. Antibiotic use can also cause direct harm through allergy, toxicity, drug interaction, disruption of protective microbial communities, and C. difficile infection. Those effects make antibiotic use different from a private consumption choice: current use can alter the future effectiveness of a shared therapeutic resource, while delayed or inadequate therapy can harm the patient immediately ([5] [6] [7]).

The ethical problem is therefore not "use antibiotics" versus "save antibiotics." It is how to preserve prompt effective treatment for patients who need it while avoiding exposure that has little expected benefit. In severe sepsis or another life-threatening suspected bacterial infection, the cost of delay can dominate and justify broad empirical coverage. Once microbiology, imaging, physiology, and clinical response reduce uncertainty, the balance can change: therapy can be stopped if infection is unsupported, narrowed to the identified organism, converted from intravenous to oral administration, or shortened to an evidence-based duration. Stewardship is a repeated decision process under changing evidence, not a single gate at the moment of prescribing ([5] [6]).

WHO's 2015 Global Action Plan organized the response around five linked objectives: improve awareness, strengthen surveillance and research, reduce infection through sanitation and prevention, optimize antimicrobial use in humans and animals, and build the economic case for sustainable investment in medicines, diagnostics, vaccines, and other interventions. This architecture corrected a common category error. New antibiotics are necessary, but innovation alone cannot compensate for uncontrolled infection, poor prescribing, weak laboratories, or environmental release; conversely, conservation without access leaves patients undertreated. Effective treatment must be both available and used appropriately ([4]).

### One Health widened the unit of control

Resistance genes and resistant organisms move through systems that do not respect professional boundaries. Human and veterinary antimicrobial use can select resistant populations. Manure, sewage, healthcare waste, pharmaceutical manufacturing effluent, soil, water, food production, wildlife, and travel can connect reservoirs and transmission routes. UNEP identifies pharmaceuticals and other chemicals, agriculture and food, healthcare, and poorly managed municipal waste and sanitation as important environmental value chains. WOAH likewise treats animal disease prevention, veterinary oversight, antimicrobial-use surveillance, responsible use, and safe disposal as parts of the response ([12] [13]).

One Health does not imply that every resistant human infection originated in agriculture or that every environmental detection establishes a clinical pathway. Direction, magnitude, and causality must be measured for particular organisms, genes, exposures, and settings. Its durable contribution is a systems boundary: control fails if human hospitals optimize prescribing while farms, communities, supply chains, or waste systems continue to amplify and exchange resistance. The author's synthesis is that One Health is best used as a coordination and measurement framework, not as a slogan that substitutes for source attribution ([12] [13]).

## Core Concepts

### Resistance arises through variation, selection, and transmission

Bacterial populations can resist antibiotics through intrinsic properties or acquired change. Intrinsic resistance is built into the organism's normal structure or physiology, such as low outer-membrane permeability to a drug. Acquired resistance can arise from mutation in the bacterium's chromosome or from acquisition of resistance determinants through horizontal gene transfer. Transformation takes up DNA from the environment, transduction moves DNA through bacteriophages, and conjugation transfers mobile genetic elements through cell contact. Plasmids, transposons, and integrons can package multiple determinants, allowing one selection pressure to maintain resistance to several drug classes ([13] [14]).

The major biochemical mechanisms are also distinct. Bacteria can destroy or modify a drug, as beta-lactamases do to beta-lactam antibiotics. They can alter or protect the drug target, reduce entry by changing permeability or porins, increase active efflux, or bypass the inhibited pathway. Several mechanisms can coexist in one strain, increasing the resistance level or broadening the drugs affected. The genotype-phenotype relationship is not always one-to-one: expression, copy number, regulatory change, drug concentration, inoculum, biofilm state, and host site can influence whether a resistance determinant produces clinically relevant non-susceptibility ([14]).

Selection pressure changes the relative fitness of susceptible and resistant variants. When an antibiotic concentration suppresses susceptible competitors, a resistant variant can expand even if resistance carried a fitness cost before treatment. Selection occurs in pathogens and in commensal organisms exposed during therapy; the latter can become reservoirs of mobile resistance genes. Excessive spectrum, unnecessary treatment, subtherapeutic exposure, and longer-than-needed duration can enlarge the selective opportunity. The exact relationship is not linear or immediate, however, because fitness costs, compensatory evolution, co-selection, transmission, and infection-control practices also determine what happens after use changes ([8] [14]).

Transmission is analytically separate from emergence. A resistant strain can arise rarely but spread efficiently through hands, equipment, food, water, travel, or close contact. Conversely, frequent selection may remain localized if transmission is interrupted. This separation explains why stewardship and infection prevention are complements. Stewardship reduces the ecological opportunity for resistant organisms; hand hygiene, isolation where indicated, environmental cleaning, vaccination, sanitation, and source control reduce the number of infections and opportunities for spread. A program that changes prescribing but ignores transmission is controlling only one term in the process ([4] [8]).

### Stewardship is a closed-loop clinical system

IDSA and SHEA define antibiotic stewardship as coordinated interventions that improve and measure appropriate use by promoting an optimal regimen, including drug choice, dose, duration, and route. A practical closed loop begins with a syndrome and a severity assessment. The prescriber decides whether bacterial infection is plausible, which specimens can still be obtained before therapy, how urgently treatment must begin, which organisms and resistance patterns are credible, and whether source control is needed. Empirical therapy should be broad enough for immediate risk but no broader than justified by the patient's condition, exposures, allergies, organ function, prior cultures, recent antibiotics, and local antibiogram ([6]).

The next step is explicit reassessment. Stewardship programs often formalize an antibiotic time-out after enough clinical and laboratory information has accumulated. The review asks: Is infection still the best explanation? Did cultures or rapid tests identify an organism or resistance marker? Has the patient stabilized? Can coverage be narrowed? Can intravenous treatment become oral? Is the dose correct for the site, organism, pharmacokinetics, and organ function? What is the shortest duration supported for this syndrome, response, and source control? Documenting indication, planned duration, and review date turns these questions into an auditable process rather than an informal hope ([5] [6]).

Two hospital interventions have the strongest implementation support. Preauthorization requires approval before selected antibiotics are started, helping protect agents with high toxicity, cost, ecological impact, or specialized indications. Prospective audit and feedback reviews therapy after prescribing and gives patient-specific recommendations, preserving initial clinician autonomy while using new information. CDC and IDSA/SHEA identify these approaches as core components, usually strengthened by facility-specific guidelines, clinical pathways, pharmacy expertise, infectious-disease input, and leadership commitment. Education alone can raise awareness, but information without workflow, feedback, and accountability is usually insufficient ([5] [6]).

Measurement closes the loop. Useful process measures include antibiotic days of therapy, standardized consumption, proportion of treatment that follows local guidance, culture collection before therapy, time to active treatment, de-escalation, intravenous-to-oral conversion, and duration by syndrome. Outcome measures include mortality, readmission, treatment failure, adverse drug events, C. difficile infection, length of stay, and selected resistance patterns. Balancing measures are essential: a fall in antibiotic use is not success if delayed therapy, undertreatment, or mortality rises. Resistance rates should be interpreted over suitable time horizons and with infection-control and case-mix data because they are affected by importation, outbreaks, testing, and transmission as well as prescribing ([5] [6] [7]).

### Diagnostics reduce uncertainty only when connected to action

Conventional cultures and antimicrobial susceptibility testing remain the foundation of targeted treatment, but their value depends on specimen quality, timing, laboratory capacity, and clinical interpretation. A contaminated specimen can provoke unnecessary therapy; failure to sample before antibiotics can erase the evidence needed to narrow treatment. Local antibiograms aggregate susceptibility results to guide empirical choices, but hospital-wide averages may conceal important differences among intensive-care units, wards, specimen types, and patient groups. Diagnostic stewardship therefore includes deciding when to test, how to collect and transport samples, how laboratories report results, and how clinicians act on them ([5] [6]).

Rapid molecular or phenotypic tests can identify organisms or resistance markers earlier than conventional workflows. Speed alone is not the endpoint. A result changes outcomes only if it reaches someone able to interpret it and promptly change therapy. A 2024 network meta-analysis of bloodstream-infection studies found that rapid diagnostic testing combined with an antimicrobial stewardship program was associated with lower mortality and faster optimal therapy than conventional blood culture alone, whereas rapid testing without stewardship did not show the same survival benefit. The interaction illustrates a general rule: information and implementation are joint inputs ([9]).

Biomarkers can support, but not replace, clinical judgment. In acute respiratory infections, an individual-patient-data meta-analysis found that procalcitonin-guided algorithms reduced antibiotic exposure and antibiotic-related adverse effects without worsening treatment failure, with lower measured mortality in the pooled trials. That evidence was specific to protocolized respiratory-infection settings and did not establish that one biomarker safely governs every infection, immunocompromised population, or source. Stewardship uses a test within its validated decision context rather than converting a probability signal into a universal rule ([10]).

### AWaRe translates stewardship into a portfolio discipline

WHO's Access, Watch, and Reserve classification groups antibiotics partly by their resistance-selection potential and stewardship priority. Access antibiotics are preferred first- or second-choice options for many common infections and should be widely available. Watch antibiotics have higher resistance potential and require more selective indications and monitoring. Reserve antibiotics are last-resort options for confirmed or strongly suspected multidrug-resistant infections and require protection without being made inaccessible to patients who need them. The accompanying AWaRe book provides syndrome-specific guidance on agent, dose, route, and duration for common infections ([11]).

AWaRe is not a patient-level substitute for culture results, allergies, organ function, local resistance, or severity. It is a population-level portfolio tool that makes national and institutional consumption patterns visible. The 2024 UN political declaration set a target for at least 70 percent of global human antibiotic consumption to consist of Access antibiotics by 2030. A high Access share can indicate more favorable use, but it cannot by itself prove correct diagnosis, dose, or duration; a facility could meet the ratio while still prescribing too often. The author's synthesis is that AWaRe should be paired with syndrome-level appropriateness and patient outcomes ([2] [11] [15]).

### Access and excess are simultaneous failures

Stewardship is often described as reducing use, but global practice contains both overuse and lack of access. Some patients receive antibiotics for viral illness, colonization, contaminated cultures, or durations unsupported by evidence. Others cannot obtain timely diagnosis or an effective first-line or Reserve agent, encounter substandard products, or receive treatment without laboratory support because the health system offers no reliable alternative. WHO reports that some lower-income settings record little or no Reserve antibiotic use despite clinical need, indicating access barriers rather than absence of resistant infection ([1] [2]).

This dual failure changes program design. Restriction without reliable supply, diagnostics, trained staff, referral, and financial protection can convert stewardship into rationing. Unlimited empirical use without those same capacities can accelerate resistance and still miss the responsible pathogen. Equitable stewardship therefore aims for appropriate access: the right effective antibiotic should be available promptly when expected benefit is high, while low-value exposure should be prevented. That objective aligns patient safety with conservation rather than placing them in opposition ([1] [2] [4]).

### One Health control acts at several prevention layers

In human healthcare, vaccination, water and sanitation, hand hygiene, device management, environmental cleaning, ventilation where relevant, and rapid source control reduce the incidence of infection and therefore the need for antibiotics. In animal systems, vaccination, husbandry, biosecurity, veterinary diagnosis, and responsible prescribing can reduce disease and antimicrobial demand. In agriculture, healthcare, manufacturing, and municipalities, waste controls can reduce the release of active compounds, resistant organisms, and resistance genes into connected environments. Integrated surveillance can then examine use and resistance across sectors rather than assuming that one sector's data explain the whole system ([4] [12] [13]).

The layers operate on different causal paths. Infection prevention reduces exposure to pathogens. Stewardship changes selection pressure during treatment. Waste control changes environmental exposure. Surveillance detects trends and generates hypotheses. Research and regulation test and act on those hypotheses. New medicines restore options after prevention fails. No layer is individually sufficient, and performance in one should not excuse failure in another. This layered model is the author's synthesis of the WHO Global Action Plan, UNEP environmental framework, and WOAH One Health approach ([4] [12] [13]).

## Evidence

### Burden estimates show a large problem but depend on modeled counterfactuals

The GBD 2021 analysis assembled microbiology, hospital, pharmaceutical, mortality, insurance-claims, and surveillance data to estimate bacterial AMR burden across 204 countries and territories from 1990 through 2021 and to forecast through 2050. It estimated 4.71 million deaths associated with bacterial AMR in 2021, including 1.14 million attributable deaths. It also found large changes by age: deaths among children younger than five declined substantially over the study period, while deaths among adults aged 70 and older increased. Carbapenem resistance among Gram-negative bacteria showed a particularly large increase in burden ([3]).

The counterfactual design is useful but not a direct global count. Many locations lack representative cultures, susceptibility testing, complete cause-of-death registration, or linkage between microbiology and outcomes. Models must bridge those gaps and estimate what would have occurred with a susceptible infection or no infection. The study reports uncertainty intervals, but unmeasured bias remains possible where data are sparse. Its forecasts are conditional scenarios, not a prophecy. They are most useful for comparing plausible intervention portfolios and identifying high-burden pathogen-drug combinations, not for claiming exact future death counts ([3]).

WHO's 2025 GLASS report supplies a more operational surveillance picture. More than 23 million confirmed infections from 104 countries supported its estimates, including the finding that roughly one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections in 2023 was resistant. Participation had increased four-fold since 2016, yet completeness remained 53.8 percent and regional gaps persisted. The report also found that countries with lower surveillance coverage and weaker health systems reported higher median resistance, a pattern compatible with both heavier disease burden and systemic limitations in case detection and treatment ([2]).

These surveillance data contain selection effects. Patients who receive cultures can be sicker, previously treated, hospitalized, or more likely to have resistant infection than patients treated empirically without testing. Laboratories differ in coverage and quality, and countries differ in whether community and hospital infections are represented. Trend interpretation therefore requires consistent sampling, denominators, quality assurance, and context. The strongest conclusion is not that every untreated infection has the reported resistance prevalence, but that resistance is widespread and that the places with the weakest measurement and treatment capacity may face the greatest risk ([2]).

### Prescribing interventions improve process and can reduce exposure safely

The 2017 Cochrane review by Davey and colleagues evaluated 221 studies, including 58 randomized trials and 163 non-randomized studies, of interventions to improve hospital antibiotic prescribing. In 29 randomized trials involving 23,394 participants, interventions increased compliance with antibiotic policy by 15 percentage points, from 43 percent to 58 percent. Fourteen trials found a 1.95-day reduction in treatment duration from an 11-day baseline. Across 28 randomized trials with 15,827 participants, mortality was 11 percent in both intervention and control groups, while 15 trials suggested a 1.12-day reduction in hospital stay ([7]).

The review provides high-certainty evidence that enablement and restriction can improve prescribing and shorten therapy without a detected mortality penalty, but it also exposes incomplete implementation. A post-intervention compliance rate of 58 percent leaves substantial room for improvement. Many studies came from North America and Europe, and 62 percent of randomized trials were at high risk of bias, although sensitivity analyses restricted to lower-risk studies did not materially reverse the central conclusions. Microbial outcomes varied too much to infer a simple conversion from less use to a fixed resistance reduction ([7]).

A separate systematic review and meta-analysis by Baur and colleagues examined 32 hospital studies, 9,056,241 patient-days, and 159 incidence estimates. Stewardship programs were associated with lower incidence of infection or colonization by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Gram-negative bacteria, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and C. difficile. Estimated reductions were 51 percent, 48 percent, 37 percent, and 32 percent, respectively. Programs combined with infection-control measures performed better than stewardship alone, especially when hand-hygiene interventions were included ([8]).

Those estimates should not be generalized as a universal effect size. The review found significant heterogeneity and no detected effect for several other resistance outcomes, including vancomycin-resistant enterococci and some quinolone- or aminoglycoside-resistant Gram-negative organisms. Most included evidence was not randomized at the level needed to isolate stewardship from contemporaneous infection-control, outbreak, laboratory, or case-mix changes. The reliable inference is directional and conditional: stewardship can reduce important resistant infections, and integration with infection prevention is stronger than prescription control in isolation ([8]).

### Diagnostics work best as part of a response pathway

Peri and colleagues' 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis evaluated 88 papers and 25,682 bloodstream-infection encounters. Rapid diagnostic testing combined with stewardship was associated with lower mortality than conventional blood culture alone (odds ratio 0.72, 95 percent confidence interval 0.59-0.87) and lower mortality than conventional blood culture plus stewardship (odds ratio 0.78, 0.63-0.96). It also shortened time to optimal therapy by an estimated 29 hours versus conventional culture alone, 18 hours versus conventional culture plus stewardship, and 12 hours versus rapid testing alone. Rapid testing alone did not show the same survival benefit ([9]).

The network combined direct and indirect comparisons and reported appreciable statistical heterogeneity. Technologies, organisms, hospital workflows, baseline stewardship, definitions of optimal therapy, and study designs differed. The result therefore supports a service model rather than a guaranteed device effect: rapid identification is most valuable when a stewardship team or equivalent response process converts it into timely escalation, de-escalation, or discontinuation ([9]).

For acute respiratory infections, Schuetz and colleagues pooled individual data from 26 randomized trials, 6,708 patients, and 12 countries. Procalcitonin-guided care reduced mean antibiotic exposure by 2.4 days, from 8.1 to 5.7 days, and reduced antibiotic-related adverse effects from 22 percent to 16 percent. Thirty-day mortality was 8.6 percent in the procalcitonin-guided group and 10.0 percent in controls, while treatment failure did not differ significantly. The evidence supports protocolized use for the studied respiratory syndromes, but the trial population and algorithm boundaries matter; extrapolation to unrelated infection sites or severely immunocompromised populations requires separate evidence ([10]).

### The One Health evidence justifies coordinated controls, not simplistic attribution

UNEP's review of environmental AMR identifies healthcare, agriculture and food, pharmaceutical and chemical production, sewage, sanitation, and municipal waste as pathways through which antimicrobials, resistant organisms, and resistance determinants can enter and circulate in the environment. WOAH documents that resistant organisms and genes can move among animals, people, and shared environments and recommends disease prevention, veterinary oversight, responsible use, surveillance, and environmental safeguards. Together these sources support multisectoral risk reduction ([12] [13]).

They do not quantify one universal fraction of human AMR caused by animals or the environment. Pathways vary by pathogen, gene, geography, sanitation, food system, occupation, and healthcare exposure. Detection of a shared gene can reflect common ancestry, bidirectional transfer, or a third reservoir. Strong attribution requires genomic, epidemiological, exposure, and temporal evidence. The evidence supports acting on preventable release and transmission while continuing source-specific measurement; uncertainty about exact fractions is not evidence that the pathways are harmless ([12] [13]).

## Implications

### For clinicians: treat urgency first, then reduce uncertainty deliberately

The bedside implication is a two-speed rule. When a patient has life-threatening suspected bacterial infection, obtain appropriate cultures promptly and start therapy active against the credible pathogens; stewardship should improve that initial choice rather than delay it. When immediate danger is lower, diagnostic evaluation can precede treatment when safe. In both cases, the prescription should carry an indication, expected review point, and provisional duration so that new evidence triggers action instead of passive continuation ([5] [6]).

Reassessment should be an explicit clinical task. A negative culture is not automatically proof that no infection exists, especially after prior antibiotics or with poorly sampled sites, but it should reopen the diagnosis. A positive culture is not automatically proof of invasive disease because colonization and contamination are common. The clinician must integrate specimen quality, syndrome, source, severity, imaging, biomarkers, and response. This is why a rapid diagnostic result without stewardship support can fail: information has value only when it changes a decision correctly ([6] [9]).

Narrowing therapy is not undertreatment when the identified organism remains covered, the dose reaches the infected site, and source control is adequate. Shorter treatment is not premature when randomized evidence supports the duration and the patient has responded. Conversely, conserving a broad or Reserve agent is not justified if delay leaves a high-risk patient on inactive therapy. Appropriate escalation and appropriate de-escalation are both stewardship outcomes; a program judged only by lower consumption can reward unsafe behavior ([5] [6]).

### For hospitals and health systems: build infrastructure, not campaigns

A durable program needs accountable leadership, protected pharmacist and physician time, microbiology support, information systems, facility-specific guidance, audit and feedback or preauthorization, and routine reporting. A lecture week or poster campaign cannot replace these functions. CDC's core elements emphasize leadership commitment, accountability, pharmacy expertise, action, tracking, reporting, and education because each closes a different implementation failure. The worst operational outcome is nominal compliance: a hospital reports a committee but lacks personnel or authority to review therapy ([5]).

Local treatment guidance should combine syndrome evidence with current local susceptibility data and should identify when immediate broad coverage is warranted. Electronic order sets can require indication and duration, surface renal dosing, prompt culture collection, schedule time-outs, and route rapid results. Yet automation can also create alert fatigue or rigid protocols that ignore clinical complexity. Programs should measure recommendation acceptance, delays, exceptions, adverse outcomes, and subgroup performance rather than count alerts as success ([5] [6]).

Hospital metrics should form a causal chain. Measure antibiotic exposure, appropriateness, and diagnostic turnaround; then assess adverse drug events, C. difficile, length of stay, readmission, mortality, and selected resistance outcomes. Stratify by syndrome, service, acuity, and agent so that a hospital-wide average does not hide intensive-care or specialty problems. Compare rates over enough time to separate signal from outbreaks and changes in culturing. The author's synthesis is that the safest dashboard pairs conservation measures with timely-active-treatment and patient-harm measures, preventing the optimization of one side of the trade-off ([5] [7] [8]).

### For public health: prevent infections before optimizing prescriptions

Vaccination, safe water, sanitation, hand hygiene, environmental cleaning, device safety, maternal and neonatal infection prevention, and outbreak control reduce antibiotic demand without forcing a treatment trade-off. They also protect people for whom antibiotics are least accessible. The GBD analysis estimated that improvements in infection prevention, healthcare quality, and sepsis management prevented millions of AMR-associated deaths over past decades, while the Baur meta-analysis found stronger effects when stewardship and infection control were combined ([3] [8]).

Surveillance must connect laboratory resistance, antibiotic use, patient outcomes, and population denominators. GLASS expansion shows that participation can grow, but incomplete and selectively sampled data limit interpretation. Investments in specimen transport, laboratory quality assurance, susceptibility testing, data linkage, and trained epidemiology are interventions, not administrative overhead. Public reporting should display uncertainty and coverage, because a low measured resistance rate from sparse testing can be less reassuring than a higher rate from representative surveillance ([2]).

National antibiotic policy can use AWaRe to monitor the consumption portfolio while preserving clinical flexibility. The 70 percent Access target creates a shared direction, but national programs should also track total use, Watch and Reserve indications, availability, stock-outs, and outcomes. A country that reduces Watch use by making all antibiotics unavailable has not achieved stewardship. A country that expands access without prescription standards, diagnostics, or quality control may increase both benefit and selection. Appropriate access is the governing outcome ([2] [11] [15]).

### For One Health policy: control preventable selection and release at each source

Animal health policy should reduce disease through vaccination, biosecurity, nutrition, housing, and veterinary care; require responsible, evidence-based antimicrobial use; and monitor both use and resistance. Environmental policy should address manufacturing discharge, healthcare and farm waste, sewage treatment, and disposal of unused medicines. Food-system policy should focus on preventable infection and transmission rather than treating antimicrobial use as the sole variable. Integrated surveillance should be designed around explicit questions, such as whether the same resistant lineage or mobile element is moving between reservoirs ([12] [13]).

Coordination does not erase sector accountability. A hospital cannot defer infection control because agricultural use exists, and an animal-production system cannot defer veterinary stewardship because human prescribing is substantial. Each sector should control the selection and release it can prevent, share comparable data, and investigate cross-sector pathways with methods capable of direction and source attribution. The author's synthesis is a responsibility rule: uncertainty about the relative contribution of sectors should determine research design, not justify inaction ([12] [13]).

### For equity and financing: protect access while reducing low-value use

AMR control can worsen inequity if it is implemented as simple restriction. Patients in settings without cultures, transport, trained clinicians, or affordable alternatives may depend on empirical antibiotics because the diagnostic pathway does not exist. Prior authorization without round-the-clock expertise can delay treatment. Reserve protection without procurement can conceal stock failure. Programs should therefore audit time to effective therapy, affordability, availability, and geographic access alongside consumption ([1] [2]).

Financing should recognize antibiotics as both products and infrastructure. Revenue based only on sales volume rewards more use, while extreme restriction can make development commercially unattractive. The WHO Global Action Plan calls for investment in medicines, diagnostics, vaccines, surveillance, and prevention because preservation depends on the whole system. This topic does not evaluate particular pharmaceutical incentive designs; its health-system implication is narrower: innovation policy should reward useful availability without requiring high volume, and procurement should pair access contracts with stewardship and surveillance ([4]).

### For researchers: ask which link in the causal chain changed

Future evaluation should distinguish intervention adoption from biological effect. Did clinicians receive a recommendation? Did they act? Did exposure fall or active treatment begin sooner? Did adverse events decline? Did transmission change? Did resistance prevalence change after accounting for testing and case mix? Cluster-randomized, interrupted-time-series, and linked microbiology-prescribing-outcome designs can answer different parts of this chain. Genomic epidemiology can help separate importation from local transmission, but sequence similarity alone does not prove direction ([2] [7] [9]).

Diagnostics should be evaluated as care pathways, not as isolated assays. The Peri meta-analysis suggests that rapid tests generate their largest value when paired with stewardship response. Trials should therefore report laboratory turnaround, communication delay, time to active and optimal therapy, de-escalation, adverse events, mortality, and cost. They should state who received and interpreted the result. This design prevents a technically fast test from appearing clinically effective when the organization cannot act on it ([9]).

### For patients and the public: stewardship is appropriate treatment, not denial

Patients can support stewardship by seeking care for severe or persistent illness, understanding that antibiotics do not treat viral infections, taking prescribed therapy exactly as directed, not sharing or saving antibiotics, and returning for reassessment when advised. Clinicians and public agencies should avoid slogans that make patients solely responsible for a system problem. Prescribing incentives, appointment time, diagnostic access, over-the-counter sales, medicine quality, agriculture, sanitation, and waste controls all shape use and resistance ([1] [4] [13]).

The most useful public mental model is option preservation. An antibiotic is valuable because it can work when a dangerous bacterial infection occurs. Every avoidable exposure creates some selection risk, but every missed necessary dose creates immediate clinical risk. Stewardship preserves the option by matching treatment to evidence and revising it as evidence improves. The author's assessment is that this framing avoids both therapeutic nihilism and careless use: use effective antibiotics promptly when benefit is credible, then stop paying biological cost once the benefit is no longer supported.
