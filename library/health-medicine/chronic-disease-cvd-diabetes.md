# Chronic Disease Management -- Why Cardiovascular Disease and Diabetes Require Systemic Prevention Over Reactive Treatment

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes are overlapping but distinct chronic-disease burdens: CVD caused an estimated 19.8 million deaths in 2022, while the International Diabetes Federation estimated that 589 million adults aged 20-79 were living with diabetes in 2024 [1][2]. Their intersection matters because hyperglycaemia, hypertension, abnormal lipids, obesity, smoking, kidney disease, and social barriers often accumulate in the same person, while diabetes also damages small vessels, nerves, kidneys, and eyes [16][19]. The central claim is not that every case is preventable, but that earlier risk detection, coordinated treatment of multiple causal pathways, and reliable long-term follow-up prevent more serious outcomes than a system organized mainly around rescue after heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, or advanced diabetes complications [1][12][16][17].

## Background

The scale of the problem must be stated with compatible definitions rather than by adding unlike estimates. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that CVD caused 19.8 million deaths in 2022, about 32 percent of all global deaths, and that heart attack and stroke accounted for 85 percent of those CVD deaths [1]. The IDF Diabetes Atlas estimates that 589 million adults aged 20-79 had diagnosed or undiagnosed diabetes in 2024, that 3.4 million adults in that age range died because of diabetes or its complications, and that diabetes-related health expenditure exceeded USD 1 trillion [2]. A different WHO series estimates 830 million people aged 18 years or older living with diabetes in 2022 [19]. These estimates use different ages, data sources, definitions, and models; they describe the same broad crisis but must not be summed or treated as interchangeable [2][19].

The preventive model emerged from longitudinal observation. When the Framingham Heart Study began in 1948, its aim was to follow people without overt CVD and identify characteristics associated with later disease. The original cohort included 5,209 men and women, and subsequent generations extended the study. Framingham helped establish high blood pressure and high blood cholesterol as major CVD risk factors and contributed to the modern practice of measuring smoking, physical activity, weight, blood pressure, and lipids before symptoms appear [3]. This was a conceptual change: clinicians could estimate future risk and intervene on measurable exposures rather than regard heart attack or stroke as an abrupt event without a long antecedent period.

International evidence then tested whether the same broad risk structure appeared outside one US community. INTERHEART was a standardized case-control study of 15,152 first acute myocardial infarction cases and 14,820 controls in 52 countries. It associated smoking, an adverse apolipoprotein ratio, hypertension, diabetes, abdominal obesity, psychosocial factors, diet, physical activity, and alcohol exposure with most of the population-attributable risk of first myocardial infarction in the sampled populations [6]. The study did not prove that a fixed percentage of every individual's risk is modifiable, and its observational design cannot replace randomized intervention trials. It did show that prevention could focus on a relatively small group of recurring risk domains across regions, while the prevalence and practical importance of each domain varied [6].

Diabetes research developed along a parallel path. Type 2 diabetes was once framed mainly as a glucose disorder treated after hyperglycaemia became established. The Diabetes Prevention Program later showed that progression from high-risk dysglycaemia to diagnosed diabetes could be delayed, while UKPDS showed that better glycaemic control in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes reduced microvascular endpoints [7][8]. Those trials also clarified an important boundary: preventing diabetes onset, preventing retinopathy or kidney disease after diagnosis, and preventing myocardial infarction are different outcomes. A therapy can improve one without proving all three [7][8].

The treatment model broadened again when trials began to test blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, and multiple risk factors against clinical outcomes. Statin meta-analysis linked the magnitude of LDL reduction to fewer major vascular events, and intensive blood-pressure treatment reduced major cardiovascular outcomes in a high-risk population without diabetes, although with additional treatment-related adverse events [10][11]. Steno-2 tested a coordinated package in people with type 2 diabetes and microalbuminuria and found durable reductions in cardiovascular events and cardiovascular death [12]. These results moved the field away from a single-number model in which HbA1c alone represented successful chronic-disease management.

Cardiovascular outcome trials of glucose-lowering drugs added another distinction. Empagliflozin and liraglutide were tested on cardiovascular endpoints in people with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk, not merely on their ability to lower glucose [13][14]. The 2026 American Diabetes Association (ADA) standards consequently organize treatment around a person's atherosclerotic CVD, heart-failure, and kidney risks as well as glycaemia, and recommend therapies with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit for relevant high-risk groups [16]. Tirzepatide should not be grouped simply with GLP-1 receptor agonists: the ADA describes it as a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist [16].

The remaining gap is delivery. WHO reports that more than three quarters of CVD deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, while the IDF estimates that 42.8 percent of adults aged 20-79 with diabetes in 2024 were undiagnosed [1][2]. The ADA identifies food insecurity, housing insecurity, financial barriers, healthcare access, neighborhood conditions, and social support as factors that should inform treatment decisions [17]. The history of chronic-disease management is therefore not a simple progression from ignorance to effective drugs. It is a progression from identifying risk, to proving interventions, to recognizing that benefit depends on finding risk early, matching treatment to the relevant outcome, and sustaining care in the conditions where people live.

## Core Concepts

### CVD and type 2 diabetes overlap without becoming one disease

Type 2 diabetes is heterogeneous, but insulin resistance is a central feature. In insulin-resistant tissues, insulin becomes less effective at lowering glucose, while some actions related to lipid synthesis and lipoprotein secretion can remain active. Compensatory hyperinsulinaemia may maintain glucose for a time, but beta-cell dysfunction and inadequate insulin secretion eventually permit persistent hyperglycaemia in susceptible people [4]. This is more precise than describing beta cells as simply becoming "exhausted": current evidence supports interacting defects in insulin action, secretion, tissue metabolism, genetics, and environment rather than a single irreversible clock [4].

Atherosclerosis also has multiple stages rather than a simple blocked-pipe mechanism. Endothelial dysfunction and disturbed blood flow favor entry and retention of LDL-containing particles in the arterial wall. Modified lipoproteins, endothelial activation, monocyte recruitment, foam-cell formation, smooth-muscle responses, inflammation, necrotic-core development, and fibrous-cap disruption contribute to plaque growth and complications [5]. Hypertension increases mechanical stress, smoking injures vascular biology, and diabetes can intensify endothelial, inflammatory, and lipoprotein abnormalities. Yet atherosclerosis also occurs without diabetes, and diabetes causes important non-atherosclerotic outcomes. Management must therefore address shared pathways without assuming that glucose normalization alone removes cardiovascular risk [5][16].

Diabetes produces both microvascular and macrovascular injury. Retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy are strongly related to chronic glycaemic exposure, whereas myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, and heart failure reflect a broader mixture of blood pressure, lipids, smoking, kidney function, adiposity, age, and metabolic disease [8][16][19]. This difference explains why UKPDS could show a clear reduction in microvascular endpoints from more intensive glycaemic control without demonstrating a statistically significant macrovascular reduction during its randomized treatment period [8]. It also explains why contemporary care combines glycaemic treatment with lipid, blood-pressure, kidney, weight, and smoking interventions [16].

### Prevention has several levels and several denominators

Primordial prevention reduces the emergence of risk factors through conditions that make nutritious food, physical activity, clean air, and tobacco avoidance feasible. Primary prevention treats risk before a first cardiovascular event or before diabetes develops. Secondary prevention reduces recurrence or progression after established CVD or diagnosed diabetes complications. Tertiary care limits disability after organ damage has occurred. The author's synthesis is that calling all four activities "prevention" without naming the population and endpoint invites false comparisons: a diabetes-prevention trial in people with impaired glucose regulation does not answer the same question as a cardiovascular-outcome trial in people with established CVD [7][13][14].

The denominator controls interpretation. WHO's CVD estimate covers deaths from a broad disease family, whereas IDF's diabetes mortality model attributes deaths to diabetes and its complications in adults aged 20-79 [1][2]. Some people are represented in both disease pathways because diabetes can contribute to cardiovascular death. Adding the two mortality estimates would double-count overlapping deaths and mix different years and methods. The same rule applies to trials: event rates among high-risk patients with established CVD cannot be applied directly to low-risk adults, and relative risk reductions do not reveal absolute benefit without baseline risk and follow-up time [1][2][13][14].

Risk should therefore be treated as a profile, not a label. Two people with the same HbA1c can have different absolute cardiovascular risk because of age, smoking, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, kidney disease, prior events, and treatment history. Conversely, a person without diagnosed diabetes can have substantial cardiometabolic risk through obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, or impaired glucose regulation. The ADA's current framework reflects this by assessing atherosclerotic CVD, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease alongside glycaemia [16].

### Risk factors interact, but intervention effects remain specific

INTERHEART found that nine measured, potentially modifiable domains accounted for most population-attributable risk of first myocardial infarction in its case-control data [6]. Population-attributable risk is not the probability that one person will avoid an event if all nine domains change. It depends on exposure prevalence, estimated association, interactions, measurement, and assumptions about causality. The result supports broad prevention priorities, but treatment decisions still require randomized evidence, individual baseline risk, and attention to harms [6][10][11].

Blood pressure and LDL cholesterol are not merely markers. The CTT Collaboration analyzed about 170,000 participants in 26 randomized statin trials and found a 22 percent proportional reduction in major vascular events per 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol across the trials [10]. SPRINT randomized high-risk adults without diabetes to systolic blood-pressure targets below 120 or 140 mmHg and found fewer major cardiovascular events and deaths with the lower target, but more intervention-related serious adverse events and more acute kidney injury or failure in the intensive group [11]. These findings establish modifiable causal pathways while also showing why goals must account for population, measurement method, comorbidity, treatment burden, and adverse effects [10][11][16].

Glycaemia has an equally important but outcome-specific role. UKPDS 33 found that an intensive policy using sulfonylurea or insulin reduced microvascular endpoints by 25 percent relative to conventional management, while all-cause mortality and macrovascular endpoints were not significantly reduced during the trial [8]. UKPDS 34 found favorable aggregate and mortality outcomes for metformin versus a mainly diet-based conventional policy in a randomized subset of overweight people with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, but it also reported a concerning supplementary comparison when metformin was added early to sulfonylurea therapy [9]. The correct lesson is not that one old trial settled all drug sequencing. It is that glucose control matters, metformin has an important evidence base, and claims must preserve the trial population, comparator, endpoints, and uncertainty [8][9].

### Management is a portfolio of interventions

At the population level, prevention includes tobacco control, healthier food environments, opportunities for physical activity, air-quality improvement, and primary-care access. At the clinical level, it includes risk assessment, smoking cessation support, nutrition and activity counseling, treatment of hypertension and abnormal lipids, glycaemic management, kidney surveillance, and therapies selected for proven organ outcomes [1][16][18]. No single element substitutes for the rest. The author's synthesis is that chronic-disease management resembles a portfolio: independent and partially overlapping interventions reduce different components of risk, while concentration in one metric leaves other exposures unhedged.

Lifestyle intervention has different purposes before and after diabetes develops. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, an intensive program targeting at least 7 percent weight loss and at least 150 minutes of weekly physical activity reduced progression to diabetes in a selected high-risk population [7]. In Look AHEAD, an intensive lifestyle program achieved sustained weight differences and other benefits in adults with established type 2 diabetes but did not reduce its composite cardiovascular endpoint [15]. These results are compatible because the trials tested different populations, comparators, and outcomes. Lifestyle support remains part of care, but it should not be sold as proof of cardiovascular-event reduction when the relevant trial did not show that endpoint [7][15].

Drug choice now incorporates organ protection. The ADA states that in people with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic CVD, multiple CVD risk factors, or chronic kidney disease, an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist with demonstrated cardiovascular benefit can be used to reduce major cardiovascular events or heart-failure risk [16]. This class-level wording still requires drug-specific evidence: not every drug in a class has shown every outcome, and trial results may apply mainly to high-risk populations. Treatment also retains conventional preventive therapies for blood pressure and lipids; newer glucose-lowering drugs do not replace them [10][13][14][16].

### Integrated care is an operating system, not a visit

Chronic disease changes slowly, treatment effects accumulate, and failures often occur between visits. A functioning care system must identify an eligible population, measure risk factors consistently, initiate and intensify therapy, monitor benefit and harm, support adherence, track missed follow-up, and coordinate eye, kidney, foot, and cardiovascular care. The ADA recommends person-centered team care, integrated long-term treatment of diabetes and comorbidities, and reliable metrics for risk factors and complications [17]. WHO PEN similarly treats integrated primary care as essential in resource-limited settings, where separate disease programs can duplicate scarce staff and infrastructure [18].

Continuity is clinically active. A prescription has no effect if cost, side effects, supply interruptions, unstable housing, language barriers, food insecurity, or fragmented records prevent its use. The ADA recommends assessing these social determinants and using care coordinators, community health workers, and interprofessional teams when appropriate [17]. This corrects a common error in chronic-disease narratives: unequal outcomes cannot be reduced to unequal motivation. Individual behavior matters, but the feasible set of behaviors is shaped by material conditions, care access, and treatment burden [17].

Measurement should serve decisions rather than scorekeeping. HbA1c, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, albuminuria, kidney function, weight, smoking status, medication use, and prior cardiovascular events answer different questions [16][17]. A dashboard that records these variables but does not trigger outreach, shared decisions, or treatment review is documentation without management. The author's synthesis is that the smallest useful unit of chronic care is a closed loop: measure, interpret, act, verify, and repeat.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

### Prevention before diabetes: the Diabetes Prevention Program

The Diabetes Prevention Program randomized 3,234 adults with elevated fasting and post-load glucose and overweight to placebo, metformin, or an intensive lifestyle program. The lifestyle goals included at least 7 percent weight loss and at least 150 minutes of physical activity per week. Over the trial period, lifestyle intervention reduced diabetes incidence by 58 percent and metformin by 31 percent relative to placebo; estimated three-year numbers needed to treat were 6.9 for lifestyle and 13.9 for metformin [7]. The finding establishes that type 2 diabetes can be delayed in a selected high-risk population. It does not establish that the same relative effects apply to average-risk adults or that preventing the diagnostic threshold automatically proves fewer cardiovascular events [7].

### Glucose control after diagnosis: UKPDS

UKPDS 33 randomized 3,867 people with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes after an initial diet period to conventional or intensive glucose-control policies using sulfonylurea or insulin. Over ten years, median HbA1c was 7.0 percent in the intensive group and 7.9 percent in the conventional group. The intensive policy reduced the aggregate diabetes-related endpoint by 12 percent and microvascular endpoints by 25 percent, but did not significantly reduce diabetes-related death or all-cause mortality; hypoglycaemia and weight gain were more frequent with intensive treatment [8]. This trial supports early glycaemic control for microvascular protection while rejecting the claim that lower glucose by those regimens proved broad cardiovascular protection during the randomized period [8].

UKPDS 34 studied 753 overweight participants within UKPDS who were randomized to a mainly diet-based conventional policy or intensive metformin policy for a median 10.7 years. Metformin reduced any diabetes-related endpoint by 32 percent, diabetes-related death by 42 percent, and all-cause mortality by 36 percent relative to conventional policy [9]. The supplementary sulfonylurea-plus-metformin comparison produced a conflicting mortality signal, and the trial population and historical comparator limit direct transfer to every modern patient. The evidence supports metformin's importance but not a universal rule that ignores CVD, heart failure, kidney disease, adverse effects, or other outcome-proven therapies [9][16].

### Treating causal cardiovascular pathways: LDL and blood pressure

The CTT Collaboration pooled individual data from roughly 170,000 participants in 26 randomized statin trials. Across the trials, each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol was associated with a 22 percent proportional reduction in major vascular events and a 10 percent reduction in all-cause mortality [10]. Absolute benefit still depends on baseline risk: the same proportional effect prevents more events in a person with established vascular disease than in a person at low short-term risk. The evidence supports LDL lowering as a causal preventive intervention, not merely a favorable laboratory change [10].

SPRINT randomized 9,361 adults at high cardiovascular risk but without diabetes to systolic targets below 120 or 140 mmHg. The intensive strategy reduced the primary composite event rate from 2.19 to 1.65 percent per year, with a hazard ratio of 0.75, and reduced all-cause mortality with a hazard ratio of 0.73 [11]. Serious adverse events judged possibly or definitely related to treatment occurred in 4.7 percent of the intensive group and 2.5 percent of the standard group, and some renal outcomes were more frequent [11]. SPRINT proves benefit in its measured, non-diabetic high-risk population; diabetes-specific targets require diabetes evidence and current guidelines rather than direct copying of the trial threshold [11][16].

### Coordinating multiple targets: Steno-2

Steno-2 randomized 160 people with type 2 diabetes and persistent microalbuminuria to intensive, target-driven multifactorial therapy or conventional care for a mean 7.8 years, followed by observational follow-up. At a mean total follow-up of 13.3 years, intensive therapy was associated with lower cardiovascular death, hazard ratio 0.43, and fewer cardiovascular events, hazard ratio 0.41 [12]. The report described a 29 percent absolute reduction in the composite cardiovascular endpoint, while end-stage renal disease and retinal photocoagulation were also less frequent [12]. The sample was small and high-risk, but the coherent effects across risk factors and organs provide direct support for coordinated care rather than isolated glucose management.

### Outcome-based diabetes therapies: EMPA-REG OUTCOME and LEADER

EMPA-REG OUTCOME randomized adults with type 2 diabetes and established high cardiovascular risk to empagliflozin or placebo in addition to standard care. Cardiovascular death occurred in 3.7 percent with empagliflozin and 5.9 percent with placebo, a 38 percent relative reduction; hospitalization for heart failure occurred in 2.7 versus 4.1 percent, a 35 percent relative reduction; and all-cause death occurred in 5.7 versus 8.3 percent [13]. Genital infection was more frequent with empagliflozin, and the mechanisms of the early cardiovascular benefit were not established by the trial [13]. The results apply most directly to people resembling its high-risk participants.

LEADER randomized 9,340 people with type 2 diabetes and high cardiovascular risk to liraglutide or placebo. The composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke occurred in 13.0 percent with liraglutide and 14.9 percent with placebo, hazard ratio 0.87; cardiovascular death occurred in 4.7 versus 6.0 percent, hazard ratio 0.78 [14]. Gastrointestinal events were the most common adverse events leading to discontinuation [14]. Together, EMPA-REG OUTCOME and LEADER show why modern diabetes treatment asks which clinical outcomes a drug has demonstrated in which population, not only how much it lowers HbA1c [13][14][16].

### A necessary negative result: Look AHEAD

Look AHEAD randomized 5,145 adults with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity to an intensive lifestyle intervention or diabetes support and education. The primary cardiovascular outcome occurred at 1.83 and 1.92 events per 100 person-years, respectively, hazard ratio 0.95, and the difference was not statistically significant [15]. The trial does not show that diet, activity, fitness, or weight loss are useless; it shows that this intervention did not reduce the specified cardiovascular composite under the trial conditions. Negative trials protect the evidence base from substituting improved risk markers or plausible mechanisms for demonstrated event reduction [15].

The combined evidence supports a layered conclusion. Diabetes onset can be delayed in high-risk people; glycaemic control reduces microvascular complications; LDL and blood-pressure treatment reduce cardiovascular risk; coordinated multitarget care can outperform fragmented care; and selected SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce cardiovascular or heart-failure outcomes in defined high-risk groups [7][8][10][11][12][13][14][16]. It does not support the stronger claim that all CVD or type 2 diabetes is preventable, that one biomarker captures total risk, or that one intervention has equal benefit in every population.

## Implications

### For clinical practice: manage absolute risk and organ outcomes

The first clinical task is to define the state being managed. Is the person at risk for type 2 diabetes, living with newly diagnosed diabetes, living with established atherosclerotic CVD, experiencing heart failure, or developing chronic kidney disease? The answer changes the relevant endpoint and the expected absolute benefit of treatment [7][13][14][16]. A useful assessment combines glycaemia with blood pressure, lipids, smoking, kidney function, weight-related risk, prior vascular events, medications, adverse effects, and the person's priorities. Treating HbA1c while leaving severe hypertension, smoking, or high LDL unaddressed is not comprehensive cardiometabolic care [1][16].

Treatment should match evidence to the patient rather than copy a headline percentage. SPRINT's lower blood-pressure target benefited high-risk adults without diabetes but increased some harms; CTT's proportional LDL effect yields different absolute benefit at different baseline risks; EMPA-REG OUTCOME and LEADER enrolled high-risk diabetes populations [10][11][13][14]. Shared decision-making should therefore present baseline risk, expected benefit, adverse effects, monitoring burden, cost, and uncertainty. The ADA explicitly recommends timely evidence-based decisions that incorporate prognosis, comorbidity, preferences, and informed financial considerations [17].

The practical sequence is continuous rather than episodic. Confirm measurements, identify the largest untreated risks, agree on feasible interventions, check tolerability and access, repeat measurements, and intensify or simplify when the response is inadequate. Eye, kidney, foot, and cardiovascular surveillance should be coordinated because they arise from related exposures but require different detection and treatment pathways [16][19]. The author's synthesis is that a chronic-care plan is complete only when it specifies the next measurement, the action threshold, responsibility for follow-up, and what to do if the person cannot obtain or tolerate the plan.

### For patients: prevention is cumulative, not moral judgment

The evidence supports agency without blame. Tobacco avoidance, physical activity, dietary quality, weight management where appropriate, and medication adherence can reduce risk, but genetics, age, prior disease, income, food access, housing, medication cost, and neighborhood conditions also shape outcomes [1][4][17][19]. A person who develops diabetes has not proved a failure of discipline, and a person who takes medication has not failed prevention. Drugs and behavior change act on different causal pathways and can be complementary.

Numbers should be translated into decisions. A 22 percent proportional reduction per 1 mmol/L LDL reduction does not mean that 22 of every 100 treated people avoid an event; the absolute number depends on untreated risk and time [10]. The DPP's 58 percent reduction concerned progression to diabetes in selected high-risk participants, not a 58 percent reduction in heart attacks for everyone [7]. Asking "what outcome, in which people, over what period, compared with what alternative, and with what harms?" prevents the most common misreading of chronic-disease evidence [7][10][11].

Self-management also needs a failure-tolerant design. Daily treatment can be disrupted by side effects, cost, schedule, illness, travel, depression, unstable housing, or confusing instructions. Reporting those constraints early allows dose changes, substitutions, simpler regimens, financial support, or team involvement. The ADA recommends screening for social barriers and using community health workers or other support in underserved settings [17]. The author's assessment is that adherence problems should be treated as diagnostic information about the care system and regimen, not as a character verdict.

### For health systems: build a closed-loop chronic-care service

A health system should know its eligible population and where each person is in the pathway. Minimum operational measures include validated blood-pressure readings, glycaemic status, LDL cholesterol, smoking status, kidney function and albuminuria where indicated, prior CVD or heart failure, use of evidence-based therapies, adverse effects, and overdue follow-up [16][17]. Metrics should be stratified by insurance, race and ethnicity, language, disability, geography, and social determinants to reveal unequal reach or outcomes rather than hide them in averages [17].

Team design follows the workload. Primary-care clinicians, nurses, pharmacists, diabetes care and education specialists, dietitians, social workers, community health workers, and relevant specialists can divide medication titration, education, monitoring, navigation, and complication care [17]. The evidence does not require every setting to reproduce one staffing model. It requires explicit ownership of each task and reliable handoffs. A referral without confirmation of completion leaves the loop open.

Payment and procurement affect clinical effectiveness. A guideline-recommended drug that is unaffordable, unavailable, or intermittently supplied cannot deliver its trial effect. The ADA includes financial considerations in shared decisions and recommends system responses to social and structural barriers [17]. WHO PEN packages essential CVD and diabetes interventions for integrated primary care, particularly where resources are limited [18]. These frameworks imply that formularies, refill continuity, laboratory access, recall systems, and workforce capacity are components of treatment rather than administrative background.

The single worst system outcome is late detection followed by fragmented rescue: undiagnosed diabetes or hypertension progresses, risk factors are treated in isolation, follow-up is lost, and the first fully coordinated encounter occurs after organ damage. IDF estimates that 251.7 million adults aged 20-79 with diabetes were undiagnosed in 2024, while WHO reports that most CVD deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries [1][2]. Prevention therefore requires both case finding and capacity to confirm diagnoses, initiate treatment, and maintain long-term care. Screening without a functioning downstream pathway creates knowledge without benefit.

### For public policy: alter exposure and make primary care executable

Population policy can reduce risk before clinical treatment begins. WHO identifies tobacco use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol use, obesity, and air pollution among major modifiable or environmental CVD risks [1]. Policy instruments can change price, availability, marketing, transport, air quality, and opportunities for activity, but their effects and burdens should be evaluated rather than assumed. The relevant endpoint is improved health across the population, with attention to distribution, not merely participation in a campaign.

Clinical prevention still requires accessible primary care. WHO PEN argues for integrated NCD services because cardiovascular disease and diabetes share detection, counseling, medication, monitoring, and referral infrastructure [18]. Separate programs can be justified for specialized tasks, but blood-pressure measurement, glucose testing, cardiovascular risk assessment, essential medicines, kidney surveillance, and follow-up can often share staff and records. The author's synthesis is that integration is valuable when it removes duplicated work and missed handoffs, not when it simply adds more duties to an unsupported clinic.

Equity is part of effectiveness. WHO reports that CVD mortality and diabetes treatment gaps are concentrated in lower-resource settings, while the ADA documents how food, housing, finances, access, environment, and social support affect care [1][17][19]. A program that improves average biomarkers while widening untreated gaps has not achieved complete population benefit. Policies should therefore report reach, continuity, treatment availability, adverse effects, and outcomes by relevant subgroups, then direct additional support to points where the pathway fails.

### For researchers and evidence consumers: preserve the causal question

Future studies should state the target population, comparator, outcome, follow-up, treatment adherence, adverse events, and absolute as well as relative effects. Surrogate improvements are useful when their connection to patient outcomes is established, but they should not be described as event reduction without evidence. UKPDS, Look AHEAD, EMPA-REG OUTCOME, and LEADER illustrate why glucose, weight, and cardiovascular endpoints must remain distinct [8][13][14][15].

Evidence synthesis should also preserve contradictions. Intensive lifestyle intervention reduced diabetes incidence in DPP but did not reduce the Look AHEAD cardiovascular composite after diabetes was established [7][15]. Intensive glucose control reduced UKPDS microvascular endpoints but did not significantly reduce macrovascular disease during the randomized period [8]. SPRINT showed major benefit from intensive blood-pressure treatment but excluded diabetes and increased some harms [11]. These are not reasons to abandon prevention; they are instructions to define what each intervention has actually proved.

A compact audit can prevent overclaiming. Ask whether the source studied prevention before disease or treatment after diagnosis; whether participants had established CVD; whether the endpoint was a biomarker, diagnosis, microvascular complication, heart-failure hospitalization, major vascular event, or death; whether the effect is relative or absolute; whether harms and discontinuation were reported; and whether the care system can reproduce the intervention [7][8][11][13][14]. The author's final assessment is that systemic prevention succeeds when biological evidence and delivery evidence are joined: the right intervention reaches the right risk state, remains usable over time, and is verified against outcomes that matter.
