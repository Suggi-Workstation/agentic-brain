# Health Technology Assessment Allocates Care Better When Evidence, Opportunity Cost, Equity, and Uncertainty Are Judged Together

Health technology assessment (HTA) is a systematic, multidisciplinary process for determining the value of medicines, devices, diagnostics, procedures, programs, and delivery systems in a defined decision context. It improves coverage and reimbursement decisions only when comparative clinical evidence, economic consequences, affordability, patient experience, equity, and uncertainty remain visible together; no cost-effectiveness ratio can remove the value judgments required to allocate limited health resources [1][2].

## Background

HTA arose from a problem that neither biomedical research nor financial control could solve alone. A regulator can determine whether a medicine is sufficiently safe, effective, and well manufactured for marketing, but authorization does not establish whether it is better than the care actually displaced, whether its price represents good value, whether the delivery system can implement it, or whether a payer can fund it without reducing other care. A hospital or insurer can measure expenditure, but expenditure alone does not identify the health produced or forgone. HTA connects these questions by comparing a technology with relevant alternatives and examining intended and unintended consequences across clinical, economic, organizational, patient, social, ethical, and sometimes environmental domains [1][2][12].

The internationally developed 2020 definition describes HTA as a multidisciplinary process that uses explicit methods to determine the value of a health technology at different points in its lifecycle. Its purpose is to inform decisions that promote an equitable, efficient, and high-quality health system. The definition treats a health technology broadly: it can be a test, device, medicine, vaccine, procedure, program, or system, and its value can change with the perspective, stakeholders, comparator, setting, price, and stage of use [1]. This breadth matters because the same clinical effect can have different value when delivered through different workforces, infrastructure, budgets, or care pathways [1][12].

The modern institutional form of HTA developed as health systems acquired both more effective interventions and harder allocation problems. WHO describes HTA mechanisms as a means of making evidence-informed decisions and using health funds wisely, while emphasizing mandates, legal authority, governance, assessment and appraisal processes, and monitoring rather than a model report alone [2]. HTA is therefore both an analytic activity and an institution. The analytic work estimates comparative effects, costs, and uncertainty. The institution decides which topics enter assessment, whose evidence is heard, how conflicts are managed, how recommendations connect to coverage, and when a past decision should be reconsidered [2][10].

HTA normally separates assessment from appraisal. Assessment organizes and critically evaluates evidence: the population, intervention, comparators, outcomes, study validity, model structure, costs, health effects, and uncertainty. Appraisal interprets that evidence for a decision using stated criteria and deliberation. Canada's Drug Agency describes deliberation as the informed and critical examination of evidence and arguments, including potentially conflicting values and perspectives, before a recommendation is made [10]. This separation does not make appraisal subjective in the sense of arbitrary. It makes the normative step explicit: evidence can estimate consequences, but a committee must still judge which consequences matter, how much uncertainty is tolerable, and how efficiency should be balanced with severity, unmet need, feasibility, and equity [10].

Cost-effectiveness analysis became central because health budgets create opportunity cost. Money committed to one technology cannot be used simultaneously for another service. The relevant question is therefore not whether a technology has benefit or even whether its total cost is large; it is whether the additional health and other valued consequences justify the additional resources compared with the best relevant alternative [3][7]. In a fixed health budget, approving a technology that produces less health than the displaced care can reduce total population health even though treated patients benefit. The threshold in a cost-effectiveness framework is intended to represent, however imperfectly, the health that the system can produce at the margin and would forgo when new spending displaces existing services [7].

This logic changed evaluation from average cost accounting to incremental comparison. Analysts compare the difference in expected costs with the difference in expected outcomes between mutually relevant options. A new intervention that costs less and improves health dominates its comparator. One that costs more and produces less health is dominated. The difficult case is a technology that improves health at additional cost, because the health gained by recipients must be compared with the health opportunity cost imposed elsewhere [3][4][7]. The incremental framing also prevents historical research and development spending, political salience, or a high list price from being mistaken for evidence of value.

Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) became a common outcome because they combine duration and health-related quality of life in one generic measure, making comparisons across diseases possible. NICE's current reference case expresses health effects in QALYs and uses patient-reported health-related quality of life when possible [3]. The QALY is an analytic common denominator, not a complete theory of justice. It can omit caregiver effects, non-health consequences, process value, diagnostic knowledge, or dimensions of health not captured well by a selected instrument. It can also conceal distribution: two programs with equal aggregate QALYs may benefit groups with different baseline severity, access barriers, or lifetime health [3][14].

HTA also differs from several neighboring decisions. Regulatory approval asks whether a product meets legal standards for market entry; FDA's review process centers on safety and effectiveness, while payer coverage applies a separate legal and evidentiary standard [16][17]. Bedside treatment choice concerns a particular patient's condition, preferences, and feasible alternatives, whereas HTA usually informs population policy. Price negotiation determines the transaction terms under which access may occur; HTA can establish the evidence and value range that informs negotiation but is not the negotiation itself. Bioethical theory can justify principles such as equal moral worth, priority to the worse off, or procedural fairness; HTA operationalizes selected criteria for a concrete health-system decision but cannot settle the underlying philosophical dispute [1][10].

Institutional designs consequently differ. NICE in England combines comparative evidence, cost-utility analysis, public committee deliberation, and binding or strongly actionable guidance within a tax-funded system [3]. Canada's Drug Agency evaluates clinical, economic, patient, and clinician evidence and makes non-binding recommendations to participating public plans, which retain final authority [10][18]. Australia's PBAC requires comparative clinical and economic submissions for public medicine funding and separately examines expected utilization and financial impact [11]. The European Union now conducts joint clinical assessments for selected technologies while leaving non-clinical assessment, value conclusions, pricing, and reimbursement to Member States [9]. The United States has no single national HTA authority for all payers; Medicare coverage uses a statutory reasonable-and-necessary standard and can require evidence development for selected services [17]. These arrangements use related methods but answer different legal and budgetary questions.

The author's synthesis is that HTA is best understood as disciplined comparison under constraint. Its central achievement is not converting health into one number. It is forcing a decision maker to state the population, comparator, perspective, time horizon, consequences, uncertainty, distribution, and opportunity cost before committing shared resources. Its central limitation is equally important: formal analysis can make assumptions and trade-offs auditable, but it cannot make contested priorities disappear.

## Core Concepts

### The decision problem comes before the model

Every assessment begins with a defined decision problem. The population determines baseline risk and who is eligible; the intervention includes dose, delivery, monitoring, and stopping rules; the comparators should represent relevant current practice; outcomes include benefits and harms; and the time horizon must be long enough to capture material differences. Perspective determines which costs and effects enter the analysis. NICE primarily takes the NHS and Personal Social Services perspective for costs, while the US Second Panel recommends both a health-care-sector reference case and a societal reference case, supported by an impact inventory that shows consequences inside and outside formal health care [3][4].

Scope errors cannot be repaired by statistical sophistication. Comparing a new medicine only with placebo can overstate value when an effective treatment is already used. A lifetime model can be necessary when a short trial changes a long-term disease trajectory, but it also places more weight on extrapolation. A payer perspective can be appropriate for a reimbursement decision while excluding caregiver time or productivity that matters to society. Reference cases improve consistency by specifying standard methods, but decision makers still need additional analyses when the reference case omits material consequences [3][4][5].

The comparator is especially consequential because cost-effectiveness is relative. A technology has no universal ICER. Its incremental cost and health gain can change when the alternative is usual care, the least expensive effective option, a future standard, or no intervention. The comparator can also change by subgroup and treatment line. The assessment must therefore describe actual practice, its variation, and the pathway consequences of replacement, addition, or partial substitution [3][11].

### Comparative clinical evidence establishes the effect to be valued

Economic analysis does not rescue weak comparative evidence. The clinical review asks whether studies identify the effect of the technology relative to relevant alternatives, for the population and outcomes in scope. Randomized trials usually provide the strongest direct protection against confounding, but their usefulness still depends on allocation, follow-up, outcome measurement, treatment adherence, comparator relevance, and applicability. Nonrandomized evidence can inform long-term safety, natural history, treatment patterns, resource use, rare populations, and real-world implementation, but selection, confounding, and measurement bias must be assessed rather than hidden by sample size [3].

Evidence synthesis should preserve differences in population, intervention version, comparator, outcome definition, and follow-up. Indirect comparisons and network meta-analysis can connect treatments not compared head to head, but the validity of the network depends on whether effect modifiers are sufficiently comparable across trials. When population-adjustment methods are used, NICE requires their assumptions and limitations to be stated, and direct evidence should remain visible alongside network estimates [3]. A precise relative effect that applies to the wrong comparator or population is not decision-ready evidence.

HTA also considers outcomes that trials may undermeasure. Qualitative research and patient submissions can identify treatment burden, acceptability, stigma, caregiving effects, symptoms omitted from generic instruments, and barriers affecting uptake. NICE explicitly permits qualitative evidence on patient experience, feasibility, values, and equity; Canada's deliberative framework includes persons with lived experience and patient evidence [3][10]. Such evidence does not substitute for comparative effect estimation. It tests whether the outcomes and assumptions used in the quantitative analysis represent the lives and care pathways being evaluated.

### Economic evaluation compares incremental costs and consequences

Cost-effectiveness analysis compares alternative courses of action in both costs and outcomes. Cost-utility analysis is the form that expresses outcomes in QALYs. Cost-minimization is appropriate only when credible evidence supports sufficiently similar health outcomes, because assuming equivalence converts an unresolved clinical question into an apparent price comparison. Cost-consequence analysis lists multiple effects separately, and cost-benefit analysis converts consequences into monetary terms; these can inform decisions but answer different questions [3][4][11].

Costs should follow the selected perspective and the causal pathway. They can include acquisition, administration, testing, monitoring, management of adverse events, downstream treatment, avoided care, infrastructure, training, and end-of-life care. A societal analysis may add patient time, unpaid caregiving, productivity, education, social services, or other sectors when relevant. Transfers and prices require careful treatment because a payment can be a cost to one budget holder without being an equal social resource cost [4][5]. All important categories should be disaggregated before aggregation so that a result can be reinterpreted under another perspective.

Discounting reflects the lower present value generally assigned to costs and health effects occurring far in the future. Time horizon and discount rate can materially affect preventive interventions, curative therapies, vaccines, and treatments with high upfront cost and delayed benefit. The analyst should follow the jurisdictional reference case, explain departures, and test influential alternatives rather than choose the convention that makes a favored technology appear most attractive [3][5].

### QALYs create comparability but not moral completeness

A QALY weights each period of survival by a health-state utility, conventionally anchored so that one year in full health equals one QALY and death equals zero, with some systems allowing states valued below death. Utilities usually come from preference-based instruments completed by patients and valued using population preference weights. NICE now requires the relevant EQ-5D system and value set in its adult reference case, subject to specified exceptions when content validity is inadequate [3].

The QALY's strength is consistent comparison. A health system can compare a treatment that extends life, one that relieves disability, and one that prevents disease using the same outcome unit. Its limitations arise from both measurement and ethics. Generic instruments may be insensitive to rare symptoms, cognitive change, caregiver spillovers, process burdens, or benefits outside health. Equal aggregate QALYs do not say who receives them. Rare-disease appraisals have documented missing or insensitive patient-reported outcomes, utility values derived indirectly, short follow-up, and heavy reliance on patient input to interpret uncertainty [15].

The correct response is not to pretend that QALYs contain every value or to discard comparability without replacement. Analysts should show what the measure captures, add relevant outcomes and perspectives, test alternative utilities, and identify consequences left outside the reference case. Committees can then distinguish an empirical disagreement about health effects from a normative judgment about severity, fairness, or non-health value [3][10][14].

### ICERs, net benefit, thresholds, and opportunity cost

For a more effective and more costly technology, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio is defined as the additional cost divided by the additional QALYs relative to the comparator [3][4]:

ICER = (cost of new technology - cost of comparator) / (QALYs with new technology - QALYs with comparator)

The ICER is the additional cost per additional QALY. It should be calculated after excluding dominated options and using the relevant efficiency frontier when several alternatives exist [3]. Ratios become difficult to interpret when incremental effects approach zero, when an option saves money but loses health, or when comparisons change across multiple alternatives [3]. Net health benefit often makes the decision rule clearer [3][7]:

Net health benefit = incremental QALYs - (incremental cost / threshold)

A positive expected net health benefit means that estimated health gained exceeds the health opportunity cost implied by the threshold [3][7].

A threshold is not the price of a human life. In a budget-constrained public system, it is a decision rule intended to represent the marginal health produced by existing expenditure and therefore the health likely to be displaced. Claxton and colleagues used program-budgeting expenditure and mortality data from the English NHS, then extended mortality effects to life years and QALYs, to estimate the scale of health opportunity cost [7]. The study demonstrated that a threshold can be treated as an empirical question about system productivity, although estimates remain sensitive to data, model assumptions, omitted quality-of-life effects, and changes in budgets or practice [7].

Thresholds are also institutional choices. NICE's March 2026 manual presents net health benefit using values of GBP 25,000 and GBP 35,000 per QALY after a government-directed threshold change [3]. That range is specific to NICE's decision context and should not be exported as a universal constant. Budgets, prices, disease burdens, existing services, and marginal productivity differ across countries and payers. A threshold borrowed without a local opportunity-cost argument can create an appearance of rigor while systematically approving interventions that displace more health than they produce [2][7].

### Cost-effectiveness and budget impact answer different questions

Cost-effectiveness asks whether additional outcomes justify additional resources relative to alternatives. Budget impact asks whether the expected uptake and spending consequences fit a particular budget over a relevant short- to medium-term period. An intervention can be cost-effective but unaffordable at rapid scale because a large eligible population creates a large near-term cash requirement. It can also have a small budget impact but poor value because it produces little benefit per unit of additional cost [8].

A budget impact analysis estimates the eligible population, current treatment mix, expected uptake, acquisition and implementation costs, offsets, and changes over time from the perspective of a named budget holder. ISPOR's task force recommends decision-maker-specific populations and scenarios, including alternative uptake and access assumptions [8]. The analysis should be consistent with the clinical and economic assumptions used in cost-effectiveness work, while recognizing that its time horizon, outputs, and financial perspective differ [8].

Affordability should not silently replace value. A large budget impact may justify phased implementation, price negotiation, eligibility conditions, capacity planning, or explicit reconsideration of the budget. It does not show that the intervention is inefficient. Conversely, dividing a budget impact by the number of patients does not produce an ICER because it lacks comparative health outcomes. NICE treats net budget impact separately from clinical and cost effectiveness, and Australia's PBAC submission structure likewise separates economic evaluation from expected use and financial impact [3][11].

### Model structure translates evidence into the decision horizon

Decision-analytic models combine evidence that no single study usually contains. A short trial may estimate a treatment effect, a registry may provide long-term event rates, preference studies may provide utilities, and administrative data may provide resource use. A model maps these inputs into expected costs and outcomes for each alternative over the decision horizon [3][6]. Its structure should reflect the clinical pathway and causal question rather than the software most familiar to the analyst.

Decision trees are useful for short pathways with discrete events. State-transition models represent movement among recurring health states over time. Patient-level simulation can represent histories, interactions, and heterogeneous risks that cohort averages cannot. Diagnostic models often link test accuracy to treatment decisions and then to patient outcomes because end-to-end trials following the whole pathway are uncommon [3]. Whatever the form, assumptions about disease progression, treatment duration, waning, adverse events, recurrence, mortality, and resource use should be explicit and clinically validated.

A model is conditional evidence, not a forecast guaranteed by mathematics. ISPOR-SMDM guidance identifies conceptualization, implementation, parameter estimation, uncertainty, transparency, and validation as connected responsibilities. Validation includes checking code, face validity, consistency with external data, comparison with other models, and explaining why discrepancies occur [6][13]. For devices, user skill, training, organizational change, rapid iterations, and learning curves can make effectiveness context-dependent and cause an assessment to become outdated quickly [12].

### Uncertainty must be carried into the decision

Uncertainty has several forms. Stochastic uncertainty reflects random variation among otherwise similar patients. Parameter uncertainty concerns imperfect estimates of effects, costs, utilities, and transition rates. Structural uncertainty concerns how the model represents disease and care. Heterogeneity concerns real differences among patients or settings, while methodological uncertainty concerns choices such as perspective, discounting, and extrapolation [6]. A single confidence interval around one input cannot represent all of them.

Deterministic sensitivity analysis changes selected inputs or assumptions to show which factors drive results. Scenario analysis tests coherent alternative structures, such as different treatment waning, uptake, or comparator pathways. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis assigns distributions to uncertain parameters and propagates them jointly through the model, producing a distribution of costs and effects and the probability that each option is cost-effective across thresholds. Arbitrary variation of every input by the same percentage is not a substitute for evidence-based uncertainty distributions [6].

Value-of-information analysis asks whether reducing uncertainty is worth the cost and delay of additional research. The expected value of perfect information places an upper bound on the value of eliminating decision uncertainty; more specific analyses can identify which parameters or study designs deserve research. The result depends on the population affected, the lifetime of the decision, whether adoption can be reversed, and whether further evidence can change practice. Uncertainty is therefore not automatically a reason to reject or approve. It is information for choosing among adoption, restricted use, coverage with evidence development, further research, or delay [6][17].

### Equity and deliberation govern what averages omit

An average ICER can conceal unequal disease prevalence, access, uptake, treatment effect, and opportunity cost. Distributional cost-effectiveness analysis estimates how costs and QALYs are distributed across social groups and can show effects on a health-inequality gap. NICE's current support methods use deprivation groups, require evidence for differential uptake or benefit, and present health benefit and opportunity cost by group [14]. Distributional analysis expands the evidence; it does not mechanically dictate how much aggregate health should be traded for a fairer distribution.

Severity, unmet need, rarity, caregiving burden, stigma, feasibility, and procedural fairness often enter through modifiers or deliberation. NICE explicitly recognizes decision modifiers and value judgments that go beyond estimated QALYs, while Canada's framework considers clinical value, unmet need, social and ethical considerations, economic considerations, and health-system impact [3][10]. These criteria should be stated before the decision where possible, applied consistently, and recorded in the rationale. Otherwise, deliberation can become an untraceable exception granted to the most organized claimant.

Patient experience has two roles. It supplies evidence about outcomes, burdens, preferences, and implementation that formal studies may omit. It also gives affected people a legitimate place in a decision about public resources and access. Neither role means that one testimony estimates a population treatment effect. A defensible process triangulates lived experience with comparative and qualitative evidence, discloses disagreement, and explains how patient evidence changed the scope, model, or recommendation [3][10].

### Rare diseases, diagnostics, and devices require adaptation, not exemption

Rare-disease assessments often face small and heterogeneous populations, uncertain natural history, short or single-arm studies, immature survival data, outcomes poorly represented by generic utility instruments, and high per-patient prices. The documented response across HTA systems is not one uniform rule. It includes broader evidence, explicit uncertainty, special pathways, modifiers, managed access, and patient or expert input [3][15]. Rarity alone does not prove high value, and relaxing evidence standards without a plan for post-launch learning can make uncertainty permanent.

Diagnostics create value only through what their information changes. Accuracy is an intermediate outcome unless the test itself directly benefits or harms the patient. An assessment must link true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives to treatment decisions, downstream outcomes, resource use, and equity. NICE prefers end-to-end evidence but permits linked-evidence modeling when such trials are unavailable [3]. This prevents a more accurate test from being assumed beneficial when no effective treatment follows, false positives trigger harmful cascades, or results arrive too late to alter care.

Medical devices can depend on operator skill, maintenance, interoperability, infrastructure, procurement, and organizational redesign. Their product cycles may be shorter than those of medicines, and incremental versions can arrive before long-term evidence matures. WHO's 2025 guidance therefore treats selection, adoption, procurement, management, and reassessment as lifecycle questions and discusses rapid, adaptive, and real-world approaches [12]. Assessment should model training and learning, specify which version was studied, and test transfer to ordinary settings rather than treating market authorization as proof of system value.

### Real-world evidence and reassessment make HTA a lifecycle process

Real-world data from registries, claims, electronic records, routine outcomes, and device surveillance can inform natural history, treatment patterns, safety, adherence, resource use, effectiveness, and subgroup outcomes. These data inherit the purposes and biases of the systems that generated them. Provenance, coding, missingness, treatment timing, confounding, selection, and outcome validity must be assessed before a large database is treated as strong evidence [3].

Conditional adoption can connect access with learning when evidence is promising but insufficient. CMS coverage with evidence development permits coverage of selected items or services only in a qualifying study and prospectively re-examines evidence; NICE can recommend managed access for medicines and later reconsider the recommendation [3][17]. Such arrangements are credible only when the evidence gap, study design, responsible party, funding, decision date, and consequence of an unfavorable result are specified in advance.

Reassessment is necessary because prices fall, comparators change, utilization expands, safety signals emerge, skills improve, and real-world effectiveness can differ from trial efficacy. HTA can therefore operate from early development through adoption, post-market evidence, restriction, and disinvestment [1][12]. The author's synthesis is that a recommendation should be treated as a dated decision under stated conditions, not a permanent property of the technology.

## Evidence

### The English case shows how an explicit reference case supports, but does not replace, judgment

NICE's technology evaluation manual is a current institutional case of formal cost-utility assessment. Its reference case specifies the NHS and Personal Social Services cost perspective, QALYs as the health outcome, systematic evidence synthesis, relevant comparators, a sufficient time horizon, discounting, modeling, and uncertainty analysis. Following a 2026 policy change, NICE presents net health benefit at GBP 25,000 and GBP 35,000 per QALY [3]. The method makes trade-offs reproducible, while committees still examine evidence quality, patient experience, inequality, implementation, modifiers, and uncertainty [3][14].

The same case demonstrates why cost-effectiveness and affordability must remain separate. NICE evaluates clinical and cost effectiveness, while its process also contains a net budget-impact test and routes for commercial agreements or varied implementation timing. Its manual was updated in 2025 to raise the budget-impact test from GBP 20 million to GBP 40 million [3]. A technology can therefore pass the value test yet require an implementation response because near-term expenditure is concentrated. The institutional finding is not that one calculation overrides the other, but that each answer is recorded for the decision it actually supports [3].

### Empirical threshold research turns displacement from an abstraction into an estimable effect

Claxton and colleagues developed a conceptual framework for the NICE threshold and used English NHS program-budgeting expenditure and mortality data to estimate how changes in spending affected mortality across disease programs. They then translated mortality effects into life years and QALYs by adding estimates of quality of life and non-mortality health effects [7]. Their central empirical estimates were below the threshold range used by NICE at the time, although the report emphasized uncertainty and data limitations [7].

The method matters more than exporting its historical point estimate. It defines the threshold as the marginal health effect of expenditure under a constrained budget, not public willingness to pay, gross domestic product per person, or a conventional number inherited from another country [7]. It also exposes the distribution problem: patients displaced by a new technology are usually not identified in the appraisal, yet their expected health loss is real [7]. The author's assessment is that opportunity-cost research should be updated as budgets, prices, productivity, and data improve, and should be reported separately from political decisions about expanding the health budget.

### Method standards converge on transparency, perspective, and uncertainty

The Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine developed recommendations through expert consensus and external review. It recommended two reference cases, one for the health-care sector and one for society, plus an impact inventory showing which consequences were included [4]. CHEERS 2022 used an international task force to produce a 28-item reporting checklist for economic evaluations, including decision context, perspective, comparators, time horizon, outcomes, costs, model assumptions, uncertainty, distributional effects, stakeholder engagement, and conflicts [5].

ISPOR-SMDM modeling guidance separately distinguished stochastic, parameter, structural, and heterogeneity-related uncertainty and recommended deterministic and probabilistic analysis, cost-effectiveness acceptability information, and value-of-information methods [6]. These are consensus and reporting standards rather than randomized tests of HTA itself. Their convergent finding is procedural: a decision model becomes more credible when the question, perspective, data, assumptions, uncertainty, and limitations are exposed to scrutiny rather than hidden behind one base-case ICER [4][5][6].

### Rare-disease appraisals show the measurement gap that deliberation must not conceal

Whittal and colleagues reviewed 24 NICE appraisals of non-oncology orphan treatments and corresponding appraisals in the Netherlands, France, and Germany. Patient-reported outcome evidence was absent from six NICE appraisals; when present, measures often failed to show change, omitted domains important to patients, or were uncertain. Utility values came from direct EQ-5D data in only seven cases, with the remainder using mapping, vignettes, literature, or other methods. In eight NICE cases, patient surveys or committee input helped interpret missing or uncertain quality-of-life evidence [15].

This document analysis does not show that every rare-disease technology is undervalued or that patient testimony can replace utilities. It shows a recurrent mismatch between what generic instruments measure and what small, heterogeneous populations experience. The policy implication is to develop outcomes and natural-history evidence earlier, show alternative utility assumptions, and use managed access only when new data can resolve a named uncertainty [15]. A special pathway without an evidence plan can preserve the very uncertainty invoked to justify it.

### Devices and diagnostics expose why system context belongs in the assessment

WHO's 2025 medical-device guidance reviews HTA across selection, adoption, procurement, management, and reassessment. It identifies short product cycles, incremental modification, device-user interaction, learning curves, infrastructure, and real-world data as reasons device value can vary across settings [12]. The method is a policy and methods synthesis rather than a comparative clinical trial. Its finding is that a device cannot be assessed as a molecule with a purchase price: operator proficiency, maintenance, workflow, consumables, interoperability, and organizational capacity can change both effectiveness and cost [12].

NICE's diagnostic methods provide a complementary case. Because end-to-end trials from test through treatment and patient outcomes are uncommon, the manual permits linked-evidence models that connect accuracy to changes in management and downstream health [3]. This approach also exposes weak links. If evidence establishes accuracy but not that clinicians act on the result, or establishes changed treatment without credible outcome evidence, the model should carry that gap as uncertainty rather than claim clinical utility from accuracy alone [3].

### National arrangements use common evidence but retain different authority

Canada's Drug Agency assesses clinical, economic, patient, clinician, and implementation evidence, then deliberates across clinical value, unmet need, social and ethical considerations, economics, and health-system effects. Its recommendations to public plans are non-binding, so provincial and territorial programs retain decisions based on mandates, priorities, and financial resources [10][18]. Australia's PBAC submission guidance requires comparative clinical evidence, a full cost-effectiveness or cost-minimization analysis as appropriate, expected use, and financial impact for public medicine funding [11].

The European Union case divides labor differently. Regulation (EU) 2021/2282, applicable since 12 January 2025, creates joint clinical assessments for selected technologies and common procedures for cooperation. The joint work addresses relative clinical effects, while Member States retain non-clinical assessment and final authority over value, pricing, and reimbursement [9]. The design aims to reduce duplicated clinical submissions without pretending that budgets, prices, legal duties, and social priorities are identical across countries [9].

The US Medicare case illustrates the regulatory-coverage boundary. FDA review concerns market authorization based on safety and effectiveness, while Medicare covers items and services under statutory benefit and reasonable-and-necessary rules [16][17]. CMS may use coverage with evidence development when evidence is promising but insufficient for unrestricted coverage, limiting coverage to qualifying studies and later reconsidering the decision [17]. The cases show that approval, assessment, coverage, and price are linked but distinct institutional acts.

## Implications

### For public payers: make displacement and affordability explicit

A payer should state the budget constraint and the decision rule before reviewing a favored technology. The assessment should show incremental health, incremental cost, expected net health benefit, budget impact, uptake, implementation requirements, and uncertainty separately. When a technology is cost-effective but creates a large near-term budget impact, the response should identify the actual constraint: price, rollout speed, workforce, infrastructure, or the budget itself. Phased introduction or negotiation is more transparent than silently applying a harsher cost-effectiveness standard only to technologies with visible spending [3][8].

Coverage criteria should follow the evidence and model. A treatment may be cost-effective only for a high-risk subgroup, after failure of a lower-cost therapy, at a confidential discount, or with a stopping rule. The recommendation should state these conditions and provide an operational way to identify eligible patients. If eligibility cannot be implemented or audited, the base-case result does not describe the policy that will occur. Budget impact scenarios should also test leakage beyond the modeled population and changes in treatment mix [8][11].

Opportunity cost should appear in committee reasoning even when its victims cannot be named. Funding one technology can displace staff time, diagnostics, primary care, surgery, or other medicines rather than one visible program. A positive recommendation above the stated threshold should therefore explain the modifier or evidence that justifies accepting additional displacement. A rejection below the threshold should explain the uncertainty, feasibility, or evidentiary problem rather than imply that the ratio alone decided the case [3][7][10].

### For analysts: build an auditable chain from evidence to decision

The analysis should begin with a protocol and a decision map. Each important model parameter should trace to a source, population, date, unit, and transformation. Treatment effects should preserve the estimand and comparator from the clinical evidence. Resource quantities should be separated from prices. Utilities should identify the instrument, respondent, valuation set, mapping, and timing. Extrapolation should distinguish observed data from assumptions, and validation should compare predictions with independent evidence when available [3][5][6].

Uncertainty analysis should correspond to real uncertainty. Sampling error belongs in parameter distributions; alternative survival functions, waning rules, or care pathways belong in structural scenarios; subgroup differences belong in heterogeneity analysis; and perspective or discount choices belong in methodological scenarios. Analysts should not vary every input by an arbitrary percentage and call the result comprehensive. They should show the expected decision, probability of error, consequences of error, and whether feasible research could change the recommendation [6].

A base-case ICER should never be the only output. Disaggregated costs and outcomes, incremental results for every comparator, the efficiency frontier, net health benefit, deterministic drivers, probabilistic results, scenario analyses, budget impact, and distributional consequences allow reviewers to find where disagreement begins. CHEERS improves reporting but is not a conduct-quality certificate; compliance with a checklist cannot validate a biased clinical effect or inappropriate structure [5].

### For clinicians and patients: distinguish population guidance from individual care

An HTA recommendation describes expected value for a defined population under specified use. It does not prove that every eligible person should receive the technology. Bedside decisions still require contraindications, prognosis, alternatives, treatment burden, patient goals, and preferences. A restricted recommendation can also be broader or narrower than a product's regulatory label because coverage addresses comparative value and implementation rather than only legal marketing authorization [3][16][17].

Patient participation should shape outcomes and assumptions before the model is complete. Patients and carers can identify benefits omitted from trial endpoints, burdens hidden inside an administration code, differences between temporary and tolerable adverse effects, and access barriers that change uptake. Their evidence should be documented with the same care as other evidence: who contributed, how perspectives were gathered, which experiences differed, and what changed in the assessment [3][10]. This prevents participation from becoming ceremonial while avoiding the opposite error of treating one story as a population effect estimate.

Decision communication should use natural language and absolute quantities. A patient-facing explanation can state the expected health gain, important harms, treatment burden, uncertainty, and why the payer judged the additional cost acceptable or not. If the recommendation depends on a commercial discount, the public rationale should still explain the direction and decision boundary as far as confidentiality permits. Transparency is incomplete when the public sees a recommendation but cannot identify the evidence, criteria, and unresolved uncertainty that produced it [2][5][10].

### For rare diseases: pair flexibility with a learning contract

Small populations can make conventional trials, long follow-up, utility measurement, and comparative evidence difficult, but uncertainty should be characterized rather than converted into assumed benefit. Natural-history registries, external controls, Bayesian methods, patient-reported outcomes, caregiver measures, and expert elicitation can contribute when their assumptions and biases are explicit. The analysis should show how conclusions change under plausible disease progression, treatment waning, survival, utility, and eligible-population estimates [3][15].

A special threshold or modifier is a social choice about priority, not a statistical correction for small sample size. Committees should state whether they are giving additional weight to severity, lifetime health loss, unmet need, rarity itself, or another feature. They should also consider the opportunity cost borne by patients outside the rare-disease program. This keeps compassion and fairness inside the decision rather than hiding them in optimistic modeling [7][10][15].

Managed access is defensible when it functions as a learning contract. The unresolved question, dataset, comparator, outcomes, sample size, analysis plan, funding, review date, and exit rule should be agreed before coverage expands. If the technology would remain funded regardless of the result, data collection is surveillance or description rather than a genuine resolution of decision uncertainty. Reassessment should be capable of narrowing, expanding, repricing, or withdrawing use [3][17].

### For diagnostics and devices: evaluate the pathway that produces benefit

A diagnostic assessment should map every consequence of a result: who is tested, prevalence, indeterminate results, false positives and negatives, confirmatory testing, altered treatment, delay, anxiety, complications, outcomes, and resource use. A test with higher sensitivity can reduce missed disease and simultaneously increase false positives, procedures, and cost. The model should identify which link is directly observed and which is borrowed or assumed [3].

Device assessment should specify the model and version, user, setting, training, maintenance, consumables, infrastructure, cybersecurity or interoperability where relevant, and the learning period. A trial in expert centers can overstate ordinary effectiveness, while early evaluation during a learning curve can understate mature performance. Scenario analysis should test both risks. Procurement should not select the lowest unit price while excluding the costs of installation, staff, downtime, service, upgrades, and disposal [12].

Rapid product iteration creates a reassessment problem. Evidence about one version should not be transferred automatically to a successor, but requiring a complete new assessment for every minor change can freeze useful innovation. The proportionate response is to define which technical changes could alter clinical effect, workflow, safety, cost, or equity and trigger targeted evidence or review. WHO's lifecycle framing supports this link between assessment, technology management, and post-market learning [12].

### For health systems: institutional design determines whether good analysis changes care

An HTA body needs a legal mandate, independence safeguards, conflict management, transparent topic selection, technical capacity, public methods, stakeholder involvement, appeal or correction routes, links to budgets and procurement, and monitoring of implementation. WHO's institutional guide treats these as separate design tasks because a rigorous report has little effect when the responsible payer can ignore it without explanation or when no implementation authority exists [2].

Capacity should match the decision portfolio. Full de novo models are not necessary for every topic. Rapid reviews, adaptation of trusted assessments, horizon scanning, and multicountry collaboration can conserve scarce expertise, especially in smaller systems. Adaptation still requires local comparators, costs, epidemiology, service capacity, equity, and legal context. The EU joint clinical assessment model demonstrates one division of labor: shared clinical analysis with national non-clinical appraisal and final decisions [9].

Institutions should monitor both process and impact. Useful measures include timeliness, publication, stakeholder participation, conflict compliance, recommendation uptake, access, expenditure, outcomes, inequality, evidence generation, and reassessment [2]. Counting reports alone can reward throughput without showing whether care improved or low-value services were displaced [2]. The author's synthesis is that HTA succeeds when it creates a repeatable learning system: decisions are made with explicit evidence and criteria, implementation is observed, and new evidence can revise the result.

### For governance: preserve the boundary between analysis and judgment

Technical analysis should estimate consequences as accurately as possible and show uncertainty without advocacy. Deliberation should state the criteria and value judgments used to interpret those consequences. Price negotiation should then seek terms consistent with the assessed value and budget, while regulatory agencies continue to enforce safety, efficacy, quality, and post-market duties. Combining these functions without clear roles can allow a price concession to masquerade as clinical evidence or a regulatory authorization to masquerade as value [9][10][16][17].

The worst failure is a precise ratio attached to the wrong decision. It can arise from an irrelevant comparator, an unrepresentative population, a utility instrument that misses the benefit, a model that assumes away harm, a threshold imported from another system, or uptake that makes the budget model fictional. Prevention requires a scope agreed before analysis, independent review, traceable assumptions, patient and clinical challenge, uncertainty analysis, and a recommendation that names its conditions [2][3][5][6].

No method can make scarcity morally neutral. QALYs can compare health, ICERs can express incremental efficiency, budget models can show affordability, and distributional analysis can reveal who gains and loses. A public decision must still determine how to treat severity, disability, rarity, responsibility, age, caregiving, innovation, and equal access. The defensible standard is not value-free HTA, which is impossible, but value-explicit HTA in which evidence, opportunity cost, equity, and reasons are open to examination [1][10][14].

## Common Pitfalls

### Treating authorization as reimbursement evidence

Market authorization establishes that a product may be marketed for a labeled use under the regulator's standard. It does not establish comparative value against local care, affordability, implementation feasibility, or coverage under a payer's legal mandate [16][17]. The fix is to use regulatory reviews as clinical evidence inputs while conducting the separate comparative and economic assessment needed for coverage.

### Reporting one favorable ICER

A single ratio can conceal dominated comparators, unstable denominators, selective scenarios, structural assumptions, and distributional effects. The fix is to present disaggregated results, all relevant comparisons, net benefit, deterministic and probabilistic uncertainty, budget impact, and the conditions under which the decision changes [3][5][6].

### Confusing a low budget impact with good value

A small population or low uptake can make an inefficient technology affordable, while a common and highly efficient intervention can create a large total expenditure. The fix is to keep cost-effectiveness and budget impact as connected but separate analyses and state the action taken for each result [8].

### Using QALYs as if they settle equity

Aggregate QALYs measure health, not the fairness of their distribution. The fix is to examine baseline severity, access, uptake, subgroup effect, caregiver impact, and opportunity cost; use distributional analysis where evidence permits; and record any modifier or value judgment explicitly [10][14].

### Calling uncertainty innovation

Novelty, rarity, and small studies can explain uncertainty but do not turn unknown benefit into positive benefit. The fix is to show structural and parameter uncertainty, use conservative and alternative scenarios, calculate the value of further information when useful, and attach conditional coverage to a real reassessment rule [6][15][17].

### Assessing a test or device outside its system

Accuracy without changed management does not guarantee patient benefit, and device performance without training, maintenance, and workflow does not guarantee ordinary effectiveness. The fix is to model the complete diagnostic or delivery pathway, verify local capacity, and reassess when the product, operator experience, or context changes [3][12].
