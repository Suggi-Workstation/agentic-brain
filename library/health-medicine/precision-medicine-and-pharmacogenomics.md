# Precision Medicine and Pharmacogenomics Improve Care Only When Patient Differences Change a Validated Clinical Decision

Precision medicine uses genomic, clinical, environmental, and behavioral differences to choose prevention, diagnosis, or treatment for a defined patient rather than an imaginary average patient. Pharmacogenomics is its medication-focused branch: it asks whether inherited or acquired genomic variation should change drug selection, dose, or monitoring. The central discipline is that a measurable difference matters only when an analytically reliable test predicts a clinically relevant outcome and leads to an action whose benefits exceed its harms ([1] [3] [4]).

## Background

### From disease labels to mechanisms

Conventional medical categories group patients by symptoms, anatomy, pathology, and expected clinical course. Those categories remain indispensable, but people with the same label can have different disease mechanisms, rates of progression, treatment responses, and risks of toxicity. The 2011 National Research Council report that helped define precision medicine proposed a continuously learning knowledge network linking molecular observations with clinical, environmental, and outcome data. Its goal was not to assign every person a unique therapy. It was to refine disease classification whenever a reproducible patient characteristic identifies a subgroup for which a different clinical decision is justified ([1]).

The United States Precision Medicine Initiative, announced in 2015, translated that concept into two connected research programs. Its near-term emphasis was cancer, where tumor mutations already selected some targeted therapies, and its longer-term objective was to study health and disease through large cohorts combining molecular, clinical, physiological, behavioral, and environmental data. Collins and Varmus explicitly described precision medicine as an evidence-building program: creative molecular hypotheses had to be tested rigorously before entering practice. They also stated that molecular approaches would enrich rather than replace prevention, diagnosis, and effective conventional treatment ([2]).

This distinction prevents an important category error. Precision medicine is not synonymous with whole-genome sequencing, and genomic information is not automatically more important than age, organ function, comorbidity, previous treatment, exposure, adherence, or patient preference. A treatment decision can be precise because it uses a tumor protein, renal function, infection susceptibility, or a germline variant, provided that the feature is measured reliably and changes expected benefit or harm. Conversely, an elaborate genomic profile that does not change management is descriptive information rather than a demonstrated clinical intervention ([2] [3]).

### Pharmacogenetics became pharmacogenomics

Variation in drug response was recognized before the human genome could be sequenced. Some patients metabolized a medicine unusually slowly, experienced an immune-mediated reaction, or failed to activate a prodrug. Pharmacogenetics initially studied one inherited variant and one drug phenotype at a time. Pharmacogenomics broadened the inquiry to multiple variants, genes, pathways, and sometimes genome-wide information. In clinical use, the terms often overlap, but both concern genomic influences on pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, efficacy, and toxicity ([4]).

The main pharmacokinetic mechanisms involve absorption, transport, metabolism, and elimination. A variant that reduces enzyme activity may raise exposure to an active drug, lower exposure to an active metabolite, or prevent activation of a prodrug. Pharmacodynamic variation changes the drug target or downstream pathway. Immune variation can create susceptibility to a severe adverse reaction. These mechanisms establish biological plausibility, but a plausible mechanism is not sufficient proof that testing improves care. The effect must be large enough, the variant must be detected accurately, an effective alternative must exist, and the result must arrive before the decision is made ([3] [4] [6]).

Regulatory and professional infrastructures developed to translate selected associations into practice. The US Food and Drug Administration maintains one table of evaluated pharmacogenetic associations and another table of biomarkers appearing in approved drug labeling. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium, or CPIC, publishes peer-reviewed recommendations explaining how an available genotype result should alter prescribing. CPIC deliberately answers how to use a result, not whether every patient should be tested, because the value of ordering a test also depends on prevalence, timing, alternatives, cost, and the clinical setting ([5] [6] [7] [19]).

### Precision oncology supplied an early proving ground

Cancer made molecular stratification clinically visible because tumors that arise in different organs can share an actionable driver, while tumors with the same anatomical label can have different drivers. Somatic biomarkers can identify a target, predict resistance, or estimate whether an immune therapy is more likely to work. Some tests are companion diagnostics paired with a specific treatment; others provide broader profiles that may identify an approved therapy, an off-label hypothesis, or eligibility for a biomarker-defined trial ([7] [18]).

Tumor biomarkers and inherited pharmacogenomic variants answer different questions. A somatic tumor alteration often identifies the disease mechanism or treatment target, whereas a germline variant is present across the person's cells and may predict metabolism, transport, immune reaction, or inherited disease risk. Both can affect one prescription, and both require a validated assay and an interpretable clinical context. A report that mixes tumor and germline findings without marking that boundary can confuse treatment selection, inherited risk, and implications for relatives ([7] [17] [18]).

### The field moved from association to implementation

Early genomic medicine was dominated by discovery: identify a variant associated with a phenotype and replicate the association. Clinical medicine requires a longer chain. The laboratory must detect the variant accurately; the variant must predict the relevant phenotype in the population and setting; the result must support a different action; and that action must improve outcomes enough to justify testing and downstream consequences. The CDC's ACCE framework names these layers analytic validity, clinical validity, clinical utility, and ethical, legal, and social implications. Its 44-question structure also asks about population, intervention, facilities, education, monitoring, access, and safeguards ([3]).

That progression explains why precision medicine contains both durable successes and unresolved claims. A strong single-gene effect linked to a severe, preventable drug reaction can be highly actionable. A small statistical association with a common disease may add little beyond ordinary clinical risk factors. A result can be valid but not useful because no better action exists, or useful in one setting but not another because turnaround time, population ancestry, comparator care, or available alternatives differ. Precision therefore describes the quality of the decision pathway, not the quantity of molecular data collected ([3] [4]).

## Core Concepts

### Patient characteristics are decision variables, not identities

A precision decision begins with a defined clinical question. For prevention, the question may be whether risk is high enough to justify earlier surveillance or an intervention. For diagnosis, it may be whether a molecular feature distinguishes diseases that look similar clinically. For treatment, it may be whether a biomarker predicts benefit, toxicity, dose, or resistance. The useful unit is therefore not the person in the abstract but a person-decision pair: this patient, facing this choice, at this time, with these available alternatives ([1] [3]).

Genomic information is one layer in that pair. Age, kidney and liver function, interacting medicines, disease severity, previous response, pregnancy, adherence, environment, and patient goals can change the same prescription. The FDA cautions that a listed gene-drug association does not by itself mean testing is recommended before prescribing, and that genotype is only one of the factors needed for safe and effective use. A pharmacogenomic result should therefore modify a complete clinical model rather than displace it ([6]).

This framing also separates stratification from determinism. A variant changes a probability or expected response; it rarely guarantees an outcome. HLA-B*57:01 creates a clinically important susceptibility to abacavir hypersensitivity, but a positive result is not itself a drug reaction. CYP variants produce metabolizer phenotypes, yet exposure can also change through drug interactions, organ dysfunction, dose, and adherence. Precision medicine is probabilistic medicine with better covariates, not a claim that DNA fixes destiny ([4] [6] [8]).

### Germline, somatic, and non-genomic biomarkers play different roles

Germline variants are inherited or arise before development of the individual and are generally stable across tissues. They can affect drug metabolism, transport, targets, or immune recognition. Because the result persists, a preemptive germline panel can potentially inform many future prescriptions. That reuse is a principal argument for testing before an urgent prescription rather than ordering one gene at a time after the decision window has closed ([4] [19]).

Somatic variants arise in a subset of cells and are central to cancer. A tumor can acquire a targetable alteration, lose the target under treatment pressure, or contain multiple subclones with different susceptibilities. The biomarker is therefore tied to the sampled tissue and time. A prior tumor profile may not represent a later resistant lesion, and a blood-based assay may detect a different mixture than a tissue biopsy. NCI describes biomarker testing as a way to measure genes, proteins, and other substances that may identify a treatment or a biomarker-defined trial; it does not imply that every detected alteration has an effective matched therapy ([18]).

Non-genomic biomarkers remain part of precision medicine. Protein expression, metabolites, imaging features, physiological measurements, microbial susceptibility, and ordinary laboratory values can stratify treatment. The FDA's pharmacogenomic labeling table includes germline variants, somatic mutations, gene expression, chromosomal abnormalities, and selected protein biomarkers when prescribing information differs. The shared logic is predictive actionability, not a privileged data type ([7]).

### Pharmacogenomic mechanisms create distinct actions

A pharmacogenomic result can lead to four broad actions. First, it can contraindicate a drug when a variant predicts a serious immune or toxic reaction. HLA-B*57:01 and abacavir are the canonical example: carriers should not receive the drug because screening can prevent immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity ([6] [8]). Second, it can select an alternative drug when the original agent is unlikely to be activated or tolerated. CYP2C19 loss-of-function variants can reduce formation of clopidogrel's active metabolite, creating a rationale for another antiplatelet agent in defined cardiovascular settings ([4] [12]).

Third, genotype can change dose. Reduced metabolism may require a lower starting dose, while rapid metabolism or reduced activation may require another strategy. CPIC guidelines translate diplotypes into phenotypes and then into prescribing recommendations, with an explicit grade for evidence and recommendation strength. The genotype-to-phenotype translation is essential because clinicians act on predicted function, not on an uninterpreted string of alleles ([5] [19]). Fourth, a result can change monitoring rather than the medicine itself, for example by prompting closer toxicity surveillance or an earlier response assessment. The correct action depends on the therapeutic index, available alternatives, severity of the outcome, and confidence in the association ([4] [6]).

These mechanisms also show why panels require curation. A panel may accurately genotype hundreds of variants while only a subset has evidence for a prescribing action. CPIC classifies level A and B gene-drug pairs as having enough evidence for at least one recommended prescribing action, while C and D pairs do not support an actionable recommendation. More genes on a report therefore do not necessarily create more useful information; they can create more uncertain or distracting output ([19]).

### Analytic validity is the first gate

Analytic validity asks whether the laboratory reliably detects and reports the genomic feature. It includes sensitivity, specificity, repeatability, robustness, coverage, copy-number detection, allele phasing, and the performance of the bioinformatics pipeline. A false genotype can cause a wrong dose, an avoidable drug exclusion, or false reassurance. Assay validation must match the types of variants being claimed; a method that detects common single-nucleotide variants may miss copy-number changes, structural variants, or rare alleles that alter phenotype ([3] [17]).

The ACMG and Association for Molecular Pathology standards emphasize that sequence interpretation is a controlled clinical process, not merely a sequencing output. For Mendelian disease variants they recommend the standardized categories pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, and benign, using multiple forms of evidence. They also recommend clinical laboratory quality systems and qualified interpretation because the complexity of sequencing creates error opportunities after the instrument produces reads ([17]). Pharmacogenomic laboratories need analogous discipline for allele definition, phenotype assignment, and version traceability ([5]).

A result can be analytically correct but incomplete. Targeted consumer genotyping may test a limited set of common variants and leave rare or structurally complex alleles unmeasured. Different laboratories can therefore return different functional interpretations if their variant coverage, reference data, or translation rules differ. The absence of a tested variant is not the same as proof of normal function, and a report must state what was and was not assayed ([16] [17]).

### Clinical validity links the result to a phenotype

Clinical validity asks how well the measured feature predicts the outcome of interest in the intended population. It includes effect size, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, penetrance, prevalence, and modifiers. A statistically significant association can have weak predictive value if the effect is small, the outcome is rare, or important variants were omitted. Validity must be evaluated in the population where the test will be used because allele frequencies, linkage patterns, environmental exposures, and background care can differ ([3] [14]).

For pharmacogenomics, the phenotype may be enzyme activity, drug concentration, adverse reaction, treatment response, or a patient-important event. Evidence that genotype changes concentration is not automatically evidence that testing improves survival, symptoms, or serious toxicity. The FDA separates associations supporting therapeutic management from those indicating only a potential effect on safety or response and those demonstrating only a pharmacokinetic effect. This layered presentation is a practical warning against treating every mechanistic association as equally actionable ([6]).

Clinical validity is also context-specific. A CYP2C19 result may be important for one drug and irrelevant for another. A tumor mutation can predict response to one inhibitor while co-mutations or tissue context create resistance. A polygenic score trained in one ancestry can lose accuracy in another. The test report, evidence review, and clinical decision must therefore define the gene, variant set, phenotype, drug or disease, population, and care setting rather than use the generic label "genetic risk" ([3] [13] [14]).

### Clinical utility requires a better outcome from a changed action

Clinical utility is the decisive gate. It asks whether using the test changes management and produces more benefit than harm compared with the actual alternative. Utility can include avoiding a severe adverse reaction, improving treatment response, shortening time to an effective dose, reducing invasive diagnostic work, or enabling a therapy that would otherwise be missed. It must also count test error, delay, anxiety, incidental findings, treatment substitution, cost, privacy risks, and opportunity cost ([3]).

A test has no demonstrated utility merely because clinicians change prescriptions after seeing it. The changed action must improve an outcome. Implementation endpoints such as turnaround time, result delivery, and clinician adherence are necessary intermediate measures, but they do not replace clinical endpoints. Randomized or otherwise well-controlled comparisons are especially important when a biomarker effect is modest, usual care is already effective, or the intervention bundles testing with education and decision support ([3] [9] [10] [12]).

Utility can be high even when only a minority test positive if the prevented outcome is severe and the action is clear. It can be low despite a common result if the recommended alternative is no better, more toxic, or unavailable. The threshold should therefore combine probability, consequence, treatment difference, test burden, and certainty. The author's synthesis is that actionability is the product of evidence and decision leverage: a result matters most when it moves a patient between clearly different management paths.

### Reactive and preemptive testing solve different timing problems

Reactive testing is ordered for a specific decision, such as before starting abacavir or after a cancer diagnosis. Its advantage is a direct indication and a focused interpretation. Its weakness is delay: a result that returns after therapy starts may not prevent an early adverse event, and repeated single-gene tests can duplicate cost and sample collection ([4] [8] [9]).

Preemptive testing measures a curated panel before a particular drug is needed and stores actionable phenotypes for later use. Its value depends on reuse, long-term data governance, and clinical decision support that retrieves the result at prescribing. CPIC assumes that preemptive genotypes will increasingly be available and therefore concentrates on how to use them. The result must be represented in computable form, linked to the current recommendation, and presented without overwhelming the clinician with non-actionable findings ([5] [19]).

A preemptive program is not simply a sequencing purchase. It requires identity matching, consent, laboratory quality, allele translation, an electronic health record representation, medication-triggered alerts, pharmacist or genetics support, update rules, and audit. It must also decide how to handle a guideline change after the original report. The durable asset is a governed genotype plus an updateable interpretation layer, not a static PDF buried in the record ([5] [17] [19]).

### Precision prevention needs absolute risk and an effective response

Prevention extends beyond pharmacogenomics. Monogenic variants can identify high inherited risk, while polygenic risk scores aggregate many common variants with small effects. Other patient characteristics, including age, family history, exposures, biomarkers, and prior disease, usually remain necessary. A risk estimate has clinical value only if it is calibrated in the target population and changes a prevention action whose net benefit is established ([3] [14]).

Polygenic prediction illustrates both promise and danger. Current scores have often been trained mainly in populations of European ancestry and can be several times more accurate there than in other ancestry groups. Using them without population-specific validation can systematically allocate surveillance or prevention less accurately to already underserved groups. Diversity is therefore a component of validity, not an optional equity supplement ([14]). NIH's All of Us program was designed to create a more diverse linked genomic and health resource; analysis of nearly 250,000 participants identified more than 275 million previously unreported variants, with half of the genomic data coming from participants of non-European genetic ancestry ([15]).

The same net-benefit logic that governs screening applies. Earlier or more intensive surveillance is useful only when the risk model identifies a group for whom the changed pathway improves outcomes. It can also generate false positives, overdiagnosis, procedures, anxiety, and opportunity cost. The correct comparison is the complete risk-guided program versus current care, not the score's discrimination statistic in isolation ([3]).

### Evidence and recommendations must remain versioned

Genomic interpretation changes as new variants, functional studies, trials, and drug alternatives appear. A variant can be reclassified, a gene-drug recommendation can be strengthened or withdrawn, and a tumor target can acquire a new approved therapy. Laboratories and health systems therefore need versioned knowledge, traceable interpretations, and a policy for reanalysis or notification ([5] [17]).

This requirement creates a division of labor. Laboratories validate measurement and report limits. Expert groups synthesize gene-drug evidence. Regulators define labeling and companion diagnostics. Health systems implement results in workflows. Clinicians integrate the result with the patient, and patients decide among reasonable alternatives. Precision medicine fails when any layer silently transfers its responsibility to another, such as a consumer report implying a treatment change or an electronic alert presenting an association as a command ([6] [16] [19]).

## Evidence

### HLA-B*57:01 and abacavir show a complete action pathway

PREDICT-1 is a model of high clinical utility because the test, outcome, and action were tightly specified. The double-blind prospective trial randomized 1,956 previously unexposed people with HIV-1 infection from 19 countries to prospective HLA-B*57:01 screening with exclusion of carriers from abacavir or to standard care without prospective screening. The allele prevalence was 5.6 percent. Screening reduced clinically diagnosed hypersensitivity from 7.8 percent to 3.4 percent and eliminated immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity, from 2.7 percent in control to zero in the screened group. The negative predictive value for immunologically confirmed hypersensitivity was 100 percent in this predominantly white study population ([8]).

The result was actionable because the adverse event was serious, the variant effect was strong, the test preceded exposure, and another treatment could be used. The study also used skin-patch confirmation to improve outcome specificity rather than relying only on a clinical syndrome that could be misclassified. This combination of analytic measurement, clinically valid association, alternative therapy, and randomized outcome evidence explains why HLA-B*57:01 screening became a benchmark rather than merely another drug-label association ([6] [8]).

### PREPARE supports panel-guided prescribing but defines its limits

The PREPARE implementation study tested whether a 12-gene panel linked to Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group recommendations could reduce adverse drug reactions across 18 hospitals, nine community health centers, and 28 community pharmacies in seven European countries. Investigators enrolled 6,944 adults receiving a first prescription for a guideline-covered drug and assigned clusters in a crossover design to genotype-guided treatment or standard care. Among 1,558 participants with an actionable interaction for the index drug, clinically relevant adverse reactions occurred in 21.0 percent of the guided group and 27.7 percent of control participants, corresponding to an odds ratio of 0.70 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.54 to 0.91 ([9]).

The study shows that a panel can affect multiple prescribing settings rather than one specialist use. It also shows why evidence must retain its qualifications. The trial was open label, the primary analysis focused on participants with an actionable drug-gene result, follow-up lasted 12 weeks, and 97.7 percent of enrolled participants self-reported European, Mediterranean, or Middle Eastern ethnicity. The result supports a curated panel embedded in recommendations; it does not validate every commercial panel, every reported association, or equal performance in every population ([9]).

### Warfarin demonstrates that biological truth does not guarantee incremental benefit

Warfarin dose is influenced by CYP2C9 and VKORC1, but two major randomized trials published together reached different practical conclusions. In EU-PACT, 455 participants in the United Kingdom and Sweden received either a genotype-guided algorithm with point-of-care results or standard loading. Among 427 analyzed participants, mean time in the therapeutic international normalized ratio range over 12 weeks was 67.4 percent with genotype guidance and 60.3 percent with standard dosing, an adjusted difference of 7.0 percentage points; excessive anticoagulation was also less frequent in the guided group ([11]).

COAG compared a genotype-plus-clinical algorithm with a clinical algorithm rather than with fixed standard loading. Among 1,015 randomized participants, the mean time in therapeutic range during the first four weeks was 45.2 percent with genotype guidance and 45.4 percent with clinical guidance, an adjusted difference of -0.2 percentage points. There was no significant improvement in the combined outcome of excessive anticoagulation, major bleeding, or thromboembolism, and Black participants had worse anticoagulation control with the genotype-guided algorithm ([10]).

The paired evidence shows that comparator quality, result timing, algorithm content, population representation, and follow-up can determine incremental utility. Genotype may explain part of dose variation while adding little to a strong clinical algorithm, or an incomplete variant panel may perform poorly in a population it does not represent. The author's synthesis is that a replicated genotype-phenotype association establishes a possible input; only a context-specific comparison establishes whether adding that input improves care ([10] [11] [14]).

### CYP2C19-guided antiplatelet therapy shows the difference between signal and proof

TAILOR-PCI randomized 5,302 patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention to a genotype-guided strategy or conventional clopidogrel therapy. The primary analysis compared carriers of CYP2C19 loss-of-function variants. At 12 months, the composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, stent thrombosis, or severe recurrent ischemia occurred in 35 of 903 carriers assigned to genotype-guided therapy, 4.0 percent, and 54 of 946 assigned to conventional therapy, 5.9 percent. The hazard ratio was 0.66, but its 95 percent confidence interval of 0.43 to 1.02 crossed one and the prespecified primary comparison did not reach statistical significance at P=0.06 ([12]).

The trial is not evidence that CYP2C19 has no pharmacological effect. It is evidence that the tested strategy did not meet its prespecified threshold for the primary clinical endpoint under that design. Secondary and later analyses can inform further study, but they should not be substituted for the primary result. This distinction matters throughout precision medicine: mechanistic association, changed prescribing, intermediate response, and patient-important outcomes are different evidentiary claims ([6] [12]).

### NCI-MATCH found real signals but not universal tumor-agnostic benefit

NCI-MATCH tested a broad precision-oncology strategy in patients with advanced, treatment-refractory malignancies. Nearly 6,000 patients underwent screening and molecular profiling, and 1,593 patients were assigned across 38 phase 2 substudies that paired a genomic alteration with a targeted therapy. Among the first 27 reported substudies, seven met the trial's signal-seeking definition of a positive arm. At least one response occurred in 22 arms, but the aggregate objective response among evaluable participants in those reported arms was 79 of 765, or 10.3 percent ([13]).

These results reject both extremes. Molecular matching was not futile: selected alteration-treatment pairs produced activity across tumor types and generated hypotheses for later development. It was also not a universal rule that any apparently actionable alteration should be treated with any matching inhibitor. Tumor lineage, co-alterations, pathway dependence, prior treatment, resistance, and drug properties remained important. NCI-MATCH therefore supports biomarker-defined experiments and selected successes while demonstrating the limits of association-only matching ([13] [18]).

### Regulation and guidelines encode different evidence levels

The FDA pharmacogenetic association table separates gene-drug relationships that support therapeutic management from those showing a potential effect on safety or response and those showing a pharmacokinetic effect only. It explicitly states that listing an association does not necessarily recommend testing before prescribing and directs clinicians to approved labeling. The separate biomarker-labeling table shows that genomic information can appear in indications, dosing, contraindications, warnings, clinical pharmacology, or clinical studies, with different practical consequences ([6] [7]).

CPIC adds an implementation layer by grading gene-drug evidence and recommendation strength, translating diplotypes into phenotypes, and publishing actionable prescribing guidance. Its scope begins when a reliable result is already available. The combination of FDA labeling, CPIC guidance, and direct clinical evidence is stronger than any one source alone, but disagreement or different scope must be surfaced rather than hidden. A label can mention a biomarker without requiring an action, and a guideline can explain how to act without asserting that universal testing is cost-effective ([5] [6] [19]).

### Population diversity is part of performance evidence

Genomic evidence has often overrepresented European-ancestry populations. Polygenic risk scores developed from those data can be several times more accurate in European-ancestry individuals than in other groups, creating a mechanism by which apparently individualized medicine could systematically worsen allocation of prevention or surveillance. Pharmacogenomic panels can face a related problem when common tested alleles capture less functional variation in another population ([10] [14]).

More diverse reference data improve discovery and interpretation but do not by themselves prove clinical utility. NIH reported that whole-genome data from nearly 250,000 All of Us participants contained more than 275 million previously unreported variants and that half of the genomic data came from participants of non-European genetic ancestry. That resource can reduce discovery bias and support better calibration. Each intended test and action still needs population-specific validation, outcome evidence, and equitable access to follow-up care ([3] [15]).

## Implications

### For patients: ask what decision changes and what outcome improves

A patient should be able to state the decision that a precision test is intended to inform. Useful questions include: What exact variant or biomarker is measured? How reliably does the test detect it? What outcome does it predict? How large is the absolute difference in benefit or harm? What treatment, dose, monitoring plan, or prevention pathway changes? What happens if the result is positive, negative, or uncertain? What evidence shows that acting on the result improves health compared with current care? These questions follow the ACCE sequence from measurement to utility and prevent a technically impressive report from being mistaken for a demonstrated intervention ([3]).

Patients should also distinguish inherited and tumor findings. A tumor result may guide the current cancer treatment without implying that relatives carry the alteration. A germline result can have lifelong prescribing or disease-risk implications and can be relevant to biological relatives. Consent and counseling should address that distinction, the possibility of uncertain or incidental findings, data storage, future reinterpretation, and whether results will be shared back when knowledge changes ([7] [17] [18]).

Direct-to-consumer testing has narrower limits than many consumers assume. When the FDA first authorized a direct-to-consumer pharmacogenetic report in 2018, it specified that the test detected 33 variants, did not determine whether a medication was appropriate, did not provide medical advice, and should not be used independently to start, stop, or change treatment. It also stated that medical decisions should follow discussion with a licensed clinician and confirmation through clinical pharmacogenetic testing. A consumer result can therefore begin a clinical conversation, but it is not an autonomous prescription ([16]).

A negative result also requires caution. A panel may not include every functional allele, rare variant, structural change, or relevant gene. Normal predicted metabolism does not remove non-genetic causes of toxicity or nonresponse. Patients and clinicians must continue ordinary monitoring, report symptoms, and consider interactions, dose, organ function, adherence, and disease progression. Genotyping can refine clinical vigilance; it does not replace it ([4] [6] [16]).

### For clinicians: use a decision ladder rather than a genomic shortcut

The clinical workflow should begin with the indication and the available alternatives, not with the test report. First define the decision and outcome. Then confirm that the assay is clinically validated for the claimed variant type and population. Determine whether the association concerns pharmacokinetics, an intermediate phenotype, or a patient-important outcome. Check current labeling and a maintained guideline. Integrate genotype with organ function, interacting medicines, comorbidity, prior response, and patient preference. Finally, document both the recommendation and the uncertainty ([3] [5] [6]).

Timing is part of utility. A high-quality result that arrives after an urgent first dose may not prevent early toxicity. Reactive testing should have a turnaround compatible with the decision, while preemptive testing should be retrievable as structured data when a relevant medicine is ordered. If no result is available in time, clinicians should not infer genotype from ancestry, family history, or previous tolerance; they should use the safest evidence-based clinical alternative and ordinary monitoring ([4] [5]).

A guideline should be read for scope. CPIC explains how to use available results and does not answer every question about whether testing should be ordered. FDA labeling can be informational, actionable, recommended, or required depending on the drug and biomarker. A tumor profiling report can rank hypotheses that remain investigational. Clinicians should communicate these categories explicitly: required, recommended, reasonable, investigational, or unsupported. Blurring them transfers uncertainty to the patient without naming it ([6] [7] [19]).

Uncertain variants should not be forced into action. The ACMG and AMP framework treats a variant of uncertain significance as a distinct category rather than a weakly positive result. Clinical action should rest on the complete evidence and the test's intended use. Reclassification policies matter because a static interpretation can become obsolete while the genomic measurement remains unchanged ([17]).

### For health systems: the intervention is the full information pathway

A safe pharmacogenomic program requires more than ordering capacity. It needs validated laboratories, standardized allele and phenotype terminology, identity controls, structured electronic results, medication-linked decision support, access to pharmacists or genetics expertise, versioned guidelines, and outcome monitoring. The system should record which test version and allele set produced a phenotype, because future knowledge may reveal that an apparently normal result was incomplete ([5] [17] [19]).

Clinical decision support should be selective. An interruptive alert is justified when the result is reliable, the risk is material, and an immediate action is clear. Lower-certainty information can be available without blocking workflow. Alert design should identify the gene-drug pair, phenotype, recommended alternative or dose, evidence source, and date of the knowledge rule. It should also allow a clinician to document why another decision was chosen. A flood of weak genomic alerts can create fatigue that degrades response to the few high-value warnings ([5] [6]).

Implementation should be evaluated as a causal chain. Measure the proportion of eligible patients tested, turnaround time, result availability before prescribing, frequency of actionable interactions, clinician adherence, treatment changes, adverse events, effectiveness outcomes, subgroup performance, and cost. A program can fail because the assay is wrong, the result is late, the alert is ignored, the alternative is inaccessible, or the association has limited utility. Aggregate test counts cannot identify which link failed ([3] [9]).

Preemptive panels need a long time horizon and a reuse policy. Their economic case depends on how often results influence future prescriptions, whether testing replaces later assays, and whether interpretation is maintained. The panel should be curated to actionable genes rather than expanded merely because sequencing additional bases is cheap. Storage and reinterpretation costs are part of the intervention, as are counseling and confirmatory testing ([3] [5] [19]).

### For prevention and diagnosis: require a complete downstream pathway

A genomic risk estimate should not trigger surveillance merely because its discrimination statistic is higher than chance. The program must define eligibility, threshold, interval, confirmatory testing, intervention, stopping rule, and patient-important outcome. Risk-guided prevention should be compared with a clinical model that already uses age, family history, exposures, and conventional biomarkers. The incremental question is whether the genomic feature changes enough decisions to improve net benefit ([3] [14]).

Diagnostic precision has the same requirement. A molecular subclass is useful when it resolves a clinically important ambiguity, changes prognosis accurately, selects an effective treatment, or avoids an unnecessary intervention. It is less useful when it renames a condition without changing care. The National Research Council's proposed molecular taxonomy was meant to improve classification and treatment through a learning network, not to replace clinical phenotypes with sequence labels ([1]).

Biomarker-guided cancer care should separate established companion diagnostics from exploratory profiling. For an approved biomarker-treatment pair, the relevant assay, indication, and labeling define the action. For a broader panel, a detected alteration may support trial enrollment rather than off-label treatment. NCI-MATCH shows why this distinction is necessary: some matched pairs produced meaningful signals, but the aggregate response was limited and most tested substudies did not meet the positive-arm threshold ([13] [18]).

### For researchers and regulators: design evidence around decisions

Discovery studies should identify mechanisms and associations, but implementation claims require representative prospective evidence. Trials should prespecify the tested variant set, treatment algorithm, comparator, turnaround time, adherence, primary outcome, subgroup analyses, and handling of multiple tests. They should measure both benefits and harms and distinguish an actionable-genotype analysis from an all-tested-population analysis. PREPARE, COAG, EU-PACT, and TAILOR-PCI show that design choices can materially change the clinical conclusion ([9] [10] [11] [12]).

Evidence should be transportable. Reports need ancestry and population descriptors, allele frequencies, assay coverage, background care, alternative treatments, and health-system context. A model trained in a narrow population should not be marketed as universally precise. Diverse discovery cohorts such as All of Us can improve the evidence base, but regulators and guideline groups still need to test whether a result changes outcomes in each intended use ([14] [15]).

Regulation should preserve distinctions among analytic validity, clinical validity, and utility. Authorization of an assay does not necessarily prove that every treatment claim made from its output improves outcomes. Drug labeling that mentions a biomarker does not necessarily require testing. Public tables and labels should therefore be read at the level of the specific recommendation, not counted as a single category of "actionable" products ([6] [7] [16]).

Post-implementation monitoring is necessary because the evidence, assay, and treatment environment change. Systems should look for false or discordant results, adverse outcomes after recommendations, unequal access, differential performance by subgroup, and alert overrides. When an alternative drug becomes safer, cheaper, or standard for everyone, a genotype-guided strategy can lose incremental value even if the underlying association remains true. Clinical utility is therefore a maintained property of a decision pathway, not a permanent property of a variant ([3] [5]).

### For equity: test the system, not just the genome

Precision medicine can reduce trial-and-error care, but it can also concentrate benefit among people with access to testing, specialist interpretation, expensive alternatives, and longitudinal records. Unequal data representation can make estimates less accurate for some groups, while unequal delivery can prevent a valid result from producing the recommended action. Equity assessment must therefore cover enrollment, assay performance, variant interpretation, decision support, access to alternatives, follow-up, and outcomes ([3] [14] [15]).

Ancestry is not a substitute for genotype, and broad racial categories are not biological dosing algorithms. Population history can affect allele frequencies and evidence transportability, but individual treatment should use measured clinical and genomic variables when validated. The worse result among Black participants in COAG illustrates the danger of an incomplete algorithm rather than a reason to deny genomic research or testing to a group. Better representation, relevant variant coverage, and subgroup validation are the corrective steps ([10] [14]).

The single worst outcome is a system that calls care individualized while using incomplete evidence to route disadvantaged patients toward less effective care. Prevention requires prospective subgroup evaluation, conservative use when calibration is uncertain, access to the recommended alternative, and transparent reporting of who benefits and who does not. The author's assessment is that precision medicine earns its name only when its evidence is at least as precise about populations, workflows, and outcomes as it is about molecular measurements.

### A practical decision rule

The author's synthesis is a five-question rule for any precision-medicine claim. Is the patient characteristic measured reliably? Does it predict the relevant outcome in this population and setting? Does the result change a concrete action? Has that action improved patient-important outcomes compared with current care? Can the complete pathway be delivered equitably and updated over time? A "no" at an early question should stop the claim from advancing to a stronger one.

This rule explains both the field's success and restraint. HLA-B*57:01 screening passes the chain for abacavir hypersensitivity. Curated multi-gene prescribing has promising outcome evidence but retains population and implementation limits. Warfarin and antiplatelet trials show that an association can be real while incremental utility depends on design and context. Precision oncology shows that molecular matching can identify valuable subgroups without making every detected alteration actionable. Precision medicine is therefore not medicine made certain by more data; it is medicine made more testable by defining which difference should change which decision, for whom, and with what evidence ([8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13]).
