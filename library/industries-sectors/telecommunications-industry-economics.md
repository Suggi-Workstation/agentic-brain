# Telecommunications Industry Economics -- Scale Lowers Unit Cost, but Reinvestment and Churn Limit Returns

Telecommunications operators combine scarce rights, long-lived networks, recurring customer relationships, and wholesale agreements to sell connectivity. Scale and density can lower unit cost, but neither guarantees durable returns because operators must keep reinvesting while price competition, churn, regulation, and technological substitution redistribute value across the industry [1][2][8]. The central economic question is therefore not how much traffic a network carries, but whether its coverage, capacity, and customer base earn more than the full cost of building, operating, renewing, and financing them.

## Background

Telecommunications began as a set of geographically bounded voice networks whose value depended on connecting one subscriber to other subscribers. Fixed-line systems required local loops, exchanges, long-distance transport, interconnection, rights of way, and a billing relationship. Because duplicating every local connection was expensive, many countries initially organized the industry around public or regulated monopolies. Liberalization then separated functions that had been bundled inside one operator: network ownership, retail service, long-distance carriage, equipment, and interconnection. Regulation increasingly required incumbent networks to connect rivals or provide wholesale access where an essential facility could not be economically duplicated [11].

Mobile service changed the access layer without eliminating the fixed-cost logic. A mobile network operator needs licensed or otherwise authorized spectrum, radio sites, backhaul, a core network, support systems, distribution, and customer service. Spectrum and sites determine where radio capacity can be created, but demand, traffic, and coverage determine whether those resources are productively used. The OECD describes spectrum management as a simultaneous problem of efficient use, competition, coverage, and investment; it cautions that auction design, licence conditions, sharing, and secondary markets can change all four outcomes [5].

Internet access then transformed telecommunications from a voice business into a general connectivity business. Fixed copper networks were upgraded, cable television systems became broadband networks, fibre was pushed toward premises, mobile systems moved through successive technology generations, and satellites developed new broadband roles. The ITU estimated that mobile broadband coverage reached 96 per cent of the global population in 2025, while 2.2 billion people remained offline and large quality and affordability gaps persisted [9]. Coverage is therefore necessary but not sufficient: a network can pass a population without producing adoption, adequate traffic, or an affordable service.

The industry's modern forms allocate the same underlying risks differently. A fixed incumbent may own ducts, poles, copper, fibre, exchanges, and a retail base. A cable operator can reuse a video distribution footprint and upgrade shared coaxial segments with deeper fibre and new electronics. A wholesale-fibre operator builds passive or active access infrastructure and sells capacity to retail providers instead of acquiring every end customer itself. A mobile network operator owns or controls spectrum and radio infrastructure, while a mobile virtual network operator purchases wholesale access and concentrates on branding, distribution, pricing, or a niche. A tower company owns passive sites and leases space to several operators. A satellite operator finances a space and ground system whose economics depend on constellation scale, terminal cost, launch and replacement, spectrum coordination, gateway capacity, and demand in locations where terrestrial alternatives are weak [2][10][13].

Convergence increasingly makes these categories overlap. The FCC's 2024 marketplace review describes cable companies using mobile virtual network arrangements and Wi-Fi offload, mobile operators selling fixed wireless access, and satellite and terrestrial providers entering hybrid relationships [4]. A household can buy mobile, fixed broadband, voice, television, security, cloud, or device finance in one bundle even when several physical networks and wholesale contracts sit underneath. Convergence can lower acquisition and service costs or raise retention by deepening the relationship, but it can also conceal weak stand-alone economics through discounts and internal transfer prices.

The cost structure remains capital intensive. The OECD's cross-operator analysis reports that fixed and mobile operators require continuous capital expenditure to preserve service quality and competitive position; total capital expenditure in its Bloomberg sample rose from USD 231.5 billion in 2008 to USD 317.6 billion in 2022 [2]. Network construction also creates sunk, location-specific assets. Fibre in one street, a tower in one coverage grid, a gateway in one geography, or spectrum licensed for one territory cannot be redeployed as freely as generic financial capital. The World Bank and ITU consequently treat investment incentives, open infrastructure access, interconnection, sharing, authorization, and affordability as linked regulatory questions rather than independent rules [11].

Demand is recurring but not guaranteed. Most retail operators collect subscription revenue, often summarized as average revenue per user, or ARPU. A fixed network also depends on take rate, the share of premises able to buy the service that actually subscribe. Mobile and fixed providers monitor churn, the share of subscribers who leave during a period. NTIA identifies ARPU, churn, take rate, cost per home passed, capital expenditure, operating expenditure, competition, and middle-mile access as core variables in the economic viability of a broadband network [1]. These measures connect the physical system to customer behavior.

Traffic growth is similarly ambiguous. More data can improve utilization and customer value, but it can require spectrum, electronics, backhaul, fibre, power, and software investment without an equal increase in revenue. ITU data show that fixed networks still carry most Internet data even though mobile broadband subscriptions are far more numerous, and that economies where broadband costs no more than 2 per cent of monthly income have much higher median traffic per subscription [9][10]. Traffic therefore records use, not profit. Its economic value depends on price, incremental cost, congestion, service quality, and the investment needed to support the next unit.

This topic analyzes the industry's playing field: spectrum rights, density, scale, customer economics, wholesale relations, sharing, regulation, convergence, and capital cycles. It does not describe how to engineer a fibre route, radio network, satellite constellation, or switching system; those physical layers are covered in `library/engineering-infrastructure/telecommunications-physical-infrastructure.md`. It also does not value an individual operator. Company analysis would require market-specific licences, network maps, contracts, financial statements, and a purchase price.

## Core Concepts

### Coverage, capacity, and utilization are different economic quantities

Coverage measures where a service can be offered under a defined performance standard. Capacity measures how much traffic a network can carry in a place and time. Utilization measures how much of that capacity customers actually use. A mobile operator can cover a broad territory with low-frequency spectrum and a sparse grid while lacking enough capacity in dense locations. A fibre operator can pass millions of premises but earn little if few households subscribe. A satellite constellation can cover a continent while gateway, terminal, beam, or spectrum constraints limit commercially usable capacity [1][5][10].

The author's synthesis is that telecommunications investment should be reconciled through a conversion chain: authorized resource, constructed footprint, serviceable location, activated customer, carried traffic, billed revenue, and cash return after sustaining investment. Announced spectrum, homes passed, sites built, or satellites launched are intermediate states. They become economically valuable only when the complete system can serve demand at a price that covers operating, renewal, and financing costs.

### Density lowers unit cost, but broad scale can add complexity

Networks exhibit density economies because fixed facilities serve more customers or traffic when demand is concentrated. A fibre route through a dense neighbourhood can divide trenching, permitting, feeder, and maintenance cost among many premises. A mobile site in a dense market can spread rent, backhaul, power, and radio equipment across more subscribers, subject to congestion and the cost of adding sectors or sites. NTIA identifies low population density, long distance per passing, difficult terrain, and longer middle-mile requirements as reasons that rural deployment costs more per potential customer [1].

Scale is related but different. A larger operator may obtain purchasing power, brand reach, roaming agreements, software reuse, centralized operations, and broader traffic pooling. It may also inherit duplicated systems, regional regulation, complex product sets, labour commitments, and legacy networks. Elliott and coauthors derive mobile economies of scale from the physics of data transmission and estimate a structural model in which more firms reduce prices but also reduce download speeds because network resources are divided more finely [8]. Their result does not prove that concentration is desirable; it identifies a trade-off between market power and productive scale.

The author's assessment is that density should be measured at the economically relevant network layer. National subscriber share is a weak proxy for the density of customers on a local fibre route, traffic in a radio sector, or demand behind a satellite beam. Scale creates durable advantage only where it lowers full unit cost or improves service after accounting for the complexity and reinvestment required to maintain it.

### Spectrum is both a productive input and a policy instrument

Radio spectrum is not consumed like fuel, but exclusive or coordinated access to a band can be scarce. Propagation differs by frequency: lower frequencies generally cover larger areas and penetrate obstacles more effectively, while wider higher-frequency bands can supply more capacity over shorter distances. Spectrum holdings interact with site density, antennas, backhaul, traffic, and technology; no one measure independently determines service quality [5][8].

Licensing converts this shared physical resource into enforceable operating rights. Auctions can reveal relative willingness to pay and assign licences transparently, but revenue maximization is not identical to economic efficiency. The OECD recommends considering coverage, investment, and competition together and documents awards that trade fee revenue for coverage obligations or permit sharing and secondary use [5]. Elliott and coauthors estimate that the marginal social value of spectrum in their French mobile model exceeds both firms' willingness to pay and observed auction prices [8]. This result is model-specific, but it shows why a licence price alone cannot measure the resource's total value.

High spectrum cost can constrain network investment, while a very low price without obligations can transfer scarcity rent to operators. Coverage conditions can direct capital toward unprofitable locations, but excessive or inflexible obligations can weaken entry or delay deployment. The author's synthesis is that spectrum policy should maximize service value over the licence period, not the auction receipt alone. The relevant evidence includes use, coverage, capacity, price, quality, entry, and the opportunity cost of alternative users [5][8].

### Fixed access, cable, mobile, wholesale fibre, and satellite carry different risks

A fixed fibre network commits substantial civil and access capital before take-up is known. Its economics depend on cost per premises passed, connection cost, take rate, ARPU, churn, wholesale revenue, operating cost, and the pace of overbuild. Once a capable passive plant exists, electronics can sometimes be upgraded without rebuilding the entire route, but maintenance, repair, active equipment, customer installation, and financing continue [1]. Greenfield construction is usually more expensive than expansion through reusable ducts, poles, routes, or adjacent operating resources [1].

Cable begins with a shared access architecture and an installed video footprint. It can spread upgrades across an existing base, but shared segments, legacy equipment, and competitive fibre builds influence the timing and cost of reinvestment. A cable operator that adds mobile through an MVNO can bundle services and lower churn, but wholesale mobile payments and offload economics determine whether reported mobile growth adds profit [4].

A mobile network transforms spectrum, sites, radios, backhaul, core systems, and distribution into coverage and capacity. Its capital can be staged by band, geography, and technology, but licences and nationwide quality expectations create continuing obligations. Customer acquisition is faster than building a fixed drop, yet switching can also be faster where number portability, device compatibility, and competitive offers reduce friction. Mobile economics therefore combine a physical capacity business with a high-volume customer-retention business [3][4].

A wholesale-fibre model substitutes a smaller number of retail counterparties for direct customer acquisition. It can improve network utilization by letting several retail brands share one footprint, but the wholesaler faces counterparty concentration, regulated or negotiated access prices, construction risk, and the possibility that retail partners favour another network. Retail providers gain a variable-cost route to market but surrender control over physical coverage, repair, upgrade timing, and part of the margin [2][11].

Satellite broadband changes the density equation. It can reach remote, mountainous, maritime, or sparsely populated areas where terrestrial construction is uneconomic, and new low Earth orbit systems reduce latency relative to older geostationary architectures [10][13]. The network still requires a constellation, launch and replenishment, gateways, spectrum coordination, terminals, ground operations, and enough users or wholesale traffic to support the capital base. Ofcom reported more than 110,000 active Starlink connections in the United Kingdom in 2025, mostly in rural areas, but many customers bought satellite service where terrestrial options also existed [12]. Satellite is therefore both a coverage complement and a competitive substitute at the edge of terrestrial economics.

### ARPU, take rate, and churn connect the network to customer value

ARPU measures average revenue, not average profit. It can rise through price, faster tiers, device finance, roaming, bundles, or a richer customer mix. It can fall through competition, prepaid mix, wholesale growth, family plans, or the separation of device and service revenue. The CRTC reported 2024 Canadian mobile ARPU of CAD 68.41 including devices and approximately CAD 50.95 excluding devices, demonstrating that the denominator and revenue perimeter materially change the result [3].

Take rate determines how many passed premises contribute revenue. A fibre build with high cost per passing and low adoption can remain uneconomic despite technically complete coverage. Churn determines how long revenue persists and how frequently acquisition, installation, device subsidy, credit, or retention costs must be repeated. The author's synthesis is a customer contribution model: expected service revenue less network usage, wholesale, service, bad-debt, and retention costs, multiplied by a retention distribution, then reduced by acquisition and installation cost. A low monthly churn rate can still destroy substantial value when the customer base is large and replacement costs are high.

Churn also contains competitive information. CRTC data show that fixed Internet churn differed by provider type in 2024, with independent wholesale-based operators experiencing higher churn and other facilities-based operators lower and declining churn [3]. The FCC cites evidence that fixed-broadband churn tends to be higher where retail choice and competition are stronger, all else equal [4]. Churn should not be interpreted as service failure without segment and market evidence, but it is a direct test of whether the customer relationship is durable.

### Interconnection, roaming, and wholesale access make rival networks complements

No operator can efficiently build every destination, route, tower, duct, or geographic footprint. Interconnection lets users on one network reach another. Roaming lets a subscriber use a visited network. Wholesale access lets a retail provider buy a network input instead of building it. These arrangements expand service and entry, but their price and technical terms determine whether access is an efficient complement or a barrier [11].

Cost-based or otherwise regulated access can restrain an incumbent bottleneck and support retail competition. If the price is too high, entry can be foreclosed; if it ignores irreversible investment and downside risk, the owner may have weaker incentives to build or upgrade. The World Bank and ITU therefore frame access pricing as a balance: reward investment in very-high-capacity networks, encourage efficient sharing of passive infrastructure, and preserve affordability [11].

The build-or-buy decision changes over time. A new entrant can use roaming, unbundling, bitstream, or an MVNO agreement to acquire customers before its own facilities reach scale. The same access can become a permanent business model where duplicating infrastructure is inefficient. The author's assessment is that regulators should test whether wholesale terms enable efficient entry while preserving a credible path for facilities investment where duplication can improve competition, quality, or resilience.

### Infrastructure sharing trades duplication for coordination

Sharing ranges from passive co-location of towers, ducts, poles, shelters, and power to active sharing of radio equipment, backhaul, or spectrum. It can lower cost, accelerate coverage, reduce environmental impact, and help smaller operators reach viable scale. It can also reduce independent network differentiation, facilitate coordination, expose participants to a common failure, or weaken incentives for unilateral investment [6][7].

BEREC's 2018 regulator survey reported estimated savings ranges of 16 to 35 per cent for passive-sharing capital and operating expenditure, 33 to 35 per cent of capital expenditure and 25 to 33 per cent of operating expenditure for active sharing without spectrum, and 33 to 45 per cent of capital expenditure for active sharing including spectrum [6]. BEREC cautioned that fixed-cost savings may not pass through promptly and that consumer benefits depend on market structure and competition [6]. These ranges describe reported arrangements, not universal engineering coefficients.

Koutroumpis, Castells, and Bahia studied 140 operators in 29 European countries over 2000-2019. Their difference-in-differences analysis associated sharing with lower operator capital expenditure, improved margins, lower ARPU, wider 3G and 4G coverage, and higher network quality, with smaller operators often benefiting most [7]. The authors also report heterogeneous effects by sharing type, technology cycle, and operator position. The defensible conclusion is conditional: sharing can improve both investment efficiency and competition when commercial independence and regulatory safeguards remain credible.

### Pricing, bundling, and convergence determine who captures the surplus

Telecommunications pricing often separates access, usage, speed, device, content, and service features. Flat-rate plans reduce billing uncertainty and can stimulate use, while usage tiers manage congestion or segment willingness to pay. Bundles can reduce acquisition and billing cost, distribute discounts across products, and make switching more difficult. They can also shift profit between services, making a weak product appear healthy because the customer relationship is valued at the group level.

Convergence intensifies this ambiguity. Fixed wireless can compete with cable or fibre for some households; fibre backhaul supports mobile capacity; Wi-Fi can offload mobile traffic; satellite can extend mobile or fixed coverage; cable companies can resell mobile; and mobile operators can bundle home broadband [4][10]. The author's synthesis is that each service should first be analyzed on a stand-alone contribution basis and then on an incremental household or enterprise basis. A bundle creates economic value only when shared acquisition, retention, infrastructure, or willingness to pay exceeds the discount and complexity it introduces.

### Regulation determines both rivalry and the investment horizon

Telecommunications regulation addresses scarce spectrum, interconnection, bottlenecks, consumer protection, universal access, quality, security, and emergency resilience. These objectives can conflict. More retail competitors can lower prices but fragment traffic across duplicated networks. Sharing can lower cost but reduce infrastructure independence. Low wholesale prices can promote service entry but weaken expected facility returns. High licence fees can raise public revenue but reduce deployable capital [5][6][8][11].

Regulatory credibility matters because network assets are long lived and location specific. Frequent retrospective rule changes increase the option value of waiting and raise the return investors require. Permanent protection from competition creates the opposite problem: it can preserve high prices and weak service. The author's assessment is that good regulation makes obligations, access rules, renewal, quality measures, and dispute procedures predictable while retaining the ability to address demonstrated market power or exclusion.

Universal-service policy fills the locations where private revenue cannot cover efficient cost. The economic gap should be measured after testing all suitable technologies, shared infrastructure, wholesale revenue, public anchor demand, and realistic adoption. Competitive tenders, milestone payments, coverage obligations, and transparent subsidies can reveal or reduce the required support. Cross-subsidies inside opaque retail prices may preserve service, but they make it difficult to identify who pays and whether a lower-cost technology or operator could meet the same objective [1][5][10].

### The capital cycle can erase the apparent moat

High fixed cost and low short-run marginal cost can make an installed network look protected. If demand and pricing support high returns, however, rivals can overbuild fibre, acquire spectrum, enter through wholesale, deploy fixed wireless, or introduce satellite capacity. Existing operators may also duplicate upgrades to defend share. Capital arrives with a lag; demand, prices, and technology can change before the projects mature [2][3][4].

Reinvestment prevents a simple distinction between growth and maintenance. A new radio generation can require spectrum, radios, antennas, software, transport, and device migration. Fibre can reduce some legacy operating costs but requires construction, customer connections, and electronics. Satellites require replacement as well as initial deployment. Copper and older mobile systems may be decommissioned only after customers and obligations migrate. The author's synthesis is that depreciation, retirement, and replacement should be modeled by asset layer rather than treated as one percentage of revenue.

A telecommunications moat is therefore strongest where density, rights, customer relationships, and operating capability reinforce one another. It is weakest where the operator owns expensive capacity but customers can switch, wholesale buyers control demand, regulation caps returns, or a substitute changes the cost curve. The practical test is return on incremental capital after normalized churn, competition, and sustaining investment, not EBITDA growth during one rollout phase.

## Evidence

### Broadband viability evidence identifies the governing variables

NTIA's 2025 overview begins with the deployment problem in unserved and underserved locations. It identifies low population density, rural or remote location, and difficult geography as reasons for higher capital and operating cost. Longer distance per passing raises cost per home passed, difficult construction increases labour and equipment cost, and a longer middle mile may be needed to reach the Internet core [1]. This is a policy guide rather than a controlled econometric study, but it provides an explicit project-level framework.

The framework separates capital expenditure from operating expenditure and links the network to recurring subscription revenue. It identifies ARPU, churn, take rate, cost per home passed, competition, middle-mile access, cost of capital, and alternative wholesale or government revenue as viability inputs [1]. The method is useful because it prevents technical completion from being confused with economic sustainability. Its limitation is that it does not supply one benchmark for every geography or technology.

The author's synthesis from this evidence is that a deployment case should reconcile four denominators: premises or population passed, serviceable locations, subscribers, and paying traffic. Cost per passing describes construction density; take rate converts the footprint into customers; ARPU converts customers into revenue; churn determines how durable that revenue is. A project can fail at any conversion even when the preceding metric looks strong.

### Cross-operator financial evidence shows continuing investment and lower returns

The OECD's 2024 financing study assembles operator, capital-market, and industry data to examine who funds broadband networks. It reports that communication-operator revenue grew at a compound annual rate of 2.1 per cent between 2008 and 2022, while inflation-adjusted mobile ARPU declined over 2008-2023 [2]. Regional 2023 ARPU in its data was highest in the United States and Canada at USD 42, followed by Asia-Pacific at USD 24, Europe at USD 17, and Latin America at USD 8 [2]. These are regional aggregates affected by service mix, income, pricing, devices, and methodology, not direct measures of consumer surplus.

The same study reports total fixed and mobile capital expenditure of USD 317.6 billion in 2022, 37 per cent above its 2008 sample value, and notes that the sector requires continuing investment [2]. It also reports that operator return on invested capital declined to about 5 per cent in 2022, while margins and returns varied by market and firm. The OECD does not identify competition as the sole cause: capital cost, management, market conditions, and competitive intensity can all contribute [2].

This evidence rejects two simple claims. The first is that traffic growth automatically raises ARPU; real ARPU can fall while demand and service quality rise. The second is that a large capital budget proves value creation; returns can decline despite sustained investment. The relevant comparison is incremental and through-cycle: what service, revenue, operating saving, or churn reduction did the capital produce, and did the resulting cash return exceed its financing and replacement cost?

### Canadian regulator data separate fibre, cable, wholesale, and mobile outcomes

The CRTC's 2026 market report uses regulatory data collections to compare Canadian fixed and mobile markets through 2024. It reports CAD 9.1 billion of wireline capital expenditure in 2024 and wireline capital intensity of 39.9 per cent, down from a 2022 peak as major incumbents moved toward completion of planned fibre rollouts [3]. Cable and other facilities-based providers increased capital intensity as they invested in fibre, cable, and fixed wireless to compete [3]. This is evidence of different positions within the same investment cycle, not one sector-wide capital requirement.

Retail and wholesale positions also diverged. Wholesale services accounted for 7.1 per cent of telecommunications revenue in 2024, and high-speed Internet represented 18.9 per cent of wholesale revenue [3]. Fixed Internet ARPU declined as subscribers rose and revenue flattened, while churn increased for some incumbents and wholesale-based providers. Facilities-based alternatives reported lower and declining churn [3]. The regulator expects fibre wholesale rules introduced through Telecom Regulatory Policy CRTC 2024-180 to change future competition, so the observed 2024 structure is not a stationary equilibrium.

Mobile investment was stable near its recent peak while capital intensity declined as revenue increased [3]. Mobile ARPU fell in 2024, and the difference between ARPU including devices and service-only ARPU was more than one-third [3]. This evidence demonstrates why operator comparisons must separate network model, rollout phase, wholesale position, devices, and customer mix. A blended industry average can conceal an incumbent harvesting a completed build, a cable company defending its footprint, an alternative constructing new fibre, and a reseller absorbing wholesale price changes.

### The United States shows convergence and a changing capacity cycle

The FCC's 2024 Communications Marketplace Report evaluates prices, churn, investment, spectrum, coverage, and barriers across communications markets [4]. It describes convergence through cable MVNOs and Wi-Fi offload, mobile fixed wireless, and satellite-terrestrial competition. It reports that fixed wireless captured a large share of recent broadband additions while cable growth slowed, though the report cites industry-party evidence and market conditions vary geographically [4].

For mobile, the FCC reports CTIA service ARPU declining from USD 35.74 in 2021 to USD 33.56 in 2023. It also reports nearly USD 163 billion of network investment over five years and USD 30.2 billion in 2023, down about 22 per cent from 2022 [4]. The Commission notes that equipment instalment plans, unlimited data, multiple-line pricing, and removal of overage charges affect ARPU. Capital decline after a build phase therefore cannot be interpreted without coverage, capacity, technology, and operator filings.

The convergence evidence changes competitive analysis. A fixed provider may face a facilities-based fibre rival, a cable network, a mobile fixed-wireless product, and satellite at different quality and price points. These products are neither perfect substitutes nor isolated silos. The author's synthesis is that substitution should be measured by customer switching, performance, location, and total price rather than declared from the underlying technology label.

### Sharing evidence shows both measurable savings and competition conditions

BEREC's 2018 report gathered information from European national regulators on passive, active, backhaul, roaming, and spectrum-sharing arrangements. Reported cost-saving ranges rose with the depth of sharing, reaching 33 to 45 per cent of capital expenditure for active sharing including spectrum [6]. Regulators also cited faster or broader coverage and easier entry, while identifying risks to infrastructure competition, service differentiation, investment, wholesale access, and spectrum awards [6]. The report is a survey and provisional analysis; its ranges should not be treated as causal estimates.

Koutroumpis, Castells, and Bahia provide a broader empirical test. Their 2023 study links operator financial and network outcomes to sharing agreements for 140 operators in 29 European countries over 2000-2019 [7]. Using difference-in-differences specifications and robustness checks, they find associations with capital savings, improved profit margins, lower ARPU, expanded 3G and 4G coverage, higher data traffic, and better quality. Passive sharing produced particularly strong capital, margin, coverage, and price results, while active sharing had stronger quality effects [7].

The paper also finds that effects differ by agreement type, operator size, market position, and technology cycle; smaller operators often gained more [7]. The authors report a decline in market concentration following sharing in their sample, but this historical result does not prove that every future agreement is pro-competitive. The combined evidence supports case-specific review: sharing can convert duplicated fixed cost into coverage and price benefits, yet governance, independence, spectrum, access, and the ability to invest separately remain material.

### Mobile market structure evidence formalizes the scale-price trade-off

Elliott, Houngbonon, Ivaldi, and Scott develop a structural model of mobile competition using French consumer and infrastructure data [8]. Their framework links price, consumer demand, network load, investment, download speed, operator count, and spectrum. Equilibrium simulations show that more firms reduce prices but can also reduce download speed because each operator has a smaller traffic pool and infrastructure base over which to exploit scale [8].

The result does not imply that fewer firms always improve welfare. The authors explicitly model the market-power cost of consolidation and find a trade-off whose optimum depends on consumers and market conditions [8]. Their spectrum analysis also estimates social value beyond private willingness to pay. The evidence is useful because it joins engineering constraints with industrial organization; its limitation is that estimates from one national mobile market do not automatically transfer to fixed fibre, cable, satellite, or another country's institutions.

### Coverage data show complementarity rather than one winning technology

ITU's 2025 global data show near-universal mobile broadband coverage but persistent use, affordability, and quality gaps [9][10]. In economies where entry-level broadband costs no more than 2 per cent of monthly income, median monthly traffic was about 15 GB per mobile subscription and 365 GB per fixed subscription [10]. This comparison supports a layered market: mobile supplies broad personal reach, while fixed networks often carry much higher traffic per subscription.

Ofcom's 2025 UK data provide a national case. Fixed wireless from mobile operators was available to 96 per cent of premises, full-fibre coverage continued to expand, and 4G from at least one operator covered 96 per cent of the landmass and more than 99 per cent of premises [12]. Low Earth orbit satellite connections exceeded 110,000, mostly in rural areas, while only a minority were at locations lacking decent fixed or fixed-wireless broadband [12]. Satellite therefore filled genuine gaps and competed where terrestrial service existed.

The author's synthesis is that technology substitution is local and workload specific. Fibre can offer high shared household capacity where civil construction is economic. Mobile provides mobility and wide coverage but shares spectrum capacity. Fixed wireless can monetize a mobile footprint for home access. Cable can upgrade an installed network. Satellite changes the cost of remote reach but bears constellation and terminal economics. Industry returns depend on which technology is the least costly adequate service in each geography, not on a universal ranking.

## Implications

### For investors: value the conversion chain, not the coverage headline

Investment analysis should begin by identifying the operator's actual model. Separate fixed incumbent, cable, fibre overbuilder, wholesale fibre, mobile network, MVNO, tower, and satellite economics. Then map the scarce rights and assets, customer relationship, wholesale dependencies, regulatory obligations, and reinvestment cycle. A national subscriber count or consolidated EBITDA margin is not enough when different segments bear different construction, churn, and replacement risks [1][2][3].

The operating dashboard should connect physical and customer measures. For fixed access, track premises passed, serviceable premises, connections, take rate, ARPU, churn, cost per passing, connection cost, network operating cost, and overbuild. For mobile, track population and geographic coverage, spectrum by band, sites, traffic, congestion, service ARPU, device revenue, churn, acquisition and retention cost, roaming, and capital expenditure. For wholesale networks, add tenant concentration, contracted minimums, price escalators, utilization, repair obligations, and competing footprints. For satellite, add launched and operating capacity, replacement schedule, gateways, terminal subsidies, spectrum rights, serviceable beams, subscriber density, and terrestrial substitution [1][3][5][10][13].

Revenue quality must be reconciled. Device finance should not be mistaken for recurring service economics. Pass-through revenue, roaming, wholesale, and bundles should be separated from retained margin. ARPU should travel with its numerator and denominator. Churn should be cohort based and converted into replacement cost and expected relationship duration. The CRTC's device-inclusive and service-only ARPU difference shows how easily one metric can combine distinct businesses [3].

Capital expenditure needs an asset-layer bridge. Classify new coverage, capacity densification, technology migration, customer connections, maintenance, resilience, software, spectrum, and replacement. A declining capital-intensity ratio can reflect rollout completion, underinvestment, revenue growth, a temporary equipment cycle, or a shift to leasing. An increasing ratio can reflect a valuable new footprint or a destructive defence of market share. The OECD's combination of sustained capital expenditure and declining ROIC is a warning that volume invested and value created are not synonyms [2].

The moat test should identify a falsifiable mechanism. Density is a moat only if competitors cannot reach comparable unit cost through sharing, wholesale access, another technology, or a better footprint. Spectrum is a moat only if the band, quantity, licence duration, and deployment economics create service customers value. Bundling is a moat only if it lowers cost or churn more than the discount. Regulation is not a moat when it can change after poor affordability or service. A customer base is not a moat when switching and reacquisition costs consume its contribution.

The downside case should invert the thesis. Combine slower take-up, higher churn, price pressure, a wholesale reset, cost inflation, technology migration, interest expense, and competitor capacity. Ask whether the operator can finish committed projects and preserve service without dilutive finance or asset sales. The single worst outcome is a leveraged network with low utilization that still requires heavy reinvestment to remain relevant. Preventing it requires staged capital, contract protection, liquidity, and a valuation that does not assume peak adoption or permanent scarcity.

### For operators: optimize durable utilization, not gross traffic or footprint

Management should allocate capital to the bottleneck that customers will pay to remove. In one market that may be rural coverage; in another it may be urban capacity, fibre backhaul, indoor service, resilience, latency, or installation speed. The operating target should be a measurable change in customer value and unit economics, not a technology generation or coverage statistic by itself.

Fixed operators should stage construction against credible take rate and competition. Dense routes can support direct build, while low-density areas may need shared ducts, public support, fixed wireless, satellite, or wholesale aggregation [1][10]. Customer installation should be planned with the outside plant because a passed home that cannot be connected promptly is not revenue-ready. Commercial teams should report cohort take-up and churn by build vintage so capital allocation can distinguish early adoption from a durable plateau.

Mobile operators should coordinate spectrum, sites, backhaul, and demand. Buying spectrum without a funded deployment plan leaves capital idle; building sites without suitable spectrum or backhaul leaves capacity constrained. Network-sharing decisions should specify which layers remain independent, how quality and investment decisions are governed, how failures are isolated, and how each party can differentiate. BEREC's cost ranges are large enough to matter, but its competition cautions show why governance is part of the economic design [6].

Wholesale access can be used strategically rather than defensively. A network with spare capacity can earn contribution from MVNOs, retail ISPs, enterprises, public agencies, or mobile backhaul. The incremental revenue should be compared with congestion, service, billing, support, and the possibility of strengthening a retail rival. Conversely, a retail provider should compare wholesale dependence with the cost and option value of selective facilities ownership. The reversible choice is often a staged hybrid: buy access where scale is insufficient, build where density and strategic control justify it.

Churn reduction should be treated as capital allocation. Better reliability, installation, service, transparent pricing, relevant bundles, and fair retention practices can extend customer life. Lock-in created through confusing contracts or switching friction may improve short-term churn while raising regulatory and reputation risk. The author's assessment is that durable retention is revealed when cohorts stay despite credible alternatives, not when exit is procedurally difficult.

Convergence should retain denominator discipline. A fixed-mobile bundle can share acquisition, billing, support, and customer data. Wi-Fi offload can reduce mobile-network cost; mobile can extend a fixed brand beyond its footprint; fibre can support mobile backhaul. Management should nevertheless maintain stand-alone transfer prices and contribution statements. Without them, one segment can subsidize another while consolidated revenue conceals value destruction.

Capital planning should include the full migration path. New fibre and mobile generations coexist with legacy systems until customers, emergency services, devices, wholesale partners, and regulation permit retirement. Spectrum refarming can free capacity but requires compatible devices and coverage. Satellite and tower assets require replacement or renewal. The author's synthesis is that every technology case should include build, parallel operation, migration, decommissioning, and residual obligations; omitting the overlap systematically understates capital need.

### For regulators and competition authorities: protect rivalry without forcing wasteful duplication

Regulators should begin with the service problem and the bottleneck. Retail competition can be strong even when one passive network is shared, while several nominal networks can provide weak competition if switching, coverage, or wholesale terms are poor. Market definition should reflect actual customer substitution by location, quality, and use case. The FCC's convergence evidence and Ofcom's technology-specific adoption data show why a technology label alone is insufficient [4][12].

Spectrum awards should optimize long-run service rather than upfront revenue. Transparent auctions can promote efficient assignment, but coverage, investment, competition, renewal, sharing, and secondary markets need explicit design [5]. A high reserve price that leaves spectrum unsold or drains deployable capital can reduce social value. A low price without use conditions can encourage warehousing. Coverage obligations should be measurable and technologically realistic, with enforcement based on delivered service rather than an announced build.

Infrastructure sharing should receive case-specific review. Passive sharing usually preserves more independent service design than active or spectrum sharing, but even passive concentration can create access or resilience risk. Regulators should examine governance, information exchange, geographic scope, spectrum, independent upgrade rights, failure isolation, MVNO access, exit, and the treatment of new entrants. The empirical European evidence supports sharing's potential benefits; it does not eliminate the need to monitor collusion, foreclosure, or underinvestment [6][7].

Wholesale access rules should make efficient entry possible while preserving a credible investment return. Cost, risk, asset life, demand uncertainty, and service quality should be reflected in access prices. Where civil infrastructure is the bottleneck, ducts, poles, towers, and sites may deserve different rules from active electronics or retail service. The World Bank and ITU recommendation is to reward high-capacity investment while promoting reuse of passive infrastructure and affordability [11]. The worst regulatory outcome is a rule that deters both incumbent investment and entrant construction while preserving neither low prices nor strong service.

Merger analysis should measure the complete post-transaction equilibrium. Fewer networks can raise market power, yet scale or density can lower cost and improve quality. Claimed efficiencies should identify the asset, geography, implementation cost, time, and consumer pass-through. The Elliott model demonstrates the theoretical and empirical trade-off in mobile service; BEREC and Koutroumpis and coauthors show that sharing can sometimes obtain cost benefits without full ownership consolidation [6][7][8]. Authorities should therefore compare a merger with sharing, roaming, wholesale, spectrum transfer, and targeted remedies rather than only with the status quo.

Universal-service intervention should be technology neutral at the objective level and specific at the contract level. Define the minimum service, geography, resilience, affordability, and duration. Compare fibre, cable extension, fixed wireless, mobile, satellite, community networks, and shared backhaul on lifecycle cost and actual user needs [1][10][13]. Award support competitively where possible, require milestones and open reporting, and reclaim funds when obligations are not met. Public subsidy should buy an observable connectivity outcome, not an untested assumption that one technology is always future-proof.

### For customers, enterprises, and public buyers: compare the complete service promise

Retail customers should compare total price, contract term, installation, speed under realistic conditions, data policy, device cost, support, and exit. A low introductory price can be offset by later increases or switching friction. A high headline speed can be irrelevant when local congestion, weak in-home distribution, or limited upstream capacity determines experience. The regulator data show that prices, ARPU, churn, and technology adoption move separately, so one advertised number cannot summarize value [3][4][12].

Enterprises should map dependence on access, resilience, and supplier concentration. A dual-carrier contract does not create resilience if both carriers share the same duct, tower, backhaul, gateway, or power source. An MVNO can provide excellent commercial service while depending on one host network. Satellite can provide path diversity, but gateways, terminals, weather, spectrum, and terrestrial handoff remain. Procurement should request physical and contractual dependency evidence, restoration commitments, and tested failover rather than count provider logos [10][11].

Public buyers can improve network economics by aggregating demand, offering anchor contracts, coordinating rights of way, and opening existing ducts, towers, poles, or buildings on fair terms. They should avoid exclusive arrangements that foreclose future competition without a demonstrated financing need. The author's synthesis is that the best public contract makes demand credible enough to support construction while preserving contestability after the initial subsidy or term.

### A disciplined telecommunications industry assessment

The author's synthesis is a twelve-step framework. First, define the service, geography, customer, and performance threshold. Second, map the physical and wholesale value chain. Third, identify spectrum, rights of way, sites, ducts, gateways, and other scarce inputs. Fourth, bridge authorized capacity to constructed, serviceable, subscribed, and utilized capacity. Fifth, decompose revenue into service, device, wholesale, roaming, bundle, and pass-through components. Sixth, measure ARPU, take rate, churn, acquisition, installation, and service cost by cohort. Seventh, classify capital expenditure by coverage, capacity, migration, resilience, replacement, and customer connection. Eighth, reconcile operating cash with depreciation, sustaining capital, spectrum payments, leases, and financing. Ninth, test density and scale at the correct network layer. Tenth, model sharing, wholesale, and competitor responses. Eleventh, run low-adoption, high-churn, price, regulatory, and technology-substitution cases together. Twelfth, compare return on incremental capital with the cost of capital and the value already implied by the purchase price [1][2][3][5][6][8].

This framework yields the central conclusion. Telecommunications scale is valuable because common assets, rights, systems, and customer relationships can spread cost and improve service. It is not self-protecting because every advantage can be offset by low utilization, competitive price, churn, wholesale dependence, regulation, or the next investment cycle. Durable industry economics exist where an operator converts scarce, hard-to-reproduce network capabilities into retained customer value while funding renewal without consuming the cash the network appears to generate.
