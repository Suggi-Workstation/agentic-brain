# Banking Industry Economics -- A Funding Franchise Creates Value Only While Trust, Selection, and Liquidity Hold

Commercial banks combine transaction services, deposits, wholesale funding, equity capital, lending, and fee businesses inside a regulated intermediary whose revenues often arrive before its full credit, liquidity, and compliance costs are known. A durable banking franchise therefore depends not on leverage or low deposit rates alone, but on retaining trusted funding, selecting risks that survive a cycle, operating payment and information systems reliably, and holding enough capital and liquidity to remain credible when customers can move money quickly.[3][5][9][10]

## Background

Banks arose to solve related but distinct coordination problems. Savers and businesses need safe, liquid claims that can transfer value on demand, while borrowers need larger and longer commitments than individual savers may be willing to supply directly. Banks pool deposits, screen and monitor borrowers, provide payment accounts, and transform the timing and liquidity of claims. Research recognized by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences formalized why this arrangement can improve credit allocation while remaining vulnerable to runs: depositors hold short, redeemable claims against institutions whose loans are longer-lived and difficult to liquidate without loss.[1]

The public charter changes this business from ordinary borrowing and lending into regulated infrastructure. A licensed bank may accept insured deposits and participate in payment, settlement, and central-bank arrangements under the applicable legal framework, but it must also comply with restrictions on activities, ownership, capital, liquidity, governance, risk management, and resolution. The Basel Committee's 2024 Core Principles organize minimum supervisory standards around licensing, permissible activities, ownership, consolidated supervision, governance, capital, credit risk, liquidity, operational risk, disclosure, and corrective action. Those principles are global benchmarks rather than one identical statute for every jurisdiction, and proportionality allows national authorities to apply them to institutions with different sizes and risk profiles.[9]

Deposit insurance became a central trust mechanism because individually rational withdrawals can destroy a solvent but illiquid intermediary. In the United States, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation protects eligible deposits to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each insured bank; the Deposit Insurance Fund is financed by assessments on insured institutions and income on its investments. Insurance covers deposits rather than investment products and reduces the insured customer's incentive to run, but it does not protect every balance or remove losses from shareholders, managers, uninsured creditors, or the insurance fund.[2] The broader regulatory bargain therefore couples a valuable funding privilege with supervision, insurance premiums, activity constraints, and loss-absorbing capital.[2][9]

Modern banking contains several business models rather than one uniform production function. A BIS cluster analysis separates retail-funded commercial banks, wholesale-funded commercial banks, trading-oriented banks, and universal banks by loan intensity, deposit reliance, wholesale and interbank funding, and trading activity. Retail- and wholesale-funded commercial banks can hold similar loan-heavy asset mixes while differing sharply in liabilities. Capital-markets businesses add underwriting, advisory, trading, custody, securities financing, and market-making revenue, but they also add inventory, counterparty, conduct, and market risks. Universal banks place several of these activities under one group, creating diversification and cross-selling opportunities alongside organizational and resolution complexity.[3]

The deposit franchise is both a liability structure and a distribution business. Banks spend on branches, mobile applications, call centers, cybersecurity, compliance, service, and brand in order to attract accounts that may pay below wholesale market rates. Large and small banks can serve different customer preferences rather than compete as interchangeable deposit warehouses. Research on the deposit business finds that large banks tend to provide stronger liquidity services, pay lower average rates, and attract customers with lower rate sensitivity, while smaller banks often compete more directly through yield and local relationships. Branch location, digital convenience, payment capability, perceived safety, and service quality therefore help determine the total price a depositor accepts.[4]

Physical distribution remains economically relevant even as banking digitizes. A 2025 Federal Reserve geospatial study compared household, employment, branch, and other service locations between 2014 and 2024. It found that the fall in branch counts did not produce a broad collapse in proximity to the nearest branch, although access changed unevenly and approximately 1.68 million households had low branch access in 2024. The finding distinguishes aggregate continuity from local harm: digital delivery and remaining branch density can preserve average access while closures still matter for particular rural, low-density, older, cash-dependent, or small-business customers.[7]

The industry's boundaries have also become porous. Nonbanks provide payments, mortgages, consumer credit, business credit, securities financing, asset management, private credit, and technology without always combining insured deposits, a bank charter, and a full banking balance sheet. The FSB reported that nonbank financial intermediation assets reached $256.8 trillion in 2024, or 51.0 percent of global financial assets in its monitored jurisdictions, after growing 9.4 percent during the year. Yet banks and nonbanks remain linked through deposits, loans, repo, securities holdings, custody, and credit lines. Competition can move a profit pool outside a bank while leaving the bank as funder, settlement institution, or emergency liquidity provider.[14]

This topic therefore concerns the banking playing field: who controls funding, customer access, information, risk selection, payments, and regulated balance-sheet capacity; how those positions affect competition and profits; and why apparent advantages can reverse under stress. Monetary policy matters here only when it changes funding, asset yields, competition, or customer behavior. The detailed accounting of one bank belongs in financial analysis, and the valuation of its common equity belongs in valuation. Industry economics instead asks which functions are scarce, which costs are delayed, and which structures can remain profitable without shifting unacceptable losses to depositors, counterparties, or the public.[3][9][14]

## Core Concepts

### The bank sells liquidity while holding risk

A commercial bank does not merely borrow short and lend long. It sells a bundle: a payment account, safekeeping, immediate access, operational continuity, information processing, credit commitments, and confidence that money will remain usable during stress. On the asset side it originates or purchases loans and securities whose values depend on borrower performance, collateral, interest rates, and market liquidity. On the liability side it issues deposits and other claims whose holders can demand cash, transfer balances, or decline to roll funding. Equity and retained earnings absorb losses before most creditors, while liquid assets and borrowing capacity address the different problem of paying on time.[1][9][10]

The distinction between solvency and liquidity governs the industry. A bank can own loans with positive expected value yet fail if withdrawals arrive before those loans repay and cannot be refinanced or sold without severe loss. It can also hold ample cash temporarily while its assets are worth less than its liabilities. Capital, liquidity, and earnings are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable. Capital absorbs adverse value changes, liquidity meets cash demands, and earnings replenish capital only if reported revenue ultimately exceeds funding, operating, credit, conduct, and tax costs.[9][10][11]

Commercial banks also supply contingent liquidity through credit lines. Gatev, Schuermann, and Strahan examined bank stock-return volatility and unused loan commitments and found that transactions deposits reduced the incremental volatility associated with commitments, especially during tight-liquidity periods. Their result identifies a useful portfolio synergy: customers may draw credit lines when market funding is scarce while other customers move money into transaction deposits. The hedge is empirical and conditional, not a guarantee that every deposit base is stable or every commitment book is safe.[6]

### Deposits are a franchise, a service obligation, and a run option

A deposit franchise has value when customers accept a total package whose explicit interest cost is below an equivalent market funding rate after service and operating costs. The package may include branches, mobile access, bill payment, payroll, cash management, cards, fraud protection, advice, and integration with lending. The relevant cost is therefore not the posted deposit rate alone. It includes acquisition, technology, service, compliance, insurance assessments, incentives, and the liquidity required to meet withdrawals. A bank that reports cheap deposits but spends heavily to acquire transient balances may not possess a low-cost franchise.[2][4][11]

Deposit competition is heterogeneous. Rate-sensitive customers can compare online offers and move balances, while operating accounts may remain because payment integration, payroll, receivables, treasury workflows, and switching effort matter. Large institutions can use national brands, dense payment capabilities, and broad product sets; community banks can use local knowledge, relationship continuity, and responsiveness. The NBER study of large and small banks attributes much of their pricing difference to customer preferences, location, and technology rather than treating concentration as the sole explanation. This means a lower deposit rate can reflect a valued liquidity service, market power, customer inertia, or some combination that must be tested rather than assumed.[4]

The same franchise can become runnable. Drechsler, Savov, Schnabl, and Wang model deposits priced below market as a valuable bank asset that disappears when balances leave. In their deposit-franchise-run mechanism, a run can occur even when loans are liquid because the loss of low-cost funding destroys franchise value. High rates can increase the value at risk, and uninsured balances make the withdrawal option more consequential. Their model implies that a large spread between market and deposit rates is not pure durable profit; it can represent an asset whose survival depends on customer retention and sufficient capital.[5]

Insurance changes but does not eliminate this mechanism. Insured retail balances have less reason to run for credit protection, while uninsured corporate, institutional, or high-net-worth balances may move quickly if they question solvency or need operational certainty. Concentration matters independently of the insured share: many accounts controlled by customers in one industry, investor network, treasury platform, or advisory channel can behave as one funding source. The 2023 turmoil showed that digital access and rapid information transmission can compress withdrawals that older planning assumptions spread across days or weeks.[2][10]

### Asset-liability management converts a spread into a survival problem

Net interest income begins with the difference between returns on loans and securities and the cost of deposits and other funding. That spread is not locked in. Assets and liabilities reprice on different schedules; borrowers can prepay or default; depositors can demand higher rates or leave; hedges require collateral and counterparties; and securities may lose market value before maturity. The bank must manage both near-term income sensitivity and the economic value of longer cash flows. A favorable current margin can coexist with a large duration loss or with credit exposures whose cost appears later.[10][11][12]

Wholesale funding expands capacity but changes the failure mode. Interbank borrowing, brokered deposits, repurchase agreements, Federal Home Loan Bank advances, commercial paper, and long-term debt can diversify or replace customer deposits and can be raised in large amounts. They also tend to reprice more directly with markets, depend on collateral or continued access, and can contract when confidence falls. The BIS business-model evidence shows that liability composition differentiates loan-heavy retail-funded and wholesale-funded banks even when their asset profiles look similar. Two banks making similar loans can therefore have different profitability and resilience because one funds through diversified customer relationships and the other through price-sensitive markets.[3]

Asset selection must be evaluated together with funding. Relationship lending can produce information and switching costs that improve pricing or credit decisions, but it can also concentrate a bank in local property, one industry, or correlated borrowers. Marketable securities can supply liquidity but lose value when rates rise. Floating-rate loans protect asset yield but can weaken borrowers. Fee commitments and undrawn lines create contingent claims. The author's synthesis is that every asset should be paired with the liability behavior, capital consumption, liquidity demand, and operational process it creates; product-level revenue without that pairing is not an industry profit measure.[6][9][11]

### Credit selection reveals costs slowly

A loan produces interest before the lender knows its final loss. Early delinquency can remain low during expansion, collateral can appear strong near a cycle peak, and a newly originated portfolio has not seasoned through adverse conditions. Reported growth can therefore combine genuine customer value with weaker underwriting, longer maturities, looser covenants, higher concentrations, or unrecognized fraud. The bank's economic cost includes expected loss, unexpected loss borne by capital, collection and workout expense, liquidity effects, and the opportunity cost of capacity tied to a weak borrower.[9][11]

Selection occurs through information, incentives, and process. Banks decide which markets to enter, what data to collect, which exceptions to permit, how loan officers are paid, who can approve concentration, and how independent review challenges growth. Scale can improve data, model development, specialization, and diversification, but centralized systems may discard soft information or propagate one model error across a large portfolio. Local relationship banking can learn from repeated contact but may produce correlated exposures or weak challenge. Neither size nor intimacy is a substitute for observed cohort performance and conservative loss recognition.[4][7][9]

Regulation influences the price and supply of credit by assigning capital, liquidity, governance, disclosure, and examination requirements. These constraints can make an apparently profitable loan unattractive after risk and capital are recognized. They can also push activity toward institutions or contracts outside the bank perimeter. The FSB's monitoring shows that nonbanks are not simply external competitors: banks finance them, take their deposits, provide repo and derivatives, hold or distribute their securities, and share exposures to the same assets. Regulatory migration can therefore change where risk is booked without removing banking-system dependence on it.[14]

### Profit pools differ across retail, commercial, and investment banking

Retail banking earns spreads and fees from household deposits, mortgages, cards, consumer loans, and payments. Its advantages can include numerous accounts, insured funding, recurring transactions, and cross-product data. Its costs include large service and technology platforms, fraud, credit losses, branch or acquisition expense, consumer compliance, and price competition. A retail deposit is valuable only after the cost of maintaining its account and the stability of its behavior are measured; a card balance is profitable only after funding, rewards, fraud, servicing, and credit loss.[2][4]

Commercial banking serves businesses through operating accounts, cash management, payments, revolving lines, term loans, trade finance, custody, and advisory services. The operating relationship can produce stable balances and information about cash flows, while the credit line can make the bank a liquidity insurer. Profit can be distributed across deposit spreads, loan spreads, treasury fees, foreign exchange, and ancillary services, so a single product may look unattractive while the relationship is profitable. This logic becomes dangerous when cross-sell expectations excuse underpriced credit or when concentrated operating deposits are assumed to be permanent.[5][6]

Investment and wholesale banking earn underwriting, advisory, trading, market-making, custody, prime brokerage, securities financing, and transaction fees. These businesses can be less dependent on traditional loans but more exposed to markets, counterparties, operational complexity, and volatile activity. A universal group may diversify revenue and distribute products across a broad client base, yet it must allocate capital, liquidity, risk limits, and control cost among units that can transfer exposures to one another. The BIS evidence finds clearer and more stable performance for commercial models than for trading-oriented models over its sample, while also showing that classifications can change as banks alter strategy.[3]

Payments and fee businesses can strengthen a deposit franchise because customers are less likely to move an account embedded in payroll, receivables, cards, or treasury systems. They can also be separate profit pools contested by networks, processors, software firms, fintechs, and nonbank platforms. The bank may own the deposit and authorization decision while another firm controls the customer interface. An industry map should therefore assign the customer relationship, transaction fee, credit loss, fraud loss, settlement obligation, data, and regulatory burden to the participant that actually bears each item rather than treating all payment volume as bank revenue.[13][14]

### Scale lowers some costs and raises other ones

Banking contains fixed costs in core systems, cybersecurity, compliance, data, model governance, product development, and access to markets. Scale can spread those costs, support specialist teams, diversify exposures, and improve bargaining with vendors and counterparties. It can also finance wider branch and digital distribution. The international evidence reviewed by Federal Reserve researchers, however, found that consolidation benefits were clearest at relatively small sizes and that evidence for broad economies of scope or managerial-efficiency gains was limited. Some mergers raised revenue rather than simply reducing cost, and integration gains could take years to emerge.[8]

Large size also creates complexity, internal transfer-pricing problems, correlated strategies, harder resolution, and systemic externalities. A broad group can move funding or risk across legal entities only when rules, collateral, systems, and local supervisors permit it. Technology scale can make service cheap per transaction while making an outage consequential to more customers. Market perception of public support can lower funding cost without representing productive efficiency. The author's synthesis is that scale should be decomposed into unit-cost savings, service gains, diversification, bargaining power, integration expense, control burden, and externalized failure cost.[8][9][10]

Consolidation changes competition unevenly. A merger can preserve a threatened community franchise, combine technology budgets, diversify exposures, or remove duplicated capacity. It can also reduce local deposit or small-business alternatives, increase switching frictions, and create an institution whose complexity absorbs the expected savings. National digital competition does not automatically erase local differentiation, just as branch presence does not prove that customers lack online options. Market definition should follow the product and customer: operating accounts, insured household deposits, relationship credit, syndicated loans, custody, and trading may have different relevant competitors.[4][7][8]

### Regulation is part of the production function

Compliance is not merely an overhead line added after a bank chooses its products. Licensing determines entry; capital and liquidity rules affect product economics; consumer and conduct rules shape distribution; anti-money-laundering controls influence onboarding; resolution requirements affect legal structure and eligible liabilities; and supervision can force remediation or constrain growth. Regulation can support trust and lower the private cost of funding by making the charter credible, while also limiting leverage and requiring resources that nonbank competitors may not bear in the same form.[2][9]

The regulatory perimeter therefore affects competition. If two firms provide economically similar credit or payment services under different loss-absorption, liquidity, disclosure, and resolution rules, the lower private price may partly reflect risk shifted to customers, counterparties, or public backstops. Equal regulation is not always appropriate because business models differ, but the comparison must include the state-contingent promise each firm makes. The FSB's bank-nonbank linkage data and the Basel Core Principles support a functional question: who can run, who supplies emergency funding, where losses land, and which authority can intervene before disruption spreads?[9][14]

## Evidence

### Balance-sheet clusters identify distinct business models

Roengpitya, Tarashev, Tsatsaronis, and Villegas use cluster analysis on balance-sheet ratios for 178 banks over 2005-2015 to classify business models. Their robust categories include retail-funded and wholesale-funded commercial banks, while trading and universal models are less stable across specifications. The two commercial models are both loan-intensive but differ in deposits, stable funding, wholesale debt, and interbank activity. They report lower cost-to-income ratios and more stable return on equity for the commercial models than for the trading model; banks switching into the retail-funded model improved relative return on equity by an average 2.5 percentage points, while banks switching into wholesale funding deteriorated by 5 percentage points relative to non-switchers.[3]

The method matters because it does not infer a business model from a bank's name. It groups institutions from observable balance-sheet choices and then examines performance and transitions. Its sample contains large banks and ends in 2015, so the findings do not establish a permanent ranking for every jurisdiction or later digital entrant. The defensible conclusion is narrower: liability structure carries economically important information beyond the loan share, and post-crisis movement toward retail funding was associated with better relative performance in that sample.[3]

### Deposit research separates service value from a low posted rate

D'Avernas, Eisfeldt, Huang, Stanton, and Wallace combine a model of bank location and service choice with empirical evidence on rates, branches, local demographics, and depositor sensitivity. They find that large banks offer stronger liquidity services, pay lower deposit rates on average, and serve customers with lower rate elasticity; small banks consistently pay higher rates in their data. Their counterfactual analysis identifies branch location as a primary driver of profitability and rate-setting flexibility as a secondary but material factor. The study challenges a one-variable claim that low deposit rates arise solely from concentration.[4]

This evidence supports treating deposit spread as the price of a bundle rather than as a free funding advantage. It does not prove that every large bank provides value equal to the forgone interest or that customer inertia has no role. A bank-specific or market-specific analysis still needs switching behavior, service use, complaints, account fees, digital quality, and actual retention. The method's contribution is to show how technology, location, and heterogeneous preferences can jointly produce different deposit business models.[4]

Drechsler, Savov, Schnabl, and Wang provide a complementary structural model rather than a branch-demand estimate. They model below-market deposits as a franchise asset that vanishes when funds leave and use the model to identify vulnerable banks around the 2023 regional-bank crisis. A key result is that shortening assets to reduce run exposure can create insolvency exposure when rates fall; capital is needed to protect against both states. The study shows why franchise value and run risk are two sides of the same low-cost funding position.[5]

Gatev, Schuermann, and Strahan use bank stock-return volatility, unused commitments, and transaction-deposit measures to test a different mechanism. They find that unused commitments increase volatility less at banks with more transaction deposits and that this synergy strengthens during tight-liquidity conditions. Read with the franchise-run model, the evidence rejects both simple extremes. Deposits can hedge credit-line draws at the portfolio level, yet concentrated or uninsured deposits can still run when confidence in one institution breaks.[5][6]

### Branch consolidation has not affected every place equally

Adams and Sherlund construct a geospatial dataset linking branches, households, workers, and other service locations and compare access from 2014 to 2024. Their first result is that branch decline did not materially increase the typical distance from households and businesses to the nearest office. Their distributional results still identify low-access households and small changes in particular low-density and rural areas. For people whose access declined, increases in nearest-branch distance were generally less than 0.1 mile, while approximately 1.68 million households met the study's low-access measure in 2024.[7]

The method improves on counting closures because one closing can have little effect where alternatives remain and a large effect where none do. The result also does not measure every dimension of service: travel time, disability access, language, cash needs, broadband, relationship continuity, and small-business credit may differ even at the same distance. The industry implication is to analyze distribution at the customer and location level rather than declaring either that branches are obsolete or that every closure creates a banking desert.[7]

### Consolidation evidence makes scale conditional

Amel, Barnes, Panetta, and Salleo review international empirical work on financial-sector consolidation and efficiency. They find evidence that mergers can capture scale economies up to relatively modest size, but little general evidence that combinations reliably produce economies of scope or managerial-efficiency gains. Some studies find revenue improvements, risk changes, or delayed gains rather than immediate cost reduction. They also emphasize measurement choices: a merger can raise service quality and cost together, or appear efficient only because risk is not fully recognized.[8]

This review does not resolve the current optimal bank size because technology, regulation, and product delivery have changed since its 2002 publication. It does supply a durable methodological warning. A merger thesis should specify which systems, branches, funding sources, risks, and products create the gain; identify integration cost and timing; and test whether lower observed cost reflects true productivity, greater risk, market power, or a public-support advantage. Aggregate asset size is not the mechanism.[8]

### Fintech competition compresses some margins and changes operations

Xu uses Brazilian bank-level data and variation in exposure to fintech competition to estimate effects on incumbent commercial banks. The paper reports that a one-standard-deviation increase in fintech exposure reduced average commercial-bank lending rates by 3.7 percentage points, improved operational efficiency, narrowed net interest margins, and reduced overall profitability. It finds no discernible aggregate change in nonperforming-loan or provisioning ratios and reports that banks shifted toward higher-value, lower-risk borrowers and more favorable terms. The institutional setting includes digital banks, nonbank credit companies, digital account opening, and the Pix instant-payment system.[13]

The result is a country-specific causal estimate, not a universal coefficient for all banking systems. Brazil's rates, regulation, market structure, and digital infrastructure shape the magnitude. The mechanism is still useful: technology can lower distribution and search cost, intensify price competition, and force incumbents to improve operations without immediately changing realized credit loss. It also shows why fintech is not one category. A deposit-taking digital bank, a balance-sheet nonbank lender, a peer-to-peer platform, and a payment interface compete with different bank functions and use different funding.[13]

### Current official data show profits and latent costs together

The FDIC's second-quarter 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile aggregates regulatory reports from 4,238 insured commercial banks and savings institutions. It reports $90.1 billion of quarterly net income, a 1.37 percent return on assets, a 3.32 percent net interest margin, and an eighth consecutive quarterly increase in domestic deposits. At the same time, it reports $326.7 billion of unrealized losses on held-to-maturity and available-for-sale securities and continuing weakness in particular commercial real-estate portfolios. This is a descriptive industry snapshot, not a causal study or proof that the median bank shares the aggregate profile.[12]

The FDIC's 2026 Risk Review adds an annual and risk-specific decomposition. It reports that funding costs fell faster than asset yields in 2025, raising the industry net interest margin by 8 basis points to 3.30 percent, while unrealized securities losses remained elevated and banks continued to build borrowing capacity. It also identifies lending to nondepository financial institutions as one of the fastest-growing post-crisis loan segments and notes concentration at the largest banks. Read together, the two reports show that current earnings, deposit growth, securities marks, wholesale borrowing, and off-bank credit exposure must be analyzed separately before they are combined into a conclusion about industry quality.[11][12]

### The 2023 turmoil demonstrates nonlinear funding failure

The Basel Committee's report on the 2023 banking turmoil is a supervisory synthesis of failures and market stress across the United States and Switzerland. It identifies rapid growth, concentrated business models, weak governance and risk management, long-duration assets, reliance on uninsured deposits, and deficient contingency funding as interacting vulnerabilities. At Silicon Valley Bank, announced securities losses and a planned capital raise preceded withdrawals of about $42 billion on March 9 and requests for approximately $100 billion more on March 10. Other institutions experienced distinct combinations of confidence loss, concentrated deposits, and operational liquidity constraints.[10]

The evidence does not support reducing every failure to social media, unrealized losses, or one missing ratio. It supports a joint mechanism: a fragile business model can remain profitable while confidence holds, then lose funding faster than assets can be sold, pledged, or refinanced. The Committee concludes that liquidity standards cannot prevent every run and that supervision, operational preparedness, governance, and credible funding plans remain necessary. This is the clearest case for treating low-cost funding as conditional rather than permanent.[10]

### Nonbank growth changes rather than removes the banking boundary

The FSB's 2025 monitoring exercise covers 29 jurisdictions representing more than 90 percent of global GDP. It reports that nonbank financial intermediation grew 9.4 percent in 2024 to $256.8 trillion, twice the growth rate of banking in the monitored aggregate, and represented 51.0 percent of global financial assets. Its bank-nonbank case study identifies deposits placed by nonbanks, bank lending and repo to nonbanks, and nonbank holdings of bank securities as principal links. This broad sector-balance-sheet method reveals system structure but cannot by itself determine the risk of one private-credit loan or investment fund.[14]

The evidence qualifies the claim that nonbanks simply displace banks. They can take customer-facing lending, payments, or investment activity while relying on bank accounts, credit lines, leverage, custody, settlement, or securities distribution. The banking profit pool may migrate from loan spread to funding, infrastructure, or risk-management fees, while a stress can send contingent demands back to bank balance sheets. Competitive analysis therefore requires both market share and dependency mapping.[14]

## Implications

### For industry analysts: build the bank from funding outward

Begin with liability behavior rather than a generic asset-growth forecast. Separate insured household deposits, uninsured operating balances, rate-sensitive savings, brokered deposits, public funds, interbank liabilities, secured borrowing, and long-term debt. For each source, record explicit rate, service cost, concentration, collateral, insurance status, behavioral duration, withdrawal channel, and access under stress. The deposit franchise should be measured after branches, technology, payments, compliance, insurance assessments, and acquisition expense, not by comparing the posted deposit rate with a market benchmark alone.[2][4][5]

Then map assets and commitments to the liabilities and capital they consume. Loans should be grouped by borrower, collateral, repricing, vintage, covenant, concentration, and loss emergence rather than only by accounting label. Securities should be tested for duration, market value, pledgeability, and legal location. Undrawn commitments, derivatives, settlement obligations, and collateral calls should enter the same liquidity scenarios as deposit withdrawals. The output is not one ratio but a state-contingent map of cash, loss, and funding demands.[6][9][10][11]

A profit-pool bridge should separate net interest income, credit cost, fee revenue, trading and advisory revenue, payment income, operating expense, conduct cost, capital use, and liquidity insurance. Retail, commercial, and investment banking should not be combined until internal transfers are reconciled. A cheap deposit allocated to one business cannot also be credited in full to another; a cross-sold service is valuable only if the relationship's total return exceeds its risk and support cost. The author's synthesis is that business-line profit should be restated after a common funding curve, expected loss, unexpected-loss capital, and operational burden.[3][9][12]

Finally, distinguish a cyclical tailwind from a structural franchise. A steeper curve, temporarily slow deposit repricing, benign credit, or securities gains can widen current earnings without improving customer loyalty or underwriting. Conversely, technology and compliance spending can depress current efficiency while strengthening service and control. The FDIC's 2025-2026 evidence shows both improved margins and persistent unrealized losses, so the analyst should explain which earnings can repeat after deposit rates, credit costs, and asset marks normalize rather than annualize the latest quarter.[11][12]

### For bank management: price the full relationship and rehearse its failure

Management should assign every relationship a complete economic ledger. For a household, include balance stability, rate, fees, payment activity, service contacts, fraud, credit products, and acquisition cost. For a business, include operating deposits, treasury services, payment flows, committed and drawn credit, collateral, foreign exchange, advice, and concentration. Relationship pricing is defensible when profitable services support one another transparently; it becomes a control failure when hoped-for cross-sell is used to hide an underpriced loan or when uninsured operating deposits are treated as permanent capital.[4][5][6]

Deposit retention should be studied as behavior, not asserted as a label. Segment customers by purpose, insurance, common ownership, common adviser, transfer capability, rate sensitivity, and communication network. Compare modeled decay with observed flows during rate changes and adverse news. A long historical life can reflect an environment that never tested digital mobility or solvency concern. The 2023 evidence warrants intraday and multiday scenarios in which concentrated balances leave together and public disclosures alter customer behavior before ordinary committees can meet.[5][10]

Contingency funding must be operational. Management should know which assets are unencumbered, where they are held, which legal entity owns them, what haircuts apply, which documents and approvals are needed, whether collateral can move, and whether borrowing channels have been tested. A nominal liquidity total is not available cash if systems, custodians, legal restrictions, or market confidence prevent timely monetization. The worst case is a bank that discovers during a run that its supposed buffer cannot reach the entity or payment system experiencing outflows.[9][10]

Growth controls should follow delayed cost. New cohorts need vintage tracking, independent review, concentration limits, and compensation that reflects eventual credit performance rather than origination volume alone. Fee and trading businesses require conduct, counterparty, and tail-risk attribution. Digital onboarding needs fraud, identity, cyber, and service controls at the same scale as customer acquisition. The principle is simple: no growth metric should mature faster than the evidence needed to test its cost without an explicit conservative allowance for uncertainty.[9][11]

### For regulators: protect the function without freezing the industry

Regulators should treat trust infrastructure as an economic input. Clear licensing, credible supervision, deposit insurance, resolution capacity, and central-bank liquidity arrangements can make deposits and payments more reliable and lower the private cost of intermediation. Those benefits create moral hazard if owners and managers retain upside while public mechanisms absorb avoidable loss. Capital, insurance assessments, governance, corrective action, and credible loss allocation are therefore the price of the charter's funding advantage, not unrelated burdens.[1][2][9]

Proportionality should reflect risk rather than size alone. A smaller institution with rapid growth, concentrated uninsured funding, long-duration assets, weak operational liquidity, or a specialized customer network can transmit stress. A large diversified bank may have stronger systems while creating greater complexity and systemic consequence. Supervisory intensity should therefore incorporate growth, funding concentration, business-model opacity, substitutability, interconnectedness, and execution capability alongside total assets.[9][10]

Competition policy should define markets by function and customer. Local branches may remain important for cash, advice, soft information, and small-business relationships even when national digital offers discipline deposit rates. A merger can improve technology and resilience while reducing a community's practical alternatives. Review should test branch and digital access, deposit pricing, relationship-credit continuity, system integration, and resolution consequences rather than relying on institution count or national market share alone.[4][7][8]

The perimeter should follow dependency as well as legal form. Nonbank credit and payment providers can improve competition, reduce lending rates, and force bank efficiency, as the Brazilian evidence suggests. They can also depend on bank funding, deposits, repo, settlement, or emergency lines, as the FSB data show. Regulators should map those links and align disclosure, liquidity, counterparty, and consumer safeguards with the actual promise made, while avoiding the assumption that every nonbank activity should be regulated exactly like an insured deposit-taking bank.[13][14]

### For customers and corporate treasurers: convenience and yield are not the only variables

Depositors should distinguish insured from uninsured balances and understand that coverage applies by depositor, insured bank, and ownership category. A high quoted rate may be appropriate compensation, a temporary acquisition offer, or a sign that a bank needs funding; the rate alone does not determine safety. Operating customers should consider payment continuity, fraud controls, service access, transfer limits, legal ownership, and contingency accounts in addition to yield.[2]

Corporate treasurers should map cash by bank, legal entity, ownership category, operational purpose, and insurance status. They should know which accounts support payroll, collections, collateral, or settlement and how quickly alternatives can be activated. Diversifying balances can reduce one-bank exposure but create reconciliation and control burdens, so the design should be tested before a crisis. The aim is not to react to every rumor but to make failure of one provider survivable without contributing to avoidable disorder.[2][10]

Borrowers should also evaluate the durability of credit, not only its initial spread. A line from a bank with stable funding and a broad relationship can be more valuable in stress, while a nonbank or capital-markets alternative may offer speed, specialization, or price. The Gatev evidence suggests that deposits and commitments can complement one another during market stress, but no lender is immune to its own funding constraint. Firms should understand covenants, renewal rights, collateral, syndication, draw conditions, and the lender's capacity across a cycle.[6][14]

### For long-term capital allocation: avoid mistaking leverage for a moat

An attractive banking industry structure combines trusted and diversified funding, disciplined risk selection, efficient distribution, reliable payments, useful customer information, scalable controls, and capital that protects the franchise in adverse states. None is sufficient alone. High leverage can magnify a small spread while leaving little room for error; a large branch or digital network can acquire customers whose balances are unprofitable; fee growth can conceal conduct or counterparty risk; and consolidation can create bargaining power without durable efficiency.[4][8][9]

The most important inversion is to ask what destroys the apparent advantage. A low-cost deposit franchise fails when customers leave, demand full market rates, or lose trust. Scale fails when integration, complexity, outages, or systemic costs outrun unit savings. Relationship lending fails when familiarity weakens challenge or concentrates risk. Regulation fails when compliance replaces judgment or activity migrates beyond visibility. Diversification fails when different businesses depend on the same confidence, collateral, or liquidity source.[5][8][10][14]

The author's synthesis is a ten-part industry test. First, identify the charter and public protections. Second, decompose funding by cost, insurance, concentration, and behavior. Third, map customer distribution across branch, digital, and intermediated channels. Fourth, separate payment, lending, treasury, advisory, and market businesses. Fifth, assign credit, market, liquidity, conduct, operational, and counterparty costs to their sources. Sixth, measure scale benefits after integration and control expense. Seventh, test switching costs for customer value as well as inertia. Eighth, map nonbank competitors and dependencies. Ninth, stress asset losses and funding outflows together. Tenth, ask whether capital and liquidity preserve the franchise without extraordinary support.[2][3][4][9][10][14]

This framework yields the industry's central conclusion. Banking can create durable economic value because a trusted intermediary joins money, information, credit, and payments more efficiently than isolated contracts can. That value survives only when the bank prices the complete cost of its promises and can perform them through a cycle; otherwise low funding cost, rapid growth, and leverage are not moats but deferred evidence of fragility.[1][5][6][10]
