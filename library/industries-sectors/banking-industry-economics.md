# Banking Industry Economics -- A Funding Franchise Creates Value Only While Trust, Selection, and Liquidity Hold

Commercial banks combine transaction services, deposits, wholesale funding, equity capital, lending, and fee businesses inside regulated intermediaries with materially different asset and liability models, while credit, liquidity, and compliance costs can emerge after revenue is booked.[3][9][10] Low-beta uninsured deposit franchises can also lose value if balances leave.[5] The author's synthesis is that a durable banking franchise requires trusted funding, risk selection that survives a cycle, reliable operating systems, and enough capital and liquidity to remain credible when customers can move money quickly.

## Background

Banks address related but distinct coordination problems. Savers and businesses need safe, liquid claims that can transfer value on demand, while borrowers need longer commitments than individual savers may be willing to supply directly. In the Diamond-Dybvig model summarized by the Royal Swedish Academy, banks pool deposits, screen and monitor borrowers, provide payment accounts, and transform the timing and liquidity of claims. The arrangement can improve credit allocation while remaining vulnerable to runs because depositors hold short, redeemable claims against institutions whose loans are longer-lived and difficult to liquidate without loss.[1]

The public charter changes this business from ordinary borrowing and lending into regulated infrastructure. Taking deposits from the public is generally reserved for licensed and supervised banks; access to deposit insurance and payment, settlement, or central-bank facilities depends on jurisdiction-specific eligibility. Banks are subject to rules and supervision covering permissible activities, ownership, capital, liquidity, governance, risk management, and recovery or resolution. The Basel Committee's 2024 Core Principles organize minimum supervisory standards around those functions. They are global benchmarks rather than one identical statute for every jurisdiction, and proportionality makes requirements commensurate with an institution's risk profile and systemic importance rather than size alone.[9]

Deposit insurance became a central trust mechanism because, in the Diamond-Dybvig model, withdrawals can force an intermediary to liquidate long-term assets at losses; a credible guarantee removes an insured depositor's reason to join a rumor-driven run.[1] In the United States, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation protects eligible deposits to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each insured bank. The Deposit Insurance Fund is financed by assessments on insured institutions and interest earned on funds invested in U.S. government obligations. Insurance covers eligible deposits rather than non-deposit investment products, and balances above the applicable limits may remain uninsured.[2] The author's interpretation is that this funding privilege must be paired with supervision, insurance assessments, activity constraints, and loss-absorbing capital to limit moral hazard.[1][9]

Modern banking contains several business models rather than one uniform production function. A BIS cluster analysis of 178 banks across 34 jurisdictions separates robust retail-funded and wholesale-funded commercial-bank models from less robustly identified trading and universal clusters. The two commercial models can hold similar loan-heavy asset mixes while differing sharply in deposits, stable funding, wholesale debt, and interbank activity.[3] The author's industry map treats underwriting, advisory, trading, custody, securities financing, and market-making as distinct capital-markets profit pools with inventory, counterparty, conduct, and market risks. These activities may coexist within a universal group, but the BIS paper's universal category is a statistical hybrid rather than evidence that every member offers the complete service set.[3]

The deposit franchise is both a liability structure and a distribution business. U.S. evidence for 2001-2020 compares 14 designated large banks with smaller institutions and finds that the large banks provide stronger measured liquidity services, pay lower rates on average, and serve customers with lower rate sensitivity. The paper models branches, service technology, and per-unit servicing cost rather than treating the posted rate as the complete price; it also describes small banks as competing through local knowledge, personalized service, and community ties.[4] A later BIS working paper finds that U.S. digital banks paid higher savings and small-time-deposit rates, showed somewhat stronger policy-rate pass-through, and responded more to social-media activity than traditional banks over its sample.[20] Together these findings show that branch location, digital capability, customer preferences, and service quality can differentiate deposit competition, but they do not make every large, small, or digital bank interchangeable within its category.

Physical distribution remains economically relevant even as banking digitizes. A 2025 Federal Reserve Board staff FEDS paper links 2010 and 2020 census-block household and employment data to branch locations in mid-2014 and mid-2024, together with service-location and mobile-coverage data from other vintages. Despite a 19 percent decline in branches, average household nearest-branch distance rose only 0.02 to 0.28 mile across density groups. In 2024, 1.68 million households met the paper's relative low-access definition, about half in rural blocks. Its mobile analysis measures potential coverage overlap, not a causal digital-substitution effect.[7] Separate Federal Reserve field research found that rural closures particularly burdened some older customers, small businesses, cash users, and people without reliable transport, illustrating why modest aggregate distance changes do not rule out local harm.[25]

The industry's boundaries have also become porous. Nonbanks provide credit, securities financing, asset management, and private finance without combining all the functions of an insured commercial bank. The FSB reports that nonbank financial intermediation assets reached $256.8 trillion in 2024, or 51.0 percent of total financial assets in its 29-jurisdiction monitoring aggregate, after 9.4 percent growth. Banks and nonbanks remain linked through deposits, lending, repo and derivatives, securities holdings, custody and other client services, and credit lines. Activity can therefore shift outside banks while funding and risk-transmission dependencies remain.[14]

The topic's analytical scope is the banking playing field: who controls funding, customer access, information, risk selection, payments, and regulated balance-sheet capacity; how those positions affect competition and profits; and why apparent advantages can reverse under stress. Funding and activity mix differ across bank models,[3] regulation defines important production constraints,[9] and bank-nonbank dependencies extend the competitive map beyond chartered banks.[14] Monetary policy matters here only when it changes those industry variables. Detailed accounting of one bank belongs in financial analysis, and valuation of its common equity belongs in valuation.

## Core Concepts

### The bank sells liquidity while holding risk

A commercial bank does not merely borrow short and lend long. It provides payment accounts and immediate access to deposits while channeling funds into longer-term loans and evaluating and monitoring borrowers.[1] It also sells operational continuity, credit commitments, and confidence that balances will remain usable during stress. On the asset side it holds loans and securities whose values depend on borrower performance, collateral, interest rates, and market liquidity. On the liability side it issues deposits and other claims whose holders can demand cash, transfer balances, or decline to roll funding. Regulatory capital is intended to absorb recognized losses, while liquid assets and borrowing capacity meet cash outflows; operational-resilience standards require critical services to continue through disruption.[9][10]

The distinction between solvency and liquidity governs the industry. A bank can own loans with positive expected value yet fail if withdrawals arrive before those loans repay and cannot be refinanced or sold without severe loss. It can also hold ample cash temporarily while its assets are worth less than its liabilities. Capital, liquidity, and earnings are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable. Capital absorbs losses recognized under the applicable accounting and prudential treatment, liquidity meets cash demands, and earnings replenish capital only if revenue ultimately exceeds funding, operating, credit, conduct, and tax costs.[9][10] The FDIC's 2026 Risk Review illustrates the separation: 2025 net interest margins improved while unrealized securities losses remained elevated and banks increased borrowing capacity.[11]

Commercial banks also supply contingent liquidity through credit lines. In a 1990-2002 sample of large publicly traded U.S. banks, Gatev, Schuermann, and Strahan found that the positive association between unused commitments and bank stock-return volatility was smaller at banks with higher transaction-deposit shares, especially when commercial-paper/Treasury-bill spreads were wide.[6] The authors interpret this as a conditional portfolio synergy: customers may draw credit lines when market funding is scarce while other customers move money into transaction deposits. The result is observational and does not guarantee that every deposit base is stable or every commitment book is safe.

### Deposits are a franchise, a service obligation, and a run option

A deposit franchise has value when customers accept a package whose interest cost is below a comparable market benchmark after operating cost. In the large-versus-small-bank model, relevant costs include per-unit servicing, fixed branch expense, and fixed investment in liquidity-service technology; U.S. deposit-insurance assessments are an additional cost borne by insured institutions.[2][4] The author's full service map adds mobile access, bill payment, payroll, cash management, cards, fraud protection, advice, and integration with lending. A bank reporting cheap deposits does not necessarily possess a low-cost franchise unless acquisition, technology, service, compliance, and liquidity costs are included.

Deposit competition is heterogeneous. The large-versus-small-bank study attributes much of the observed pricing difference in its U.S. sample to customer preferences, branch location, and service technology rather than concentration alone; it also finds lower average rate sensitivity among customers of its 14 designated large banks.[4] The digital-bank study finds higher savings and small-time-deposit rates and greater pass-through at identified digital banks, with stronger responses where social-media activity is higher.[20] The author's synthesis is that operating workflows and switching effort may make some balances less rate-sensitive, while transparent online offers make others easier to move. A low deposit rate can therefore reflect valued service, market power, inertia, or a combination that must be tested rather than assumed.

The same franchise can become runnable. Drechsler, Savov, Schnabl, and Wang model the future spread on low-beta uninsured deposits as a valuable bank asset that disappears when balances leave. In their deposit-franchise-run mechanism, withdrawals can destroy that value even when bank assets are fully liquid. Higher interest rates increase the franchise value at risk, and sufficient capital must cover its potential loss. The authors emphasize that uninsured deposits alone are not the mechanism; vulnerability arises from the low-beta uninsured portion whose below-market pricing creates franchise value.[5]

Insurance changes but does not eliminate withdrawal risk. Balances within applicable FDIC coverage limits have less reason to run for protection against bank credit loss, whereas balances above those limits remain exposed absent an exception.[1][2] FDIC transaction-level evidence from the three spring 2023 failures found that fully insured retail depositors generally did not run, while large depositors were more likely to withdraw even after insurance status and deposit type were considered; the largest 0.5 percent of depositors held 39-62 percent of domestic deposits at the three banks.[15] The 2023 turmoil also showed that mobile access and rapid information transmission can remove frictions and accelerate outflows; the Basel report discusses daily or intraday monitoring rather than assuming a bank has the LCR's 30-day horizon to respond.[10][21]

### Asset-liability management converts a spread into a survival problem

Net interest income begins with the difference between returns on loans and securities and the cost of deposits and other funding. That spread is not locked in. The OCC identifies repricing, basis, yield-curve, and options risk, including borrower prepayments, depositor withdrawal and pricing options, and mismatched asset and liability horizons.[16] Banks therefore evaluate both near-term earnings sensitivity and the economic value of longer cash flows; a favorable current margin can coexist with a large duration loss.[9][16] The FDIC reported this coexistence in recent aggregates: funding costs fell more than asset yields in 2025, raising net interest margin, while unrealized securities losses remained elevated.[11]

The author's liability taxonomy includes interbank borrowing, brokered deposits, repurchase agreements, Federal Home Loan Bank advances, commercial paper, and long-term debt as alternatives or complements to customer deposits. Market-linked sources may reprice rapidly, depend on collateral or continued access, and contract when confidence falls. The FDIC specifically documents banks' use of brokered deposits, Federal Home Loan Bank advances, and pledged borrowing capacity in its 2026 funding review.[11] The BIS business-model evidence shows that loan-heavy retail- and wholesale-funded banks can have similar asset profiles but materially different deposit, stable-funding, wholesale-debt, and interbank positions.[3] The author's synthesis is that similar loan books can therefore have different resilience because their liabilities behave differently under stress.

Asset selection must be evaluated together with funding. Relationship lending can accumulate soft information through repeated loan-officer contact, while organizational layers can impede its collection and transmission.[17] The same relationship can concentrate a bank in local property, one industry, or correlated borrowers; that concentration warning is the author's synthesis. Marketable securities can supply liquidity but lose value when rates rise, and floating-rate loans can protect asset yield while increasing borrower payment burdens.[11][16] Undrawn credit lines create contingent liquidity demands; in Gatev et al.'s sample, transaction deposits partially hedged their association with stock-return volatility.[6] The author's synthesis is that every asset should be paired with the liability behavior, capital consumption, liquidity demand, and operational process it creates.

### Credit selection reveals costs slowly

A loan produces interest before the lender knows its final loss. Basel credit-risk standards therefore require banks to manage the full credit life cycle, including underwriting, monitoring, concentration, early loss identification, provisions, and reserves.[9] The author's synthesis is that early delinquency can remain low during expansion, collateral can appear strong near a cycle peak, and a newly originated portfolio has not seasoned through adverse conditions. Reported growth may therefore combine genuine customer value with weaker underwriting, longer maturities, looser covenants, concentrations, or fraud. Economic cost includes expected loss, loss borne by capital beyond the expected estimate, collection and workout expense, liquidity effects, and capacity tied to a weak borrower.

Selection occurs through information, incentives, and process. Banks decide which markets to enter, what data to collect, which exceptions to permit, how loan officers are paid, who can approve concentration, and how independent review challenges growth.[9] Berger and Udell model relationship lending as the accumulation and communication of soft information, and argue that organizational structure affects whether loan officers can transmit and use it.[17] Basel standards separately require independent model validation, understood model limitations, underwriting governance, and concentration controls.[9] The author's synthesis is that scale can support data, specialization, and diversification but can also propagate one model error, while local intimacy can improve information yet weaken challenge or concentrate exposure. Neither size nor intimacy substitutes for cohort performance and conservative loss recognition.

Regulation influences the price and supply of credit by assigning capital, liquidity, governance, disclosure, and examination requirements.[9] These constraints can make an apparently profitable loan unattractive after risk and capital are recognized, and may push activity toward contracts or institutions outside the bank perimeter. The FSB documents that banks lend to nonbanks, receive their deposits, transact through repo and derivatives, and may hold nonbank debt securities; nonbanks also hold bank-issued securities, while both sectors can share common asset exposures.[14] The author's interpretation is that regulatory migration can change where risk is booked without removing bank-nonbank dependence.

### Profit pools differ across retail, commercial, and investment banking

The author's profit-pool map separates retail banking into household deposits, mortgages, cards, consumer loans, and payments. Eligible household deposits can provide insured funding.[2] Deposit profitability also depends on rates, customer sensitivity, branch and service-technology costs, and location.[4] The broader economic ledger must include service and acquisition expense, fraud, credit loss, rewards, compliance, and technology. Under this framework, neither a deposit nor a card balance is profitable merely because its gross interest spread or fee revenue is positive.

The author's commercial-banking map includes operating accounts, cash management, payments, revolving lines, term loans, trade finance, custody, and advisory services. Transaction accounts and unused commitments both provide liquidity on demand, and Gatev et al. find a conditional risk-management association between them in their historical sample.[6] Low-beta uninsured business deposits can nevertheless create runnable franchise value.[5] Profit may be distributed across deposit spreads, loan spreads, treasury fees, foreign exchange, and ancillary services, so relationship economics should not be inferred from one product. The control risk is that expected cross-sell may excuse underpriced credit or concentrated operating balances may be treated as permanent.

The author's investment- and wholesale-banking map includes underwriting, advisory, trading, market-making, custody, prime brokerage, securities financing, and transaction fees. These activities exchange less traditional loan exposure for market, counterparty, conduct, and operational risks, and a universal group must allocate capital, liquidity, limits, and controls across units. The BIS cluster evidence is narrower: its commercial models were more robustly identified, had lower cost-to-income ratios and more stable return on equity than the trading model, and banks changed classifications during the 2005-2015 sample.[3]

Payment activity is a separate value chain rather than a synonym for bank revenue. GAO describes card acceptance as involving fees to issuers, networks, and processors and identifies interchange, network, processing, and other fee categories.[23] Brazil's framework also includes banks, digital banks, nonbank credit companies, payment institutions, Pix, and open finance, although Xu's measured bank-performance treatment is fintech-lender exposure rather than Pix use.[13] The author's value-chain analysis is to assign the customer relationship, fee, credit loss, fraud loss, settlement obligation, data, and regulatory burden to the participant that actually bears each item; the related payment-network topic develops that allocation in detail.

### Scale lowers some costs and raises other ones

Banking contains fixed operating costs, but the size at which they are exhausted is empirical rather than fixed. A 2002 international review found scale benefits mainly up to relatively modest size and little general evidence that mergers reliably produced scope or managerial-efficiency gains; it also warned that results depend on period, country, risk treatment, and service quality.[8] Later U.S. evidence is less categorical. Kovner, Vickery, and Zhou find a robust inverse relation between bank-holding-company size and normalized noninterest expense without evidence that it disappears above a particular threshold.[24] An FDIC study of community banks below $10 billion estimates that the cost-minimizing loan portfolio rose from about $350 million in 2000 to $3.3 billion in 2019, while roughly 90 percent of potential scale efficiency was reached by $300 million and 95 percent by $600 million; the authors describe the estimates as noncausal.[18]

Large size can also create organizational complexity, integration burdens, and systemic consequences.[8][9][10] Funding and risk cannot always move freely across a group's legal entities because national law, local supervision, collateral, systems, and internal practices may constrain transfers.[10] Santos finds that the largest banks had a larger bond-funding advantage than the largest nonbanks and nonfinancial corporations in 1985-2009, a difference consistent with - but not proof of - perceived too-big-to-fail support.[22] The author's synthesis is that scale should be decomposed into operating-cost savings, service gains, diversification, integration expense, control burden, transfer constraints, and any externalized failure cost rather than treated as one automatic advantage.

Consolidation changes competition unevenly. The 2002 review finds that mergers can spread fixed cost and may diversify portfolios, but that average efficiency gains are limited, integration can take years, and deposit and small-business-lending markets can remain local.[8] The 2025 access paper separately finds that branch decline affected locations unevenly and explicitly does not estimate customer demand or competition.[7] Deposit competition also varies with location, preferences, service technology, and rate sensitivity.[4] The author's synthesis is that market definition should follow the product and customer: operating accounts, insured household deposits, relationship credit, syndicated loans, custody, and trading need not have the same competitors.

### Regulation is part of the production function

Compliance is not merely overhead added after a bank chooses its products. Licensing determines entry; capital and liquidity rules affect product economics; customer-due-diligence controls influence onboarding; recovery and resolution planning can require changes to business, managerial, operational, or ownership structures; and supervisors can require remediation or restrict activities.[9] Deposit insurance is intended to support public confidence.[2][9] The author's interpretation is that a credible charter and safety net may lower private funding costs while prudential requirements limit leverage and consume resources that competitors outside the bank perimeter may not bear in the same form.

The regulatory perimeter therefore affects competition. A lower observed price may reflect different loss-absorption or liquidity promises; analysts should test rather than assume that risk has shifted to customers, counterparties, or public backstops. Equal regulation is not always appropriate because business models differ, but the comparison should include the state-contingent promise each firm makes. Basel standards identify intervention, liquidity, disclosure, and safety-net functions, while the FSB documents regulatory-arbitrage concerns and bank-nonbank transmission channels.[9][14] The author's functional questions are: who can run, who supplies liquidity, where losses land, and which authority can intervene before disruption spreads?

### Competition can strengthen or weaken stability through different channels

The author's market-structure distinction is that concentration and competitive conduct are different variables. A market with few banks may remain contestable when entry, switching, transparent pricing, and alternative channels discipline incumbents; a market with many banks may remain segmented by charter rules, information, geography, service integration, or customer inertia. The U.S. deposit study shows that location, technology, service quality, and customer preferences explain substantial pricing differences, challenging a concentration-only account.[4] Licensing and prudential rules also determine who may accept public deposits and which safeguards accompany entry.[9]

Two rival mechanisms explain why the competition-stability relationship is ambiguous. Under the competition-fragility view, stronger competition compresses margins and franchise value, potentially increasing incentives to take risk. Under the competition-stability view, greater loan-market power permits higher borrower rates, which can worsen adverse selection, moral hazard, and repayment risk. Berger, Klapper, and Turk-Ariss test both mechanisms using 8,235 banks in 23 developed countries.[19]

Their cross-country regressions associate greater market power with lower overall bank risk, consistent with the franchise-value channel, but also with greater loan-portfolio risk, consistent with the borrower-risk channel. Higher equity-capital ratios partly offset the portfolio effect.[19] These observational results do not identify one universal optimal concentration level. They show that market power, asset risk, total bank risk, and capitalization are distinct variables whose relationships can point in different directions.

The author's synthesis is that competition policy and prudential policy cannot be evaluated independently. Digital-bank competition can alter deposit-rate levels and policy-rate pass-through,[20] while market power can preserve franchise value yet worsen borrower risk.[19] Capital, liquidity, supervision, deposit-insurance design, and resolution rules mediate which channel dominates.[9] An industry analysis should therefore measure conduct, barriers, customer switching, risk selection, and loss absorption rather than infer stability or consumer benefit from institution count alone.

## Evidence

### Balance-sheet clusters identify distinct business models

Roengpitya, Tarashev, Tsatsaronis, and Villegas use cluster analysis on balance-sheet ratios for 178 banks across 34 jurisdictions over 2005-2015 to classify business models. Their robust categories include retail-funded and wholesale-funded commercial banks, while trading and universal models are less stable across specifications. The two commercial models are both loan-intensive but differ in deposits, stable funding, wholesale debt, and interbank activity. They report lower cost-to-income ratios and more stable return on equity for the commercial models than for the trading model. Comparing two years before and after a switch, retail-funded switchers had a median 2.53-percentage-point improvement in relative return on equity versus peer groups, while wholesale-funded switchers had a median 5.07-point deterioration; the small switcher samples and observational design limit causal interpretation.[3]

The method matters because it does not infer a business model from a bank's name. It groups institutions from observable balance-sheet choices and then examines performance and transitions. Its sample contains large banks and ends in 2015, so the findings do not establish a permanent ranking for every jurisdiction or later digital entrant. The defensible conclusion is narrower: liability structure carries economically important information beyond the loan share, and post-crisis movement toward retail funding was associated with better relative performance in that sample.[3]

### Deposit research separates service value from a low posted rate

D'Avernas, Eisfeldt, Huang, Stanton, and Wallace combine a model of bank location and service choice with U.S. evidence on rates, branches, local demographics, and depositor sensitivity. Their main 2001-2020 data define 14 designated large banks. In RateWatch data for money-market, savings, 12-month-CD, and checking products, those banks paid 22.2-48.4 basis points less than smaller banks; Call Report evidence confirms lower total and savings rates but no significant difference for time or transaction deposits. Large-bank customers also had lower estimated rate sensitivity. Counterfactuals identify branch location as the primary modeled driver of profitability and pricing flexibility as secondary but material.[4]

This evidence supports treating deposit spread as the price of a differentiated rate-and-service bundle rather than as a free funding advantage. It does not prove that every large bank provides value equal to forgone interest or that customer inertia has no role. The author's proposed bank-specific extension is to examine switching behavior, service use, complaints, account fees, digital quality, and actual retention. The paper's narrower contribution is to show how technology, location, and heterogeneous preferences can jointly produce different deposit business models.[4]

Drechsler, Savov, Schnabl, and Wang provide a complementary structural model rather than a branch-demand estimate. They model the future spread on low-beta uninsured deposits as a franchise asset that vanishes when funds leave and identify vulnerable banks around the 2023 regional-bank crisis. Shortening assets can deter a franchise run but create insolvency exposure when rates fall; their model requires capital sufficient to cover potential loss of the low-beta uninsured franchise in order to avoid both states.[5]

Gatev, Schuermann, and Strahan use stock-return volatility, unused commitments, and transaction-deposit measures for large publicly traded U.S. banks in 1990-2002. They find a smaller positive association between commitments and volatility at banks with more transaction deposits, with the relationship stronger during tight market liquidity.[6] This conditional historical association is not evidence that all deposits hedge all commitments. The FDIC's three-bank transaction study supplies the separate concentration result: large depositors were more likely to run, and the largest 0.5 percent held 39-62 percent of domestic deposits at the failed banks.[15]

### Branch consolidation has not affected every place equally

Adams and Sherlund relate 2010 and 2020 census-block household and employment data to mid-2014 and mid-2024 branch locations. Despite a 19 percent branch decline, average household nearest-branch distance rose by 0.02 to 0.28 mile across density groups. In 2024, 1.68 million households met the paper's relative low-access definition, which identifies zero access or access at least two standard deviations below the relevant area mean. In a later workforce and commercial-business discussion, the authors state that nearest-branch distance generally rose by less than 0.1 mile among areas experiencing access declines; that statement does not describe every household group.[7]

The method improves on counting closures because one closing can have little effect where alternatives remain and a large effect where none do. It measures geographic distance, density-adjusted branch access, and potential 4G/5G coverage, but not actual travel time, digital adoption or service quality, affordability, disability or language access, cash-service needs, relationship continuity, or credit outcomes.[7] The author's implication is to analyze distribution at the customer and location level rather than declare either that branches are obsolete or that every closure creates a banking desert.

### Consolidation evidence makes scale conditional

Amel, Barnes, Panetta, and Salleo review international empirical work on financial-sector consolidation and efficiency. Their 2002 review finds scale economies mainly up to relatively modest size in the studies then available, little general evidence that mergers produced scope or managerial-efficiency gains, and mixed evidence on revenue and diversification. Some integration gains appeared only after two to four years. The authors also warn that studies often fail to control adequately for service quality or portfolio risk, so those differences can be misclassified as efficiency.[8]

The historical review does not resolve current optimal size because technology, regulation, output definitions, and product delivery have changed. Two later U.S. studies illustrate the uncertainty. Kovner, Vickery, and Zhou relate detailed bank-holding-company expenses to size and find continued normalized noninterest-cost advantages without a clear upper threshold.[24] Jacewitz, Kravitz, and Shoukry estimate community-bank cost curves for 2000-2019 and find that the cost-minimizing loan portfolio rose materially over time, while most potential savings were achieved well below the estimate and the design remained descriptive rather than causal.[18] The author's synthesis is that a merger thesis must name the systems, funding, risks, timing, and integration costs that create its claimed gain; aggregate asset size is not the mechanism.

### Competition evidence separates market power from deposit technology

Berger, Klapper, and Turk-Ariss regress measures of loan risk, overall bank risk, and equity capital on several market-power measures for 8,235 banks in 23 developed countries. Greater market power is associated with lower overall risk but higher loan-portfolio risk, while higher equity-capital ratios partly offset the portfolio effect.[19] The cross-country design supports the coexistence of competition-fragility and competition-stability channels; it does not establish that changing concentration will mechanically produce either outcome in one jurisdiction.

Brei, Cornelli, Gambacorta, and Hofmann merge U.S. Call Reports, Summary of Deposits, branch-level RateWatch data, and geotagged social-media activity. They identify digital banks by branch, investment, expense, and nationwide-pricing patterns and compare deposit-rate levels and policy-rate pass-through. Digital banks paid higher savings and small-time-deposit rates, showed somewhat stronger pass-through, and responded more strongly where social-media use was higher.[20] The findings concern deposit pricing rather than the causal speed of a bank run; the Basel and FDIC evidence supplies the separate outflow and concentration record.[10][15][21]

### Fintech competition compresses some margins and changes operations

Xu uses quarterly Brazilian regulatory data for 2012-2024 and constructs a bank-specific shift-share, or Bartik, exposure from each bank's pre-2017 loan-segment mix and subsequent fintech market shares by segment. The regressions include bank and time fixed effects, and the paper reports balance and pre-trend diagnostics plus leave-one-out, earlier-base-year, and instrumental-variable robustness checks. A one-standard-deviation increase in estimated fintech-lender exposure reduced average commercial-bank lending rates by 3.7 percentage points, narrowed net interest margins, lowered administrative cost relative to assets, and reduced return on equity and assets. The coefficients for nonperforming-loan and provisioning ratios were not statistically significant; the author interprets banks as shifting toward higher-value, lower-risk borrowers and more favorable terms.[13]

The result is a country-specific causal estimate, not a universal coefficient for all banking systems. Brazil's rates, regulation, market structure, and digital infrastructure shape the magnitude. It shows that fintech-lender competition can reduce incumbent lending rates, margins, and administrative-cost ratios without a statistically discernible aggregate effect on nonperforming-loan or provisioning ratios in this sample. The paper distinguishes deposit-taking digital banks, balance-sheet nonbank credit companies, and peer-to-peer platforms; it does not estimate equivalent effects for generic payment interfaces, and its Pix-user control has little measured effect on the bank outcomes.[13]

### Current official data show profits and latent costs together

The FDIC's second-quarter 2026 Quarterly Banking Profile aggregates regulatory reports from 4,238 insured commercial banks and savings institutions. It reports $90.1 billion of quarterly net income, a 1.37 percent return on assets, a 3.32 percent net interest margin, and an eighth consecutive quarterly increase in domestic deposits. It also reports $326.7 billion of unrealized losses on held-to-maturity and available-for-sale securities. Past-due and nonaccrual rates on non-owner-occupied commercial real-estate loans eased during the quarter but remained elevated at the largest banks: 3.08 percent at institutions above $250 billion of assets versus a 0.59 percent pre-pandemic average. The income increase also included $5.5 billion of securities gains, primarily one-time equity-security gains. This is a descriptive industry snapshot, not proof that the median bank shares the aggregate profile.[12]

The FDIC's 2026 Risk Review adds an annual and risk-specific decomposition. It reports that funding costs fell faster than asset yields in 2025, raising industry net interest margin by 8 basis points to 3.30 percent, while unrealized securities losses remained elevated and banks continued to build borrowing capacity. Lending to nondepository financial institutions was the fastest-growing post-crisis loan segment and was concentrated at the largest banks, although the FDIC cautions that revised Call Report instructions and loan reclassification contributed to reported 2025 growth.[11] Read with the quarterly profile, the evidence shows why current earnings, deposit growth, securities marks, borrowing capacity, and off-bank credit exposure must be analyzed separately.[11][12]

### The 2023 turmoil demonstrates nonlinear funding failure

The Basel Committee's report on the 2023 banking turmoil synthesizes failures and market stress across the United States and Switzerland. It identifies rapid growth, concentrated business models, weak governance and risk management, long-duration assets, reliance on uninsured deposits, and deficient contingency funding as interacting vulnerabilities. At Silicon Valley Bank, announced securities losses and a planned capital raise preceded deposit outflows of over $40 billion on March 9; management expected more than $100 billion of additional outflows on March 10.[10] The FDIC's later transaction-level study shows how depositor size, insurance status, and concentrated balances shaped the three U.S. runs.[15]

The evidence does not support reducing every failure to social media, unrealized losses, or one missing ratio. It supports a joint mechanism: a fragile business model can remain profitable while confidence holds, then lose funding faster than assets can be sold, pledged, or refinanced. The 2023 report notes that the LCR and NSFR cannot prevent all runs and must be complemented by supervision, operational preparedness, governance, and credible contingency funding.[10] The Committee's 2024 follow-up adds analysis of outflow rates and liquidity-risk factors while reaffirming that the immediate priority is full and consistent Basel III implementation rather than prejudging a standard change.[21]

### Nonbank growth changes rather than removes the banking boundary

The FSB's 2025 monitoring exercise covers 29 jurisdictions representing more than 90 percent of global GDP. It reports that nonbank financial intermediation grew 9.4 percent in 2024 to $256.8 trillion, twice the 4.7 percent growth rate of banking in the monitoring aggregate, and represented 51.0 percent of that aggregate's total financial assets. The FSB identifies three principal surveyed bank-NBFI links: deposits placed by nonbanks, bank lending and repo or other credit exposure to nonbanks, and nonbank holdings of bank-issued securities. Aggregated sector balance sheets reveal system structure but cannot determine the risk of one private-credit loan or fund.[14]

The evidence qualifies the claim that nonbanks simply displace banks. Nonbanks may provide credit or investment intermediation while remaining connected to banks through deposits, lending, repo, derivatives, credit lines, custody and other client services, or common asset holdings.[14] The FSB documents these dependencies and their stress-transmission channels, not the distribution of each industry's profits. The author's interpretation is that a bank profit pool may migrate from loan spread to funding, infrastructure, or risk-management fees; testing that hypothesis requires both market-share and dependency mapping.

## Implications

### For industry analysts: build the bank from funding outward

The author's funding-outward method begins by separating insured deposits, low-beta uninsured franchises, rate-sensitive balances, brokered deposits, public funds, interbank liabilities, secured borrowing, and long-term debt. FDIC coverage depends on depositor, insured bank, and ownership category, and insured institutions pay assessments.[2] The large-versus-small-bank model supplies rate, service, branch, and technology costs; the franchise-run model adds operating cost, deposit beta, insurance status, and retention risk.[4][5] For each source, record explicit rate, service cost, concentration, collateral, insurance status, behavioral duration, withdrawal channel, and availability under stress rather than infer franchise value from the posted rate alone.

Then map assets and commitments to the liabilities and capital they consume. Basel standards require integrated risk management, funding plans, collateral analysis, off-balance-sheet exposures, concentration controls, and operationally feasible monetization.[9] The 2023 case evidence adds legal-entity constraints, rapid outflows, and failed contingency-funding execution.[10] FDIC data support testing securities for duration, market value, pledgeability, and undrawn credit lines for contingent draws.[11] Gatev et al. support analyzing unused commitments jointly with transaction-deposit flows.[6] The detailed borrower, covenant, derivative, settlement, and collateral-call map is the author's state-contingent scenario framework.

The QBP separately reports net interest income, noninterest income, provision expense, securities gains, and noninterest expense.[12] BIS business-model evidence shows that funding composition, efficiency, net interest income, and return on equity differ across models.[3] Basel standards require material risks and liquidity impacts to enter internal pricing, performance measurement, and product approval.[9] The author's profit-pool bridge extends those inputs by reconciling internal transfers and allocating credit cost, conduct cost, capital, liquidity insurance, and operational burden so one deposit or cross-sold service is not credited twice.

Finally, distinguish a cyclical tailwind from a structural franchise. The FDIC reported net interest margin of 3.30 percent in 2025, up 8 basis points, with $306 billion of unrealized securities losses; in the second quarter of 2026, net interest margin was 3.32 percent and unrealized losses were $326.7 billion.[11][12] The author's implication is that steeper curves, slow deposit repricing, benign credit, or one-time securities gains can widen current earnings without proving durable loyalty or underwriting quality. Technology and compliance spending can likewise depress current efficiency while strengthening service and control. Analysts should explain which earnings can repeat after deposit rates, credit costs, and asset marks normalize rather than annualize the latest quarter.

### For bank management: price the full relationship and rehearse its failure

The author's relationship-ledger method records balance stability, rates, fees, service contacts, fraud, credit, and acquisition cost for households, and operating deposits, treasury services, payment flows, commitments, collateral, foreign exchange, advice, and concentration for businesses. The empirical inputs are narrower: customer rate sensitivity and willingness to pay for liquidity services vary across depositors,[4] transaction deposits and commitments show a conditional portfolio association,[6] and low-beta uninsured business deposits can be runnable.[5] Relationship pricing is defensible only when profitable services support one another transparently rather than when hoped-for cross-sell hides underpriced credit.

Deposit retention should be studied as behavior, not asserted as a label. The franchise-run model makes insurance status, deposit beta, operating cost, and retention central variables.[5] FDIC transaction data show that depositor size and concentration predicted flight at the three spring 2023 failures, while Basel evidence documents accelerated outflows and public-information effects.[10][15] The author's management extension is to segment by purpose, common ownership, common adviser, transfer capability, and communication network, compare modeled decay with observed flows, and run intraday as well as multiday scenarios rather than rely on a historically long account life.

Contingency funding must be operational. Management should know which assets are unencumbered, where they are held, which legal entity owns them, what haircuts apply, which documents and approvals are needed, whether collateral can move, and whether borrowing channels have been tested. A nominal liquidity total is not available cash if systems, custodians, legal restrictions, or market confidence prevent timely monetization. The worst case is a bank that discovers during a run that its supposed buffer cannot reach the entity or payment system experiencing outflows.[9][10]

Basel standards support risk-aligned compensation, independent controls, credit underwriting, concentration limits, operational resilience, and customer due diligence.[9] The author's implementation rule is that new cohorts need vintage tracking and compensation linked to eventual credit performance; fee and trading businesses need conduct, counterparty, and tail-risk attribution; and digital onboarding needs fraud, identity, cyber, and service controls at the same scale as acquisition. No growth metric should mature faster than the evidence needed to test its cost without a conservative allowance for uncertainty.

### For regulators: protect the function without freezing the industry

Licensing, supervision, deposit insurance, crisis-management and resolution frameworks, and secure payment systems support confidence and continuity.[1][2][9] The Academy's summary also warns that deposit insurance can encourage risk-taking and that capital rules may therefore be necessary.[1] The author's interpretation is that public protections should be paired with insurance assessments, loss-absorbing capital, governance, corrective action, and credible loss allocation so owners and managers do not retain upside while public mechanisms absorb avoidable losses.

Proportionality should reflect risk rather than size alone. A smaller institution with rapid growth, concentrated uninsured funding, long-duration assets, weak operational liquidity, or a specialized customer network can transmit stress. A large diversified bank may have stronger systems while creating greater complexity and systemic consequence. Supervisory intensity should therefore incorporate growth, funding concentration, business-model opacity, substitutability, interconnectedness, and execution capability alongside total assets.[9][10]

Competition policy should define markets by function and customer. Branch proximity still matters geographically,[7] and Federal Reserve field research identifies burdens on some older rural customers, small businesses, cash users, and people without reliable transport.[25] Deposit pricing is also differentiated by location, service technology, preferences, and digital-bank business models.[4][20] The 2002 merger review shows that deposit and small-business-credit markets can remain local and integration gains can take years.[8] The author's review framework therefore tests branch and digital access, pricing, relationship-credit continuity, system integration, and resolution consequences rather than infer competition from institution count or national market share alone.

The perimeter should follow dependency as well as legal form. Fintech lenders can intensify competition, reduce incumbent commercial-bank lending rates, and lower administrative-cost ratios, as Xu's Brazilian estimates suggest; the paper does not identify those effects for payment providers or Pix.[13] NBFIs can remain connected to banks through deposits, lending, repo, derivatives, custody or other client services, and credit lines.[14] The author's policy synthesis is to map those dependencies and align disclosure, liquidity, counterparty, and consumer safeguards with the promise made without assuming that every nonbank activity should be regulated exactly like an insured deposit-taking bank.

### For customers and corporate treasurers: convenience and yield are not the only variables

Depositors should distinguish insured from uninsured balances and understand that FDIC coverage is determined by depositor, insured bank, and ownership category.[2] A quoted rate alone does not establish whether an institution or product is insured; customers should verify both against FDIC coverage rules.[2] The author's operational checklist also considers payment continuity, fraud controls, service access, transfer limits, legal ownership, and contingency accounts rather than yield alone.

Corporate treasurers should map cash by bank, legal entity, ownership category, operational purpose, and insurance status; the FDIC rules determine the coverage treatment.[2] Basel's 2023 case evidence supplies the concentrated-outflow and operational-continuity premise.[10] The author's practical extension is to identify which accounts support payroll, collections, collateral, or settlement, prepare tested alternatives, and balance reduced one-bank exposure against reconciliation and control burdens.

Borrowers should evaluate the durability of credit, not only its initial spread. In Gatev et al.'s 1990-2002 large-bank sample, transaction deposits attenuated the stock-volatility exposure associated with unused commitments during tight market liquidity.[6] The FSB shows that nonbank alternatives can have different leverage, maturity-transformation, and liquidity vulnerabilities rather than a universal speed or price advantage.[14] The author's borrower checklist covers covenants, renewal rights, collateral, syndication, draw conditions, and lender capacity across a cycle.

### For long-term capital allocation: avoid mistaking leverage for a moat

The author's long-term industry test combines trusted and diversified funding, disciplined risk selection, efficient distribution, reliable payments, useful customer information, scalable controls, and capital that protects the franchise in adverse states. Deposit evidence shows that distribution and customer preferences matter,[4] consolidation evidence shows that market power need not deliver durable efficiency,[8] and Basel standards require capital, liquidity, risk management, and operational resilience.[9] The synthesis is that none is sufficient alone: leverage can magnify a spread while narrowing error tolerance, networks can acquire unprofitable balances, and fee growth can conceal conduct or counterparty risk.

The most important inversion is to ask what destroys the apparent advantage. A low-beta deposit franchise loses value when customers withdraw or rates converge toward market rates.[5] Scale can fail when integration cost, organizational complexity, or systemic consequence outweighs unit savings.[8] Operational systems can fail to keep critical services available,[9] and nominally different businesses can share collateral, funding, or asset exposures.[14] The author's extensions are that familiarity can weaken challenge, compliance can displace judgment, and apparent diversification can vanish when several businesses depend on the same confidence or liquidity source.

The author's synthesis is a ten-part industry test. First, identify the charter and public protections.[2][9] Second, decompose funding by cost, insurance, concentration, and behavior.[3][5][15] Third, map customer distribution across branch, digital, and intermediated channels.[4][7][20] Fourth, separate payment, lending, treasury, advisory, and market businesses. Fifth, assign credit, market, liquidity, conduct, operational, and counterparty costs to their sources.[9] Sixth, measure scale benefits after integration and control expense.[8][18][24] Seventh, test switching costs for customer value as well as inertia. Eighth, map nonbank competitors and dependencies.[14] Ninth, stress asset losses and funding outflows together.[10][21] Tenth, ask whether capital and liquidity preserve the franchise without extraordinary support.[9]

Banking can create value by combining payment accounts and liquid claims with longer-term credit and delegated borrower monitoring, thereby reducing intermediation costs.[1] Low-beta uninsured franchise value is runnable and capital-sensitive,[5] transaction deposits can conditionally offset unused-commitment exposure in a historical large-bank sample,[6] and rapid growth, concentrated funding, long-duration assets, and weak controls can interact nonlinearly under stress.[10] The author's central conclusion is that durable value survives only when a bank prices the complete cost of its promises and can perform them through a cycle; otherwise low funding cost, rapid growth, and leverage may be evidence of fragility rather than moats.
