# Global Supply Chain Dynamics -- The Collapse of Just-in-Time and the Rise of Resilience as a Competitive Imperative

Global supply chain strategy has undergone its most fundamental
transformation since the containerization revolution of the 1960s.
The lean, cost-optimized just-in-time (JIT) model that dominated
global manufacturing for four decades -- pioneered by Toyota in the
1950s, perfected in the 1980s, and globalized in the 1990s and 2000s --
has been broken by a cascade of shocks: COVID-19, semiconductor
shortages, the Russia-Ukraine war, US-China decoupling, and attacks on
Red Sea shipping. In its place, a new paradigm is emerging: just-in-case
supply chains that prioritize resilience, redundancy, and geopolitical
alignment over marginal cost savings. This shift is not a temporary
adjustment but a structural repricing of supply chain risk that will
reshape industry profitability, competitive advantage, and capital
allocation for decades.

## Background

The modern global supply chain is a product of four converging forces:
the containerization of shipping (standardized containers revolutionized
port logistics after 1956), the liberalization of trade under the WTO
framework (1995), the rise of Chinese manufacturing capacity after WTO
accession (2001), and the information technology that made it possible
to coordinate production across dozens of countries in near real time.

Toyota's Just-in-Time system, formalized by Taiichi Ohno in the 1950s
and 1960s, was the intellectual foundation. Postwar Japan had almost
no natural resources, limited capital, and surplus labor. Ohno's insight
was to reverse the logic of American mass production: instead of
building huge inventories to buffer against uncertainty, build systems
that eliminate uncertainty at the source. Parts arrive exactly when
needed; defects are caught immediately; nothing is produced without
a downstream demand signal (the kanban system). The result was
astonishing: Toyota reduced inventory costs by 70-80% relative to
Detroit automakers while simultaneously improving quality.

In the 1980s, Western manufacturers studied Toyota and imported its
methods. The term "lean production" was coined by John Krafcik at MIT
in 1988. By the 1990s, JIT had become manufacturing orthodoxy. The
fall of the Berlin Wall and China's opening created an extraordinary
opportunity: JIT principles could now be applied across borders.
Companies moved production to the lowest-cost location for each
component, creating supply chains that stretched across dozens of
countries. The logic was irresistible: lower labor costs, scale
economies in component production, and minimal inventory carrying
costs. Between 1990 and 2020, global trade in intermediate goods
grew faster than trade in finished goods, as supply chains fragmented
across borders.

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake and Thailand floods offered an early
warning. Toyota, the JIT pioneer, discovered that a single supplier
of a specialized microcontroller (Renesas) was the sole source for
a critical component used across multiple vehicle platforms. When
the Renesas plant was damaged by the earthquake, Toyota lost
production of 150,000 vehicles. This was the first crack in the JIT
consensus: extreme efficiency had produced extreme fragility. But
the lessons were not widely internalized. The consensus held that
these were "black swan" events -- unpredictable and unmodelable --
rather than predictable features of a tightly coupled system.

COVID-19 shattered that consensus. Within weeks of the Wuhan lockdown
in January 2020, manufacturers worldwide discovered they had no
visibility into their tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers. The assumption
that someone else held buffer inventory turned out to be wrong at
every level. Semiconductor shortages followed in 2021-2022, not
because chip demand was unprecedented but because the automotive
industry had canceled orders during the pandemic and lost its place
in the queue to consumer electronics. The Russia-Ukraine war in 2022
disrupted energy, grain, and neon gas supplies. Houthi attacks on
Red Sea shipping in 2023-2024 forced container ships around the Cape
of Good Hope, adding two weeks and millions in cost per voyage.

Each shock on its own might have been absorbed. The accumulation of
shocks -- pandemic, war, decoupling, climate disruptions -- forced a
fundamental reassessment. The prevailing assumption of the JIT era --
that supply chain risk could be managed through diversification and
just-in-case thinking was unnecessary because markets would always
clear -- has been replaced by a new governing assumption: supply
chain resilience is not an optional insurance policy but a competitive
necessity.

## Core Concepts

### Just-in-Time (JIT) and the Toyota Production System

JIT is a production philosophy built on three pillars: pull-based
production (downstream demand signals pull work through the system,
rather than upstream forecasts pushing it), continuous flow (work
moves through production without waiting, queuing, or batching), and
takt time (production pace is synchronized with customer demand
rate). The goal is the systematic elimination of waste (muda): no
overproduction, no waiting, no unnecessary transport, no excess
inventory, no unnecessary motion, no defects, and no over-processing.

JIT was never supposed to be fragile. The Toyota Production System
includes a second pillar, Jidoka (automation with a human touch),
which means that any worker can stop the line when a defect is
detected. The system is designed for problems to surface immediately
so they can be solved at the root cause. In theory, JIT + Jidoka
creates a system that is both efficient AND robust. In practice, the
globalization of JIT stripped away the robustness elements while
keeping the efficiency demands.

### The Economics of Globalized Supply Chains

Why did supply chains globalize? The logic was based on comparative
advantage extended to component-level production. If China could
produce a particular electronic subassembly at 60% of the US cost,
and Vietnam could produce textiles at 50%, and Mexico could handle
final assembly with tariff advantages, the optimal strategy was to
locate each step where it was cheapest. This "smile curve" distribution
of value -- high-value design at one end, high-value branding at the
other, low-value manufacturing in the middle -- appeared to justify
offshoring most production.

The hidden assumption was that coordination costs were negligible.
Container shipping costs fell by roughly 90% in real terms between
1960 and 2010. Trade barriers fell. Communication costs approached
zero. In this environment, the carrying cost of inventory (typically
20-30% of inventory value per year including warehousing, insurance,
obsolescence, and cost of capital) appeared to dominate. Carrying
two weeks of inventory cost more than shipping from China with a
four-week lead time, because the freight cost was so low.

This calculation ignored three costs: the cost of disruption (a
factory idled for weeks because a container is stuck at a port), the
cost of complexity (the managerial overhead of coordinating suppliers
across a dozen time zones and legal jurisdictions), and the cost of
fragility (the non-linear impact of correlated failures -- when
multiple suppliers are disrupted simultaneously, the system's
recovery time multiplies rather than adds).

### The Bullwhip Effect

The bullwhip effect, first described by Jay Forrester at MIT in 1961
and formalized by Hau Lee and colleagues at Stanford in the 1990s,
describes how small fluctuations in end-consumer demand amplify into
large swings in orders upstream in the supply chain. A retailer
experiencing a 5% increase in demand might increase orders by 10%
(to rebuild safety stock). The wholesaler sees the 10% increase and
orders 20% more from the manufacturer (adding its own safety margin).
The manufacturer sees the 20% increase and orders 40% more raw
materials. By the time the signal reaches the raw material supplier,
a 5% demand increase has become a 40% order surge.

During COVID-19, the bullwhip effect went into overdrive. As demand
patterns shifted violently (from services to goods, from offices to
homes, from travel to stay-at-home), forecasting models built on
historical data became worthless. Companies placed precautionary
orders to secure capacity. Suppliers, seeing surging orders, expanded
capacity. When demand normalized, the overhang of inventory and
capacity triggered a reverse bullwhip: order cancellations cascaded
upstream, leaving suppliers with excess capacity and inventory that
took quarters to work through.

### The Shift: Reshoring, Nearshoring, and Friendshoring

The post-pandemic supply chain strategy lexicon has three key terms:

**Reshoring** (or onshoring): bringing production back to the home
country. The US CHIPS Act of 2022 allocated $52 billion to subsidize
domestic semiconductor fabrication. TSMC is building a $40 billion
fab in Arizona. Intel is building new fabs in Ohio. The logic is
not just resilience but strategic autonomy: advanced semiconductors
are now treated as a national security asset, not a commercial good.

**Nearshoring**: moving production to a nearby country rather than
a distant one. For US companies, Mexico has been the primary
beneficiary, with foreign direct investment surging in 2023-2025 as
manufacturers shifted production from China to locations that share
a land border and free trade agreement with the US. For European
companies, Turkey, Morocco, and Eastern Europe have gained share
relative to East Asia.

**Friendshoring**: locating production in politically aligned
countries. This represents a departure from the WTO-era assumption
that trade is apolitical. The US is encouraging semiconductor and
critical mineral supply chains to relocate to allies: Japan, South
Korea, India, and the EU. China is building parallel supply chains
with its own allies through the Belt and Road Initiative.

The empirical evidence suggests the shift is real but gradual. Zhang
and Doan (2023), using Japanese firm-level data, found that importers
significantly and persistently increased their inventories of
intermediate inputs after the pandemic, particularly firms with high
ex-ante import shares and those that experienced supply chain
disruptions in China. This suggests a structural shift from JIT to
just-in-case inventory management rather than a temporary adjustment.
However, they caution that the sample period ended in Q2 2021, so
the permanence of the shift is still being established.

### Concentration Risk: The Semiconductor Case

The most extreme example of supply chain concentration risk is
advanced semiconductors. As of 2024, Taiwan Semiconductor
Manufacturing Company (TSMC) produces approximately 90% of the
world's most advanced logic chips (sub-7nm nodes). A single company
on a geopolitically contested island controls the production of the
component on which modern computing, telecommunications, defense
systems, and artificial intelligence depend. The US Department of
Commerce's 2022 semiconductor survey found that median inventory of
chips had fallen from 40 days in 2019 to fewer than 5 days in 2021.

The concentration is not accidental. Semiconductor fabrication is
among the most capital-intensive industries in existence. A single
advanced fab costs $20-30 billion and requires years to construct
and qualify. The industry naturally concentrates in a few locations
because the fixed costs are enormous and the learning curve is steep.
But the strategic vulnerability this creates -- that a disruption at
TSMC could halt global production of iPhones, data center GPUs, and
military systems simultaneously -- has made semiconductor supply chain
diversification a priority for every major government.

### How Supply Chain Design Affects Industry Structure

Supply chain architecture shapes competitive dynamics in ways that
traditional industry analysis often misses. Four channels are
particularly important:

**Barriers to entry**: Industries with complex, globalized supply
chains have a built-in barrier: a new entrant must not only develop a
competitive product but also assemble and qualify a supplier network
across multiple countries. This is part of why Tesla's vertical
integration was strategically essential and why Apple can maintain
margins that its competitors cannot match.

**Bargaining power**: Supply chain concentration shifts bargaining
power. When a single supplier (like TSMC for advanced chips) controls
a bottleneck, it captures a disproportionate share of industry profits.
Conversely, when buyers can play suppliers against each other, margins
flow downstream. The smartphone profit pool -- where Apple captures
roughly 80% of industry profits despite having only 20% of unit market
share -- is partly a story of supply chain bargaining power.

**Fixed vs variable cost structure**: JIT supply chains reduce fixed
costs (inventory carrying costs, owned manufacturing facilities) but
increase the risk of variable cost spikes when disruptions occur. A
firm with a just-in-case supply chain has higher fixed costs
(inventory, owned capacity) but lower exposure to spot-market price
spikes during crises.

**Industry consolidation**: Supply chain disruptions tend to accelerate
consolidation. When a disruption hits, firms with stronger balance
sheets can maintain production (by paying premium freight, buying from
brokers at elevated prices) while weaker competitors lose market share
they may never recover. The semiconductor shortage of 2021-2022
illustrated this: large automakers with direct relationships to chip
manufacturers fared better than smaller ones dependent on distributors.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

The shift from JIT to just-in-case is supported by multiple lines of
evidence across different methodologies and geographies.

**Firm-level inventory data**: Zhang and Doan (2023) analyzed quarterly
data from Japanese firms from 2015 to 2021. They found that importing
firms -- those exposed to international supply chains -- significantly
and persistently increased their inventories of raw materials,
intermediate inputs, and finished goods after the pandemic. The effect
was strongest for firms with high pre-pandemic import shares and
multinational firms that experienced supply chain disruptions in China.
This is direct evidence of a structural, not cyclical, shift in
inventory behavior.

**Aggregate US data**: The US Census Bureau's Manufacturing and Trade
Inventories and Sales data shows that the inventory-to-sales ratio,
which had been declining for decades as JIT principles diffused,
stabilized and began to rise after 2020. From a low of approximately
1.25 in 2020, the ratio climbed to roughly 1.35 by mid-2023, reflecting
a deliberate rebuilding of inventory buffers across the economy.

**Reshoring announcements**: The Reshoring Initiative, which tracks
company announcements of production returning to the US, reported
record levels of reshoring job announcements in 2022 and 2023, driven
by both manufacturing and services (especially software and IT).
Cumulative reshored jobs since 2010 exceeded 1.6 million by 2023.
While these are announcements, not necessarily completions, the trend
is unmistakable.

**Supply chain stress indices**: The Federal Reserve Bank of New York's
Global Supply Chain Pressure Index (GSCPI), which integrates
transportation cost data and manufacturing indicators, spiked to
unprecedented levels in late 2021 (reaching 4.3 standard deviations
above the mean), moderated through 2022, and has since fluctuated at
elevated levels relative to the pre-pandemic baseline. The World Bank's
supply chain stress index similarly shows sustained elevation above
long-term trends, suggesting that supply chains have not returned to
their pre-pandemic equilibrium.

**Policy evidence**: The passage of the US CHIPS and Science Act ($52
billion, 2022), the European Chips Act (43 billion euros, 2023), and
similar initiatives in Japan, South Korea, and India represent the
largest peacetime industrial policy interventions in decades. These
are not temporary stimulus measures but structural subsidies designed
to permanently alter the geography of semiconductor production. The
scale of these commitments signals that governments now view supply
chain resilience as a public good that markets undersupply.

**Survey evidence**: McKinsey's 2023 Global Supply Chain Leader Survey
found that 73% of supply chain executives reported increasing inventory
levels, 67% were dual-sourcing critical components, and 44% were
nearshoring or regionalizing their supply base. More tellingly, 85%
said they expected these changes to be permanent rather than temporary
responses to recent disruptions. The Chartered Institute of Procurement
and Supply (CIPS) recorded sustained high levels of supply chain
disruption concern among procurement managers through Q3 2025, driven
by US tariff uncertainty, US-China tensions, and Middle East instability.

**Bullwhip effect empirics**: Song and Zhang (2025) examined the
relationship between the bullwhip effect and cost behavior using a
large sample of US firms. They found that firms experiencing greater
demand variability amplification (bullwhip) exhibit higher cost rigidity
-- costs that do not fall as quickly as revenues during downturns. This
is because firms build organizational structures, supplier relationships,
and capacity commitments that are hard to unwind. The implication:
bullwhip-induced volatility creates permanent cost disadvantages, not
just temporary disruptions.

## Implications

**For investors**: Supply chain strategy has become a material factor
in equity analysis. The JIT consensus encouraged investors to reward
asset-light, low-inventory business models. The just-in-case era
requires a more nuanced assessment. Companies that invested early in
supply chain resilience -- dual-sourcing, regionalized production,
strategic inventories -- may appear to have lower returns on capital
in normal times but prove dramatically more valuable during disruptions.

Investors should ask four questions of every company in their portfolio:
(1) What is the geographical concentration of the company's supply
base, and how exposed is it to geopolitical flashpoints? (2) What is
the company's inventory strategy -- has it shifted from JIT to
just-in-case, and if so, is the market pricing the higher capital
requirements correctly? (3) Does the company have the bargaining
power to pass supply chain cost increases to customers, or will
margin compression absorb the shock? (4) Is the company a beneficiary
or victim of reshoring -- does it own capacity in the markets where
production is returning, or is it a low-cost producer losing its cost
advantage?

Sectors with the highest supply chain risk: semiconductors (geographic
concentration), automotive (long, complex supply chains with thousands
of components), pharmaceuticals (dependence on Chinese and Indian API
manufacturing), and defense (single-source suppliers for critical
components). Sectors that may benefit: logistics and warehousing
(increased inventory means increased demand for storage), industrial
automation (reshoring creates demand for labor-replacing capital
equipment), and domestic manufacturing in countries receiving reshored
production.

**For managers**: The supply chain strategy reset creates both threats
and opportunities. Companies that cling to pure JIT in the face of
structural disruption risk will be repeatedly blindsided. Companies
that blindly shift to maximum inventory and full vertical integration
will destroy returns on capital. The winning strategy is likely a
hybrid: maintain JIT discipline for predictable, diversified supply
chains while building just-in-case buffers and redundancy for
concentrated, critical, or geopolitically exposed nodes.

Akinbolajo (2022) proposes a "hybrid sourcing framework" that enables
organizations to balance efficiency and resilience. The framework
suggests segmenting the supply base into three tiers: (1) commodity
inputs where cost-minimizing JIT remains appropriate, (2) critical
inputs with multiple viable suppliers where dual-sourcing adds modest
cost for significant resilience, and (3) bottleneck inputs with few
suppliers and high disruption impact where strategic inventories,
long-term supply agreements, and vertical integration are justified.

**For policymakers**: The supply chain transformation requires
government action in three areas. First, industrial policy to
subsidize domestic production of strategically critical goods
(semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, rare earth processing) where
private markets undersupply resilience. Second, trade policy that
creates a stable framework for friendshoring -- if every country
pursues autarky, the costs will be enormous. Third, infrastructure
investment in ports, rail, and digital supply chain visibility tools
so that the physical and informational arteries of trade can
handle more inventory and more regionalized flows.

**For the global economic order**: The shift from efficiency to
resilience in supply chain design is fundamentally a shift from a
globalization model built on the assumption of geopolitical stability
to one that prices geopolitical risk. If this trend continues, it
implies structurally higher production costs, structurally higher
inflation, and structurally lower growth in trade relative to GDP.
The World Bank has warned that widespread reshoring to high-income
countries could drive 52 million additional people into extreme
poverty, as low-income countries that benefited from manufacturing-led
development lose their pathway to growth.

The most likely outcome is not the end of globalization but its
reconfiguration. Supply chains will regionalize into three blocs:
a North American bloc centered on the US and Mexico, a European bloc
centered on the EU with extensions into Turkey and North Africa, and
an Asian bloc centered on China with extensions into Southeast Asia.
Within each bloc, trade will remain relatively free and supply chains
will be tightly integrated. Between blocs, trade in strategically
sensitive goods will face increasing restrictions. This is a world
of managed trade, not free trade -- and it has no historical precedent
in the post-WWII economic order.

**For industry structure**: The supply chain reset will accelerate
industry concentration. Large firms can afford dual-sourcing,
strategic inventories, and regionalized production. Small firms
typically cannot. During the semiconductor shortage, large automakers
like Toyota (which had stockpiled chips after the 2011 earthquake)
maintained production while smaller competitors faced months-long
shutdowns. The same dynamic will play out across industries: supply
chain resilience is a fixed-cost investment that large incumbents can
spread across higher volumes, creating a new form of scale advantage.
