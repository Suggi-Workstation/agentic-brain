# Disruption Theory -- A Foothold and Asymmetric Incentives Can Unseat Incumbents

Disruption theory is a conditional account of how an entrant can begin in an overlooked market, improve without losing the advantages of its business model, and eventually challenge established firms whose incentives point elsewhere. It does not mean that every important technology, successful startup, or declining incumbent is disruptive, and the evidence supports using it as one lens rather than as a universal law of competition [3][4][5][8].

## Background

Disruption theory began as an attempt to explain a specific puzzle: why firms with technical resources, attentive managers, and strong customer relationships sometimes lost industry leadership during market and technology transitions. Clayton Christensen's early research examined the disk-drive industry, where incumbent producers often led improvements that existing customers valued but struggled when smaller drives first served emerging computer markets. Joseph Bower and Christensen presented the practitioner version as "disruptive technologies" in 1995, and Christensen and Bower published the resource-allocation account in an academic journal in 1996. Christensen's 1997 book, "The Innovator's Dilemma," extended the argument through cases in disk drives, excavators, steel, retail, and other industries [1][2][4][9].

The original question was narrower than the modern business use of the word "disruption." It concerned a pattern in which established firms performed well at sustaining improvements for known customers but underinvested in products that initially served small, low-margin, or newly forming markets. Those products could be technically simpler or inferior on established performance measures while being cheaper, smaller, more convenient, or otherwise suitable for customers the incumbent did not prioritize. If the entrant later improved enough for mainstream use while preserving its cost or accessibility advantage, market leadership could shift [1][2][3][4].

The theory changed after its first formulation. Later work distinguished low-end disruption from new-market disruption, shifted emphasis from technology alone to the business model in which a technology is deployed, and treated disruptiveness as relative to a firm's customers and profit formula rather than as an intrinsic property of a product. A 2018 intellectual history by Christensen and coauthors described the theory's movement from a descriptive account of technology change toward a causal theory centered on resource allocation, customer dependence, business models, and asymmetric incentives [4].

This history matters because statements from different versions of the theory are not interchangeable. The early literature often used stronger language about incumbent failure and the necessity of separate organizations. Later research recognized that incumbents sometimes respond successfully, that the same technology can be sustaining for one firm and disruptive for another, and that disruption may proceed slowly, stall, or never occur. Later work also identified response options beyond a fully separate unit, including ambidextrous structures, partnerships, acquisitions, hybrid products, investment in the incumbent trajectory, and retreat to a defensible niche [4][6][7].

The theory also differs from the broader concepts of radical innovation and creative destruction. A radical innovation is defined primarily by new technological knowledge or a large departure from prior products; a disruptive path is defined by market entry, an initial foothold, asymmetric incentives, and movement toward the mainstream. A breakthrough can therefore be sustaining if it gives an incumbent's best customers better performance, while a technically modest product can be disruptive if it establishes a viable foothold that the incumbent is motivated to ignore. A systematic review covering 1,078 articles on six innovation traditions found disruptive and radical innovation to be distinct research streams with different causal mechanisms and organizational implications [3][11].

Precision is essential because the wrong label implies the wrong response. Christensen, Raynor, and McDonald used Uber to show the problem: although Uber altered taxi competition, it entered a mainstream market with a service many customers considered better, rather than beginning with a low-end or new-market foothold. They classified the original iPhone as sustaining relative to other smartphones but argued that its later ecosystem followed a disruptive path relative to laptops as devices for internet access. These classifications are contestable, but they demonstrate the theory's unit of analysis: a product and business model relative to a specified incumbent, customer group, performance measure, and period [3].

The original topic's claim that excellent management "systematically destroys" market leaders was too strong. The theory proposes a mechanism by which otherwise sensible management can create vulnerability under particular conditions; it does not establish that good management generally causes failure or that an entrant on a disruptive path must win. Independent studies have found mixed correspondence between canonical cases and the theory, while author-led reviews have acknowledged boundary conditions, anomalies, and alternative explanations [4][5][6][8][9][10].

The most defensible use of disruption theory is therefore diagnostic. It asks whether an entrant has a protected foothold, whether incumbent and entrant incentives are asymmetric, whether the entrant can improve toward mainstream requirements without surrendering its advantage, and whether the incumbent's resources, processes, values, and complementary assets permit an effective response. Each question can be tested against industry evidence; none should be assumed from the popularity of the word "disruption" [3][4][5].

## Core Concepts

### Sustaining Innovation and Disruptive Paths

A sustaining innovation improves performance on dimensions valued by an incumbent's established customers. It can be incremental or technologically radical. Better capacity, reliability, speed, or image quality is sustaining when it advances the performance trajectory on which firms already compete. Incumbents often possess the customer knowledge, technical resources, distribution, and complementary assets needed to perform well in these contests. In the disk-drive research, established firms led many technically demanding improvements when those improvements answered existing customers' requirements [2][4].

A disruptive innovation follows a different competitive path. It initially offers a performance package that mainstream customers may regard as inadequate, but it fits an overlooked segment through lower cost, simplicity, convenience, accessibility, or another attribute. The entrant must then improve enough to attract more demanding users while retaining the feature or business-model advantage that made the foothold viable. An inferior initial product is not sufficient: without a viable market, improvement path, and advantage that survives movement upmarket, an entrant is merely weak rather than disruptive [3][4].

The distinction is circumstantial rather than technological. The internet supported existing mail-order retailers in ways that were sustaining to their established processes, while creating a different challenge for store-based retailers. A technology can be radical without being disruptive, and a business-model change can be disruptive without a major technical breakthrough. Markides argued that technological, business-model, and product innovations should not be placed in one undifferentiated category because their competitive effects and appropriate incumbent responses differ [7].

### Low-End and New-Market Footholds

A low-end foothold appears when incumbents concentrate on demanding, profitable customers and provide more performance than less-demanding customers need or will pay for. An entrant uses a lower-cost model to serve that neglected tier. The incumbent may rationally retreat because defending the segment would dilute margins, require a conflicting cost structure, or divert resources from attractive customers. The entrant gains time to refine its operations before confronting the mainstream [3][4].

A new-market foothold converts nonconsumers into consumers. The relevant customers previously lacked the money, skill, access, or convenience required by the established solution. Early personal computers, for example, did not satisfy the data-processing requirements of mainframe or minicomputer customers, but they enabled individuals and small organizations to perform tasks that had been inaccessible. New-market disruption is not simply entry into any new geography or demographic; the new value proposition must make consumption possible for people for whom the incumbent solution was impractical [3][4][6].

The foothold condition prevents hindsight from doing all the analytical work. A successful entrant that attacks an incumbent's core customers with a better product is not disruptive in the theory's precise sense, even if the industry changes dramatically. Conversely, an entrant can begin on a disruptive path and fail for reasons the theory does not explain. Success is not part of the definition, and failure does not prove that a path was non-disruptive [3][4].

### Performance Trajectories, Overshoot, and "Good Enough"

The classic model places two trajectories in the same market: the rate at which suppliers improve performance and the rate at which customers can use or value that performance. Overshoot occurs when improvement on an established dimension exceeds what some customers require. At that point, attributes such as price, convenience, size, ease of use, or accessibility can become more important. A product that was initially inadequate may become good enough on the old dimension while remaining superior on the new one [2][3][4].

Overshoot is a hypothesis to measure, not an automatic feature of every market. It requires a specified customer tier and performance dimension. Different segments can value different levels of performance, and the incumbent technology can continue improving while the entrant advances. Ecosystem dependencies, standards, complementary technologies, and regulation can accelerate, delay, or prevent substitution. Later research therefore treats the shape and speed of trajectories as an empirical question rather than as parallel lines that inevitably cross [4][8].

The entrant also needs what later literature calls an extendable core: a technology or business model that can satisfy more demanding customers without accumulating the incumbent's full cost structure. If moving upmarket requires the entrant to add the same labor, assets, channels, and complexity as the incumbent, its advantage can disappear. A low price at entry is therefore weaker evidence than a credible explanation of why the entrant can improve while preserving favorable economics [4].

### Asymmetric Motivation and Resource Allocation

The central causal claim is not that incumbents are unaware or technically incapable. It is that their organizations allocate resources toward opportunities supported by powerful customers, identifiable demand, large markets, and attractive margins. Projects for emerging markets often have uncertain customers, small initial revenue, and economics that compare poorly with the incumbent's existing opportunities. Middle managers seeking approval and business units seeking growth can rationally deprioritize them even when senior leaders understand the technology [2][4].

Entrants face the opposite comparison. A small market can be material to a small firm, and a lower gross margin can be acceptable under a different cost structure. Competition within the foothold later pushes successful entrants toward more profitable customers, while incumbents retreat toward tiers they find more attractive. Adner's demand-side modeling, summarized in the 2018 intellectual history, formalized this asymmetry: both sides can move upmarket in pursuit of profitability even though the combined movement exposes the incumbent [4].

Customer attention is not itself the error. The danger is treating the expressed needs of current customers as the complete market. A firm still requires those customers to finance operations, and sustaining investment can be the correct defense against direct competitors. The strategic problem is to maintain that core while separately testing latent needs, nonconsumption, and low-end segments that the current planning system cannot evaluate fairly [2][3][6].

### Resources, Processes, Values, and Business Models

The resources-processes-values framework separates three kinds of organizational capability. Resources include people, cash, technology, equipment, brands, and relationships. Processes are recurring ways of converting resources into results, including product development, budgeting, market research, sales, and resource allocation. Values are the criteria by which managers prioritize opportunities, including acceptable margins, market sizes, risk, and strategic fit [4].

Resources are generally more transferable than processes and values. An incumbent may employ capable engineers and own the required patents but still use planning, channel, and compensation systems designed for its established business. The later theory therefore shifted from a simple technology problem to a business-model problem: an innovation is difficult when profitable commercialization requires a cost structure, revenue model, customer relationship, or success metric that conflicts with the incumbent's organization [4].

A separate unit is one response to this conflict, not a universal prescription. Separation is most plausible when the new opportunity needs a materially different cost structure, profit formula, customers, or scale. The new unit then needs sufficient autonomy to develop appropriate processes and values, while senior leadership protects access to resources. However, separation creates coordination costs and can forfeit useful assets. Research reviewed in 2018 identifies alternatives such as organizational ambidexterity, partnerships, acquisitions, hybrid offerings, deliberate extension of the old trajectory, and repositioning [4][6][7].

### Relativity, Time, and Outcomes

Disruption is relative to a particular incumbent and value network. The same product can sustain one firm and disrupt another because their customers, channels, economics, and performance criteria differ. Analysis must therefore name the incumbent and the market being disrupted. Calling a technology inherently disruptive erases the mechanism the theory is meant to explain [3][4].

Disruption is also a process rather than an event. Entry, foothold formation, product improvement, movement toward the mainstream, incumbent reaction, and market-share change occur over time. Complete replacement is not required: later scholarship distinguishes loss of market leadership from firm exit, and old and new technologies can coexist. An entrant that remains confined to a niche may be commercially successful without completing a disruptive trajectory [3][4].

Finally, outcomes have multiple causes. Regulation, complementary assets, scale economies, acquisition, managerial choices, technological races, and ecosystem structure may explain success or failure better than customer-driven resource allocation in a given case. The theory earns explanatory value only when evidence supports its mechanism and rules out plausible alternatives [5][8][9].

## Evidence

### The Disk-Drive Foundation

Christensen's foundational evidence came from repeated changes in disk-drive form factors. His account described established producers improving capacity and recording performance for mainframe or minicomputer customers while entrants commercialized smaller drives for emerging computer markets. As the smaller architectures improved, they entered applications previously served by larger drives. The 1996 academic article argued that incumbents possessed substantial technical capability but directed resources toward projects backed by powerful current customers [2][4][9].

An author-led account later described a database of nearly 5,000 drive models introduced between 1975 and 1995 and classified 116 technologies: 111 sustaining changes and five smaller-form-factor disruptions. It reported incumbent leadership in all 111 sustaining cases and loss of leadership after the five disruptive changes. These figures are important as the theory's original empirical claim, but they depend on the authors' definitions and case classification rather than on a randomized or prospective design [4][12].

Historical reappraisal has both preserved and narrowed this case. Nicholas found support for the sequence in which 14-inch producers served mainframes, 8-inch drives served minicomputers, 5.25-inch drives served personal computers, and 3.5-inch drives later challenged 5.25-inch leaders. He also documented complications: Seagate remained a major manufacturer after the transition that was said to disrupt it, some incumbents crossed generations, industry-life-cycle effects mattered, and later quantitative work emphasized cannibalization and incumbent cost advantages. The case supports customer and market-segment mechanisms, but not an invariant rule of incumbent extinction [9].

### Canonical Cases and the 77-Case Test

King and Baatartogtokh tested the reach of the theory by asking 79 industry experts about 77 cases cited in "The Innovator's Dilemma" and "The Innovator's Solution." They operationalized four elements: a sustaining trajectory, overshoot of customer needs, incumbent capability to respond, and incumbent floundering because of the disruption. Only seven cases, or 9 percent, were judged to contain all four elements. Experts agreed that incumbents floundered because of a disruptive competitor in about 62 percent of cases, not in nearly every case [5].

The study did not prove that the entire theory is false. Its coding depended on expert judgment, and later debate questioned whether four elements captured the evolving theory completely. It did show that many examples used to illustrate disruption had additional motives or outcomes, including legacy costs, changing economies of scale, and large numbers of entrants. In at least 30 percent of the 77 cases, the researchers identified an expansion of opportunity that attracted many competitors, making probability and selection among entrants relevant. Their recommendation was to retain useful parts of disruption theory alongside classical strategic analysis rather than use one prescription for all cases [5].

### Broader Technology Evidence

Sood and Tellis constructed a different model and tested 36 technologies across seven markets. Their lower-attack technologies were introduced as frequently by incumbents as by entrants, were not generally cheaper than older technologies, and rarely disrupted firms. They also reported multiple crossings in technology performance and coexistence among rival technologies, challenging simple assumptions that a lower trajectory will cross once and permanently replace the old technology [8].

Their definitions and technology-level design differ from the later business-model version of disruption theory, so the results are not a direct test of every refined proposition. They nevertheless provide independent evidence against treating entrant origin, low initial performance, low price, and incumbent failure as a fixed bundle. They also demonstrate why trajectory claims need longitudinal data rather than a retrospective story about a successful entrant [4][8].

### Critiques of Definition and Prediction

Danneels identified ambiguity in what counted as a disruptive technology, questioned whether retrospective cases could support ex ante prediction, reviewed evidence of successful incumbents, and challenged automatic reliance on spin-offs. He argued that the historical cases were valuable for theory building but vulnerable to hindsight and selection effects. He also distinguished a short-term focus on expressed customer demands from a broader market orientation that can investigate latent and future needs [6].

Markides argued that disruptive technological innovation, business-model innovation, and radical product innovation should be treated as distinct phenomena. They can attract different customers and create conflict with incumbent value chains, but they do not necessarily produce the same market evolution or require the same response. His review found that incumbents confronting business-model innovation can have several options beyond imitation through a separate unit [7].

Lepore's historical critique attacked the use of selected cases as a predictive law and disputed details of several canonical histories, including the treatment of Seagate. Nicholas's later review judged parts of that critique carefully researched while also observing that Christensen's theory evolved and that the disk-drive history still generated useful questions. Taken together, the exchange supports a narrower conclusion: historical cases can reveal mechanisms, but the proposed mechanism must be tested against alternative histories and data [9][10].

### Evidence for a Conditional Theory

The 2018 intellectual history assembled evidence that some incumbent responses work under specified conditions. Studies of newspapers, semiconductors, computers, pharmaceuticals, and other settings found roles for structural separation, threat framing, alliances, acquisitions, complementary assets, and ambidexterity. The review also documented that the pace of substitution depends on both new and old technologies and on their ecosystems. This evidence does not restore a universal prediction; it identifies circumstances under which the resource-allocation mechanism is more or less likely to matter [4].

Netflix illustrates the mechanism without proving its generality. Its initial DVD-by-mail service was less attractive to Blockbuster customers who wanted immediate new releases, but it served movie enthusiasts, early DVD adopters, and online shoppers. Streaming later made the offering attractive to mainstream rental customers. The case fits a path from a fringe segment toward the core, but its outcome also involved changing broadband, licensing, capital, and execution. It is an explanatory example, not a sufficient forecast for another industry [3].

The empirical record therefore supports neither wholesale rejection nor automatic application. Disruption theory usefully directs attention to overlooked customers, nonconsumption, business-model conflict, resource allocation, and asymmetric incentives. Its strongest claims become unreliable when those conditions are assumed, when cases are selected only after entrant success, or when alternate causes are not tested [4][5][6][8][9].

## Implications

### For Industry Analysis: Specify the Market Before Naming the Pattern

An analyst should begin by naming the product category, customer tier, geography, performance dimensions, incumbent, entrant, and period. "Online services disrupted retail" is too broad to test. A useful proposition would identify which online model served which nonconsumers or low-end users, what traditional retailers optimized, and when the entrant began attracting mainstream customers. Relativity and time are part of the claim, not qualifications to add later [3][4].

The analyst should then distinguish three questions. Is the new offering technologically radical? Does it improve the incumbent's established performance trajectory? Does it begin from a low-end or new-market foothold and move toward the mainstream? These questions can produce different answers. Treating them separately prevents the common error of labeling every breakthrough or successful platform as disruptive [3][7][11].

### For Incumbents: Diagnose Conflict Before Choosing Structure

The following guidance is a synthesis of the reviewed evidence. First, compare the opportunity with the incumbent's planning metrics: market size, expected margin, payback period, channel economics, service requirements, and customer priority. Second, map which processes and values would reject the opportunity even if the necessary people and technology were available. Third, determine whether the conflict is temporary and manageable inside the core or whether a distinct business model is required [2][4][6].

A separate unit is justified when the opportunity must be profitable at a scale, margin, or cost structure that the parent cannot accept and when mainstream processes would predictably redirect it toward current customers. Autonomy should cover the processes and metrics that create the conflict; it need not sever access to every parent resource. Senior leaders must also decide how the new unit will obtain capital, capabilities, and market access without being forced to meet the core business's thresholds prematurely [3][4].

Separation should not be reflexive. If the innovation sustains the core, isolating it can waste complementary assets and slow adoption. If an incumbent can manage exploration and exploitation through ambidextrous leadership, acquire or partner with an entrant, introduce a useful hybrid, extend the incumbent technology, or reposition into a durable niche, those strategies may be superior. The evidence does not support the original topic's claim that integration is always impractical or that an internal response almost always fails [4][6][7].

The core business should not be dismantled merely because a potential disruptor exists. The 2015 clarification explicitly warns against the slogan "disrupt or be disrupted." Incumbents should continue sustaining investments for current customers while running bounded tests of the foothold threat. The relevant governance problem is to protect learning about the new model without abandoning profitable obligations before evidence warrants it [3].

### For Entrants: A Foothold Is the Beginning, Not the Advantage

An entrant should test whether the target segment is genuinely unattractive or inaccessible to incumbents. Low price alone is not enough; the entrant needs a cost structure and value proposition that work at the foothold's volume and willingness to pay. A new-market entrant must also show that nonconsumers can adopt the product with less money, skill, or infrastructure than the incumbent solution requires [3][4].

The decisive question is whether the entrant can improve along dimensions mainstream customers value without losing its original advantage. This requires evidence about technology progress, unit economics, quality, distribution, regulation, and complements. If each performance gain adds the same cost and complexity borne by incumbents, movement upmarket may erase the asymmetry. An entrant confined to a niche can still be a good business, but it is not evidence of completed disruption [4][8].

Entrants should also expect incumbent behavior to vary. A direct attack on profitable core customers can trigger rapid sustaining innovation, acquisition, legal action, bundling, or price response. A foothold reduces immediate incumbent motivation; it does not eliminate incumbent capability. Scenarios should include successful defense, coexistence, acquisition, and failed entrant execution rather than only displacement [3][4][5].

### For Investors: Do Not Turn a Mechanism Into a Valuation Shortcut

"Disruptive" is not an investment conclusion. The label does not establish market size, competitive advantage, financing durability, governance quality, or intrinsic value. Some entrants on disruptive paths fail, while some successful entrants win through sustaining innovation. An investor must separately evaluate business economics and price, even when the industry pattern fits the theory [3][4].

A useful investment application is to treat disruption as a scenario with observable milestones. Possible milestones include foothold retention, improving performance, stable or improving unit economics during expansion, entry into adjacent customer tiers, and an incumbent response constrained by conflicting economics. Disconfirming evidence includes an incumbent product that matches the entrant without damaging the core, entrant costs converging on incumbent costs, regulation blocking expansion, or customers refusing to trade established performance for the new attributes. This milestone approach is the author's synthesis of the conditional evidence [3][4][5][8].

Incumbent financial statements can supply clues but not proof. Rising margins, shrinking low-end volume, or asset divestment might reflect upmarket movement, but they might also reflect ordinary mix changes, cyclical demand, or restructuring. Entrant growth might indicate a foothold, a temporary subsidy, or direct mainstream competition. Industry analysis must connect accounting observations to customers, operating processes, and competitive behavior before inferring disruption [2][4][5].

### Practical Diagnostic Framework

The author synthesizes the following eight-step framework from the reviewed sources. Each step is a question that can fail; a convincing answer at one step does not substitute for evidence at another [3][4][5][6][8].

1. **Define the reference market.** Which incumbent, customer group, application, geography, and period are being analyzed?

2. **Classify the entry path.** Did the entrant begin with overserved low-end users, nonconsumers, or the incumbent's mainstream customers?

3. **Measure the tradeoff.** On which established dimensions is the entrant initially worse, and on which new dimensions is it better?

4. **Test incumbent motivation.** Why would defending the foothold be economically or organizationally unattractive to the incumbent?

5. **Test entrant economics.** Can the entrant profit or learn at the foothold's price, volume, and service level without indefinite subsidy?

6. **Test the improvement path.** What technology, process, ecosystem, or business-model mechanism permits movement toward mainstream requirements while preserving the entrant's advantage?

7. **Compare response options.** Can the incumbent respond through the core, a separate unit, ambidexterity, acquisition, partnership, a hybrid, trajectory extension, or niche repositioning?

8. **Seek rival explanations and disconfirmation.** Do regulation, scale, complementary assets, managerial error, capital access, chance among many entrants, or another theory explain the outcome better?

The framework is deliberately more demanding than identifying a small entrant and a large incumbent. Its purpose is to make the causal chain visible before the outcome is known. If evidence does not establish a foothold, asymmetric incentives, a viable improvement path, and constrained incumbent response, the safer conclusion is not "disruption" but "competitive change of uncertain type" [5][6][8].

For strategy, the durable insight is narrower than the original topic claimed. Well-managed firms can become vulnerable when the opportunities their systems rationally prioritize differ from those that will matter after customer needs and business models change. That possibility warrants structured attention, but mixed empirical evidence requires conditional forecasts, multiple models, explicit alternatives, and revision as the market evolves [2][4][5][8][9].
