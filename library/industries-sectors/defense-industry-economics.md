# Defense Industry Economics -- Program Duration Converts Scarcity Into Both Advantage and Risk

Defense contractors operate in a market where a few public buyers select long-lived programs, allocate technical and cost risk through contracts, and regulate who may build, maintain, or export the resulting systems. That structure can create durable revenue, installed-base advantages, and high barriers to entry, but backlog becomes cash only through funded, compliant, on-schedule execution across fragile production and sustainment networks [1][2][3][9].

## Background

The defense industry is not a conventional market in which many consumers choose finished products from interchangeable suppliers. For major weapon systems, the United States Department of Defense is often the dominant or only domestic buyer, while allied demand may be routed through government-to-government or licensed commercial channels. The buyer defines operational requirements, controls appropriations and contract awards, accepts or rejects performance, and may influence industry structure because the survival of a production line can depend on a small number of programs. The seller, meanwhile, must combine research, engineering, secure facilities, specialized labor, certified processes, regulated suppliers, and long-duration capital before a system produces recurring revenue. The economic relationship is therefore a bilateral dependency: concentrated public purchasing power confronts a concentrated and highly specialized supply base [3][5][10].

The industry's present concentration partly reflects the budget contraction and consolidation that followed the Cold War. The Department of Defense reported in 2022 that the number of major aerospace and defense prime contractors fell from 51 to five after the 1990s consolidation wave. It also reported declines in suppliers for major categories, including tactical missiles from 13 to three, fixed-wing aircraft from eight to three, and satellites from eight to four [3]. These counts do not prove that every relevant market has only those firms, because competition is program- and capability-specific. They do show why ordinary assumptions about easy entry and plentiful substitutes are unsafe. A nominally large industrial base can contain narrow component markets in which only one or two qualified sources can meet technical, security, schedule, and volume requirements [3][5][15].

Defense acquisition also runs on a much longer clock than normal commercial purchasing. Requirements development, technology maturation, engineering, testing, production approval, annual appropriations, lot awards, fielding, modernization, and sustainment can span decades. GAO's 2026 annual assessment covered 104 of the Department's costliest programs and reported a portfolio exceeding $2.4 trillion. Although the assessed portfolio contained four fewer programs than in the preceding report, its cost increased by more than $50 billion. The expected cycle from program start to initial capability rose to just over 12 years, and that average excluded six programs that had not set new initial-capability dates [1]. Long duration gives an incumbent time to accumulate design knowledge and manufacturing experience, but it also exposes both parties to inflation, requirements change, obsolescence, supplier exit, workforce loss, and forecasting error [1][10].

The public budget cycle separates strategic demand from legally available money. No Future Years Defense Program was released with the 2026 request, so CBO analyzed the one-year request using the 2025 FYDP as the latest five-year plan. The request totaled $961 billion, including $113 billion in mandatory funding provided by the 2025 reconciliation act. Acquisition funding totaled $384 billion: $295 billion of base-budget funding, divided between $153 billion for procurement and $142 billion for research, development, test, and evaluation, plus $89 billion of mandatory acquisition funding, divided between $52 billion for procurement and $37 billion for research and development [8]. CBO found that the missing FYDP and reliance on reconciliation funding made future costs unusually uncertain. These portfolio totals are signals, not guarantees to an individual contractor: quantities, timing, and program mix can change between request, appropriation, obligation, award, and delivery [8][9].

Contracting is the mechanism that converts public demand into a particular allocation of risk. Federal Acquisition Regulation Part 16 places contract types on a spectrum. Under a firm-fixed-price contract, the contractor bears maximum responsibility for performance cost and the resulting profit or loss. Under cost-plus-fixed-fee, the contractor bears minimal responsibility for performance cost while the negotiated fee is fixed. Incentive contracts occupy positions between those poles [2]. The economic question is not whether one type is universally superior, but whether uncertainty is assigned to the party able to estimate, control, and absorb it. Fixed price can reward productivity when design, quantity, and input costs are sufficiently stable. The same form can produce severe losses when used for immature development, volatile inputs, or requirements that are difficult to specify [2][9].

Production economics differ from development economics. Development creates knowledge, prototypes, software, tooling, and test evidence under substantial uncertainty. Production converts a stable design into repeatable units, allowing learning, supplier commitments, and capacity utilization to lower unit cost. Sustainment then supplies parts, repairs, upgrades, training, software, engineering support, and logistics across the installed fleet. One platform can consequently support several economic phases and contract types at once. Lockheed Martin's 2025 filing described the F-35 as its largest program, representing 27 percent of consolidated sales, and stated that the program consists of development, production, and sustainment contracts [9]. The example demonstrates why a program should be analyzed as a lifecycle rather than as a single award.

Security and export rules are part of the production function, not external footnotes. The International Traffic in Arms Regulations govern defense articles, technical data, and defense services under the Arms Export Control Act; registration, licenses, agreements, end-user controls, and limits on retransfers can determine which people, affiliates, suppliers, and customers may participate [12]. The National Industrial Security Program Operating Manual establishes requirements for protecting classified information disclosed to or developed by contractors, including entity and personnel eligibility, safeguarding, information-system security, subcontracting, visits, and foreign ownership, control, or influence [13]. Compliance raises fixed cost and slows some transactions, but established permissions, facilities, systems, and experienced personnel can also become hard-to-replicate capabilities.

This topic therefore examines the defense industry's structural economics: concentrated buyers, program selection, contract risk, budget and production cycles, supply constraints, backlog conversion, installed fleets, intellectual property, security, exports, consolidation, and lifecycle execution. It does not evaluate military strategy, recommend individual securities, or judge a specific procurement policy in isolation. The governing question is how the institutional playing field determines who bears risk, where profit pools form, and whether apparent competitive advantages survive a complete program cycle.

## Core Concepts

### Concentrated buyers create monopsony power and dependency

A major defense market can combine one dominant buyer with only a few credible sellers. The buyer can set qualification standards, demand cost and pricing data where authorized, select contract forms, decide whether to compete or sole-source work, control quantities, and defer awards. This gives the government bargaining power that ordinary customers rarely possess. Yet the power is incomplete when only one supplier controls a qualified design, a unique production process, necessary technical data, or scarce capacity. The market can then resemble bilateral monopoly: each side has leverage, neither can switch quickly, and the negotiated outcome depends on outside options, time pressure, information, and the cost of interruption [2][3][7].

Customer concentration changes how revenue quality should be assessed. A government program may have high strategic priority and a long expected life, but annual sales still depend on appropriations, options, lot negotiations, acceptance, and contractor performance. Lockheed Martin reported that 72 percent of its 2025 sales came from the U.S. government and that one program, the F-35, generated 27 percent of total sales [9]. Those figures illustrate both durability and concentration. The author's synthesis is that public-customer concentration is neither automatically safe nor automatically weak: it can reduce credit risk while increasing political, program, and negotiating dependence.

The buyer's incentives also differ from those of a private consumer. The government values readiness, security, interoperability, surge capacity, domestic capability, alliance commitments, and strategic options in addition to price. The 2024 National Defense Industrial Strategy organized its priorities around resilient supply chains, workforce readiness, flexible acquisition, and economic deterrence [4]. Capacity that appears inefficient in peacetime may have option value during urgent demand. Conversely, a protected production line can consume resources without producing competitive cost or timely output. Economic analysis must therefore separate the value of resilience from an unlimited willingness to pay for redundancy [4][15].

### Program selection determines the economic franchise

Winning a major program can establish a contractor's role for years because the initial selection influences later production, modernization, and sustainment. The value is not confined to the first development contract. A successful platform can generate follow-on lots, spares, repairs, software updates, mission-system upgrades, training, logistics, and international sales. The installed fleet creates recurring needs and accumulates operating data, supplier relationships, tooling, and user familiarity. The contractor that designed and integrated the system may be best placed to perform that work, especially where technical data or interfaces are proprietary [7][9].

The franchise is conditional. Development can be underpriced, production quantities can be cut, a program can be restructured, sustainment can shift to government depots or competitors, and technical or schedule failures can destroy follow-on value. GAO's annual assessments repeatedly connect immature technology, unstable design, and slow development with adverse cost and schedule outcomes [1]. RAND's review of three decades of acquisition research likewise concludes that acquisition strategies must be tailored to a program's characteristics rather than forced into one model, and that long-timeline programs benefit from stability while evolutionary technology benefits from simpler designs and limited requirement changes [10].

Program selection should therefore be evaluated as a distribution of lifecycle outcomes. The upside case includes production scale, a large installed base, recurring sustainment, upgrades, and export opportunities. The downside includes unreimbursed development losses, low-rate production, capital stranded by cancellation, responsibility for defects, and expensive supplier support after quantities shrink [1][7][9][10]. The author's assessment is that a rational contractor values a program by expected lifecycle cash flows after risk, not by headline contract value or strategic prestige.

### Contract type allocates uncertainty, not just price

Firm-fixed-price contracts place maximum cost responsibility on the contractor, while cost-reimbursement contracts shift more allowable cost risk to the government and require greater oversight [2]. That distinction affects bidding, accounting, behavior, and capital needs. A fixed-price contractor can retain gains from performing below the negotiated price, but cost growth can reduce or eliminate profit. A cost-reimbursement contractor is better protected against uncertain allowable costs, but its fee is constrained and the government monitors cost and performance more closely [2]. Incentive arrangements can share cost or performance results around negotiated targets.

The right contract form depends on maturity. Repetitive production with a stable design, known suppliers, and reliable labor standards can support firmer pricing. Frontier development with unresolved technical choices and uncertain test results cannot be estimated with the same confidence. Lockheed Martin's 2025 filing states that development contracts often use cost-reimbursable forms, while some existing development work is fixed price; it also warns that fixed-price development and production options are inherently riskier because revenue is fixed while completion-cost estimates can vary significantly [9]. That is a primary-source description of the economic asymmetry: the contract fixes revenue before engineering uncertainty has been retired.

Contract mix also affects working capital and earnings volatility. Revenue recognized as work progresses does not necessarily arrive in cash on the same schedule. Billing terms, progress payments, milestones, acceptance, retained amounts, supplier payments, inventory, and cost overruns can move cash earlier or later than reported sales [2][9]. Under fixed price, a contractor may finance an overrun until completion and may never recover it. Under cost reimbursement, cash conversion can be more stable if costs are allowable, documented, and billed promptly, but disputes or disallowances still matter. The author's synthesis is that contract profit, accounting revenue, backlog, and cash are four different quantities and should never be treated as synonyms.

### Backlog is conditional future work

Backlog measures awarded work not yet recognized as sales under the company's definition. It can provide visibility because large defense programs convert over multiple years. Lockheed Martin reported year-end 2025 backlog of approximately $193.6 billion, more than two and a half years of its 2025 sales [9]. The same filing shows why backlog must be interpreted carefully: performance depends on government budgets, contract funding, options, production readiness, suppliers, and successful execution [9].

The author's assessment is that backlog quality has at least six dimensions. First is funding: how much is already obligated rather than dependent on future appropriations or options? Second is margin: is the work cost-reimbursable, mature fixed-price production, or risky fixed-price development? Third is duration: how far into the future must labor and material be estimated? Fourth is concentration: how much depends on one program or customer decision? Fifth is conversion capacity: can factories and suppliers deliver at the planned rate? Sixth is cancellation or modification exposure: what rights do the customer and contractor possess? A large backlog with weak funding, scarce parts, or negative expected margin can be less valuable than a smaller, executable book.

### Learning curves and production stability can lower cost

Repeated production can reduce labor hours and improve yield as workers, engineers, tools, suppliers, and quality systems learn. Stable quantities can also justify economic-order purchases, dedicated tooling, workforce retention, supplier investments, and redesigned processes. Current 10 U.S.C. 3501 permits a defense multiyear property contract when it produces savings relative to annual contracts or supplies necessary defense-industrial-base stability that annual contracts cannot provide. It also requires a substantially stable need, a reasonable funding expectation, a stable design with non-excessive technical risk, and realistic cost and cost-avoidance estimates [16]. FAR 17.105-1, although it implements that statute, still states a significant-savings threshold alongside similar stability and realism criteria [17]. The texts are not verbatim aligned after the statute's 2023 and 2025 amendments, so an acquisition team must reconcile controlling law, implementing rules, and any program-specific authorization rather than treating an old checklist as timeless.

Learning is not automatic. Design changes can invalidate work instructions and tooling; interrupted rates can disperse trained workers; a fragile supplier can constrain the whole line; and very low quantities can spread fixed cost over too few units. RAND's historical review of multiyear and block-buy programs found that savings mechanisms and estimates varied, with economic-order quantities, cost-reduction initiatives, production rates, and learning all affecting results [14]. The author's synthesis is that a learning curve is an empirical property of a specific design and production system, not a discount that should be applied mechanically.

Rate stability creates a conflict between efficiency and flexibility. A predictable multiyear quantity can lower unit cost, but it reduces the government's ability to respond to technical problems or changing priorities and may create cancellation liability. Annual lots preserve options but can cause suppliers to avoid investment, price uncertainty into bids, or lose skilled labor between awards [11][14][16][17]. The economic decision is therefore a real-options trade-off: pay for flexibility when uncertainty is high; commit when design, requirement, funding, and either savings or industrial-base-stability evidence are strong enough that stability is worth more than the option to change.

### Capacity is a network property

Prime-contractor floor space is not the same as deliverable capacity. Output depends on engineering release, tooling, test equipment, skilled labor, cleared facilities, software, castings, forgings, energetic materials, electronics, critical minerals, machine capacity, quality approvals, transport, and lower-tier working capital. A production ramp fails when any binding input cannot expand at the required rate. The National Defense Industrial Strategy consequently treats resilient supply chains and workforce readiness as central priorities rather than assuming that a prime award creates supply [4].

Visibility declines below the first tiers. GAO reported in 2025 that Department efforts had collected useful supplier information for selected weapon systems but still lacked enough lower-tier visibility to identify and proactively address some foreign-dependency risks. One effort had country-of-origin information for first- and second-tier suppliers for 30,000 of 40,000 F-35 parts, while broader data collection did not consistently reveal deeper dependencies [6]. This evidence supports a practical distinction: an approved vendor list identifies direct counterparties, not necessarily the mines, materials, processors, machine tools, firmware, or sole-source components that determine resilience.

Spare capacity also has an owner and a price. A supplier will not maintain idle labor, equipment, or inventory indefinitely without compensation or credible demand. Government can use advance procurement, capacity reservations, multiyear commitments, cost sharing, stockpiles, or direct industrial-base investment, but each transfers risk and can preserve inefficient assets if poorly targeted [4][11][15]. The author's assessment is that capacity should be measured by qualified output under stress, including shared dependencies across programs, rather than by announced factory area or theoretical machine hours.

### Intellectual property divides innovation from contestability

Defense systems combine contractor-funded, government-funded, and mixed-funding intellectual property. The associated data rights determine whether the government can use or disclose drawings, manuals, software, interfaces, and process information for maintenance, modification, or competition. GAO found that selected programs experienced vendor lock because of data-rights shortfalls and that these constraints can raise cost and lengthen repair time once systems enter sustainment [7].

Proprietary knowledge can be a legitimate return on innovation. Requiring unlimited transfer of every design or manufacturing method can deter investment or force the government to pay for rights it may not need. The opposite extreme makes the original supplier unavoidable even when price, lead time, or readiness deteriorates. The economic task is to identify critical lifecycle data before award, price alternative rights, require delivery and validation, and preserve modular interfaces where practical [1][7][10].

Installed-base advantage is strongest when three assets reinforce one another: lawful technical control, accumulated operating knowledge, and a responsive support network. It becomes socially costly when advantage rests only on missing data that prevent a qualified alternative from competing [7][9]. The author's synthesis is that durable advantage should arise from continuing superior capability, not accidental information gaps. For the buyer, the reversible choice is to negotiate options and interfaces early; after a fleet is fielded, bargaining power can be expensive to recover.

### Security, certification, and exportability create barriers and constraints

Classified work requires approved organizations, eligible personnel, controlled facilities, secure information systems, reporting, training, subcontractor controls, and safeguards against unauthorized disclosure [13]. These requirements impose recurring cost and limit the immediately available labor and supplier pool. They also create a capability that cannot be reproduced simply by purchasing ordinary industrial equipment. A cleared, compliant organization with experienced security and program personnel can therefore possess a genuine barrier to entry, although the barrier protects information rather than guaranteeing technical or commercial excellence.

Export controls shape scale. DDTC administers the ITAR for defense articles, technical data, and defense services, and its licensing guidance states that most transfers to foreign persons require authorization unless an exception applies [12]. International sales can spread development and production cost, raise rates, sustain a supplier base, and extend a platform's installed fleet. Yet licenses, end-use restrictions, retransfer controls, technology-release limits, national configurations, offsets, and foreign approvals can delay or prevent that scale [12][13]. Exportability designed late can require costly redesign; designed early, it may still require separation of sensitive capabilities.

The author's assessment is that certification and control systems are double-edged economic assets. They can protect technology, qualify a firm for scarce work, and increase switching cost. They can also create fixed overhead, slow collaboration, and exclude commercial innovators. A durable defense franchise integrates compliance into engineering and supply-chain architecture so that security is not a late bottleneck and export demand is not assumed before authorization.

### Consolidation can create scale while reducing resilience

Mergers can eliminate duplicate overhead, stabilize a financially weak supplier, combine complementary technologies, and support investment that a smaller firm could not finance. They can also remove a design team, reduce the number of bids, concentrate a critical input, and give a vertically integrated supplier the ability or incentive to disadvantage rival primes. GAO found that the Department assessed an average of about 40 defense mergers and acquisitions per year in fiscal years 2018 through 2022, while previously published Department statistics indicated roughly 400 defense transactions annually; GAO also found limited monitoring of whether identified risks later materialized [5].

The correct unit of analysis is the capability market, not the corporation count. Five diversified primes may still compete vigorously for one class of work while only one qualified source exists for a propulsion component, seeker, casting, or secure software function. Conversely, preserving two nominal suppliers does not ensure resilience if both depend on the same lower-tier source [3][6]. The author's synthesis is that consolidation should be evaluated against innovation, surge, switching, data access, vertical foreclosure, shared dependencies, and the cost of sustaining multiple design and production teams.

## Evidence

### Portfolio evidence shows persistent duration and execution risk

GAO's 2026 annual assessment examined 104 of the Department's costliest acquisition efforts using program cost, schedule, technology, and development information. The assessed portfolio exceeded $2.4 trillion and grew by more than $50 billion from the preceding assessment despite including four fewer programs. The expected cycle from program start to initial capability rose to just over 12 years and excluded six programs that had not set replacement delivery dates. GAO also found that 18 of 40 programs entering the middle-tier pathway from 2018 through 2025 began with immature technologies, and that reviewed middle-tier efforts were ending without fieldable capability [1]. The method is stronger than a single-company anecdote because it reviews a broad government portfolio, although it focuses on costly programs and does not measure every contract or supplier.

The evidence links program economics to development discipline. Immature technology and slow, linear approaches keep uncertainty alive while organizations, inflation, requirements, and suppliers continue to change. RAND's synthesis of three decades of acquisition research reaches a compatible conclusion: program strategies should be tailored, requirements and technology choices should be stabilized where possible, and modular or evolutionary approaches can reduce the consequences of long timelines [10]. Together, the sources support a conditional claim rather than a universal rule. Long programs are not doomed, but unresolved uncertainty compounds with time.

For industry analysis, the implication is measurable. A contractor with a large development portfolio should be evaluated for technology maturity, requirement stability, contract type, testing progress, remaining cost estimate, supplier readiness, and whether production is beginning before design risk has been retired [1][2][9]. Aggregate backlog and sales growth do not reveal those exposures. Cost growth under cost reimbursement may expand revenue while weakening affordability; cost growth under fixed price may reduce margin and cash. The same operational problem can therefore produce opposite accounting effects depending on the contract.

### Contract rules and company disclosures show how risk reaches financial statements

FAR Part 16 explicitly states that firm-fixed-price places maximum cost risk on the contractor and that cost-plus-fixed-fee places minimal performance-cost responsibility on the contractor [2]. Lockheed Martin's 2025 Form 10-K supplies an observed company mix: its portfolio was about 60 percent fixed price and 40 percent cost type, 72 percent of sales came from the U.S. government, and year-end backlog was about $193.6 billion [9]. It also warns that fixed-price development work is riskier because costs can vary while revenue remains fixed [9].

This case should not be generalized mechanically to every contractor. Shipbuilders, service providers, missile producers, electronics suppliers, and research firms can have different contract, customer, and working-capital profiles [2][9]. It nevertheless demonstrates that defense revenue is a portfolio of legal risk allocations. The author's synthesis is that the analytical bridge should run from each material program's technical status and contract form to estimated cost at completion, margin, billing, cash investment, and remaining backlog.

The 2025 filing also shows lifecycle concentration. The F-35 generated 27 percent of Lockheed Martin's total sales through a combination of development, production, and sustainment [9]. This supports the installed-base thesis but also reveals dependence: technical, budget, delivery, or sustainment changes in one platform can affect a large share of company economics. A fleet franchise is therefore valuable precisely because it is difficult to replace, and risky for the same reason.

### Budget evidence explains why demand visibility is not funding certainty

CBO's 2026 analysis reported a $961 billion Department budget request, including $113 billion in mandatory funding provided by the 2025 reconciliation act. Acquisition funding totaled $384 billion, 18 percent more in real terms than the amount appropriated for 2025 and 21 percent more than the 2025 FYDP had anticipated for 2026. The composition qualified that increase: base-budget acquisition funding was $295 billion, 9.0 percent below the amount appropriated for 2025 and 7.4 percent below the prior FYDP's expectation, while mandatory funding supplied the difference [8].

No FYDP accompanied the 2026 request. CBO therefore used the last five-year plan and considered alternative interpretations of whether the reconciliation-supported increase was temporary or enduring. A supplier considering a new furnace, energetic-material line, secure facility, or workforce expansion cannot infer a durable production profile from one total. The author's synthesis is that the split between base and mandatory funding, together with appropriation, award, and quantity risk, can cause rational suppliers to demand higher prices, shorten commitments, or avoid specialized investment even when strategic statements call for surge capacity [8].

Multiyear procurement is one response, but its current legal threshold is more nuanced than the 2008 GAO checklist. Section 3501 now permits the required threshold finding to rest on cost savings or necessary defense-industrial-base stability, while retaining findings on stable need, funding, design, technical risk, and realistic estimates [16]. FAR 17.105-1 still states significant savings and similar stability conditions [17]. Historical GAO and RAND work shows why the remaining evidence matters: savings can arise from economic-order purchases, cost-reduction initiatives, learning, and production-rate decisions, while cancellation, changed quantities, and weak baselines can erase them [11][14]. These findings reject both extremes: annual contracting is not always prudent flexibility, and multiyear commitment is not automatically a discount or a capacity guarantee.

### Industrial-base evidence shows concentration and hidden dependencies

The Department's 2022 competition report documented major consolidation among primes and weapon categories and connected lower competition with risks to cost, innovation, capacity, and supply [3]. GAO's later merger review found a wide gap between the hundreds of estimated annual defense transactions and the smaller number assessed by the responsible Department office, as well as limited ex post monitoring [5]. The two sources are institutionally related but measure different things: the Department describes market structure and policy, while GAO evaluates the process used to see and manage merger risk.

Supply-chain data add another layer. GAO's 2025 foreign-dependency review found that Department initiatives had improved visibility into selected upper tiers but did not provide enough lower-tier information to identify and address all relevant dependencies proactively [6]. GAO's 2022 industrial-base review similarly found that the Department lacked a consolidated implementation framework and sufficient progress measures for risk mitigation [15]. These findings do not mean every lower-tier dependency is dangerous. They show that resilience claims require evidence at the point where substitution, qualification, and capacity actually bind.

The author's synthesis is that concentration has three distinct forms. Horizontal concentration reduces the number of competing suppliers at one level. Vertical concentration gives one participant control of an input needed by rivals. Common-mode concentration occurs when apparently independent suppliers share the same lower-tier source, site, material, software, or equipment. Corporate market-share tables detect the first imperfectly and can miss the other two. Program-level bills of material, data rights, facility mapping, and qualification timelines are needed to understand deliverable competition [3][5][6].

### Sustainment evidence demonstrates the value and danger of installed fleets

GAO's 2025 data-rights review examined selected systems in sustainment and found incomplete planning and vendor-lock effects where programs lacked necessary technical data and rights. The selected programs relied on sole-source arrangements for some depot work, with consequences that could include higher costs and longer repair times [7]. This is direct evidence that choices made during development and contracting can determine competition many years later.

The result does not imply that every original manufacturer is exploiting lock-in or that all sustainment should be competed. The original developer may possess unique knowledge, equipment, software, warranties, and safety accountability. Some detailed manufacturing information may be legitimately proprietary, and acquiring broader rights has a price [7]. The economic finding is narrower: data-rights planning is an option purchased early. If omitted, alternatives can become unavailable or expensive after the installed base creates urgent readiness needs.

Installed fleets can still be strong franchises when support performance is good. Recurring parts, repairs, training, upgrades, and software can be less exposed to new-platform competitions than initial development. The network of depots, field representatives, suppliers, test equipment, and user knowledge can improve response and create switching costs. The author's assessment is that the quality of this moat should be measured by readiness, repair cycle time, affordability, upgrade speed, and customer control of critical interfaces, not by sole-source status alone [7][9].

### Security and export rules are observable entry costs

ITAR places defense articles, services, and technical data within a licensing and control system administered by the Department of State. DDTC guidance describes registration and authorization requirements for covered exports, retransfers, technical assistance, and foreign-person access [12]. NISPOM requires contractors handling classified information to maintain organizational, personnel, physical, information-system, subcontracting, and international safeguards [13]. These are legally observable capabilities, not vague claims of relationship capital.

The economic effect is conditional. An established contractor can spread compliance systems over many programs, while a new entrant may face a large fixed cost before competing for classified or export-controlled work. The rules can also restrict how an incumbent recruits, collaborates, sources, or sells [12][13]. The author's synthesis is that security qualification creates advantage only when paired with technical relevance and execution. Compliance is necessary for some markets but is not evidence that a firm can design, produce, or sustain the required system.

## Implications

### For investors and industry analysts

Analysis should begin at the program level and aggregate upward. For each material program, identify the customer, acquisition phase, contract form, funded and unfunded backlog, remaining performance obligations, expected production rate, technical maturity, supplier bottlenecks, required capital, sustainment role, data rights, export assumptions, and downside liability. This structure prevents a portfolio dominated by mature cost-type sustainment from being valued like one dominated by fixed-price development, even when both report similar sales [1][2][7][9].

The author's assessment is that backlog should be converted into a risk-weighted schedule rather than multiplied by a standard margin. Mature, funded production with stable suppliers deserves different assumptions from unfunded options, early development, or fixed-price work already experiencing cost pressure. Analysts should reconcile bookings to appropriations and awards, backlog to sales, sales to billings, billings to operating cash, and operating cash to capital expenditure and supplier support. The single worst analytical error is to treat political demand as immediate, profitable production capacity [8][9].

Margin analysis should distinguish favorable learning from risk retirement. A production margin can improve because labor hours fell, yield rose, rate increased, or supplier terms improved. It can also improve because an estimate changed after a milestone reduced expected risk. The opposite applies to charges: one loss can reveal a program-specific estimate correction rather than a permanent segment margin, while repeated fixed-price development losses can reveal poor bidding or weak risk controls [1][9]. The author's assessment is that cash and physical delivery are stronger corroboration than a margin narrative alone.

Program concentration should be tested in both directions. A dominant program can create a deep moat through scale, fleet size, supplier investment, and follow-on work. It can also make the company hostage to one budget line, acceptance problem, or redesign. Lockheed Martin's disclosed F-35 share illustrates the scale of this duality [9]. A portfolio is diversified only when its programs do not share the same customer decision, bottleneck, facility, technology, or geopolitical assumption.

Capital-cycle analysis is essential. High demand and urgent policy can attract new factories, second sources, and supplier investment, but capacity takes time to qualify. If many projects mature after demand normalizes, pricing and utilization can fall. If investment remains below need because awards are unstable, scarcity and schedule delays can persist [4][6][11]. Investors should track qualified output, delivery rate, lead time, overtime, supplier advances, inventory, capital commitments, and customer-funded capacity rather than relying on announced demand.

The moat test should be specific. Scale is durable when it lowers full cost and improves delivery, not when it merely enlarges overhead. An installed fleet is durable when support creates readiness and affordable upgrades, not when missing data block alternatives. Intellectual property is durable when it embodies continuing innovation and integration knowledge, not when ownership is unclear. Security and certification are durable when they are current, auditable, and attached to scarce technical capability. Backlog is durable when it is funded, profitable, and producible. Each mechanism has a measurable failure condition [7][9][13].

### For defense-company managers

Bid discipline should connect technical maturity to contract risk. A fixed price is economically sound only when the team can bound requirements, design, supplier quotes, labor, test, schedule, inflation, and liability. Competitive pressure does not remove uncertainty; it only tempts the bidder to ignore it. Management should require independent estimate challenge, explicit risk ranges, supplier commitments, and a cash downside case before accepting fixed-price development or distant production options [2][9].

Program selection is more important than revenue maximization. A prestigious development win can consume engineering talent and cash while blocking better opportunities. Management should compare the lifecycle option value with the cost of exclusivity, capital, security infrastructure, intellectual-property concessions, and uncapped execution exposure. The reversible approach is staged commitment: retire technology and interface risk before locking large quantities or distant fixed prices where the customer permits it [1][10].

Capacity plans should follow the constraint tree. Start with required delivered units and work backward through final assembly, test, major subsystems, critical processes, materials, tooling, labor, security, and lower-tier finance. For each bottleneck, identify qualification time, alternate sources, shared program demand, inventory policy, and who pays for reserve capacity. A prime that expands final assembly without solving a constrained casting, motor, chip, or test stand creates visible capital but no additional system output [4][6].

Supplier relationships require economic realism. Small and specialized suppliers may face working-capital strain when they must buy material, hire cleared workers, or expand equipment before receiving payment. Long but uncertain demand can be worse than a smaller stable order because it invites investment without protecting recovery. Advance payments, long-lead funding, multiyear commitments, shared tooling, and fair change mechanisms can improve capacity, but they should be tied to verified milestones and resilience outcomes [4][11][15].

Data and export architecture should be designed with the product. Management should identify what is proprietary, what the government has funded, what interfaces customers need for competition and sustainment, and what information can be released to allies or suppliers. Export variants, modular boundaries, cybersecurity, and classified components should be planned before the production configuration is frozen. Late separation of controlled technology can delay sales and fragment support [7][12][13].

Sustainment should be managed as performance, not entitlement. Installed fleets create recurring work only if parts arrive, repairs close, software updates function, and readiness improves. Contractor and government roles should be explicit; technical data deliveries should be validated; obsolescence should be forecast; and repair capacity should be measured under surge. The worst outcome is a fleet that is locked to one supplier but still unavailable. Preventing it protects both customer trust and the economic franchise [7].

### For public buyers and policymakers

The buyer should allocate risk to the party that can manage it. Fixed price is useful when requirements and designs are stable and suppliers can control cost. It is destructive when imposed on unresolved development merely to create the appearance of certainty: bidders either price a large contingency, underbid and later lose money, or avoid the competition [2][9]. Cost reimbursement is justified by uncertainty only when paired with capable oversight, knowledge-building milestones, and a path toward firmer pricing as uncertainty falls.

Competition policy should preserve capabilities, not just company counts. Merger review should examine program-level bidders, critical inputs, design teams, data access, vertical incentives, and common lower-tier dependencies. Reviews also need follow-up because predicted efficiencies or risks may not materialize as expected [3][5]. When only one source is viable, the government can use cost visibility, data rights, modularity, public depots, second-source options, or targeted capacity investment rather than pretending that a sole-source award is a competitive market.

Budget stability should be treated as an industrial input. If the government wants lower unit costs, supplier investment, and surge capacity, it must provide credible quantities and timing where uncertainty is low enough to commit. Multiyear procurement can support that objective when the applicable statutory, regulatory, and program-specific findings are satisfied; either cost savings or industrial-base stability can justify the threshold finding under current law, but neither removes cancellation, funding, design, or technical risk [11][14][16][17]. For unstable technology, smaller increments and open interfaces preserve learning and alternatives better than premature scale.

Resilience policy should focus on bottlenecks whose failure would stop multiple systems. Supply mapping must extend below named prime and first-tier contractors to facilities, processes, materials, software, and foreign dependencies. Mitigation should be proportionate: some risks justify inventory, qualification of an alternate, or geographic diversity; others justify direct investment or a protected reserve line. Duplicating every input would destroy scale without eliminating common dependencies [4][6][15].

Intellectual-property strategy should be completed before bargaining power shifts. The government should identify the data and rights needed for testing, safety, repair, modification, cybersecurity, competition, and export support; evaluate the price of alternative license scopes; require timely delivery; and verify that delivered data are usable [7]. It need not demand every trade secret. It should avoid discovering after fielding that critical maintenance or recompetition is impossible.

Security and export control should protect sensitive capability while making permitted collaboration predictable. Clear release boundaries, timely licensing, interoperable security procedures, and early foreign-disclosure planning can support allied demand and supplier participation without treating control as an afterthought [12][13]. The economic objective is not maximum diffusion. It is authorized scale that preserves technology protection and avoids unnecessary redesign or idle capacity.

### For suppliers and potential entrants

A supplier should evaluate the complete compliance and cash burden before treating a defense award as attractive revenue. Required quality systems, cybersecurity, export controls, classified handling, audits, long-lead material, specialized tooling, and slow qualification can absorb more capital than the contract's face value suggests [12][13]. Dependence on one prime or one program can reproduce the concentration risk seen at the top of the industry.

Entrants should seek a narrow capability where commercial technology, faster iteration, lower cost, or a scarce process creates a genuine advantage. Attempting to reproduce an incumbent's complete platform, cleared workforce, supplier network, and certification base is usually less reversible than entering through a modular subsystem or service. Open interfaces and government demand for new suppliers can create access, but the entrant still needs evidence that the program will fund integration and production rather than only prototypes [1][4][10].

Negotiations should price uncertainty explicitly. A supplier should separate nonrecurring engineering, recurring production, tooling, capacity expansion, inflation, minimum quantities, change liability, cancellation, and data rights. When the prime or government requests a low fixed price before these variables are known, the supplier can propose indexed adjustments, milestones, options, or cost-sharing rather than hiding risk in an optimistic estimate [2][11].

### A disciplined defense-industry assessment

The author's synthesis is a twelve-step framework. First, define the specific capability market and public buyers. Second, map credible primes and critical lower tiers. Third, separate development, production, modernization, and sustainment. Fourth, identify contract form and who bears each uncertainty. Fifth, trace the budget from plan through appropriation, obligation, and award. Sixth, grade backlog by funding, margin, duration, concentration, capacity, and cancellation exposure. Seventh, test technology, design, and requirement maturity. Eighth, build a constraint tree for qualified output. Ninth, assess learning, rate stability, and capital-cycle response. Tenth, identify data rights, installed-base control, security, and export boundaries. Eleventh, model cost, schedule, quantity, supplier, and policy downside together. Twelfth, compare through-cycle cash return with the capital and irreversible capability required [1][2][6][7][8][9][11][16][17].

The author's central conclusion is conditional. Defense programs can create unusually durable economic positions because public customers commit to specialized systems whose production, security, integration, and sustainment knowledge accumulate over decades. The same duration magnifies forecasting error, political dependence, fixed-price losses, supplier fragility, and lock-in. Durable value belongs not to the contractor with the largest announced backlog, but to the production system that repeatedly converts authorized demand into accepted capability, customer readiness, and cash without requiring hidden subsidies from future contracts.
