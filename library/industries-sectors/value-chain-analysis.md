# Value Chain Analysis -- Why Some Links in an Industry Capture Outsize Profits While Others Are Condemned to Commodity Returns

Value chain analysis is the strategic framework, introduced by Michael
Porter in his 1985 book "Competitive Advantage," that disaggregates a
firm into its strategically relevant activities to understand the
sources of cost advantage and differentiation. More than an internal
mapping exercise, the value chain reveals a deeper truth about industry
economics: not all activities are equally capable of generating
sustained profits. In every industry, some links in the chain --
design, intellectual property, brand ownership, platform control --
capture the majority of economic value, while others -- assembly,
commodity production, generic distribution -- are structurally condemned
to thin margins regardless of how well they are managed. For investors
and strategists, the value chain is the tool that answers the question:
where, in the sequence of activities from raw materials to end consumer,
does the money actually get made?

## Background

The value chain emerged from Porter's broader research program on
competitive strategy at Harvard Business School. In his 1980 book
"Competitive Strategy," Porter had introduced the Five Forces
framework, which explained why some industries are more profitable
than others by analyzing external competitive pressures. But Five
Forces left a critical question unanswered: within a given industry,
why do some companies earn higher returns than others?

"Competitive Advantage" (1985) answered that question by turning the
analyst's gaze inward. Porter argued that competitive advantage cannot
be understood by looking at a firm as a whole -- the firm is too broad
a unit of analysis. Instead, advantage arises from the many discrete
activities a firm performs in designing, producing, marketing,
delivering, and supporting its product. Each of these activities can
contribute to a firm's relative cost position and create a basis for
differentiation.

The value chain concept was a radical departure from the prevailing
strategic frameworks of the 1980s. The Boston Consulting Group's growth-share
matrix and the McKinsey/GE multifactor portfolio matrix treated firms
as black-box portfolios of businesses. Porter insisted that strategy
required understanding what actually happens inside the firm: the
specific, concrete activities that create value and incur costs.
This activity-based view of the firm was later extended into a full
theory of strategic positioning and remains one of the most durable
frameworks in business strategy, still taught at business schools and
used by consulting firms globally nearly four decades after its
introduction.

Before Porter, the dominant model for understanding firm structure was
the functional organization chart (R&D, manufacturing, marketing,
sales). Porter argued that functions are too aggregated to reveal
competitive advantage. A single "marketing" function might contain
activities as diverse as advertising (which creates brand value),
sales force management (which creates distribution efficiency), and
market research (which creates customer insight) -- each with different
economics and different strategic importance. The value chain
disaggregates these broad functions into strategically distinct
activities, making the sources of advantage visible.

## Core Concepts

### The Activity-Based View of the Firm

At its core, the value chain is built on the premise that a firm is a
collection of activities, and competitive advantage comes from
performing these activities either more cheaply (cost advantage) or
better (differentiation advantage) than competitors. Porter divides
these activities into two categories: primary activities and support
activities.

**Primary activities** are directly involved in creating, delivering,
and supporting the product or service. There are five generic categories:

1. **Inbound logistics:** Activities associated with receiving, storing,
   and distributing inputs. This includes material handling,
   warehousing, inventory control, and returns to suppliers. For a
   manufacturer like Toyota, this means the just-in-time parts delivery
   system. For Amazon, it means the receiving and sorting at fulfillment
   centers.

2. **Operations:** Activities that transform inputs into the final
   product. This includes machining, assembly, packaging, equipment
   maintenance, testing, and facility operations. Operations is where
   physical transformation happens -- a factory assembling iPhones,
   a refinery processing crude oil, a software team writing code. Porter
   is careful to note that operations is not the same as manufacturing:
   service operations (a consultant preparing a deliverable, a bank
   processing a loan application) are equally part of this category.

3. **Outbound logistics:** Activities associated with collecting, storing,
   and physically distributing the product to buyers. This includes
   finished goods warehousing, order processing, scheduling, and
   delivery vehicle operations. Walmart's outbound logistics -- its
   cross-docking distribution system and private truck fleet -- was a
   primary source of its cost advantage for decades.

4. **Marketing and sales:** Activities that make buyers aware of the
   product and enable purchase. This includes advertising, promotion,
   sales force deployment, channel selection, channel relations, and
   pricing. Apple's marketing and sales activities -- its minimalist
   advertising, controlled retail stores, and premium pricing strategy --
   are arguably its most visible source of differentiation.

5. **Service:** Activities associated with maintaining or enhancing
   the product's value after sale. This includes installation, repair,
   training, parts supply, and product adjustment. For enterprise
   software companies like SAP or Oracle, service (implementation
   consulting, ongoing support) often generates more revenue than the
   initial license sale.

**Support activities** sustain and enable the primary activities. There
are four generic categories:

1. **Firm infrastructure:** Activities that support the entire value
   chain, including general management, planning, finance, accounting,
   legal, government relations, and quality management. Unlike other
   support activities, infrastructure typically supports the entire
   chain, not individual primary activities. A firm's planning and
   control systems can be a powerful source of advantage -- GE's
   legendary management development system under Jack Welch is an
   example of infrastructure as competitive weapon.

2. **Human resource management:** Activities involved in recruiting,
   hiring, training, development, and compensation of all personnel.
   HRM affects competitive advantage through its impact on the skills,
   motivation, and cost of employees in every activity. Investment
   banking, consulting, and law firms -- where human capital is the
   primary asset -- derive their competitive advantage almost entirely
   from superior HRM activities.

3. **Technology development:** Activities related to improving products
   and processes. This includes not just R&D in the narrow sense, but
   process improvements in every activity: logistics technology (real-time
   tracking), operations technology (automation), marketing technology
   (CRM systems), and service technology (diagnostic tools). Porter
   emphasizes that technology development occurs in every activity, not
   just those associated with engineering or R&D departments. Walmart's
   early investment in satellite-based inventory tracking was a
   technology development activity in its logistics function that
   transformed retail economics.

4. **Procurement:** The function of purchasing inputs used in the
   firm's value chain, not the purchased inputs themselves. This includes
   raw materials, supplies, machinery, laboratory equipment, office
   equipment, and buildings. Procurement spans the entire firm -- the
   quality of purchased inputs affects costs and differentiation in
   every activity. How a company manages its supplier relationships
   and negotiates input costs can be a significant source of advantage.

### Margin, Value, and the Activity-Cost Link

The value chain is organized around the concept of margin. A firm is
profitable if the value it creates exceeds the collective cost of
performing its value activities. Value is what buyers are willing to pay,
and superior value comes from offering lower prices than competitors
for equivalent benefits or providing unique benefits that more than
offset a premium price. Every activity in the chain either adds to the
buyer's willingness to pay or adds to costs -- and the strategic art
lies in understanding which activities do which, and how they interact.

Activities are not independent. They are connected by linkages: the
way one activity is performed affects the cost or effectiveness of
others. A well-designed product (technology development activity) can
reduce after-sale service costs (service activity). Higher-quality raw
materials (inbound logistics/procurement) can reduce manufacturing
defects (operations) and warranty claims (service). Managing these
linkages, not just optimizing individual activities, is the source of
sustainable competitive advantage. A competitor can copy an individual
activity -- they can match a logistics system or replicate a
manufacturing process -- but they cannot easily replicate the intricate
web of linkages that make the activities fit together as a system.

### Industry Value Chain and Profit Pools

The firm's value chain is embedded in a larger stream of activities:
the value system, or industry value chain. This includes the value
chains of upstream suppliers (who provide inputs), the firm itself,
downstream channels (distributors, retailers), and ultimately buyers.
A firm's competitive position depends not just on its own value chain,
but on where it sits in this broader system.

This is where value chain analysis connects directly to investment
analysis through the concept of profit pools. A profit pool is the
total profit earned at each link in an industry value chain. The key
insight: profit is distributed highly unevenly across the chain. In the
smartphone industry, for example, Apple captures as much as 80-90% of
the total industry profit despite holding less than 20% of unit market
share, while the hundreds of companies involved in component supply and
contract assembly fight over single-digit margins. Apple's profit
dominance comes not from doing everything in the chain but from owning
the specific links with structural profit potential -- industrial design,
custom silicon, brand ownership, and the services ecosystem --
while outsourcing the structurally low-margin links (assembly,
commodity components) to other firms.

The strategic and investment implication is clear: where you sit in the
value chain matters far more than how well you execute at a bad link.
The best-run commodity steel producer will never earn the returns of a
mediocre intellectual property company, because the structural
economics of their respective value chain positions are fundamentally
different. This does not mean steel companies cannot be good investments
-- they can be, at the right point in the cycle, or when they have a
unique structural advantage -- but the burden of proof shifts heavily
toward management quality and timing when industry structure is
unfavorable.

### Vertical Integration and Make-vs-Buy

Value chain analysis directly informs one of the oldest strategic
questions: which activities should a firm perform internally and which
should it outsource? Vertical integration means owning multiple links
in the industry value chain. The make-vs-buy decision is a special case
of this: for any individual activity, should the firm perform it
internally or purchase it from the market?

Porter's framework suggests that vertical integration makes sense when
the activity being integrated has strong linkages with other firm
activities (coordination benefits), when the activity involves
proprietary knowledge that would be risky to share with suppliers
(appropriability concerns), or when the activity is a significant
driver of differentiation or cost position. Conversely, outsourcing
makes sense when the activity is standardized and commoditized, when
specialist suppliers can achieve scale economies the firm cannot, or
when the activity does not involve strategic differentiation.

### Digital Platforms and Value Chain Disintermediation

Digital platforms have fundamentally disrupted traditional value chains
by inserting themselves between existing links. A platform like Amazon
Marketplace does not own the traditional retail value chain activities
(warehousing for marketplace items, merchandising, direct sales) --
instead, it provides the infrastructure (search, payment processing,
trust mechanisms) that connects buyers and sellers directly, capturing
a commission on each transaction. This is disintermediation: removing
or replacing a link in the traditional value chain.

The economic power of digital platforms comes from their ability to
capture value without owning the capital-intensive links in the chain.
Platforms own the relationship with the customer (the most valuable
link), the data generated by transactions, and the rules of the
marketplace. Traditional intermediaries -- bookstores, travel agents,
record labels -- found their value chain positions eliminated when
digital platforms could connect producers directly to consumers at
lower cost and greater scale. For investors, understanding which links
in a value chain are vulnerable to platform disintermediation is
essential: if a company's value proposition is essentially matching
buyers and sellers in an information-rich market, a platform can
probably do it better and cheaper.

## Industry Profit Pool Analysis: A Deeper Look

Mapping profit pools along the value chain is a diagnostic exercise
that reveals where structural returns concentrate. The process involves
three steps: (1) define the value chain for the industry, (2) estimate
the total profit pool and its distribution across links, and (3)
analyze why profits concentrate where they do.

In the pharmaceutical industry, the profit pools are starkly uneven.
Drug discovery and patent-protected manufacturing capture massive
profits protected by intellectual property. Generic manufacturing, by
contrast, operates on thin margins because the product is commoditized
and competition is primarily price-based. Pharmacy benefit managers
(PBMs) in the U.S. occupy a contested middle ground: they extract
significant profits through their negotiating power with drug
manufacturers, but face political pressure and potential regulatory
reform that threatens their position.

In the automotive industry, the traditional profit pools favored
manufacturers (OEMs) who owned brands and controlled distribution.
But the emergence of electric vehicles has scrambled the value chain.
Battery manufacturing -- once a commodity input activity -- has become
a strategic bottleneck, with companies like CATL and LG Energy Solution
capturing profits that would historically have gone to the OEM. The
semiconductor content of modern vehicles has similarly shifted value
toward chip designers and fabricators. Value chain profit pools are not
static: technological change, regulation, and competitive dynamics
continuously redistribute them.

In retail, the profit pool distribution has been radically restructured
by e-commerce. Traditional retail value chains involved manufacturers,
wholesalers, brick-and-mortar retailers, and often additional
distributors. Amazon captured the profits not by doing all of these
better but by eliminating or compressing multiple links. Its logistics
infrastructure replaced the wholesaler-distributor-retailer chain,
allowing it to offer competitive pricing while earning returns
unavailable to traditional retailers burdened with legacy cost
structures.

## Evidence

The empirical evidence for the value chain framework's practical value
comes from multiple streams of research and observed business outcomes.

Porter (1985) based the framework on extensive field research across
hundreds of industries, documenting how activity-level analysis
revealed sources of advantage invisible at the firm or business-unit
level. In "Competitive Advantage," he presented detailed case studies
of companies including the airline industry (showing how different
carriers configured their value chains for cost leadership versus
differentiation), and heavy truck manufacturing (demonstrating how
linkages between design and after-sale service created competitive
advantage).

The profit pool concept was operationalized by consulting firms
including Bain & Company and McKinsey in the 1990s and 2000s. Orit
Gadiesh and James Gilbert of Bain published the seminal article
"Profit Pools: A Fresh Look at Strategy" in Harvard Business Review
(1998), which demonstrated how mapping profit distributions along
value chains revealed opportunities invisible in conventional market
share analysis. Their case studies showed that companies focusing on
high-profit pool segments could achieve dramatically higher returns
than those pursuing market share in broad but unprofitable segments.

The empirical record of Apple's value chain strategy provides perhaps
the clearest real-world validation. Apple's decision to own the design,
brand, and software links while outsourcing assembly has produced one
of the most extreme examples of profit pool concentration in business
history. Apple captured approximately 80-90% of global smartphone
industry profits through 2024-2025 despite holding roughly 18-20% unit
market share. As of fiscal 2025, Apple generated roughly $416 billion
in revenue and approximately $112 billion in net profit with a
net margin near 27%, while owning almost no manufacturing facilities.
The company's services segment, with gross margins near 74% compared
to hardware margins around 37%, represents a deliberate value chain
strategy: shift the profit mix toward the highest-margin links in the
chain.

The digital platform disintermediation pattern has been documented
extensively. Research on platform economics (Parker and Van Alstyne,
2005; Rochet and Tirole, 2003) demonstrated theoretically that
platforms could capture value disproportionate to their capital
investment by owning the information layer between buyers and sellers.
Empirical evidence from industries transformed by platforms -- books
(Amazon vs. Borders), travel (Expedia vs. travel agents), music
(Spotify vs. record labels), video (Netflix vs. video rental) --
consistently shows that platforms capture economic rents by
disintermediating capital-intensive links and retaining only the
information and relationship layer. In each case, the platform did not
build a better version of the existing value chain -- it eliminated
most of its links and inserted itself at the critical interface between
producer and consumer.

More recent research has extended value chain analysis into global
contexts. The global value chain (GVC) framework, developed by
researchers including Gary Gereffi, has mapped how production is
fragmented across countries, with different activities concentrated in
different locations based on factor costs, capabilities, and
institutional environments. The GVC literature demonstrates that
upgrading -- moving from low-value to high-value activities within a
chain -- is the primary pathway for economic development at the
national level, mirroring the firm-level insight that value chain
position determines economic returns.

The author's assessment is that value chain analysis has proven
remarkably durable as a strategic framework because it captures a
fundamental economic reality: the activities that create value and the
activities that capture profits are not the same thing. Understanding
this distinction, and the structural conditions that determine where
profits accrue in any given chain, is the foundation of strategic
analysis across industries.

## Implications

For investors, value chain analysis is a diagnostic tool of the first
order. Before evaluating a company's management quality, competitive
position, or valuation, the first question should be: where does this
company sit in its industry's value chain, and what structural profit
potential does that position offer? A brilliant management team at a
structurally disadvantaged link can produce good results in good times,
but over a full cycle, industry structure dominates individual
execution. Correspondingly, an average management team in a structurally
privileged position can generate attractive returns simply by not
destroying the advantage inherent in their position.

The framework directly informs vertical integration decisions. When
evaluating whether a company should acquire upstream or downstream,
investors should ask: does the target activity have strong linkages
with the acquirer's existing activities? Does it involve proprietary
knowledge or brand value that would be diluted by arms-length
transactions? Conversely, when a company announces an outsourcing
initiative, the question is whether the outsourced activity is truly
commoditized or whether it contains hidden sources of differentiation
that competitors who retain it in-house will exploit.

Profit pool mapping is an exercise in identifying where structural
returns concentrate and where they are likely to migrate. Investors
who map profit pools before they invest understand which companies
are positioned to capture value as industries evolve and which are
exposed to commoditization or disintermediation. The smartphone value
chain example is instructive: an investor who mapped profit pools in
2007 would have seen that the design-and-brand link was structurally
more attractive than the manufacturing link, even before Apple's
dominance was obvious. The structural insight preceded the outcome.

For company strategy, value chain analysis reveals where to invest
for differentiation and where cost reduction is the appropriate goal.
Not every activity can be a source of differentiation. The strategic
art lies in identifying the specific activities that drive buyer value
and focusing investment and management attention there, while running
the remaining activities at competitive parity. A company that tries
to be best-in-class at everything typically ends up best-in-class at
nothing, with a cost structure that cannot be sustained.

The framework also has implications for competitive analysis. By
mapping a competitor's value chain against one's own, a strategist
can identify precisely where the competitor has cost or differentiation
advantages and where it has vulnerabilities. These activity-level
comparisons are far more actionable than aggregate financial analysis:
instead of knowing that a competitor has a 5% cost advantage, the
firm learns that the advantage originates in a specific logistics
configuration or procurement relationship that can be targeted for
improvement.

For policy makers, value chain analysis illuminates the structural
forces that determine which economic activities remain in a country
and which migrate elsewhere. Nations that want to retain high-value
activities -- design, R&D, brand management -- must maintain the
ecosystem of support activities (education, infrastructure, legal
protections) that make those activities viable. Countries that focus
entirely on attracting low-value assembly operations without developing
the surrounding ecosystem find themselves trapped in activities that
provide employment but generate minimal economic surplus, vulnerable
to the next round of offshoring to even lower-cost locations.
