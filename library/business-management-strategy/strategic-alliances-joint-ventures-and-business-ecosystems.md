# Strategic Alliances, Joint Ventures, and Business Ecosystems -- Governing Interdependence Without Ownership Is a Design and Capability Problem

A firm that needs a capability it does not own can build it, buy the company that owns it, contract for it, or partner with the owner. Alliances, joint ventures, and ecosystems occupy the ground between buying and contracting: control, information, and returns are shared across legal boundaries. The research reviewed here indicates that results depend less on the label of the form than on three design questions: whether the structure matches the hazards of the exchange [5], whether the partners agree on their roles and positions [2], and whether the firm has built the organizational capability to manage partnerships [4].

## Background

### From make-or-buy to a continuum of partnership forms

Classical strategy analysis asked whether a firm should make a component, buy it on the market, or acquire its supplier. The study of alliances begins from the observation that many arrangements fit none of these answers. Robinson and Stuart define strategic alliances and joint ventures as "long-term collaborations between legally distinct organizations" and note that, although once relatively rare, they are now a common mechanism for organizing corporate activity, especially in high-technology and research-intensive settings [8]. Kale, Dyer and Singh open their study by reporting that more than 20,000 alliances had been reported in the preceding two years, citing earlier work, and that alliances had become one of the most important organizational forms [4].

Oxley's study of technology alliances gives the most useful taxonomy for the firm-level decision. It places alliance forms on a market-hierarchy continuum with three ordered categories: unilateral contractual agreements (for example unilateral licensing, long-term supply contracts, and research contracts), bilateral contractual agreements (technology sharing, cross-licensing, and joint research agreements), and equity-based alliances, which include equity joint ventures [5]. Equity joint ventures sit nearest the hierarchy end, in part because shared equity works as a hostage exchange: each partner effectively posts a bond equal to the value of the specific investments made by the venture [5]. The taxonomy matters because the same strategic purpose, such as access to a partner's technology, can be pursued at any point on the continuum, and each point carries different control, incentive, and leakage consequences.

### Four research traditions that explain partnership design

Four lineages, each answering a different question, converge in the current understanding.

The first is transaction cost economics, which asks which governance structure protects investments that are specific to a relationship. Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer summarize its core logic: unique complementarity arising from asset specificity makes integration attractive, and the governance choice must protect against the attendant behavioral risks [1]. Oxley extended this logic to alliances by treating the "appropriability problem" in technology contracts as a three-stage process of specifying intellectual property rights, monitoring, and enforcement [5].

The second is the knowledge and learning tradition. Mowery, Oxley and Silverman summarize Kogut's 1988 argument that equity joint ventures are more effective vehicles for transferring tacit knowledge between partners, and they pair it with Cohen and Levinthal's argument that a firm can exploit outside knowledge only if it has built the ability to absorb it [6]. This author did not read Kogut's original paper in full; the attribution rests on the secondary description in [6].

The third is the control-rights and bargaining tradition. Lerner and Merges study who holds the right to make decisions in a technology alliance and find that allocation reflects both the risk of ex post opportunism and the bargaining power of the parties [7]. Robinson and Stuart add a network lineage: the stock of past alliances forms a communication network that works as a governance mechanism, because reputations travel through it [8].

The fourth is the capability tradition. Kale, Dyer and Singh report that earlier research identified prior alliance experience as perhaps the most important factor in alliance success, on the assumption that learning effects build a relational capability, and then test whether a deliberate organizational investment adds explanatory power beyond experience [4].

### The ecosystem extension

The ecosystem concept has been used in strategy since at least Moore (1993) and Iansiti and Levien (2004); the latter stressed the "hub" or "keystone" firm as the provider of stability to a community of interdependent firms [1]. Interest has since grown quickly. Jacobides and colleagues report that in 2014 the word "ecosystem" appeared no fewer than 160 times in Alibaba's initial public offering prospectus, and that its frequency in the titles and abstracts of top strategy journals had increased sevenfold over five years [1]. They distinguish three streams: a business-ecosystem stream centered on a firm and its environment, an innovation-ecosystem stream built around a focal innovation and the actors that support it, and a platform-ecosystem stream built around a platform sponsor and its complementors [1].

Adner then proposed a definition that moves the unit of analysis from the firm or the tie to the value proposition: the ecosystem is "the alignment structure of the multilateral set of partners that need to interact in order for a focal value proposition to materialize" [2]. The practical consequence is that a firm can need partners it has no contract with, because the partners' activities determine whether its own offering reaches a customer.

### Scope of this topic

This topic treats partnership as a firm-level design problem: why a firm cooperates, how it chooses and governs a form, and how it builds the capability to do so repeatedly. It does not cover acquisitions (see the mergers topic in See Also), the structure and profit pools of whole industries, the legal doctrine that governs competitor collaboration and joint-venture formation, or the analysis of any single partnership as a case. Company disclosures are used below only to illustrate design features, and they report announced terms, not outcomes.

## Core Concepts

### Governance form follows the hazards of the exchange

Oxley's central proposition is that the choice among contractual and equity forms depends on the attributes of the transaction. Her model splits the appropriability problem into three stages: specifying the intellectual property rights being exchanged, monitoring the partner's use of them, and enforcing the agreement [5]. Where a technology is hard to specify, or the scope of activities is so wide that monitoring is hampered, more hierarchical alliance forms are chosen [5]. Tacit know-how illustrates the trap. It lowers the risk of leakage to unrelated parties, yet it raises the hazards of contracting for its transfer, because a tacit asset is hard to describe in a contract [5].

The pattern appears in other evidence. In Robinson and Stuart's biotechnology data, equity is more likely in research alliances and less likely in manufacturing deals, which occur later in development and involve fewer uncertainties, so that more comprehensive contracts can be written [8]. The logic is the same: equity substitutes for contractual completeness where contracts cannot be completed ex ante.

### Control rights follow bargaining power as well as efficiency

An alliance contract allocates many decision rights. In the Repligen-Eli Lilly negotiation described by Lerner and Merges, for example, three areas of control were in dispute: management of clinical trials, marketing strategy, and process development and manufacturing [7]. Lerner and Merges counted such rights in 200 biotechnology contracts and found that the financial position of the smaller research firm predicts how many it retains: financially healthier research firms keep more control rights [7]. Robinson and Stuart make a parallel observation: the availability of many external financing options strengthens a target's bargaining position, which may affect the allocation of control and the level of pledged funding [8]. The implication, labeled here as interpretation, is that control terms recorded in a contract partly reflect the relative outside options of the parties on the signing date, and they may become misaligned when those options change.

### Learning is a possibility, not an automatic benefit

Firms often enter alliances to acquire a partner's knowledge. Mowery and colleagues measured knowledge transfer through changes in how often one partner's patents cited the other's, before and after alliance formation. Their results show that transfer is conditional. Across all alliances they found no consistently positive pattern of interfirm learning, and a firm's prior overlap with the partner, a proxy for absorptive capacity, mattered for how much it absorbed [6]. Governance mattered too: among alliances in which partner capabilities converged, equity joint ventures supported more transfer than contract-based alliances [6]. Hoffmann, Lavie, Reuer and Shipilov add that, when a firm manages competition and cooperation with the same partner inside one organizational unit ("contextual integration"), the approach is likely to be cognitively and administratively taxing and requires "careful delineation" of the knowledge the firm is willing to share [10]. Learning and leakage are the same flow viewed from different sides.

### Network position and reputation as substitute governance

Robinson and Stuart argue that the network of past alliances governs future ones. Their finding is that as centrality (how deeply a firm is embedded in the network) and proximity (how close two firms are in it) increase, equity participation diminishes while pledged funding increases [8]. Pairs that had allied before, and pairs with shared third-party partners, were significantly less likely to use equity [8]. The mechanism is reputational: partners embedded in a network can be sanctioned, implicitly, through information that circulates to other potential partners [8]. A firm's history of alliance behavior is therefore an asset that changes the cost of structuring later deals.

### Alliance capability

Kale, Dyer and Singh define a dedicated alliance function as a separate organizational unit charged with capturing and disseminating alliance-management know-how, and describe four ways it can raise a firm's alliance capability: it acts as a focal point for learning from prior and ongoing alliances, keeps stakeholders including investors informed, improves internal coordination and resource support, and monitors and evaluates alliance performance [4]. The practices they document are concrete. Hewlett-Packard had developed 60 tools and templates in a 300-page manual, Lotus had written 35 rules of thumb covering formation through termination, and Eli Lilly conducted an annual health check of each key alliance by surveying both its own and the partner's alliance managers, which in some cases led it to replace an alliance leader [4].

### Ecosystems: alignment, not only contracts

Adner's definition has four parts: an alignment structure, a multilateral set, a set of partners, and a focal value proposition [2]. Members hold defined positions with activity flows between them, and alignment is the extent to which they agree on those positions and flows; a successful ecosystem is one in which all actors are satisfied with their positions [2]. He distinguishes two activity-based alignment risks. Co-innovation risk is the challenge partners face in developing the ability to carry out new activities; adoption chain risk concerns partners' willingness, given their priorities and incentives, to undertake required activities [2]. The ecosystem leader is the firm to whose vision of structure and roles others defer; it sets and often enforces governance rules, and its role depends on willing followership [2].

Adner and Kapoor showed why the position of a partner matters. They distinguish components, which are upstream inputs that the focal firm bundles, from complements, which are inputs that the customer bundles. Challenges in components are barriers to production and challenges in complements are barriers to adoption, and the two affect a technology leader in opposite ways [3]. A firm cannot treat "dependence on partners" as one risk.

### Alliances, supply chains, and ecosystems are different structures

Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer define an ecosystem as a set of actors with varying degrees of multilateral, non-generic complementarities that are not fully hierarchically controlled [1]. Three features separate it from neighboring forms. First, modularity is a precondition: it allows distinct but interdependent organizations to coordinate without hierarchical fiat, although modularity alone more often produces markets [1]. Second, members retain residual control over their own assets, prices, and quantities, which distinguishes an ecosystem from a supply chain in which the hub fully determines what is supplied and at what cost. On that test Toyota, which decides what it procures, from whom, and at what cost, is not the keystone of an ecosystem, whereas Apple with its App Store is, since Apple sets participation rules but not what developers produce or at what price [1]. Third, the relationships are standardized by role. They describe an ecosystem's arrangements as standardized alliances set for each role, so that a developer does not negotiate a bespoke agreement with the platform [1]. Ecosystems "overlap only partly" with alliances, and some ecosystem participation requires no formal alliance, only adherence to specifications [1].

Adner draws a complementary boundary. Networks and alliance research focus on patterns of ties; the ecosystem is delineated by the value proposition, not by actor ties, so the same two partners can belong to two different ecosystems if they cooperate on two different initiatives [2]. In supply and value chains the positions of supplier and buyer are not contested, whereas ecosystem strategy arises because positions and roles need to be aligned [2].

### Recruiting and aligning members trade off

Jacobides and colleagues note a recurrent conundrum for ecosystem design. The less fungible a member's investment, the more it wants the common enterprise to succeed and the easier it is to align current members; yet the lower the fungibility, the harder it is to recruit new participants, who fear being locked in [1]. They also observe that most ecosystem members are complementors with very limited power, noting that in July 2014 there were 2.3 million individuals working as app developers [1].

### Competition inside cooperation

Partners are frequently also rivals. Hoffmann and colleagues describe the "interplay" of competition and cooperation and give the example of Google and Samsung, who cooperate on the Android platform while competing in handsets [10]. They list organizational separation of the competing and cooperating activities as the first approach to managing the tension, and note that every separation approach only transforms the tension, still requiring some integration across boundaries [10]. For the designer of a partnership the practical content is that the governance model must specify where the cooperating activity ends and the competing activity begins.

## Design Features Visible in Four Recent Partnership Disclosures

This section uses primary company documents to illustrate how the concepts above appear in recent deal terms. It reports announced terms and dates. It does not evaluate whether any partnership succeeded, and the author did not review later developments beyond the documents cited.

### Staged, milestone-contingent commitment

In June 2024 Volkswagen Group announced an initial USD 1 billion investment in Rivian through an unsecured convertible note, with up to USD 4 billion of additional investment, for an expected total of USD 5 billion, and said it intended to form an equally controlled and owned joint venture while each company continued to operate its vehicle business separately; the ad hoc release added that a final decision on establishing the venture had not yet been made [13]. In November 2024 the announced deal size was raised to up to USD 5.8 billion. Volkswagen would invest about USD 1.3 billion at closing for background intellectual-property licenses and a 50 percent stake, and the remaining investment of up to USD 3.5 billion would come as equity, a convertible note, and debt tied to clear operational, technical, and financial milestones [13]. Rivian's shareholder letter specified conditions: a USD 1 billion equity investment at a 33 percent premium was conditioned on a Rivian gross-profit milestone, with five years to achieve it, and a USD 750 million tranche was conditioned on the joint venture testing its technology in winter testing, with a March 2028 deadline [13]. The governance terms included a co-chief-executive structure, a four-member board with two designees from each partner, and funding of 75 percent of shared platform costs by Volkswagen and 25 percent by Rivian through 2028 [13]. On 7 October 2026 Rivian reported the funding of the committed USD 1.0 billion, ten-year term loan from Volkswagen Group, which is non-recourse to Rivian, carries a 6.03 percent fixed rate, and is secured by Rivian's 50 percent interest in the joint venture [14]. Interpretation: the structure converts a single large commitment into a sequence of options that release capital only after observable progress, which addresses the monitoring stage of Oxley's appropriability problem [5].

### Cross-shareholding with restricted voting

The February 2023 Renault-Nissan-Mitsubishi announcement rebalanced the Renault-Nissan relationship. The partners would retain 15 percent cross-shareholdings with lock-up and standstill obligations; Renault would transfer 28.4 percent of its Nissan shares into a French trust, where they are voted neutrally subject to limited exceptions; voting rights of both would be capped at 15 percent of exercisable voting rights; each retained the right to nominate two representatives to the other's board; and the new agreement was to run for an initial 15 years [15]. By November 2023 Renault reported that it had transferred 28.4 percent, out of the 43.4 percent it held, into the trust [15]. In September 2023 Renault Group described the Alliance's organization moving from a standardized global model toward project-driven cooperation, with the purchasing organization evolving into distinct organizations that work project by project [15]. Interpretation: the announced design separates economic ownership from voting power and moves operational cooperation to the project level.

### Terms renegotiated as the partners' positions changed

On 28 October 2025 Microsoft described a new agreement with OpenAI under which Microsoft held an investment valued at approximately USD 135 billion, roughly 27 percent on an as-converted diluted basis; its intellectual-property rights for models and products were extended through 2032; any declaration of artificial general intelligence by OpenAI would be verified by an independent expert panel; OpenAI contracted to purchase an incremental USD 250 billion of Azure services; and Microsoft would no longer hold a right of first refusal to be OpenAI's compute provider [11]. On 27 April 2026 the partners announced an amended agreement: Microsoft's license became non-exclusive, OpenAI could serve all its products on any cloud provider while Microsoft remained its primary cloud partner with products shipping first on Azure unless Microsoft cannot and chooses not to support the necessary capabilities, Microsoft stopped paying a revenue share to OpenAI, and OpenAI's revenue-share payments to Microsoft would continue through 2030 independent of technology progress at the same percentage, subject to a total cap [12]. A comparison of [11] and [12] shows that an end point tied to a verified technology milestone was replaced by a fixed date and a cap. Interpretation: the sequence illustrates that contract terms reflect the partners' bargaining positions on the signing date and may be renegotiated when those positions change.

### An integration proposal that stopped at the control question

On 23 December 2024 Honda and Nissan signed a memorandum of understanding to consider business integration through a joint holding company, premised on Nissan's turnaround actions being executed, with Honda planning to nominate a majority of the directors [16]. On 13 February 2025 they terminated it. The joint release states that Honda proposed changing the structure to one in which Honda would be the parent and Nissan its subsidiary through a share exchange, and that both companies concluded that, to prioritize speed of decision-making and execution in a volatile market, ending the discussions was most appropriate; the partners said they would continue under their existing strategic partnership [16]. The documents do not describe the negotiation beyond these statements.

## Evidence

### Dedicated alliance functions and alliance success

Kale, Dyer and Singh (2002) combined exploratory fieldwork with a survey of firms with more than USD 500 million in 1997 sales in computers, telecommunications, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, electronics, and services. Key informants were identified at 292 firms; 78 firms (a 25 percent response) reported on 1,572 alliances formed in 1993-97 and established at least two years earlier; about 12 percent of those alliances had been terminated by 1998 [4]. Success was measured two ways: abnormal stock-market returns around announcements and managerial assessments of long-term success collected from respondents different from those who reported on the alliance function [4]. Firms with a dedicated alliance function earned average abnormal gains of 1.35 percent against 0.18 percent for firms without one, and reported that 63 percent of alliances were successful against 50 percent [4]. Of firms in the top quartile of abnormal gains, 76 percent had an alliance function, against 41 percent in the bottom quartile [4]. About 64 percent of sample firms had a function, averaging roughly five people [4]. The authors found that more alliance experience raised the hazard of establishing a function by about 18 percent per unit, and that after accounting for self-selection the direct effect of experience on success diminished; they state that the causal direction between experience and the function is difficult to disentangle with their data [4].

### Governance choice and the attributes of the transaction

Oxley (1997, read as an October 1996 working paper) estimated an ordered probit of alliance form on 201 horizontal technology-transfer alliances between public US-based manufacturing firms formed in 1970-89, drawn from a database of more than 9,000 cooperative agreements, with a 632-alliance robustness sample [5]. In the primary sample 42 percent were unilateral contractual, 33 percent bilateral contractual, and 25 percent equity-based [5]. More hierarchical forms were chosen where intellectual property was hard to specify (design and mixed transactions) or the scope of activities hampered monitoring [5]. The main model classified 151 of 201 alliances (75 percent) correctly, and attributes of the transaction, not firm-level characteristics, determined the form [5]. The author notes thin data on the specific technologies transferred and that enforcement could not be tested in a single-country sample [5].

### Alliance structure and interfirm knowledge transfer

Mowery, Oxley and Silverman (1996) analyzed 792 bilateral alliances with at least one US partner formed in 1985-86 (132 equity joint ventures, 226 unilateral contracts, 434 bilateral contracts) against a control sample of 858 non-allied firm pairs, using patent portfolios of 838 firms covering more than 275,000 patents [6]. Alliances produced significant changes in cross-citation relative to non-allied pairs, but there was no consistently positive pattern of learning across the whole sample. Within the 191 alliances showing convergence, equity joint ventures showed higher transfer than contract alliances, and among 147 non-equity convergent alliances unilateral contracts showed less transfer than bilateral ones [6]. The authors acknowledge that patents capture codified knowledge only [6].

### Control rights in biotechnology alliances

Lerner and Merges (NBER working paper, 1997; published 1998) combined case studies with a random sample of 200 biotechnology-pharmaceutical contracts from 1980-1995, counting the control rights assigned to the funding party [7]. In every regression the financial condition of the smaller firm was significantly negative at the 5 percent level, meaning healthier research firms retained more control; a one-standard-deviation increase in shareholders' equity reduced the predicted number of rights held by the larger firm by 10.4 percent [7]. Contrary to the opportunism prediction, early-stage agreements allocated more, not fewer, control rights to the funder; the authors offer one interpretation, that the danger of ex post opportunism may increase rather than decrease as a project matures [7].

### Network position and alliance governance

Robinson and Stuart (2007) analyzed all 3,854 biotechnology-pharmaceutical alliances formed in 1976-1998 from the Recombinant Capital database, with network measures built on a five-year window and a matched sample of non-alliance pairs to correct for selection [8]. Equity and pledged funding responded to network position: higher centrality and proximity predicted less equity and more pledged funding [8]. Among equity deals with public targets the mean stake was 19.9 percent, and a one-standard-deviation increase in target centrality raised pledged funding by about USD 2.5 million, roughly one third of the USD 7.7 million mean [8]. The authors caution that firms may choose governance in order to affect their future network positioning [8].

### Position in the ecosystem changes who benefits

Adner and Kapoor (2010, read as a December 2008 working paper) studied the global semiconductor lithography equipment industry from 1962 to 2005, using 33 firms, 64 firm-technology generations, and 676 firm-generation-year observations across nine technology generations [3]. Challenges were counted from 181 articles in an industry journal narrowed to 56 articles describing 78 ecosystem challenges, and the estimation used random-effects panel regression of market share [3]. Greater component challenges raised the advantage of technology leaders, whereas greater complement challenges reduced it; with high complement challenges the leader was at a slight disadvantage to later followers; and the advantage of vertical integration rose over the technology life cycle [3]. The authors state that the study covers one industry [3].

### Governing with complementors

Kapoor and Lee (2013, read as an August 2010 working paper) used a Cox hazard model on American Hospital Association survey data for 5,367 hospitals from 1995 to 2006 to explain the timing of investment in two imaging technologies [9]. Hospitals with an alliance with physicians were 32 percent (PET) and 33 percent (MRI) more likely to invest than hospitals using arm's-length relations, hospitals with broad-scope alliances were 45 percent and 65 percent more likely, and hospitals using tapered integration with managed-care organizations were 15 percent and 25 percent more likely, while full integration with complementors had an insignificant coefficient [9]. The authors state that investment propensity is not performance and make no claim that alliances are a superior form of governance [9].

## Implications

### For managers designing or restructuring a partnership

The evidence supports a sequence of questions, which this topic offers as a synthesis rather than a published checklist. First, identify what must be specified, monitored, and enforced: if the exchanged asset is tacit or the scope of joint activity is wide, the evidence on governance choice points toward more hierarchical forms, such as equity-based alliances, because contracts are hard to write ex ante [5][8]. Second, match the form to the purpose. A firm that wants to absorb a partner's capabilities should note that equity joint ventures supported more transfer than contract alliances where capabilities converged, that learning depends on the firm's own absorptive capacity, and that no broad pattern of learning appeared across all alliances [6]. Third, expect bargaining position to shape the control allocation, and compare terms against realistic alternatives for both sides [7][8]. Fourth, stage commitments where outcomes can be observed, as the milestone-tranche structures in the Volkswagen-Rivian disclosures do [13]; this is an interpretation that links Oxley's monitoring stage to a contractual device. Fifth, state exit and change provisions at the outset: the Microsoft-OpenAI sequence shows that central terms can change within six months of a major agreement [11][12], and the Honda-Nissan termination shows that a prospective integration can be abandoned over structure and speed [16].

When a partnership depends on several parties, as ecosystems do, the design question changes from "which contract" to "who must do what for the customer to receive value". Adner's framework implies mapping the activities, actors, positions, and links required for the value proposition, and identifying for each partner both the capability gap (co-innovation risk) and the incentive gap (adoption chain risk) [2]. Where those risks sit upstream, the evidence suggests different exposure from where they sit downstream [3]. The firm also needs to decide whether it intends to lead, in which case it must secure willing followers, or to follow, in which case it should assess how much of its investment is fungible if the ecosystem fails [1][2].

### For boards and senior executives

The strongest empirical claim is organizational: firms that established a dedicated alliance function reported higher alliance success and earned higher announcement returns, and the function's activities included codified guidelines, internal training, performance metrics, and health checks [4]. Board-level questions follow. Does the firm know how many alliances it has, who owns each, and how performance is measured? Is learning from earlier alliances captured in a form that survives personnel turnover? The authors caution that the causal direction between alliance experience and the function cannot be settled with their data, so the existence of a unit is evidence of association, not proof that creating one improves results [4]. Interpretation: boards should also treat large partnership announcements as statements of intended terms. The Volkswagen-Rivian announced size rose from up to USD 5 billion to up to USD 5.8 billion within five months, and several tranches were conditional on milestones not yet met when announced [13]. The amount announced is not the amount paid, and the October 2026 filing documents only the funding of one loan facility [14].

Boards of a firm that is the smaller or dependent partner have a distinct task. Interpretation: the financial-condition evidence suggests that negotiating after a financing event, when outside options are strong, preserves more control than negotiating from weakness [7][8]. Jacobides and colleagues observe that most ecosystem members are complementors with limited power, and report that video-game developers such as Electronic Arts have used multi-homing, participation in several competing platforms, to increase their ability to capture value relative to any single platform [1].

### For people evaluating the quality of a business

The anchor of this domain includes methods for evaluating business quality, and partnership dependence belongs among them. The following uses are interpretation. An owner or analyst can ask which essential capabilities rest on partners rather than on assets the firm controls; whether the terms are fixed or contingent on events; how voting rights compare with economic interests, as in the capped-voting Renault-Nissan structure [15]; and what happens to the business if a partner exits or renegotiates. Kale and colleagues report that most earlier studies found roughly half of alliances end in failure [4]; their own sample shows long-term success rates of 50 percent for firms without a dedicated alliance function and 63 percent for firms with one [4]. Interpretation: these figures are a rough prior for how often a partnership dependence will deliver what was intended, and the measures differ between studies. How an unconsolidated joint venture appears in reported leverage is an accounting question treated elsewhere in the library.

### For partners and complementors in an ecosystem

A complementor's investments are often specific to one ecosystem, and Jacobides and colleagues describe how rules for membership can change over time, citing Facebook, and contrast strict participation rules and exclusivity clauses at Nintendo with the more laissez-faire policies of Sony and Microsoft [1]. Interpretation: because the recruiting-versus-aligning trade-off pushes sponsors to look open while recruiting, a complementor should read the current rules as provisional. A complementor's first diagnostic is how much of its investment could be redeployed in another ecosystem and how the platform has treated members in the past. A second is whether its own business sits at a position where its challenges raise or erode the sponsor's advantage, because that determines the sponsor's incentive to support it [3].

### For readers who use the ecosystem vocabulary

The two definitions discussed here are not interchangeable. Jacobides and colleagues require non-generic complementarities and residual control by members, and they exclude generic complements such as electricity or boiling water for tea [1]. Adner's definition is anchored on a value proposition and treats an ecosystem as latent where alignment is not currently at issue [2]. Interpretation: calling any supplier network an ecosystem adds little analytically; a useful test is whether the partners' positions and roles need to be agreed for the offering to reach the customer.

## Common Pitfalls

- Treating the legal label as the governance. A "joint venture" can be equally owned and independently run, as announced for Volkswagen and Rivian, or can sit inside a larger relationship with its own funding conditions [13]. Interpretation: the relevant variables are who decides, who funds, who can leave, and on what terms [5][7].
- Assuming that an alliance transfers knowledge. Across 792 alliances there was no consistent positive pattern of learning, and absorptive capacity mattered [6].
- Treating prior experience as sufficient. In the sample of Kale and colleagues, experience predicted establishment of the alliance function, and its direct effect on success diminished once self-selection was accounted for [4].
- Treating all dependence on partners as one risk. Component challenges and complement challenges had opposite effects on technology leaders [3].
- Reading an announcement as an outcome. Announcements report terms and intentions; the documents reviewed here do not report results [11][12][13][15][16].

## Criticisms and Limits of the Evidence

Most quantitative evidence comes from a few settings. The governance and control-rights studies draw on biotechnology-pharmaceutical contracts [7][8] or on technology-transfer alliances in manufacturing [5], and the ecosystem studies on semiconductor lithography equipment [3] and US hospital technology adoption [9]. Adner and Kapoor and Kapoor and Lee each state that they cover one industry [3][9]. The alliance databases rely on press reports and filings, and Oxley and Mowery and colleagues acknowledge incomplete coverage and biases in geography and industry [5][6].

Several designs limit causal claims. Kale and colleagues cannot separate whether experience produces an alliance function or the reverse [4]; Robinson and Stuart caution that firms may choose governance to affect their future network positions [8]; Kapoor and Lee measure investment propensity, not performance [9]. Mowery and colleagues measure only codified knowledge through patents [6]. Lerner and Merges found a project-stage result contrary to the opportunism prediction [7], and in Oxley's study the repeat-alliance variable was not significant and the geographic-scope coefficient had the opposite sign to the one hypothesized [5].

Four of the academic papers (Oxley, Adner and Kapoor, Lerner and Merges, Kapoor and Lee) were read as working-paper versions, and the Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer and the Hoffmann and colleagues papers as accepted manuscripts; wording and coefficients in the published versions may differ. Hoffmann and colleagues is a special-issue introduction, so its descriptions of other articles are secondary [10]. The ecosystem concept itself is contested: Jacobides and colleagues adopt a narrow definition and acknowledge that an inclusive one may suit managers [1], while Adner's definition is broader [2]. The company documents report announced terms, were issued by interested parties, and do not establish outcomes. Dyer and Singh's relational view, Gulati's network studies of alliances, Kogut's joint-venture theory, and Hamel's work on learning races were not obtained in full text, so this topic does not cite from their contents; Kogut's argument appears only through the secondary description in [6].
