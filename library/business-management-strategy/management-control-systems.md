# Management Control Systems Translate Strategy Into Coordinated Action Only When Measures, Authority, and Feedback Fit

Management control systems translate an organization's strategy into objectives, decision rights, plans, measures, review conversations, rewards, and corrective action [3][4][5]. They do not control people mechanically: they shape which facts become visible, which deviations receive attention, who may respond, and whether the organization learns or merely enforces a target [1][2][12]. The author's synthesis is that a control system creates value only when its parts fit the strategy, operating uncertainty, delegated authority, and behaviors that the system itself induces.

## Background

Management control developed from accounting practices that compared plans with results, but its subject is broader than accounting compliance. Budgets, standard costs, variance reports, and responsibility accounts gave managers a repeatable way to assign expectations and identify departures. Later research widened the field from financial monitoring to the whole process by which objectives are selected, strategies and plans are formed, targets are set, incentives are attached, information returns, and action is revised [3][4]. This wider view distinguishes management control from bookkeeping: accounting records provide evidence, while control concerns how an organization uses evidence to coordinate behavior and pursue its purposes.

David Otley's 1999 framework organized performance management around five connected questions: objectives, strategies and plans, target setting, rewards, and information feedback [3]. Ferreira and Otley later expanded the framework to twelve questions so researchers and managers could examine vision, key success factors, organization structure, strategies and plans, performance measures, targets, evaluation, rewards, information flows, system use, change, and the coherence of the whole arrangement [4]. Their central warning was methodological as well as practical: isolated study of a budget, incentive plan, or dashboard can miss interactions that determine how the complete system works [4].

Robert Simons added a strategic distinction between four levers of control: belief systems, boundary systems, diagnostic control systems, and interactive control systems [1][2]. Belief systems communicate purpose and values; boundary systems state prohibited domains or minimum rules; diagnostic systems compare actual performance with critical targets; interactive systems bring senior attention and repeated discussion to strategic uncertainty [1][2]. His longitudinal study of ten newly appointed top managers over approximately eighteen months found that formal controls were used not only to monitor results but also to overcome inertia, communicate new agendas, impose timetables, and sustain attention to emerging initiatives [1].

The balanced scorecard broadened measurement beyond lagging financial outcomes. Kaplan and Norton began with a multi-company project on organizations whose intangible assets mattered to value creation, then developed a system that connected financial results with customer, internal-process, and learning or capability measures [6]. Kaplan later described its evolution from a measurement device into an interactive system for communicating and implementing strategy [6]. The development also exposed a difficulty: a list of many indicators is not a strategy. Measures become strategically useful only when managers specify the objectives, causal assumptions, responsibilities, and decisions that connect them [6][7].

Research consequently moved toward treating management controls as a package rather than as independent tools. Malmi and Brown grouped controls into planning, cybernetic, reward and compensation, administrative, and cultural controls [5]. The package perspective matters because controls are often introduced by different actors at different times and can work at cross-purposes [5]. A decentralized unit may be told to innovate, evaluated against a rigid annual profit target, denied authority over the resources needed to act, and embedded in a culture that punishes visible failure. Each control may appear reasonable alone, yet the combined message is to protect the target and avoid experimentation.

Budgeting remained central despite repeated predictions of its abandonment. Libby and Lindsay's two surveys of mid-sized and large North American organizations found that most respondents continued to use budgets for control and generally viewed them as adding value, while adapting their use rather than discarding it [9]. The surveys also documented gaming and the use of rolling forecasts, strategic linkage, bottom-up information, and less detailed or more frequently updated plans as responses to budgeting problems [9]. Henttu-Aho's later multiple-case study showed that rolling forecasts can support either proactive discussion and early action or reactive variance analysis under the continuing supremacy of the annual budget [11]. The instrument does not determine the behavior; design and use do.

The scope of management control is therefore neither surveillance nor accounting compliance. Surveillance collects observations about people; compliance tests adherence to external or internal rules. Management control includes boundaries and monitoring, but it also allocates attention, clarifies responsibility, coordinates interdependent units, updates plans, and creates forums in which assumptions can be challenged [1][3][4]. Nor is it identical to strategy formulation. Strategy selects a direction and a theory of advantage; management control makes that theory operational, tests its assumptions against results, and creates the means to correct execution or revise the strategy.

This domain boundary is important. Financial-statement mechanics and capital structure belong in finance, while detailed accounting manipulation belongs in accounting-financial-shenanigans. Individual motivation without organizational application belongs in psychology-behavior. Management control belongs here because it concerns the internal operating system through which one organization converts strategic intent into coordinated decisions, resource use, and learning, exactly within the business-management-strategy anchor's scope.

## Core Concepts

### A control system is a closed loop with an open question

The simplest control loop has six parts: choose an objective, define a plan, assign authority, observe performance, compare the observation with an expectation, and respond. Feedback closes the loop because evidence from execution changes later action [3][4]. A variance is not itself a failure. It is a difference requiring diagnosis: the plan may have been executed poorly, the measure may be wrong, the target may have been unrealistic, an external condition may have changed, or the strategy's causal assumption may be false [3][4].

The open question is what the loop should preserve. A thermostat preserves temperature because the desired state is known and the system can correct mechanically. Organizations face ambiguous goals, interacting measures, local knowledge, and changing conditions. Their control systems must therefore support both correction toward a known target and inquiry into whether the target remains useful [1][12]. Diagnostic use emphasizes the first function; interactive use emphasizes the second. Both are necessary, but the required balance depends on strategic uncertainty and the cost of error.

A useful distinction is among feedforward, concurrent, and feedback control. Feedforward control acts before commitment through planning, scenario analysis, approval limits, capability checks, or risk boundaries. Concurrent control acts while work proceeds through operating dashboards, quality checks, exception alerts, and direct coordination. Feedback control reviews outcomes after a period through variance analysis, post-investment review, customer results, or financial performance. The author's synthesis is that robust systems distribute control across all three timescales so prevention, timely adjustment, and learning do not depend on one late financial report [3][4][5].

### Beliefs and boundaries define the field of action

Belief systems describe purpose, values, and strategic commitments that managers want members to pursue [1][2]. Their function is generative: they help people use judgment when no detailed procedure covers the situation. Boundary systems are constraining: codes of conduct, risk limits, approval thresholds, prohibited markets, safety requirements, and other non-negotiable limits state where experimentation must stop [1][2]. Beliefs without boundaries can license activity that is energetic but strategically or ethically destructive. Boundaries without beliefs can produce defensive compliance in which people avoid violations but have little guidance about what to create.

These levers are not slogans and manuals in isolation. Employees infer operational beliefs from resource allocations, promotions, tolerated conduct, and the issues that leaders repeatedly discuss. A company may declare customer trust as a belief while rewarding sales regardless of cancellations or complaints. The formal message and the experienced system then conflict. The author's assessment is that belief and boundary systems pass a behavioral test only when important decisions, incentives, and exceptions are consistent with them [1][5].

### Diagnostic control manages critical performance variables

Diagnostic controls compare actual results with preset standards and direct attention toward important exceptions [1][2]. Budgets, production standards, service levels, project milestones, risk limits, and scorecards can all be used diagnostically. The value is leverage of managerial attention: stable processes need not be discussed continuously when reliable measures and escalation rules identify material departures. Diagnostic systems are especially useful where objectives are clear, cause and effect are sufficiently understood, and timely corrective action is available [1][3].

A sound diagnostic measure needs a definition, data owner, calculation rule, frequency, target, tolerance band, accountable decision owner, and prescribed review response. Without these elements, a dashboard displays numbers but does not constitute a control. Targets also need a reference class or operational basis. A number set only by negotiation can become a political settlement rather than an estimate of feasible performance. The author's synthesis is that a target should state whether it is a commitment, aspiration, forecast, external benchmark, or safety limit, because using one number for all five roles invites distortion [9][11][19].

Variance analysis should decompose rather than merely rank. A sales variance may arise from price, volume, mix, timing, currency, market size, customer retention, or data classification. A cost variance may reflect input prices, quantity, productivity, capacity utilization, quality failures, or allocation changes. Controllable and uncontrollable causes should be distinguished, but "uncontrollable" should not become a blanket exemption: a manager may not control an exchange rate yet may control exposure, hedging, contract design, or response timing. The control question is what decision was available, not whether every causal variable was owned.

### Interactive control directs attention toward uncertainty

An interactive system is a formal information system that senior managers use personally and regularly to involve subordinates in recurring discussion of strategic uncertainty [1][2]. The same budget, project report, customer metric, or forecast can be diagnostic in one organization and interactive in another. The distinction lies in use: exception reporting against a stable target is diagnostic, while repeated debate about assumptions, threats, opportunities, and possible strategic responses is interactive [1][12].

Interactive use is costly because it consumes senior attention and meeting time. It should therefore be concentrated on uncertainties that could invalidate the strategy rather than spread across every metric [1][2]. A firm facing a new distribution technology might use adoption, unit economics, customer behavior, and partner capacity interactively while leaving routine payroll or established quality metrics diagnostic. The author's synthesis is that the best interactive control is not the largest dashboard; it is the smallest evidence set capable of changing an important strategic decision.

### Targets, forecasts, and resource authorizations are different objects

A target expresses desired performance. A forecast estimates what is likely under stated assumptions. A resource authorization permits spending or commitment. An annual budget often combines all three, which creates predictable conflict [9][11][19]. If compensation depends on beating the same number that managers are asked to forecast honestly, they have reason to understate capability or defer bad news. If a forecast revision automatically changes the target, the organization may erase accountability whenever conditions worsen. If a spending authorization is treated as an entitlement, resources can remain attached to activities after the strategic case has weakened.

Separating the objects improves information. The target can remain an ambition or contract; the rolling forecast can update the expected path; and resource decisions can be revised as evidence changes [9][11]. The separation need not abolish budgets. Libby and Lindsay found that organizations generally adapted budgeting, while Henttu-Aho found rolling forecasts operating in different relationships with annual budgets across three industrial firms [9][11]. The design question is which process supplies ambition, prediction, coordination, authorization, and evaluation, and whether those functions reinforce or contaminate one another.

A rolling forecast maintains a forward-looking horizon by adding a period as another expires. Its value is not greater frequency by itself. Henttu-Aho found a proactive pattern in which realistic information and cross-functional discussion prompted action before quantified deviations emerged, and a reactive pattern in which forecasting reinforced annual-budget variance analysis [11]. Frequent updates can become bureaucratic re-budgeting if they reproduce detailed target negotiations without improving decisions. The author's assessment is that update frequency should follow decision latency and environmental volatility, while forecast detail should stop where additional precision does not alter action.

### Responsibility centers link authority to accountability

Responsibility accounting divides an organization into units whose managers are accountable for defined aspects of performance. Common forms are cost centers, discretionary cost centers, revenue centers, profit centers, and investment centers [16][17]. The classification should match decision authority. A cost-center manager can be held accountable for controllable input use; a profit-center manager needs influence over both revenue and cost; an investment-center manager also needs authority over assets or capital employed [16][17].

The controllability principle does not require ignoring shared outcomes. It requires avoiding evaluation as if a manager controlled a variable when the relevant authority sits elsewhere. Where outcomes are jointly produced, the system can combine unit measures, shared measures, and explicit interface responsibilities. Allocated corporate cost should be separated from avoidable local cost when evaluating operating decisions. Otherwise managers spend effort contesting allocations rather than improving operations, a failure mechanism also described in Jensen's analysis of budget gaming [19].

Responsibility centers make decentralization measurable, but measurement can narrow local optimization. A sales unit rewarded only for revenue may accept low-quality customers; a plant rewarded only for unit cost may produce excess inventory; a service center rewarded only for utilization may delay urgent work. The solution is not to eliminate accountability but to define the unit's contribution to the whole system, including quality, timing, risk, customer outcomes, and capital where relevant [4][16].

### Transfer prices coordinate exchanges among decentralized units

Transfer prices assign an internal value to goods, services, capacity, or knowledge transferred between responsibility centers [16][17]. They affect reported unit revenue and cost, influence make-or-buy and sourcing decisions, and shape whether managers cooperate with other units. Market-based prices can preserve external opportunity costs where a competitive market exists; cost-based prices can support coordination where no reliable market exists; negotiated prices can preserve autonomy but consume time and bargaining power [17].

No method eliminates trade-offs. A market price may include selling or distribution costs not incurred internally. Full cost can reward inefficiency or obscure incremental economics. Marginal cost can leave the supplying unit unable to recover capacity commitments. Negotiation can produce a politically powerful winner rather than an enterprise-optimal decision. The author's synthesis is that the transfer-pricing rule should state its primary purpose -- decision support, performance evaluation, capacity coordination, or tax compliance -- because one number rarely serves all purposes without adjustment [16][17].

### Balanced scorecards should encode a strategy, not collect metrics

The balanced scorecard combines financial outcomes with customer, internal-process, and learning or capability measures so managers can observe both results and proposed drivers [6]. A strategy map makes the causal theory explicit: capabilities are expected to improve processes, processes are expected to improve customer outcomes, and customer outcomes are expected to improve financial results. These links are hypotheses to test, not laws established by placing arrows on a diagram [6][7].

Ittner, Larcker, and Randall found in financial-services firms that broader use of financial and especially nonfinancial measures was associated with higher measurement-system satisfaction and stock returns, but balanced-scorecard use, economic-value measures, and causal business modeling showed almost no association with economic performance [7]. Their evidence warns against treating adoption of a branded framework as proof of effectiveness. The system must contain measures that fit the strategy, credible causal relationships, and review practices that change decisions.

Evaluation introduces another hazard. Lipe and Salterio's experiment gave participants scorecards with measures common across two divisions and measures unique to each division's strategy. Evaluators relied on common measures and ignored unique ones [15]. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer found in one firm's scorecard bonus plan that subjective weighting allowed superiors to emphasize financial measures, change criteria, ignore predictive measures, and generate complaints about favoritism; the system was eventually abandoned [8]. Comparability and discretion can therefore erase the strategic specificity a scorecard was designed to preserve.

### Incentives turn measures into behavior

A measure used for learning can behave differently when attached to pay or promotion. Jensen argued that kinked bonus schedules around budget targets create incentives to sandbag during target setting, accelerate or defer transactions, manipulate accounting, and shift performance across periods [19]. Libby and Lindsay found gaming in practice but also found widespread use of subjectivity and allowances for uncontrollable events, indicating that organizations do not all apply fixed performance contracts mechanically [9]. Van der Stede found that tighter budgetary control was associated with less slack but that reducing slack could spill into stronger short-term orientation [10].

The lesson is not that incentives or targets are inherently corrupting. It is that measured performance is endogenous to the rule attached to it. A control designer should ask how a rational, self-protective, mission-driven, or politically skilled participant could improve the measured result without improving the organization. Linear incentives, longer horizons, multiple measures, independent verification, post-period adjustment for defined exogenous events, and qualitative judgment can reduce some distortions, but each introduces costs and new discretion [8][10][19].

### Coherence and use matter more than instrument count

Malmi and Brown's package perspective implies that adding another control can reduce rather than increase control if it contradicts existing planning, rewards, administration, or culture [5]. Ferreira and Otley's framework similarly asks how information flows, system use, and system change fit the other elements [4]. Controls should therefore be designed as an architecture. A new measure needs a decision, an owner, a review forum, an incentive consequence or explicit absence of one, and a retirement condition.

Enabling controls help employees understand and improve the process; coercive controls primarily enforce compliance from above. Ahrens and Chapman's two-year restaurant-chain field study showed how management pursued efficiency and flexibility simultaneously through controls designed for repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility [14]. Wouters and Wilderom's three-year mixed-method study in a beverage company's logistics department found that experience-based, experimental development and transparent measures helped employees perceive the system as useful rather than merely supervisory [13]. The design process is part of the control because participation generates local knowledge, tests definitions, and changes whether users trust the evidence.

## Evidence

### Strategic renewal: controls can implement and revise strategy

Simons followed ten newly appointed top managers for approximately eighteen months, examining their strategies and their use of formal controls [1]. The longitudinal design captured control use during change rather than relying on one survey snapshot. Managers used formal systems to communicate beliefs, define boundaries, measure critical variables, focus debate on uncertainty, overcome inertia, impose implementation timetables, and keep new initiatives visible [1]. The study supports a dual conclusion: control can constrain execution toward a plan and can stimulate discussion that changes the plan.

Abernethy and Brownell tested this distinction with chief executives of 63 public hospitals undergoing strategic change [12]. Their model examined strategic change, budget-use style, and performance. Results were consistent with the proposition that interactive use of budgets mitigated the disruptive performance effects of strategic redirection [12]. The setting and observational design limit universal causal claims, but the study supplies evidence that the same budget can function differently when used as a learning forum rather than only as a variance contract.

### Enabling formalization: local participation can improve control quality

Ahrens and Chapman conducted a detailed two-year field study of a restaurant chain [14]. They distinguished coercive controls, which emphasize top-down centralization and preplanning, from enabling controls, which help employees respond to contingencies. Their case showed management pursuing efficiency and flexibility together through repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility in the control design [14]. The evidence rejects the simple claim that formalization necessarily destroys local judgment.

Wouters and Wilderom used action research, interviews, meeting participation, company documents, and survey data over three years in the logistics department of a medium-sized beverage manufacturer [13]. They found that building measures from existing skills and local practices, then testing and refining definitions, data, and presentation, made the performance system more enabling. Professionalism was positively associated with attitudes toward measures, and transparency of the system itself was important [13]. The case suggests that measure quality and acceptance can be produced through disciplined iteration rather than imposed as a finished dashboard.

### Budgets: persistence, adaptation, and behavioral trade-offs

Libby and Lindsay surveyed mid-sized and large organizations in Canada and the United States to test claims that budgeting was fundamentally broken [9]. Most respondents continued to use budgets for control and perceived them as adding value, although gaming was prevalent and practices were being adapted. Reported adaptations included rolling forecasts, bottom-up information, closer linkage to strategy, less initial detail, and regular updating [9]. Because the design was descriptive and cross-sectional, it cannot prove that budgeting caused performance; it does show that practice is more varied than either universal defense or universal rejection of budgets.

Van der Stede studied business units in large diversified firms headquartered in Belgium using questionnaire data [10]. Tighter budgetary control was significantly associated with less budgetary slack, but slack and managerial short-term orientation were negatively related. The study's interpretation was that suppressing one dysfunctional behavior through rigid control can shift the problem into another behavior [10]. Self-reports about gaming are a limitation, but the evidence directly challenges one-dimensional control design: a system can improve one indicator while worsening the horizon of decisions.

Jensen's analysis explains the incentive mechanism behind some gaming [19]. When rewards have thresholds, caps, and discontinuities around a negotiated target, managers can benefit from understating capability before the period and shifting revenue, expense, or effort around the target during execution. His proposed response was to remove kinks from pay-for-performance rather than abolish planning [19]. The paper is analytical and case-based rather than a broad causal test, so its claimed productivity magnitudes should not be treated as established estimates. Its mechanism remains a useful pre-mortem for any target-linked reward design.

### Scorecards: broader measurement helps only when judgment preserves strategy

Kaplan's history of the balanced scorecard traces it to a multi-company performance-measurement project and its later evolution into a strategy-management system [6]. The framework's intended contribution is to combine lagging financial outcomes with proposed operational drivers and make strategy communicable. Ittner, Larcker, and Randall tested strategic performance measurement in financial-services firms [7]. Broad use of financial and nonfinancial measures was associated with satisfaction and stock returns, yet adoption of the balanced-scorecard process and causal business modeling had almost no association with economic performance [7]. The result separates measurement breadth from branded implementation and cautions against causal claims from adoption alone.

Lipe and Salterio experimentally examined evaluations of two divisions with different strategies and scorecards containing both common and unique measures [15]. Participants' judgments were affected by common measures, while strategically unique measures had no effect [15]. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer's field evidence from a scorecard-based bonus plan showed another route to distortion: subjective weighting let superiors reduce the intended balance, shift criteria over time, and use measures unrelated to desired outcomes [8]. Together, the studies show that a scorecard can be technically balanced yet behaviorally unbalanced when cognitive simplification or unstructured discretion determines evaluation.

### Rolling forecasts: frequency does not guarantee anticipation

Henttu-Aho used a multiple-case design across three industrial firms that had recently tried to improve planning [11]. The study identified proactive and reactive patterns. In proactive planning, rolling forecasts emphasized realistic information and interactive discussion, and managers sometimes acted before monetary variance appeared. In reactive planning, rolling forecasting reinforced annual budgeting and supported formal diagnostic variance analysis and plan revision [11]. The findings show that rolling forecasts can complement budgets, but their contribution depends on how target setting, forecasting, discussion, and action are connected.

The evidence base does not establish one universal management-control design. Simons studied leadership transitions, Abernethy and Brownell studied hospitals, Wouters and Wilderom studied one logistics department, Ahrens and Chapman studied one restaurant chain, and Henttu-Aho studied three industrial firms [1][11][12][13][14]. Survey and experimental studies add breadth but simplify context or behavior [7][9][10][15]. The defensible conclusion is contingent: controls are more useful when their purpose is explicit, authority matches accountability, measures fit the strategy, feedback changes decisions, and the package anticipates the behavior it will induce.

## Implications

### For senior managers: design backward from decisions

The first design question should be which recurring decisions determine whether the strategy succeeds. Measures, reports, and meetings should then be built backward from those decisions. For each decision, management should state the objective, relevant uncertainty, accountable owner, delegated authority, evidence required, review frequency, threshold for escalation, and available corrective actions [3][4]. This reverses the common practice of collecting available data first and asking later what the dashboard means.

A practical architecture can be built in nine steps. First, state the strategic thesis and the few conditions that must be true. Second, identify prohibited actions and non-negotiable risk boundaries. Third, assign decision rights to the level holding the relevant information. Fourth, classify units as responsibility centers consistent with that authority. Fifth, separate targets, forecasts, and spending authorization. Sixth, select a small set of outcome and driver measures with named data owners. Seventh, define diagnostic tolerances and interactive uncertainties. Eighth, connect review findings to specific corrective or learning actions. Ninth, audit the package for contradictory incentives, duplicated controls, missing interfaces, and obsolete measures [1][4][5][16].

Management should record the purpose of every material measure. A measure may support prediction, coordination, evaluation, compensation, compliance, or learning. Combining purposes is possible, but the conflict must be visible. A sales forecast used to schedule production needs honesty; a sales target used to motivate stretch needs ambition; a bonus threshold changes incentives around both. If one number serves all three, the system should be presumed vulnerable until evidence shows otherwise [9][11][19].

Review cadence should follow the speed of the underlying decision. Daily quality or liquidity signals may require rapid diagnostic action; monthly operating reviews may examine process and customer drivers; quarterly strategy reviews may use interactive control to challenge assumptions; annual allocation reviews may redirect capacity and capital. The author's synthesis is that higher frequency is not inherently better. Frequency adds value only when new information arrives at that speed and someone has authority to act on it.

### For decentralized organizations: preserve local knowledge without losing enterprise coherence

Decentralization moves decisions closer to local information but creates coordination and agency costs. Responsibility centers make this trade-off explicit by connecting a manager's accountability to revenue, cost, profit, or investment authority [16][17]. The center design should be tested against actual discretion. A unit labeled a profit center but unable to influence pricing, sourcing, capacity, or product mix carries nominal accountability without real control. A unit with broad authority but only a local profit measure may rationally damage shared capabilities or customers.

Shared measures should be used where outcomes depend on interfaces. Examples include forecast accuracy between sales and operations, on-time delivery between production and logistics, customer retention across acquisition and service, or cash conversion across procurement, operations, and collections. Shared measures should not replace clear ownership; they should identify a joint output and specify each party's contribution. The author's assessment is that interface failures deserve control objects of their own because organizational charts assign units while value flows across them.

Transfer pricing should be governed as a behavioral and decision system, not merely an accounting calculation [16][17]. Managers should know whether an internal price is intended to reflect opportunity cost, recover committed capacity, evaluate a unit, encourage internal trade, or meet tax rules. Where those purposes conflict, parallel views may be needed: one price for operating decisions and a reconciliation for evaluation. Appeals and exception rules should be explicit so disagreement produces evidence rather than political bargaining.

### For performance reviews and incentives: protect truth before demanding stretch

A control system cannot correct what participants have reason to conceal. Forecasts should therefore be judged first for decision usefulness and calibration, not for whether they equal an aspirational target. Target-linked compensation should be tested for thresholds, caps, cliff effects, and period-shifting opportunities [19]. Managers should examine whether an employee can improve pay by delaying maintenance, accelerating revenue, rejecting a useful long-horizon investment, hoarding information, or redefining a measure.

Subjective judgment remains necessary where measures are incomplete, but discretion needs structure. Reviewers can document the criteria used, distinguish controllable decisions from exogenous outcomes, compare judgments across similar cases, disclose changes from prior criteria, and provide an appeal or calibration process. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer's scorecard case shows that unconstrained subjectivity can recreate financial-measure dominance and perceived favoritism [8]. Formulaic evaluation can create gaming; opaque judgment can create distrust. The design problem is disciplined judgment, not a choice between pure formula and pure discretion.

Scorecards should retain strategic uniqueness. Comparability is useful for portfolio oversight, but the Lipe and Salterio experiment shows that evaluators may overweight common measures and ignore unit-specific measures [15]. A review template can require an explicit discussion of the unique strategic objectives before overall ranking. Strategy maps should be treated as causal hypotheses: if the proposed driver changes without the expected customer or financial result, management should revise the measure, the intervention, or the strategy rather than preserve the diagram [6][7].

### For boards and owners: oversee the control architecture, not only reported results

Boards should ask how management knows whether strategy is working before the income statement delivers a verdict. That requires visibility into critical performance variables, strategic uncertainties, major boundaries, forecast changes, incentive design, and the history of corrective action [1][3][4]. A board dashboard should not reproduce the entire operating system. It should show the small set of evidence that can change a capital, risk, leadership, or strategic decision.

The board should also inspect control failures as system failures. Missing a target may reflect execution, but repeated favorable forecasts followed by misses may reflect incentive contamination. Strong local results alongside weak enterprise outcomes may reflect responsibility-center or transfer-pricing design. A proliferation of measures with unchanged decisions may reflect dashboard theater. The author's synthesis is that a post-control review should ask whether the objective, measure, target, data, authority, review process, incentive, or response rule failed -- not merely who missed the number.

Incentive oversight should include the full behavioral package. Executive pay may reward revenue growth while capital approvals punish uncertainty, culture discourages dissent, and forecasts are expected to meet external guidance. The nominal compensation metric then understates the actual control system. Malmi and Brown's package perspective and the empirical evidence on gaming support reviewing formal rewards together with planning, administrative controls, and culture [5][9][10][19].

### For employees and operational managers: controls should make work intelligible

Employees need enough transparency to understand how a measure is produced, how their actions affect it, how it connects to the wider process, and how to repair an exception. Wouters and Wilderom found that experience-based development, experimentation, professionalism, and transparency helped create an enabling performance system [13]. Ahrens and Chapman similarly identified repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility as characteristics of enabling control [14]. These findings support involving users in definition and testing without giving every user veto power over accountability.

Operational participation should focus on knowledge. Front-line employees often know which data are delayed, which classifications create noise, where a target conflicts with quality, and which exception requires local judgment. Management retains responsibility for strategic priorities and boundaries, but ignoring local knowledge can produce technically neat measures that are operationally false. The author's assessment is that a measure should not be attached to evaluation until users can reproduce its logic, identify its known limitations, and describe the action it is meant to inform.

### For investors and analysts: infer control quality from repeated operating evidence

External investors rarely observe internal control systems directly, but they can infer aspects from consistency between stated strategy, segment measures, capital allocation, incentive plans, forecast behavior, and later outcomes. Repeated changes in non-GAAP definitions, guidance that is narrowly met, acquisition targets that disappear after closing, or bonuses earned despite poor owner outcomes can indicate a system optimized for measured performance rather than enterprise value. These are signals requiring investigation, not proof of misconduct.

Good control quality appears in candid variance explanations, stable definitions, decision-relevant segment disclosure, post-investment reviews, willingness to revise strategy when assumptions fail, and incentives tied to outcomes managers can influence over appropriate horizons. The balanced-scorecard and budgeting evidence cautions against equating framework adoption with quality [7][9]. The investor's question is not whether the company uses a named system but whether information, authority, feedback, and rewards consistently produce rational action.

### Failure modes and a minimum viable control system

The worst failure is a system that suppresses truth while appearing precise. It can arise when a target is also a forecast and bonus threshold, when a local manager is accountable for uncontrollable allocations, when common metrics crowd out strategy-specific evidence, or when negative variances trigger blame before diagnosis [8][10][15][19]. Prevention requires separation of purposes, controllability, transparent definitions, independent data checks, and review forums where disconfirming evidence can change the plan.

Other failures are overcontrol and undercontrol. Overcontrol produces delay, metric gaming, short horizons, and learned helplessness; undercontrol produces local drift, hidden risk, inconsistent decisions, and weak coordination. Enabling control is the middle path: clear boundaries and outcome accountability combined with enough transparency, repair capability, and delegated judgment to respond to contingencies [13][14]. The proper balance is contingent on uncertainty, reversibility, skill, trust, and consequence, not on a universal preference for centralization or freedom [18].

A minimum viable control system has seven components: a clear objective; a named decision owner; defined authority and boundary conditions; one or more verified measures; an explicit target or forecast purpose; a review cadence; and a response rule. New elements should be added only when a demonstrated failure requires them. Controls should also have sunset or redesign triggers because strategies, data, and organizational boundaries change. The final test is behavioral: if every person acted rationally under the system's rules, would the resulting local actions still serve the organization's strategy? If not, the control design -- not merely the participant -- requires correction.
