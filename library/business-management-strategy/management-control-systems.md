# Management Control Systems Translate Strategy Into Coordinated Action Only When Measures, Authority, and Feedback Fit

Management control systems translate an organization's strategy into objectives, decision rights, plans, measures, review conversations, rewards, and corrective action [3][4][5]. They do not control people mechanically: they shape which facts become visible, which deviations receive attention, who may respond, and whether the organization learns or merely enforces a target [1][2][12]. The author's synthesis is that a control system creates value only when its parts fit the strategy, operating uncertainty, delegated authority, and behaviors that the system itself induces.

## Background

Management control developed from accounting practices that compared plans with results, but its subject is broader than accounting compliance. Budgets, standard costs, variance reports, and responsibility accounts gave managers a repeatable way to assign expectations and identify departures. Later research widened the field from financial monitoring to the whole process by which objectives are selected, strategies and plans are formed, targets are set, incentives are attached, information returns, and action is revised [3][4]. This wider view distinguishes management control from bookkeeping: accounting records provide evidence, while control concerns how an organization uses evidence to coordinate behavior and pursue its purposes.

David Otley's 1999 framework organized performance management around five connected questions: objectives, strategies and plans, target setting, rewards, and information feedback [3]. It was an inductively developed conceptual framework illustrated against budgeting, economic value added, and the balanced scorecard, not an organizational sample or causal test [3]. Ferreira and Otley later expanded it to twelve questions so researchers and managers could examine vision, key success factors, organization structure, strategies and plans, performance measures, targets, evaluation, rewards, information flows, system use, change, and the coherence of the whole arrangement [4]. Their framework is a descriptive heuristic developed from literature and experience; two brief top-management cases illustrated its use but did not validate an ideal design [4]. Its central warning remains that isolated study of a budget, incentive plan, or dashboard can miss interactions that determine how the complete system works [4].

Robert Simons added a strategic distinction between four levers of control: belief systems, boundary systems, diagnostic control systems, and interactive control systems [1][2]. Belief systems communicate purpose and values; boundary systems state prohibited domains or minimum rules; diagnostic systems compare actual performance with critical targets; interactive systems bring senior attention and repeated discussion to strategic uncertainty [1][2]. His longitudinal study of ten newly appointed top managers over approximately eighteen months found that formal controls were used not only to monitor results but also to overcome inertia, communicate new agendas, impose timetables, and sustain attention to emerging initiatives [1].

The balanced scorecard broadened measurement beyond lagging financial outcomes. Kaplan and Norton began with a multi-company project on organizations whose intangible assets mattered to value creation, then developed a system that connected financial results with customer, internal-process, and learning-and-growth measures [6]. Kaplan later described its evolution from a measurement device into an interactive system for communicating and implementing strategy [6]. The development also exposed a difficulty: a list of many indicators is not a strategy. The author's synthesis is that measures become strategically useful when managers specify the objectives, causal assumptions, responsibilities, and decisions that connect them; a strategy map states hypotheses to test rather than causal laws [6][7].

Research consequently moved toward treating management controls as a package rather than as independent tools. Malmi and Brown's editorial and conceptual synthesis grouped controls into planning, cybernetic, reward and compensation, administrative, and cultural controls [5]. The package perspective matters because controls are often introduced by different actors at different times and can work at cross-purposes [5]. A package is a configuration of controls; it becomes a system only when evidence establishes complementary or substitutive interdependencies among its parts [23]. A decentralized unit may be told to innovate, evaluated against a rigid annual profit target, denied authority over the resources needed to act, and embedded in a culture that punishes visible failure. Each control may appear reasonable alone, yet the combined message is to protect the target and avoid experimentation.

Budgeting remained central in the surveyed settings despite repeated predictions of its abandonment. Libby and Lindsay received 558 responses from mid-sized and large North American organizations drawn from 2003-2004 professional-membership directories; most respondents continued to use budgets for control and viewed them as adding value, but low response rates, especially 1.5 percent in the United States, limit generalization [9]. They documented gaming. Among respondents who planned changes, proposed responses included rolling forecasts, more bottom-up information, closer strategic linkage, and less detailed or more frequently updated plans; these were prospective changes, not established practices across the samples [9]. Henttu-Aho's later cases showed that rolling forecasts can support proactive discussion and early action or reinforce reactive variance analysis under the continuing supremacy of the annual budget [11]. A 2021 comparison of 80 Beyond Budgeting adopters with 121 non-adopters found more decentralization, flexible resource allocation, relative targets, and weaker individual incentives among adopters, but many adopters still struggled to reduce reliance on fixed annual budgets and financial performance measures [21]. Control type, design, use, interaction with other controls, and context jointly shape behavior; no one factor determines it [21][25].

The scope of management control is therefore neither surveillance nor accounting compliance. Surveillance collects observations about people; compliance tests adherence to external or internal rules. Management control includes boundaries and monitoring, but it also allocates attention, clarifies responsibility, coordinates interdependent units, updates plans, and creates forums in which assumptions can be challenged [1][3][4]. Nor is it identical to strategy formulation. Strategy selects a direction and a theory of advantage; management control makes that theory operational, tests its assumptions against results, and creates the means to correct execution or revise the strategy.

This domain boundary is important. Financial-statement mechanics and capital structure belong in finance, while detailed accounting manipulation belongs in accounting-financial-shenanigans. Individual motivation without organizational application belongs in psychology-behavior. Management control belongs here because it concerns the internal operating system through which one organization converts strategic intent into coordinated decisions, resource use, and learning, exactly within the business-management-strategy anchor's scope.

## Core Concepts

### A control system is a closed loop with an open question

The author's synthesis of a basic control loop has six parts: choose an objective, define a plan, assign authority, observe performance, compare the observation with an expectation, and respond. Feedback closes the loop because evidence from execution changes later action [3][4]. A variance is not itself a failure. It is a difference requiring diagnosis: the plan may have been executed poorly, the measure may be wrong, the target may have been unrealistic, an external condition may have changed, or the strategy's causal assumption may be false. This diagnostic list is also the author's synthesis from the framework questions in [3] and [4].

The open question is what the loop should preserve. A thermostat preserves temperature because the desired state is known and the system can correct mechanically. Organizations face ambiguous goals, interacting measures, local knowledge, and changing conditions. Their control systems must therefore support both correction toward a known target and inquiry into whether the target remains useful [1][12]. Diagnostic use emphasizes the first function; interactive use emphasizes the second. Both are necessary. The author's assessment is that their balance should reflect strategic uncertainty and the consequence of error; the cited studies directly establish the strategic-uncertainty rationale, not a universal cost-of-error rule [1][2][12].

The author's synthesis distinguishes feedforward, concurrent, and feedback control. Feedforward control acts before commitment through planning, scenario analysis, approval limits, capability checks, or risk boundaries. Concurrent control acts while work proceeds through operating dashboards, quality checks, exception alerts, and direct coordination. Feedback control reviews outcomes after a period through variance analysis, post-investment review, customer results, or financial performance. Otley and Ferreira and Otley directly discuss feedback and feedforward information; the three-timescale taxonomy and examples are an integrative model rather than a classification tested in those papers [3][4]. Robust systems distribute control across all three timescales so prevention, timely adjustment, and learning do not depend on one late financial report.

### Beliefs and boundaries define the field of action

Belief systems describe purpose, values, and strategic commitments that managers want members to pursue [1][2]. Their function is generative: they help people use judgment when no detailed procedure covers the situation. Boundary systems are constraining: codes of conduct, risk limits, approval thresholds, prohibited markets, safety requirements, and other non-negotiable limits state where experimentation must stop [1][2]. Beliefs without boundaries can license activity that is energetic but strategically or ethically destructive. Boundaries without beliefs can produce defensive compliance in which people avoid violations but have little guidance about what to create.

These levers are not slogans and manuals in isolation. Employees infer operational beliefs from resource allocations, promotions, tolerated conduct, and the issues that leaders repeatedly discuss. A company may declare customer trust as a belief while rewarding sales regardless of cancellations or complaints. The formal message and the experienced system then conflict. The author's assessment is that belief and boundary systems pass a behavioral test only when important decisions, incentives, and exceptions are consistent with them [1][5].

### Diagnostic control manages critical performance variables

Diagnostic controls compare actual results with preset standards and direct attention toward important exceptions [1][2]. Budgets, production standards, service levels, project milestones, risk limits, and scorecards can all be used diagnostically. The value is leverage of managerial attention: stable processes need not be discussed continuously when reliable measures and escalation rules identify material departures. Diagnostic systems are especially useful where objectives are clear, cause and effect are sufficiently understood, and timely corrective action is available [1][3].

The author's design test is that a diagnostic measure needs a definition, data owner, calculation rule, frequency, target, tolerance band, accountable decision owner, and prescribed review response. Without these elements, a dashboard displays numbers but does not constitute a control. Targets also need a reference class or operational basis. A number set only by negotiation can become a political settlement rather than an estimate of feasible performance. The author's synthesis is that a target should state whether it is a commitment, aspiration, forecast, external benchmark, or safety limit, because using one number for all five roles invites distortion [9][11][19].

The author's diagnostic model decomposes rather than merely ranks variance. A sales variance may arise from price, volume, mix, timing, currency, market size, customer retention, or data classification. A cost variance may reflect input prices, quantity, productivity, capacity utilization, quality failures, or allocation changes. Controllable and uncontrollable causes should be distinguished, but "uncontrollable" should not become a blanket exemption: a manager may not control an exchange rate yet may control exposure, hedging, contract design, or response timing. The control question is what decision was available, not whether every causal variable was owned.

### Interactive control directs attention toward uncertainty

An interactive system is a formal information system that senior managers use personally and regularly to involve subordinates in recurring discussion of strategic uncertainty [1][2]. The same budget, project report, customer metric, or forecast can be diagnostic in one organization and interactive in another. The distinction lies in use: exception reporting against a stable target is diagnostic, while repeated debate about assumptions, threats, opportunities, and possible strategic responses is interactive [1][12].

Interactive use is costly because it consumes senior attention and meeting time. It should therefore be concentrated on uncertainties that could invalidate the strategy rather than spread across every metric [1][2]. A firm facing a new distribution technology might use adoption, unit economics, customer behavior, and partner capacity interactively while leaving routine payroll or established quality metrics diagnostic. The author's synthesis is that the best interactive control is not the largest dashboard; it is the smallest evidence set capable of changing an important strategic decision.

### Targets, forecasts, and resource authorizations are different objects

The author's synthesis separates three objects that annual budgets often combine. A target expresses desired performance. A forecast estimates what is likely under stated assumptions. A resource authorization permits spending or commitment [9][11][19]. If compensation depends on beating the same number that managers are asked to forecast honestly, they have reason to understate capability or defer bad news. If a forecast revision automatically changes the target, the organization may erase accountability whenever conditions worsen. If a spending authorization is treated as an entitlement, resources can remain attached to activities after the strategic case has weakened.

Separating these objects is a design recommendation, not a causal finding from the cited studies. The target can remain an ambition or contract; the rolling forecast can update the expected path; and resource decisions can be revised as evidence changes [11][19]. The separation need not abolish budgets. Libby and Lindsay found that organizations generally retained budgeting, while Henttu-Aho found rolling forecasts operating in different relationships with annual budgets across three paper-industry firms [9][11]. The 2021 Beyond Budgeting comparison likewise found that many adopters retained fixed annual budgets despite using more flexible practices [21]. The design question is which process supplies ambition, prediction, coordination, authorization, and evaluation, and whether those functions reinforce or contaminate one another.

A rolling forecast maintains a forward-looking horizon by adding a period as another expires. Its value is not greater frequency by itself. Henttu-Aho found a proactive pattern in which realistic information and cross-functional discussion prompted action before quantified deviations emerged, and a reactive pattern in which forecasting reinforced annual-budget variance analysis [11]. Frequent updates can become bureaucratic re-budgeting if they reproduce detailed target negotiations without improving decisions. The author's assessment is that update frequency should follow decision latency and environmental volatility, while forecast detail should stop where additional precision does not alter action.

### Responsibility centers link authority to accountability

Responsibility accounting divides an organization into units whose managers are accountable for defined aspects of performance. Common forms are cost centers, discretionary cost centers, revenue centers, profit centers, and investment centers [16][17]. The classification should match decision authority. A cost-center manager can be held accountable for controllable input use; a profit-center manager needs influence over both revenue and cost; an investment-center manager also needs authority over assets or capital employed [16][17].

Investment centers require a measure that includes both profit and invested capital. Return on investment divides segment income by its investment base, but a manager evaluated on the segment's existing percentage return may reject a positive-value project that would reduce that percentage. Residual income subtracts a capital charge from segment income and therefore accepts a project that earns more than the organization's required return even when its percentage return is below the segment's current ROI [17]. This difference is not merely arithmetic: the measure changes which investments a rational manager will prefer.

The controllability principle does not require ignoring shared outcomes. It requires avoiding evaluation as if a manager controlled a variable when the relevant authority sits elsewhere. The author's synthesis is that jointly produced outcomes can be governed with a combination of unit measures, shared measures, and explicit interface responsibilities. Allocated corporate cost should be separated from avoidable local cost when evaluating operating decisions [17]. Otherwise managers may spend effort contesting allocations rather than improving operations, a mechanism Jensen illustrates in a company case [19].

Responsibility centers make decentralization measurable, but measurement can narrow local optimization. A sales unit rewarded only for revenue may accept low-quality customers; a plant rewarded only for unit cost may produce excess inventory; a service center rewarded only for utilization may delay urgent work. These examples are the author's scenarios, not findings from source [16]. The design response is to define the unit's contribution to the whole system, including quality, timing, risk, customer outcomes, and capital where relevant [4][16][17].

### Transfer prices coordinate exchanges among decentralized units

Transfer prices assign an internal value to goods or services transferred between responsibility centers [16][17]. They affect reported unit revenue and cost, influence make-or-buy and sourcing decisions, and shape whether managers cooperate with other units. Market-based prices use an observable external price; cost-based prices may use variable cost, full cost, or cost plus a markup; negotiated prices preserve managerial input and are especially relevant where no external market supplies a benchmark [17]. Capacity is central: with excess capacity, variable cost can be the relevant economic floor, whereas constrained capacity requires adding the contribution margin lost when an internal transfer displaces an external sale [17].

No method eliminates trade-offs. Full cost can include amounts irrelevant to the decision, a variable-cost transfer gives the supplying unit no segment profit, and negotiation can create interdepartmental disputes [17]. The author's synthesis is that the transfer-pricing rule should state its primary purpose -- decision support, performance evaluation, capacity coordination, or tax compliance -- because one number rarely serves all purposes without adjustment [16][17].

### Balanced scorecards should encode a strategy, not collect metrics

The balanced scorecard combines financial outcomes with customer, internal-process, and learning-and-growth measures so managers can observe both results and proposed drivers [6]. A strategy map makes a causal theory explicit: capabilities are expected to improve processes, processes are expected to improve customer outcomes, and customer outcomes are expected to improve financial results. The author's interpretation is that these links are hypotheses to test, not laws established by placing arrows on a diagram [6][7].

Ittner, Larcker, and Randall surveyed 148 U.S. financial-services companies. Broader use of financial and especially nonfinancial measures, relative to firms with similar strategies or value drivers, was associated with higher measurement-system satisfaction and stock returns [7]. The study found little support for the hypothesis that alignment with strategy or value drivers improved economic performance, while balanced-scorecard use, economic-value measures, and causal business modeling showed almost no association with economic performance [7]. A 2020 meta-analysis of 67 primary studies later found positive mean correlations between strategic performance measurement systems and thirteen behavioral, capability, and performance consequences, but substantial heterogeneity remained; culture, reward links, and industry moderated results [20]. Framework adoption is therefore not proof of effectiveness, and the author's recommendation that measures fit strategy should not be mistaken for Ittner and colleagues' empirical finding.

Evaluation introduces another hazard. Lipe and Salterio's experiment used 58 first-year MBA students with an average of more than five years of work experience to evaluate two apparel divisions [15]. Common-measure performance affected judgments, while the unique-measure manipulation had no statistically significant effect in that task; participants had not developed the scorecards and may have been novices in their use [15]. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer found in one financial-services firm's branch-level scorecard bonus plan that subjective weighting allowed superiors to emphasize financial measures, change criteria, ignore predictive measures, and generate complaints about favoritism; the system was eventually abandoned [8]. The author's interpretation is that comparability and discretion can erase the strategic specificity a scorecard was designed to preserve.

### Incentives turn measures into behavior

A measure used for learning can behave differently when attached to pay or promotion. Jensen argued that kinked bonus schedules around budget targets create incentives to sandbag during target setting, accelerate or defer transactions, manipulate accounting, and shift performance across periods [19]. Libby and Lindsay found gaming in their survey samples but also found widespread use of subjectivity and allowances for uncontrollable events, indicating that organizations did not all apply fixed performance contracts mechanically [9]. Van der Stede's structural-equation analysis of questionnaire responses from 153 business-unit general managers found that tighter budgetary control was associated with less slack and that slack was negatively related to managerial short-term orientation; the direct relationship between control rigidity and short-term orientation was insignificant [10]. Control style was also associated with strategy and prior performance, limiting a simple causal reading [10].

The lesson is not that incentives or targets are inherently corrupting. It is that measured performance is endogenous to the rule attached to it. The author's synthesis is that a control designer should ask how a rational, self-protective, mission-driven, or politically skilled participant could improve the measured result without improving the organization. Linear incentives, longer horizons, multiple measures, independent verification, defined adjustment for exogenous events, and structured qualitative judgment may reduce some distortions, but the cited studies do not test this complete intervention bundle [8][10][19]. Each response introduces costs and new discretion.

### Coherence and use matter more than instrument count

The package perspective implies that inconsistent controls may impair package functionality; it does not establish that every additional control reduces control [5]. Ferreira and Otley's framework similarly asks how information flows, system use, and system change fit the other elements [4]. A package is not automatically a coordinated system: controls may complement, substitute for, duplicate, or undermine one another, and those relationships require evidence [23][30]. The author's design recommendation is that a new measure should have a decision, an owner, a review forum, an explicit incentive consequence or explicit absence of one, and a retirement condition.

Enabling controls help employees understand and improve the process; coercive controls primarily enforce compliance from above [13][14]. Ahrens and Chapman's restaurant-chain field study showed enabling and coercive processes coexisting while management pursued efficiency and flexibility through repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility [14]. Wouters and Wilderom's three-year mixed-method study in a beverage company's logistics department found that experience-based, experimental development and transparency of the performance-measurement system helped employees perceive it as useful rather than merely supervisory [13]. The author's interpretation is that participation can contribute local knowledge and test definitions; neither study establishes that participation necessarily produces trust.

## Evidence

### Strategic renewal: controls can implement and revise strategy

Simons followed ten newly appointed top managers for approximately eighteen months, examining their strategies and their use of formal controls [1]. The longitudinal design captured control use during change rather than relying on one survey snapshot. Managers used formal systems to communicate beliefs, define boundaries, measure critical variables, focus debate on uncertainty, overcome inertia, impose implementation timetables, and keep new initiatives visible [1]. The study supports a dual conclusion: control can constrain execution toward a plan and can stimulate discussion that changes the plan.

Abernethy and Brownell tested this distinction with CEOs at 63 public hospitals, treating the extent of strategic change as a measured variable rather than assuming that every hospital was undergoing the same degree of change [12]. Their model examined strategic change, budget-use style, and performance. Results were consistent with the proposition that interactive use of budgets mitigated the disruptive performance effects of strategic redirection [12]. The setting and observational design limit universal causal claims, but the study supplies evidence that the same budget can function differently when used as a learning forum rather than only as a variance contract.

### Enabling formalization: local participation can improve control quality

Ahrens and Chapman conducted a single-chain field study over a little more than two years in a large U.K. full-service restaurant company [14]. The researchers visited 15 restaurants and reported 45 interviews plus 19 observations or meeting attendances, supported by archival evidence. They distinguished coercive controls, which emphasize top-down centralization and preplanning, from enabling controls, which help employees respond to contingencies. The case showed enabling and coercive processes existing side by side while management pursued efficiency and flexibility through repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility in the control design [14]. The evidence rejects the simple claim that formalization necessarily destroys local judgment.

Wouters and Wilderom used action research, interviews, meeting participation, company documents, and two waves of survey data from a panel of 42 employees between August 2002 and June 2005 in the logistics department of a medium-sized beverage manufacturer [13]. They found that building measures from existing skills and local practices, then testing and refining definitions, data, and presentation, made the performance system more enabling. Professionalism was positively associated with attitudes toward measures, and transparency of the performance-measurement system itself was important [13]. The case suggests that measure quality and acceptance can be produced through disciplined iteration rather than imposed as a finished dashboard.

### Budgets: persistence, adaptation, and behavioral trade-offs

Libby and Lindsay received 558 responses from managers in for-profit organizations with at least 100 employees: 346 from the 2003 CMA Canada directory and 212 from the 2004 IMA U.S. directory [9]. Response rates were 13.6 percent in Canada and 1.5 percent in the United States, and Quebec was excluded. Most respondents continued to use budgets for control and perceived them as adding value, while gaming remained a problem in both country samples [9]. Among respondents planning changes, proposed changes included rolling forecasts, more bottom-up information, closer linkage to strategy, less initial detail, and regular forecast updates [9]. Because the design was descriptive and cross-sectional, its low response rates and membership frames prevent generalization to all North American firms or present-day prevalence. The later Beyond Budgeting comparison shows continued variation rather than universal abandonment: many adopters still relied on fixed annual budgets and financial measures [21].

Van der Stede used a structural equation model on questionnaire data from 153 business-unit general managers in large diversified firms headquartered in Belgium [10]. Greater budget-control rigidity was significantly associated with less slack, and slack was significantly negatively associated with managerial short-term orientation; however, the direct relationship between control rigidity and short-term orientation was insignificant [10]. Differentiation strategy and prior profitability were also associated with control style, so the study does not identify a simple exogenous causal effect. Questionnaire self-reports of slack and time orientation further limit inference. The evidence supports an indirect behavioral trade-off, not a universal claim that tightening budgets directly causes short-termism.

Jensen's analysis explains the incentive mechanism behind some gaming [19]. When rewards have thresholds, caps, and discontinuities around a negotiated target, managers can benefit from understating capability before the period and shifting revenue, expense, or effort around the target during execution. His proposed response was to remove kinks from pay-for-performance rather than abolish planning [19]. The paper is an analytical essay illustrated with company examples and prior evidence, not a formal case study or broad causal test. Jensen explicitly noted the absence of high-quality aggregate cost estimates, so claimed productivity magnitudes should not be treated as established estimates. Its mechanism remains a useful pre-mortem for any target-linked reward design.

### Scorecards: measurement breadth is associated with outcomes, but causality and fit remain unsettled

Kaplan's history of the balanced scorecard traces it to a multi-company performance-measurement project and its later evolution into a strategy-management system [6]. It is an author history and conceptual account, not an independent effectiveness test. Ittner, Larcker, and Randall surveyed 148 U.S. financial-services companies [7]. Broader use of financial and nonfinancial measures than among firms with similar strategies or value drivers was associated with measurement-system satisfaction and stock returns. The study found little support for the proposed economic-performance benefit from strategy alignment, and adoption of balanced-scorecard processes and causal business modeling had almost no association with economic performance [7]. The result distinguishes observed association from a branded implementation or causal claim.

Lipe and Salterio experimentally examined 58 first-year MBA students evaluating two apparel divisions with different strategies and scorecards containing common and unique measures [15]. Common-measure performance affected judgments, while unique-measure performance had no statistically significant effect in the task. The participants averaged more than five years of work experience but had not developed the scorecards and may have been balanced-scorecard novices [15]. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer's branch-level field evidence from one major financial-services firm's scorecard bonus plan showed another route to distortion: subjective weighting let superiors reduce the intended balance, shift criteria over time, and use measures unrelated to desired outcomes [8]. Together, the studies identify possible distortions without establishing their prevalence across organizations.

The later evidence base is broader but still heterogeneous. Endrikat, Guenther, and Titus meta-analyzed associations between strategic performance measurement systems and thirteen consequences across 67 primary studies [20]. Mean correlations were positive and statistically significant for the examined behavioral, capability, and performance outcomes, but substantial heterogeneity remained after correction for statistical artifacts. National culture, linkage to rewards, and industry moderated results [20]. This supports neither categorical rejection nor universal adoption: performance measurement effects depend on how the system is configured and used.

### Rolling forecasts: frequency does not guarantee anticipation

Henttu-Aho used a multiple-case design across three paper-industry firms that had recently tried to improve planning [11]. The study conducted 22 interviews with 24 people between 2009 and 2014. It identified proactive and reactive patterns. In proactive planning, rolling forecasts emphasized realistic information and interactive discussion, and managers sometimes acted before monetary variance appeared. In reactive planning, rolling forecasting reinforced annual budgeting and supported formal diagnostic variance analysis and plan revision [11]. The findings show that rolling forecasts can complement budgets, but their contribution depends on how target setting, forecasting, discussion, and action are connected.

### Current evidence: digitalization changes both capability and control risk

Faehndrich's systematic review searched 166 highly rated accounting journals and analyzed 116 peer-reviewed articles published from January 2000 through August 2022 [22]. The review found that digitalization changes management-control tasks, instruments, organization, and behavioral effects; its sample contained 59 empirical, 44 conceptual, and 13 analytical articles [22]. Digital systems can accelerate reporting, integrate data, automate forecasts, and widen access to evidence, but greater speed and granularity do not establish data quality, causal relevance, or accountable use. The author's synthesis is that digital control requires explicit ownership of data definitions, model changes, exceptions, and human override rather than treating a software output as a decision.

Algorithmic management makes that governance problem concrete. An OECD survey of more than 6,000 firms in France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, and the United States defined algorithmic management as software that fully or partly automates tasks traditionally performed by human managers [26]. Managers reported perceived improvements in decision quality and job satisfaction, but also concerns about unclear accountability, inability to follow a tool's logic, and inadequate protection of worker health [26]. The survey establishes reported use and perceptions, not causal productivity or worker-welfare effects. A control architecture using algorithms therefore needs traceable logic, data and model monitoring, appeal and override paths, and a named human owner for consequential decisions.

### Current evidence: controls create characteristic dysfunctions

Haxhiu and Marc systematically reviewed 95 studies of result, action, personnel, and cultural controls [25]. They found recurring links between control types and gaming, data manipulation, ethical erosion, motivational crowding-out, symbolic compliance, problems from multiple controls, and design-use misalignment. Result controls dominated the literature, accounting for 44 of the 95 reviewed studies [25]. These are recurring patterns across a heterogeneous literature, not deterministic causal laws. The review fills a gap left by a treatment centered only on accounting instruments: selection and development of people, prescribed actions, and cultural mechanisms are alternative objects of control, and each can fail in different ways.

### Current evidence: context includes culture, sustainability, and resilience

Mitter and colleagues systematically reviewed 43 cross-national studies and found that 40 reported an influence of national culture on management control, while effects of budget participation on performance remained inconclusive [24]. Forty-one studies were cross-sectional, and country coverage and cultural classifications were heterogeneous. The supported conclusion is therefore that national culture is a contingency to investigate, not a deterministic stereotype or a license to infer individual behavior from country averages [24]. Multinational control design should test whether participation, formalization, reward emphasis, and social controls carry the same meaning across settings.

Sustainability is also an internal control problem rather than only an external-reporting topic. Quesado, Silva, and Oliveira mapped 157 peer-reviewed articles published from 2009 through 2023 on management control and sustainability [29]. Their bibliometric review found an established research field in which control systems integrate financial and nonfinancial issues, while relatively few organizations address all sustainability dimensions and social issues receive less attention [29]. The practical implication is bounded: environmental and social objectives require definitions, budgets, investment criteria, operating measures, review forums, and accountability if they are to influence decisions, but the bibliometric map does not itself prove which design improves outcomes.

Weber, Pedell, and Roetzel systematically reviewed links between management control and organizational resilience and developed a framework spanning anticipation, coping, and adaptation [27]. They found a fragmented and still developing evidence base rather than a validated universal design. Oelrich and Slapnicar later distinguished amplified core operational risks, for which prevention and detection remain central, from structurally novel risks, for which full prevention may be unattainable and resilience, recovery, and new governance become necessary [28]. The distinction broadens control beyond avoiding deviations: some systems must preserve the capacity to detect, contain, recover, learn, and adapt.

The evidence base does not establish one universal management-control design. Simons studied leadership transitions, Abernethy and Brownell studied hospitals, Wouters and Wilderom studied one logistics department, Ahrens and Chapman studied one restaurant chain, and Henttu-Aho studied three paper-industry firms [1][11][12][13][14]. Survey, experimental, review, and meta-analytic studies add breadth but retain context, measurement, or heterogeneity limits [7][9][10][15][20][22][24][25]. The defensible conclusion is contingent: controls are more useful when their purpose is explicit, authority matches accountability, measures fit the decision, feedback changes action, and the package anticipates the behavior and failure modes it can induce.

## Implications

### For senior managers: design backward from decisions

The author's design recommendation is to begin with the recurring decisions that determine whether the strategy succeeds, rather than with whatever data are already available. For each decision, management can state the objective, relevant uncertainty, accountable owner, delegated authority, evidence required, review frequency, threshold for escalation, and available corrective actions [3][4]. The cited frameworks organize questions and describe control use; they do not empirically validate this exact decision-backward protocol.

One derived architecture has nine steps. First, state the strategic thesis and the few conditions that must be true. Second, identify prohibited actions and non-negotiable risk boundaries. Third, assign decision rights to the level holding the relevant information. Fourth, classify units as responsibility centers consistent with that authority. Fifth, separate targets, forecasts, and spending authorization. Sixth, select a small set of outcome and driver measures with named data owners. Seventh, define diagnostic tolerances and interactive uncertainties. Eighth, connect review findings to specific corrective or learning actions. Ninth, audit the package for contradictory incentives, duplicated controls, missing interfaces, and obsolete measures [1][4][5][23]. This is the author's synthesis of the frameworks, not a tested nine-step intervention.

The author's recommendation is to record the purpose of every material measure. A measure may support prediction, coordination, evaluation, compensation, compliance, or learning. Combining purposes is possible, but the conflict must be visible. A sales forecast used to schedule production needs honesty; a sales target used to motivate stretch needs ambition; a bonus threshold changes incentives around both. If one number serves all three, the system should be presumed vulnerable until evidence shows otherwise [9][11][19].

Review cadence should follow the speed of the underlying decision. Daily quality or liquidity signals may require rapid diagnostic action; monthly operating reviews may examine process and customer drivers; quarterly strategy reviews may use interactive control to challenge assumptions; annual allocation reviews may redirect capacity and capital. The author's synthesis is that higher frequency is not inherently better. Frequency adds value only when new information arrives at that speed and someone has authority to act on it.

### For decentralized organizations: preserve local knowledge without losing enterprise coherence

Decentralization moves decisions closer to local information but creates coordination and agency costs. Responsibility centers make this trade-off explicit by connecting a manager's accountability to revenue, cost, profit, or investment authority [16][17]. The center design should be tested against actual discretion. A unit labeled a profit center but unable to influence pricing, sourcing, capacity, or product mix carries nominal accountability without real control. A unit with broad authority but only a local profit measure may rationally damage shared capabilities or customers.

The author's synthesis is that shared measures are useful where outcomes depend on interfaces. Examples include forecast accuracy between sales and operations, on-time delivery between production and logistics, customer retention across acquisition and service, or cash conversion across procurement, operations, and collections. Shared measures should not replace clear ownership; they should identify a joint output and specify each party's contribution. Interface failures deserve control objects of their own because organizational charts assign units while value flows across them.

Transfer pricing should be governed as a behavioral and decision system, not merely an accounting calculation [16][17]. Managers should know whether an internal price is intended to reflect opportunity cost, recover committed capacity, evaluate a unit, encourage internal trade, or meet tax rules. Capacity must be explicit: excess capacity can make variable cost the relevant floor, while constrained capacity introduces the contribution margin lost on displaced external sales [17]. Where operating and evaluation purposes conflict, the author's recommendation is to use reconciled parallel views and explicit appeal or exception rules rather than pretend that one number serves every purpose.

### For performance reviews and incentives: protect truth before demanding stretch

A control system cannot correct what participants have reason to conceal. Forecasts should therefore be judged first for decision usefulness and calibration, not for whether they equal an aspirational target. Target-linked compensation should be tested for thresholds, caps, cliff effects, and period-shifting opportunities [19]. Jensen's documented examples include delaying purchases, moving revenues between periods, cutting research and development or investment, and polluting information [19]. The author's broader pre-mortem should also test whether a participant can improve pay by redefining a measure or shifting cost or risk outside the measurement boundary.

Subjective judgment remains necessary where measures are incomplete, but discretion needs structure. Reviewers can document the criteria used, distinguish controllable decisions from exogenous outcomes, compare judgments across similar cases, disclose changes from prior criteria, and provide an appeal or calibration process. Ittner, Larcker, and Meyer's scorecard case shows that unconstrained subjectivity can recreate financial-measure dominance and perceived favoritism [8]. The author's synthesis is that formulaic evaluation can create gaming while opaque judgment can create distrust; the design problem is disciplined judgment, not a choice between pure formula and pure discretion.

Scorecards should retain strategic uniqueness, but evaluators need help using it. Comparability is useful for portfolio oversight, yet Lipe and Salterio's experiment found that common-measure performance affected the judgments of 58 MBA participants while the unique-measure manipulation had no statistically significant effect [15]. The result is specific to that task and participant group, not proof that practicing evaluators always ignore unique measures. A review template can require an explicit discussion of unique strategic objectives before overall ranking. Strategy maps should be treated as causal hypotheses: if the proposed driver changes without the expected customer or financial result, management should revise the measure, the intervention, or the strategy rather than preserve the diagram [6][7].

### For boards and owners: oversee the control architecture, not only reported results

The author's governance recommendation is that boards ask how management knows whether strategy is working before the income statement delivers a verdict. Relevant evidence may include critical performance variables, strategic uncertainties, major boundaries, forecast changes, incentive design, and the history of corrective action [1][3][4]. Sources [1], [3], and [4] concern management control rather than direct tests of board practice, so this is an application of their frameworks. A board dashboard should not reproduce the entire operating system; it should show the evidence capable of changing a capital, risk, leadership, or strategic decision.

The board should also inspect control failures as system failures. Missing a target may reflect execution, but repeated favorable forecasts followed by misses may reflect incentive contamination. Strong local results alongside weak enterprise outcomes may reflect responsibility-center or transfer-pricing design. A proliferation of measures with unchanged decisions may reflect dashboard theater. The author's synthesis is that a post-control review should ask whether the objective, measure, target, data, authority, review process, incentive, or response rule failed -- not merely who missed the number.

Incentive oversight should include the full behavioral package. Executive pay may reward revenue growth while capital approvals punish uncertainty, culture discourages dissent, and forecasts are expected to meet external guidance. The nominal compensation metric then understates the actual control system. Malmi and Brown's package perspective and the empirical evidence on gaming support reviewing formal rewards together with planning, administrative controls, and culture [5][9][10][19].

### For employees and operational managers: controls should make work intelligible

Employees need enough transparency to understand how a measure is produced, how their actions affect it, how it connects to the wider process, and how to repair an exception. Wouters and Wilderom found that experience-based development, experimentation, professionalism, and transparency of the performance-measurement system helped create an enabling system [13]. Ahrens and Chapman similarly identified repair, internal transparency, global transparency, and flexibility as characteristics of enabling formalization, while observing enabling and coercive processes side by side [14]. The author's recommendation is to involve users for their local knowledge without transferring final accountability for strategic priorities to every participant.

Operational participation should focus on knowledge. Front-line employees often know which data are delayed, which classifications create noise, where a target conflicts with quality, and which exception requires local judgment. Management retains responsibility for strategic priorities and boundaries, but ignoring local knowledge can produce technically neat measures that are operationally false. The author's assessment is that a measure should not be attached to evaluation until users can reproduce its logic, identify its known limitations, and describe the action it is meant to inform.

### For digital, multinational, and resilience control: make new dependencies governable

Digital control should assign ownership for source data, transformations, models, thresholds, and exceptions. A manager using an algorithmic forecast or work-allocation tool should be able to identify which data entered, what changed since the prior version, who can override it, how affected people can contest an error, and who remains accountable for the decision [22][26]. Real-time reporting without these controls can accelerate a mistake rather than accelerate learning. Because the OECD evidence records managerial perceptions rather than causal effects, organizations should test both intended benefits and worker, bias, privacy, and health effects in their own setting [26].

Multinational systems should treat national culture as a hypothesis about context, not a score assigned to every individual. Pilot evidence should test whether the same target, participative process, or reward is interpreted similarly across units; local adaptations should preserve enterprise boundaries while allowing different routes to information, voice, and motivation [24]. Sustainability controls require the same discipline: environmental or social objectives need operational definitions, decision rights, resource commitments, review forums, and consequences, or they remain detached from ordinary management [29].

Risk controls should distinguish events that can reasonably be prevented or detected from failures whose mechanisms are opaque, systemic, or outside one firm's control. The first class calls for tested boundaries, detection, and escalation; the second also calls for containment, recovery capacity, redundancy, and learning after disruption [27][28]. The author's synthesis is that resilience measures should have owners and drills just as diagnostic measures have targets and review cadences. A resilience claim without a tested recovery capability is not evidence of control.

### For investors and analysts: infer control quality from repeated operating evidence

The author's suggested external signals include consistency between stated strategy, segment measures, capital allocation, incentive plans, forecast behavior, and later outcomes. Repeated changes in non-GAAP definitions, guidance that is narrowly met, acquisition targets that disappear after closing, or bonuses earned despite poor owner outcomes can indicate a system optimized for measured performance rather than enterprise value. These are signals requiring investigation, not proof of misconduct.

The author's positive indicators include candid variance explanations, stable definitions, decision-relevant segment disclosure, post-investment reviews, willingness to revise strategy when assumptions fail, and incentives tied to outcomes managers can influence over appropriate horizons. The balanced-scorecard and budgeting evidence cautions against equating framework adoption with quality [7][9]. The investor's question is not whether the company uses a named system but whether information, authority, feedback, and rewards consistently produce rational action.

### Failure modes and a minimum viable control system

The author's assessment is that the most dangerous failure is a system that suppresses truth while appearing precise. It can arise when a target is also a forecast and bonus threshold, when a local manager is accountable for uncontrollable allocations, when common metrics crowd out strategy-specific evidence, or when negative variances trigger blame before diagnosis [8][10][15][19]. Prevention requires separation of purposes, controllability, transparent definitions, independent data checks, and review forums where disconfirming evidence can change the plan.

Other failures are overcontrol and undercontrol. Overcontrol can produce delay, metric gaming, short horizons, and learned helplessness; undercontrol can produce local drift, hidden risk, inconsistent decisions, and weak coordination. The author's synthesis favors enabling formalization: clear boundaries and outcome accountability combined with transparency, repair capability, and delegated judgment. Ahrens and Chapman observed enabling and coercive processes side by side rather than proving a universal middle point [14]. Contingency research identifies environment, technology, size, structure, strategy, national culture, and sometimes trust as relevant contextual variables; it does not establish reversibility, skill, and consequence as a validated universal list [18][24][30].

A minimum viable control system has seven components: a clear objective; a named decision owner; defined authority and boundary conditions; one or more verified measures; an explicit target or forecast purpose; a review cadence; and a response rule. This seven-part model is the author's synthesis. New elements should be added only when a demonstrated failure requires them. Controls should also have sunset or redesign triggers because strategies, data, and organizational boundaries change. The final test is behavioral: if every person acted rationally under the system's rules, would the resulting local actions still serve the organization's strategy? If not, the control design -- not merely the participant -- requires correction.
