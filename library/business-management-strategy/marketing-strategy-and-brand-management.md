# Marketing Strategy and Brand Management -- Why Targeting and Positioning Choices Outweigh Tactics, and Why Marketing's Measured Effects Are Smaller and Harder to Verify Than Practice Assumes

Marketing strategy decides which customers an organization serves and what image it presents to them, and brand management stewards the customer knowledge those choices create; tactical decisions on product, price, place, and promotion follow from that strategy and cannot repair it [1][2][3]. The evidence reviewed here shows that brand strength can be measured as a price and volume outcome [4], that competing brands differ far more in how many buyers they reach than in how loyal those buyers are [5], and that the average measured effect of advertising is small and often misstated by non-experimental methods [7][8]. Interpretation: marketing is best managed as a portfolio of testable investments with benchmarked expectations, not as a source of universal laws.

## Background

The American Marketing Association defines marketing as "the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large." Its companion definition of marketing research describes the function that links consumers, customers, and the public to the marketer through information used to identify opportunities and problems, generate and evaluate actions, monitor performance, and improve understanding of marketing as a process. Both were approved in 2017, and the AMA states that a panel of five scholars reviews them regularly [1]. Dolnicar, Gruen, and Leisch state the purpose more narrowly: to match the genuine needs and desires of consumers with the offers of suppliers particularly suited to satisfy them [2]. The two statements agree that marketing is a matching and exchange activity, not only promotion.

The segmentation tradition begins, in the account of Dolnicar and colleagues, with Wendell Smith (1956), who first proposed segmentation as a marketing strategy and defined it as viewing a heterogeneous market, one characterized by divergent demand, as a number of smaller homogeneous markets [2]. The same source describes the planning framework that grew from this idea. A strategic plan sets long-term direction and contains two decisions that are hard to reverse: which consumers to focus on (segmentation and targeting) and which image of the organization to create (positioning). A tactical plan then covers up to one year and is traditionally organized around product, price, place, and promotion [2]. Dolnicar and colleagues stress an asymmetry: the tactical plan depends entirely on the strategic plan, but not the reverse, so good tactics cannot compensate for bad strategy and only determine how quickly a wrong destination is reached [2].

The brand-equity tradition has a different origin. Keller (1993) identified two motivations for studying brand equity. One was financial: estimating a brand's value for balance sheets, mergers, acquisitions, or divestitures. The other was strategic: improving marketing productivity when costs were rising, competition was growing, and demand was flattening. Keller argued that financial valuation has little relevance if no underlying value has been created or if managers do not know how to exploit it [3]. He cited two valuation methods of the time: Interbrand's subjective multiplier of brand profits based on seven performance dimensions (leadership, stability, market stability, internationality, trend, support, and protection), and the approach of Simon and Sullivan (1990), which defines brand equity as incremental discounted future cash flows and extracts it from the financial market value of the company [3]. Ailawadi, Lehmann, and Neslin (2003) later noted that, despite wide attention to the concept, few academic researchers had addressed its measurement, partly because of disagreement over whether equity should be measured from the consumer or the firm perspective [4].

A third tradition concerns organizational orientation. The marketing concept holds that, to achieve sustained success, firms should identify and satisfy customer needs more effectively than competitors. The market-orientation literature studies how far firms behave, or are inclined to behave, in accordance with that concept, from a behavioral perspective (generating, disseminating, and responding to market intelligence) and a cultural perspective (norms and values that encourage such behavior) [6]. By mid-2004 the literature was large enough for a meta-analysis aggregating 418 effects from 130 independent samples reported in 114 studies [6].

A fourth tradition is empirical and deliberately theory-light. Ehrenberg, Uncles, and Goodhardt (2004) synthesize decades of consumer-panel analysis showing that brand performance measures follow regular patterns across more than 50 product and service categories, which a single stochastic model, the NBD-Dirichlet developed by Chatfield and Goodhardt (1975), reproduces. The authors describe their paper as a position paper [5]. Its conclusions sit uneasily with parts of the brand-equity and segmentation traditions, a tension taken up in the Criticisms section below.

The measurement tradition is the most recent. Sethuraman, Tellis, and Briesch (2011) trace systematic generalization about advertising effects to a 1984 meta-analysis of 128 estimates from 16 studies, and update it with 751 short-term estimates [7]. Gordon and colleagues (2019) note that digital advertising spending exceeded television spending for the first time in 2017 and that, despite granular data linking exposures to outcomes, few online campaigns rely on randomized trials [8]. Two further developments documented in 2025 sources complicate measurement and discovery: the uncertain future of browser-level identifiers used for tracking [11] and the spread of AI-generated answers in search [9][10]. These are treated in a dedicated section below.

## Core Concepts

### Market Research and Strategic Matching

Strategic marketing requires two inputs. One is an inventory of the organization's own strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats; the other is evidence about what consumers need or desire, which Dolnicar and colleagues say is typically investigated through market research, including qualitative work such as focus groups and interviews, and observational and experimental research [2]. The AMA's definition of marketing research makes the same point from the research side: information is used to identify and define opportunities and problems, to generate, refine, and evaluate actions, and to monitor performance [1]. Because segmentation and positioning decisions are hard to reverse [2], the quality of this evidence is a precondition for them and not an afterthought.

### Strategic and Tactical Choices Are Asymmetric

Dolnicar and colleagues divide marketing planning into a strategic layer (which consumers to serve, and what image to create) and a tactical layer (product, price, place, promotion). Their central claim is logical: tactics are chosen to serve a target segment and positioning, so a flawed target cannot be repaired by efficient execution [2]. A pricing change, a media plan, or a sales incentive is therefore evaluated against the strategic choice it serves. This is why pricing, customer acquisition, and sales design are treated as separate topics in this library, each downstream of the choices described here (see See Also).

### Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning

A segmentation criterion can be a single consumer characteristic, such as age or country, or a set of characteristics, such as benefits sought or values held [2]. Dolnicar and colleagues distinguish three strategies. A concentrated strategy serves one segment and suits resource-poor organizations facing fierce competition, at the price of depending on that segment. A differentiated strategy serves several segments with customized marketing mixes and suits mature markets where consumers can tell alternatives apart. An undifferentiated strategy, as with petrol or white bread, markets one offer to everyone and may suit resource-rich organizations or new products whose alternatives consumers cannot yet discriminate [2].

Segmentation is costly and demanding. The costs include research, customized products and communications, and continuous monitoring; Cahill, quoted by Dolnicar and colleagues, holds that a segmentation scheme must be more profitable than marketing without it, net of the cost of developing and using it [2]. Implementation barriers fall into groups: weak senior-management commitment or resources; organizational culture, including low market orientation and resistance to change; lack of training or of a qualified marketing function and analyst; financial or structural constraints; and process failures such as unclear objectives or unassigned responsibilities [2]. The authors summarize the commitment as a marriage, not a date, recommend that the decision be taken at the highest executive level, and note that if an organization is not market-oriented, even the finest segmentation analysis cannot be successfully implemented [2].

Positioning is the strategic image decision. Keller, citing Aaker, Ries and Trout, and Wind, describes its essence as a sustainable competitive advantage or "unique selling proposition" that gives consumers a compelling reason to buy that particular brand [3]. He adds that unless a brand has no competitors it will share some associations with other brands, and that shared associations help establish category membership and define the scope of competition [3].

### Segmentation at the Category Level Versus the Brand Level

Ehrenberg and colleagues separate two claims that practice often merges. Strong segmentation exists at the category or subcategory level: cat food buyers have cats, users of leaded gasoline had older cars, and presweetened cereals are eaten more by children. But the user profiles of substitutable brands within a category seldom differ, and such subcategory patterns tend to hold about equally for all substitutable brands in that category [5]. Interpretation: a segmentation claim should state whether it distinguishes categories and subcategories (needs differ) or brands (customers differ), because the cited evidence supports the first far more readily than the second.

### Category Structure, Brand Portfolios, and Channels

Ehrenberg and colleagues observe that sustainable major product differentiation between competing brands seldom occurs because sales-effective advantages are usually soon copied. Functional differentiation exists mainly within brands, as stock-keeping-unit variants such as pack sizes, flavors, and technical specifications, and competing brands in a category tend to carry much the same range of variants [5]. They also report that functionally distinct submarkets, such as decaffeinated and regular coffee, luxury cars, and unleaded and leaded gasoline, are sometimes created deliberately, after which direct competitors launch variants into each of them [5]. Interpretation: in these sources, category creation and variant portfolios are where real differentiation is located, while brand-level differentiation is supported less often.

Channel choice sits in the tactical plan: Dolnicar and colleagues describe "place" as the selection of the most suitable distribution channels to reach the target segment [2]. Ehrenberg and colleagues name securing better distribution as an area where marketing has scope, because market shares differ greatly [5]. Customer lifetime value, acquisition cost, and retention economics are developed in the customer acquisition and retention topic and are not repeated here.

### Brand Knowledge and Customer-Based Brand Equity

Keller defines customer-based brand equity as the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to the marketing of the brand. A brand has positive equity when consumers react more favorably to a marketing-mix element, such as price, promotion, or a proposed extension, than they do to the same element attributed to a fictitiously named or unnamed version of the product [3]. Brand knowledge has two components. Brand awareness consists of recognition (confirming prior exposure when given the brand as a cue) and recall (retrieving the brand when given the category or a need as a cue). Keller gives three reasons awareness matters: it raises the chance that the brand enters the consideration set; it can affect decisions among the brands in that set even when there are essentially no other associations, as when consumers adopt a rule to buy only familiar, well-established brands; and it influences the formation and strength of the associations in the brand image. Brand image is the set of associations held in memory [3].

Associations differ by type, favorability, strength, and uniqueness. Types run from attributes (product-related, or non-product-related such as price, packaging, user imagery, and usage imagery) through benefits (functional, experiential, and symbolic) to overall attitudes [3]. Keller argues that equity requires awareness plus some favorable, strong, and unique associations; if a brand is seen as identical to a prototypical version of the category, consumer response should not differ from response to a hypothetical product [3]. He also notes that competitive overlap has a downside: the number of competing brands advertising in a category can reduce consumers' ability to recall a brand's communication by creating interference in memory, an effect that distinctive retrieval cues at the point of purchase can overcome [3].

### Brand Architecture, Extension, and Dilution

Keller describes three branding strategies for a company: individual brand names for different products without mention of the company (his example is Procter & Gamble's laundry detergents), the company name for all products (General Electric, Heinz), and a hybrid or sub-brand strategy combining both (Kellogg's Corn Flakes). The latter two give access to consumers' attitudes toward the company, and the sub-brand approach also allows more specific beliefs to be created [3]. Brand extensions use an existing brand's image to inform consumers and retailers about a new product. Extension can raise awareness for the new product, because the brand node is already in memory, and let consumers infer attributes and quality, which can lower the cost of the introductory campaign [3].

Extensions carry risk. Keller reports that a brand associated with a disparate set of products may weaken product-category identification, that dilution is especially likely where existing associations are already weak, and that an unsuccessful extension can harm the core brand image most when it differs little from the original brand. He cites an econometric analysis in which the perceived sudden-acceleration problem of Audi's 5000 model spilled over and reduced demand for the 4000 and Quattro models, and an experiment in which a hypothetical lower-quality shampoo extension reduced quality perceptions of a health-and-beauty core brand while a facial-tissue extension did not [3]. He also reports that the lower-priced Cadillac Cimarron is thought to have led to declines in image and sales across the Cadillac division [3].

### Revenue Premium: Brand Equity as a Market Outcome

Ailawadi and colleagues propose measuring equity as revenue premium: the difference between the revenue a branded product generates (volume times price) and the revenue of a corresponding private-label product [4]. Revenue premium combines two components. Price premium is the higher price a brand commands. Volume premium is the extra units it sells relative to the private label. The authors classify brands into four cases: higher price and higher volume than the private label (case A), higher price and lower volume (B), lower price and higher volume (C), and lower price and lower volume (D) [4]. Revenue premium is a product-market outcome measure; the authors recommend pairing it with customer mind-set measures for diagnosis and with financial-market measures for future potential, because it explains neither the consumer sources of equity nor extendibility [4].

### Reach, Penetration, and Double Jeopardy

Ehrenberg and colleagues distinguish brand size measures (market share and penetration, the proportion of potential customers buying at least once) from loyalty measures (purchase frequency per buyer, share of category requirements, and the share of buyers who are 100 percent loyal) and switching measures (which other brands a brand's buyers also buy) [5]. Three regularities recur. Double jeopardy: small brands have far fewer buyers and also somewhat lower purchase frequency, an effect that is small unless penetration is very high. Heterogeneity: most of a brand's customers are light buyers. Duplication of purchase: the share of one brand's buyers who also buy another is roughly proportional to the other brand's penetration [5].

The authors' model treats consumers as holding steady split-loyalty repertoires and brands as differing mainly in share. Its implication, in their view, is that changes in marketing-mix inputs such as price, product, selling, and distribution mostly affect penetration and sales volume rather than loyalty [5]. They add that advertising for an established brand mainly publicizes and reminds, and that competition tends to produce equilibrium: "running hard to stand still" [5].

### Demand Creation Through the Marketing Mix

The tactical mix is traditionally product, price, place, and promotion [2]. Keller's guidance is that tactics also change what consumers know: pricing policy creates associations to a price tier and to price volatility, including the frequency and magnitude of discounts, and sales promotions involving temporary price decreases may create or strengthen a discount association with the brand, with implications for loyalty and for responses to future price changes. Marketing decisions should therefore be judged on their effect on brand knowledge as well as immediate sales [3]. Ehrenberg and colleagues report from 150 price promotions in Britain, Germany, Japan, and the United States that before-to-after repeat buying was unaffected, no before-to-after sales increase occurred, and virtually no new buyers were gained; promotions were mainly taken up by existing customers [5]. Sethuraman and colleagues find that omitting distribution from advertising-response models biases the estimated advertising elasticity upward, plausibly because advertised brands are better distributed [7].

### Market Orientation as an Organizational Capability

In the multivariate analysis of Kirca and colleagues, interdepartmental connectedness (path .36), top-management emphasis (.25), and market-based reward systems (.24) are the significant antecedents of market orientation; centralization and formalization correlate with it in bivariate tests but are not significant once the others are included [6]. The authors suggest that connectedness and reward systems may counter the tendency of centralization to diminish market orientation [6]. Market orientation affects performance partly through innovativeness, customer loyalty, and perceived quality, with a smaller direct path remaining [6]. Market orientation is therefore an organizational property built through management emphasis, information sharing, and incentives, not a department. It links this topic to organizational design and culture in the same domain.

### Causal Measurement and Attribution

Measuring what marketing causes is difficult for two reasons given by Gordon and colleagues: individual-level outcomes are volatile relative to ad spending per customer, so advertising explains only a small amount of their variation, and even small amounts of endogeneity, such as likely buyers being more likely to see the ad, can severely bias estimates [8]. In an online auction platform, exposure is shaped by user activity (activity bias), by targeting that favors users predicted to convert, and by competing bidders [8]. A randomized controlled trial addresses these threats by comparing outcomes for users randomly assigned to test and control groups; in the platform design they study, the focal ad is replaced at the last moment by the runner-up ad for control users, so the control group sees what it would otherwise have seen [8]. A comparison of exposed with unexposed users measures a mixture of treatment and selection [8].

## Digital Measurement, Privacy, and AI-Mediated Discovery

### Tracking Infrastructure

Gordon and colleagues note that their data were captured at the user level regardless of device or browser, whereas most advertising data are collected at the level of a web-browser cookie [8]. Browser-level identifiers have been a recurring uncertainty. On 17 October 2025 Google announced that it would retire ten Privacy Sandbox technologies, among them the Attribution Reporting API, Topics, and Protected Audience, citing ecosystem feedback and low levels of adoption. The same post states that Chrome will maintain its current approach to offering users third-party cookie choice, that CHIPS, FedCM, and Private State Tokens will be kept, and that Google will continue to engage on an interoperable attribution standard because marketers and publishers want scaled measurement [11]. The post is Google's own statement of its decisions; it does not measure their effect on advertisers. Interpretation: measurement practice cannot assume a stable identifier regime, which strengthens the case for experiments and first-party data.

### AI-Generated Answers and Click Behavior

Pew Research Center analyzed the browsing activity of 900 U.S. adults in March 2025, together with 68,879 Google searches whose results pages were collected in April 2025; 12,593 of those searches produced an AI summary. Fifty-eight percent of respondents conducted at least one Google search that produced an AI summary, and 18 percent of all searches in the study produced one. Users who encountered a summary clicked a traditional result link in 8 percent of visits, against 15 percent for users who did not; clicked a link inside the summary in 1 percent of visits; and ended their browsing session after the page in 26 percent of visits, against 16 percent [10]. Longer and question-form queries were more likely to trigger a summary: 60 percent of queries beginning with question words produced one [10]. Interpretation: because the queries that trigger summaries differ systematically from those that do not, the comparison of click rates is descriptive and not an experiment.

### Optimizing for Generative Engines

Aggarwal and colleagues define generative engines as systems that retrieve sources and synthesize a cited answer with a language model, and they study how content creators can raise their visibility in such answers. They build a benchmark of 10,000 queries and test nine content modifications [9]. Their findings are reported in the Evidence section. The paper states that its methods may need to adapt as generative engines evolve [9].

## Evidence

### Revenue Premium Across Grocery Brands

Ailawadi and colleagues computed revenue premium for brands in two data sets covering 1991 to 1996: weekly data from 85 Dominick's Finer Foods stores in Chicago covering 111 brands in 17 categories, and national household-panel data from Information Resources covering 102 brands in 23 categories [4]. The measure was stable (correlation with its one-year lag of .96 locally and .98 nationally) and moved with conventional wisdom about eroding brand strength: the median change in revenue premium was a decline of 11 percent in the local sample, and in the national sample the median loss was 29 percent over the six years, while private-label share rose by a median 69 percent [4]. In diapers, the category leaders Kimberly-Clark and Procter & Gamble, locked in a price war, lost almost 70 percent and 90 percent respectively of their revenue premium [4].

The decomposition mattered. In 1991 only 33 percent of local brands (54 percent nationally) had both a price and a volume premium; 55 percent locally (30 percent nationally) charged a price premium but sold fewer units than the private label. Price premium was uncorrelated with revenue premium (correlations of about zero in both data sets), whereas volume premium correlated at .79 and .75 [4]. A brand's share of category advertising was positively associated with revenue premium; its share of category deals was not significant. Average estimated self-price elasticities were -1.195 for price cuts and -0.921 for price increases among low-revenue-premium brands, against -0.747 and -0.183 among high-revenue-premium brands, so high-premium brands lost relatively little when raising price [4]. The authors stress that the market must be defined appropriately: a strong niche player can appear weak, as Gatorade's revenue premium was -$161,537 in bottled juice drinks but -$7,601,469 when aggregated into all juice [4]. The analysis is observational, and the choice of benchmark brand is the most challenging part of the calculation [4].

### Brand Performance Patterns in Consumer-Panel Data

Ehrenberg and colleagues reviewed panel data from the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, Germany, and Australasia. Averaged over the eight leading brands in 12 categories, market share fell almost tenfold from the largest to the eighth brand, whereas loyalty and switching measures stayed broadly similar or fell by a factor of two or three at most [5]. The average brand had 21 percent annual penetration, 3.2 purchases per buyer, about 12 percent 100-percent-loyal buyers, and a share of category requirements of about 27 percent; some 50 percent of its buyers bought it only once in the year [5]. The leading U.S. instant-coffee brand, bought by 11 percent of households about 3.2 times a year, had a share of category requirements of 50 percent and 12 percent 100-percent-loyal buyers, and all but one of its measures matched model benchmarks [5].

The authors applied the benchmarks to dynamics. In 150 price promotions there were no lasting sales or loyalty effects [5]. Across 22 successful new brands, average purchase frequencies were at established-brand levels almost immediately [5]. In an illustrative new-brand case, a niche plan (1 percent of households buying eight times a year) and a mass plan (8 percent buying once) were both inconsistent with the benchmark of about three purchases per buyer, which implied that a 5 percent share would require roughly 3 percent of households buying 2.7 times; the case, described as an amalgam of practical experience, ended with the plan being dropped [5]. A second case concerned Campbell's premium "Tastes of the World" soup in Britain, whose low loyalty measures were found to be normal for its size, so that its problem was too few customers [5]. The authors note systematic departures from the model, including repeat rates that erode somewhat over longer intervals and market leaders whose purchase frequencies are occasionally a unit higher than predicted [5].

### Market Orientation and Performance: A Meta-Analysis

Kirca and colleagues aggregated 418 effects from 130 samples in 114 studies, correcting correlations for measurement reliability. The corrected mean correlation between market orientation and performance was .32; for profits .27, sales .26, market share .31, and overall (perceived) business performance .46 [6]. The relationship was stronger in manufacturing (.37) than in service firms (.26), in low-power-distance (.33 versus .27) and low-uncertainty-avoidance (.34 versus .27) cultures, and in studies using subjective performance measures [6]. Environmental turbulence did not show reliable moderation: five of 14 studies supported it, which a sign test judged insufficient [6]. The mediation model attributed a total effect of .30 to market orientation, of which .13 was indirect through innovativeness, customer loyalty, and quality, with a direct path of .17 (p < .10) remaining [6]. The study aggregates correlations and cannot by itself establish causation.

### Advertising Elasticity: 751 Short-Term Estimates

Sethuraman and colleagues meta-analyzed 751 short-term and 402 long-term brand-level advertising elasticities from 56 econometric studies published from 1960 to 2008 [7]. The mean short-term elasticity was .12 (median .05), down from .22 in the 1984 meta-analysis; the mean long-term elasticity was .24 (median .10), down from an implied .41 [7]. Elasticities were significantly greater than zero in 57 percent of the 437 cases for which significance information was available [7]. They were higher for durable goods, for growth-stage products (.16 versus .11 for mature products), in Europe (.17) than in the United States (.11), and when advertising was measured in gross rating points than in money [7]. Elasticity did not fall during recessions, and the post-1980 mean (.10) was below the pre-1980 mean (.13) [7]. The authors compare advertising's elasticity with a price elasticity more than twenty times larger (about -2.62 in earlier meta-analyses) while cautioning that this ignores the profit effects of price cuts; in their illustrative calculation, which assumes a price elasticity of 2.6 and a pass-through and take-up fraction of .5, the optimal advertising-to-sales ratio fell from 8.5 to 4.6 percent when the elasticity assumption moved from .22 to .12 [7]. The study excludes experiments and business-to-business advertising [7].

### Randomized Versus Observational Measurement at Scale

Gordon and colleagues used 15 randomized advertising experiments at Facebook in 2015, with 500 million user-experiment observations and 1.6 billion impressions, and re-estimated each campaign's effect with common observational methods (matching, stratification, regression, and combinations) using covariate sets of increasing richness [8]. The observational methods mostly overestimated the experimental lift but in some cases significantly underestimated it, and in 50 percent of the studies the estimated percentage increase in purchase outcomes was off by a factor of three across all methods [8]. Among the 14 studies with a checkout outcome, the point estimates in 7 were consistently off by more than a factor of three [8].

Study 4 illustrates the range. The experimental lift was 73 percent; the exposed-versus-unexposed comparison implied 316 percent, exact matching on age and gender alone gave 222 percent, and the richest covariate set came close to the experiment for most methods. In study 9 the experimental lift was 2.4 percent, the closest observational estimate was 1,306 percent, and the authors speculate that an unusually small exposure rate of 6.6 percent left the ad-targeting mechanism ample opportunity to target likely responders. In study 15 all methods except exact matching underestimated lift [8]. Observational methods did better for registration and page-view outcomes than for checkouts, which the authors attribute to unexposed users being unlikely to reach a registration or landing page on their own [8]. The authors frame the conclusion as applying to their setting and to commonly used observational approaches based on the data usually available in the industry [8].

### Visibility in Generative Engines

Aggarwal and colleagues tested nine content modifications against an unmodified baseline on the 1,000-query test split of their benchmark, using a generative engine built on the top five Google results and a language model, and measured visibility by position-adjusted word count and an LLM-scored subjective impression [9]. Adding citations, quotations, or statistics raised visibility by roughly 30 to 40 percent on the first metric and 15 to 30 percent on the second; keyword stuffing, a standard search-engine-optimization tactic, gave little or no gain [9]. Effects varied by query domain [9]. In a simulation in which all sources were optimized at once, the fifth-ranked source gained 115 percent with added citations while the top-ranked source lost 30 percent of its visibility [9]. On Perplexity.ai, a deployed generative engine, quotation addition improved the first metric by 22 percent and statistics addition improved the second by up to 37 percent, while keyword stuffing performed 10 percent worse than baseline [9]. The metrics measure presence in answers, not sales.

## Implications

### For Marketing and General Managers

Treat the target and positioning decision as the point of highest leverage, and test it before funding tactics. The asymmetry argument implies that investment in execution is wasted when the target or image is wrong [2]. Before committing to a differentiated strategy, estimate whether the segmentation scheme will be more profitable than marketing without it after its own costs, and secure executive sponsorship and resources, since the documented barriers are largely organizational [2]. Dolnicar and colleagues associate concentrated strategies with resource-poor organizations in fierce competition, differentiated strategies with capable organizations in mature markets, and undifferentiated strategies with new products or resource-rich firms, so the depth of segmentation is a resource and market-maturity decision to be recorded with its expected payoff net of cost [2]. They add that finer segmentation, down to individual customers, is becoming more viable with e-commerce and consumer databases that let providers learn from purchase history [2]; interpretation: such approaches depend on the firm's own customer data, a point consistent with the interest in first-party data that Google cites [11].

Because the strategic choices are hard to reverse, evidence should precede them. The sources place market research (qualitative, observational, and experimental) before the segmentation and positioning decisions [2] and define the research function as the link that supplies information to identify opportunities and problems and to evaluate actions [1]. Interpretation: because the evidence on brand-level segmentation is weak for substitutable consumer goods [5], segments should be defined by what customers need and do (category-level or usage-based distinctions) and validated against observed behavior, not assumed from demographics.

Set expectations for growth using benchmarks. If a brand's loyalty measures are normal for its size, as in the Campbell's soup example, then the problem is too few customers, and the levers are availability and reach [5]. A plan that assumes a new brand can achieve a large share with unusually few, unusually loyal buyers is inconsistent with the panel evidence [5]. Conversely, brand equity should be managed as a long-term asset: Keller's guidelines call for a broad view of marketing decisions, a defined target knowledge structure, coordination across tactics, a long-term view, tracking studies and controlled experiments, and evaluation of extensions for feedback effects on the core brand [3]. Frequent discounting is a documented risk to price-related associations [3], while the panel evidence suggests that promotions mostly shift purchase timing among existing customers [5].

Budget advertising as an investment with modest average returns. A mean short-term elasticity of .12 and a median of .05 mean that many campaigns do not produce statistically detectable sales effects, and that effects differ by product type and life-cycle stage [7]. This supports concentrating spend where elasticity is plausibly higher (durable goods, early life cycle) and not cutting it reflexively in recessions, since the meta-analysis found no lower elasticity then [7]. Elasticity estimates are averages across studies; a given brand's response must be measured in its own data.

Invest in market orientation as a management system. The meta-analytic effect of market orientation on profits (r = .27) is a cost-based measure that accounts for the cost of implementing it [6]. The antecedents that matter most are connectedness among departments, visible top-management emphasis, and reward systems tied to market-oriented behavior [6], which links marketing performance to organizational design and incentives rather than to the marketing department alone.

### For Investors and Boards

A brand claim in an investment thesis is testable. Revenue premium relative to a private-label or other benchmark brand converts "strong brand" into a number that can be tracked over time, decomposed into price and volume, and compared across competitors [4]. Because the price and volume components can diverge, a brand with a large price premium and a small volume premium may be weaker than its price suggests [4]. The measure is a view of current strength, not a valuation: it depends on a sensible benchmark and market definition, and does not explain why customers pay the premium or how far the brand can extend [4]. Interpretation: an analyst should pair it with evidence on awareness and associations [3] and with panel penetration data where available [5].

Boards should ask what the marketing organization can actually attribute. Where a management team reports campaign returns derived from comparisons of exposed and unexposed customers, the evidence shows such comparisons can overstate lift several-fold and occasionally understate it [8]. A reasonable governance question is which spending has been tested with holdout experiments and which has been assessed observationally. Boards should also treat claims of precise return on advertising spend as uncertain when average effect sizes are small relative to outcome volatility [7][8].

### For Analysts and Researchers

Use experiments where feasible and report uncertainty. The platform experiments show that rich covariates reduce but do not reliably remove selection bias, and the outcomes on which observational methods did best (registrations and page views) are those that unexposed users had little way to reach on their own [8]. Meta-analytic benchmarks are useful priors but depend on sample composition: the 1984 and 2011 elasticity means differ partly because of changes in data, method, and study mix [7].

Distinguish levels of analysis. Brand-level behavior in repeat-purchase markets follows benchmarks [5]; customer-level heterogeneity and cohort economics are covered in the customer acquisition and retention topic. Market-orientation findings are correlational and stronger with subjective measures [6]. Revenue premium findings are associations [4]. Interpretation: no single source supports a strong causal claim that brand investment raises long-run profit; the evidence is converging on measurable outcomes and constraints, not on a complete causal model.

### For Content Owners and Digital Marketers

Search behavior is changing the economics of organic discovery. In the Pew sample, search pages with an AI summary were associated with fewer clicks on traditional results and more session endings [10]. The generative-engine experiments suggest that content containing verifiable citations, quotations, and statistics is more visible in synthesized answers, and that tactics from classical search optimization may not transfer [9]. Interpretation: because both studies are time-bound (a March 2025 browsing sample; a benchmark built on a particular model and engine design), the durable lesson is to measure visibility and outcomes directly and to avoid committing to a single tactic. Because the identifier landscape is also unsettled [11], planning should assume that discovery and measurement channels will keep changing.

## Criticisms

The most direct conflict is between the brand-equity tradition and the Dirichlet tradition. Ehrenberg and colleagues state that there is no consumer-behavior evidence that brands have idiosyncratic "strong" and "weak" status beyond being big or small, and that there is no evidence that brands with supposedly high consumer-based equity subsequently get bigger [5]. Ailawadi and colleagues, by contrast, find systematic differences across brands in revenue premium and in asymmetric price responses [4]. Interpretation: the findings are partly reconcilable, because the first concerns loyalty and growth mechanisms and the second concerns price and volume outcomes at a point in time, but the sources do not resolve the dispute and neither tests the other's claim.

Other limits are specific. The Dirichlet benchmarks are defined for near-steady, non-partitioned markets, and the authors report systematic departures [5]. Revenue premium depends on the benchmark brand and the market definition and omits costs and future potential [4]. The market-orientation meta-analysis cannot isolate causation and shows stronger results with subjective measures [6]. The advertising-elasticity meta-analysis excludes experiments and business-to-business advertising, and its authors report that omitted variables and model form change the estimates [7]. The measurement experiments come from one platform [8]. The generative-engine study used a particular retrieval design and model, with LLM-scored subjective metrics [9]. Keller's framework is conceptual and the 1993 paper does not test it [3].

## Common Pitfalls

Treating a price premium as brand strength without checking volume: in the grocery data, more than half of local brands charged a price premium but sold fewer units than the private label [4]. Treating demographic differences between brands' buyers as segmentation: profiles of substitutable brands seldom differ [5]. Reading a promotion's sales spike as lasting gain: the panel evidence shows no lasting effect [5]. Attributing outcomes to advertising from exposed-versus-unexposed comparisons [8]. Defining the market so narrowly that a niche brand looks strong, or so broadly that it looks weak [4]. Extending a brand into a similar category without considering feedback to the core brand [3].
