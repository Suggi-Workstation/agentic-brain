# Strategy Formulation and Execution -- Strategy Is a Coherent Set of Choices That Only Delivery and Feedback Can Prove

Strategy formulation is the process of turning a diagnosis of a firm's situation into a coherent set of choices about where to compete, which activities and capabilities to build, and what to leave undone; execution is the process by which those choices become resource commitments and realized patterns of action. The evidence reviewed here indicates that the two cannot be separated into stages: a strategy is ultimately the pattern a firm actually enacts [1], formal planning is on average associated with better performance [6][9], yet a large share of intended value is reported lost between plan and delivery [15]. Firms that renew strategy well treat realized action as feedback that revises intentions, rather than treating every deviation from the plan as failure [1][8][13].

## Background

Strategy has traditionally been defined as intended and conceived as deliberate [1]. The author's interpretation is that much of the field's later development separates three ideas that this traditional plan-centered definition bundled together: what a firm intends, what a firm does, and what a firm learns. Mintzberg and Waters, drawing on more than ten years of research on strategy formation, defined strategy as a pattern in a stream of decisions [1]. They observed that one label was being applied to two different things, an intention and a realized pattern, and that comparing the two distinguishes deliberate strategies, realized as intended, from emergent strategies, which are patterns realized despite or in the absence of intentions [1]. This distinction is the intellectual foundation for treating formulation and execution as one system instead of two stages. It also supplies the vocabulary for the later empirical debate over whether formal planning improves performance.

A second line of development concerns the content of strategy: what a firm should choose. Two traditions answered from opposite directions. The positioning tradition, represented here by Porter's 1996 article, argues that strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position through a tailored set of activities, and that operational effectiveness, meaning performing similar activities better than rivals, is necessary but not sufficient because its techniques are easy to imitate [2]. The resource-based tradition, systematized by Barney in 1991, starts from the assumption that firms are heterogeneous in the strategic resources they control and that those resources are not perfectly mobile across firms, so that differences can persist [3]. Barney presented this as a substitution for the assumptions of environmental models, which treat resources as homogeneous and perfectly mobile, while also noting a complementarity: a firm attribute counts as a resource only when it exploits opportunities or neutralizes threats in the firm's environment [3]. The author's synthesis is that the two traditions are better read as answering different questions. Positioning asks which system of activities to run, for which customers, and against which rivals; the resource view asks which assets and capabilities make that system hard to copy.

The third development is the move from static to dynamic analysis. Teece, Pisano, and Shuen proposed in 1997 a dynamic capabilities framework for wealth creation in environments of rapid technological change, in which the competitive advantage of firms rests on distinctive processes, shaped by asset positions and evolution paths [4]. Their abstract goes so far as to say that identifying new opportunities and organizing effectively and efficiently to embrace them is generally more fundamental to private wealth creation than strategizing, if strategizing means engaging in conduct that keeps competitors off balance, raises rivals' costs, and excludes new entrants [4]. Eisenhardt and Martin argued in 2000 that dynamic capabilities are specific, identifiable processes such as product development, strategic decision making, and alliancing, and that in high-velocity markets the resource-based view reaches a boundary condition [5].

A fourth question is how much firm-level choices matter relative to industry membership. Rumelt's 1991 partition of the variance in rate of return among Federal Trade Commission Line of Business reporting units found negligible corporate effects, small stable industry effects, and very large stable business-unit effects, and he concluded that the most important sources of economic rents are business-specific [10]. McGahan and Porter's 1997 analysis of U.S. public corporations attributed 2, 19, 4, and 32 percent of the aggregate variance in profitability to year, industry, corporate-parent, and business-specific effects respectively, and found that the importance of industry effects differs across economic sectors [11]. These studies do not settle how strategy causes profit differences, a limitation discussed below, but they show that the business unit is where a large share of measured variation sits. Industry structure and profit pools belong to the industries-sectors domain; this topic treats the industry as the setting within which a firm chooses.

The fifth thread is the persistent gap between intended and delivered performance. Mankins and Steele reported, from consultancy research, that companies on average deliver only 63 percent of the financial performance their strategies promise [15]. Rumelt's account of bad strategy, as summarized in a review in Strategic Direction, describes it as long on goals and short on policy or action [16]. Both observations point toward the same conclusion: formulation quality and execution quality are separable causes of poor results, and managers frequently misdiagnose which of the two is failing [15].

The scope of this topic is the firm-level chain from diagnosis to choice to commitment to feedback. It does not cover industry structure (see the Porter's five forces topic), annual budgeting and the design of performance measures (see the management control systems topic), the mechanics of capital budgeting, or how capital markets value strategy. Where those subjects touch strategy, the connection is noted and the detail is left to the topic that owns it.

## Core Concepts

### Strategy as Diagnosis, Guiding Policy, and Coherent Action

According to a review of Rumelt's Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, good strategy is a coherent set of analyses, concepts, policies, arguments, and actions that respond to a high-stakes challenge, and it has a core that Rumelt calls the kernel, with three elements [16]. The diagnosis explains the nature of the challenge and identifies which aspects of the situation are critical. The guiding policy is an overall approach to the obstacles identified in the diagnosis, and a good one rules out a vast array of possible actions. The coherent actions are the resource allocations, policies, and maneuvers that are consistent with and coordinated by the guiding policy [16]. The review also reports Rumelt's claim that most deep strategic changes come from a change in diagnosis, that is, from a change in how the company perceives its situation [16]. Rumelt lists four manifestations of bad strategy, including fluff and mistaking goals for strategy; the review also describes bad strategic objectives that take the form of "dog's dinner" lists gathering every suggestion made at planning meetings [16].

The author's assessment is that the kernel works as a test of formulation quality. A document with no diagnosis, with no policy that excludes anything, or with actions that do not follow from the policy is a statement of aspiration, however detailed its financial forecast. The source here is a secondary summary, rated medium, so the kernel is used as a working checklist and not as empirical evidence.

### Intended, Deliberate, Realized, Emergent, and Unrealized Strategy

Mintzberg and Waters define intended strategy as what leaders plan and realized strategy as the pattern actually found in action. A deliberate strategy is realized as intended, an emergent strategy is a pattern realized despite or without intentions, and an unrealized strategy is an intention that was not met [1]. They are careful to separate unrealized strategy from realized strategy that is unsuccessful in its consequences: in their example of United States escalation in Vietnam, the intention to escalate was realized, even over-realized, yet it did not achieve its objective [1]. For a strategy to be perfectly deliberate, three conditions must hold. The intentions must be precise and articulated; they must be common to virtually all actors, whether shared or accepted under controls; and they must be realized exactly as intended, which means no external force can interfere. The third condition requires an environment that is perfectly predictable, totally benign, or under the organization's full control, so the authors conclude that perfectly deliberate strategies are unlikely to be found [1]. A perfectly emergent strategy would require consistency in action over time in the total absence of intention, which they also find difficult to imagine [1]. Real strategies therefore lie along a continuum between the two [1].

The paper elaborates the continuum into eight types found in research: planned, entrepreneurial, ideological, umbrella, process, unconnected, consensus, and imposed [1]. Three of them matter for execution. In an umbrella strategy, leaders set broad boundaries and targets and others fill in the specifics, so the strategy is both deliberate and emergent, or "deliberately emergent," because the central leadership intentionally creates the conditions under which others act [1]. In a process strategy, leadership controls the process aspects of strategy, such as hiring and structure, and leaves content to other actors [1]. In an unconnected strategy, a part of the organization pursues its own pattern without direction from the center; the study of the National Film Board of Canada found that the central leadership seldom dictated the content of films [1]. The same study documents a consensus strategy: in the early 1950s the Board made its first film for television and within months found itself concentrating two-thirds of its effort in that medium, a pattern that lasted about four years and then disappeared as spontaneously as it began [1]. The practical point is that leaders choose how much to control. When leadership can direct, strategy moves toward the planned or entrepreneurial types; when it can barely nudge, strategy moves toward the emergent types [1].

### Positioning: Unique Activities, Trade-offs, and Fit

Porter separates operational effectiveness from strategy. Operational effectiveness means performing similar activities better than rivals; strategy means choosing to perform different activities, or similar activities differently [2]. The productivity frontier is the state of best practice, and improving operational effectiveness moves a firm toward it, while technology can shift the frontier outward [2]. Porter argues that competition based on operational effectiveness alone is mutually destructive, leading to wars of attrition, and that the tools of continuous improvement draw companies toward imitation and homogeneity [2]. Strategic positions, in his account, arise from three sources that are not mutually exclusive and often overlap: variety (producing a subset of an industry's products or services), customers' needs (groups of customers that differ in price sensitivity, desired features, and the information, support, and services they require, as Ikea's customers do), and customers' accessibility [2].

His Southwest Airlines example shows that a position is a set of activities, not a customer description. Southwest serves price- and convenience-sensitive travelers on particular routes, avoids large airports and long distances, turns aircraft at the gate in about 15 minutes, uses a standardized fleet of 737 aircraft, and does not offer meals, assigned seats, interline baggage checking, or premium classes of service [2]. Each omission is a trade-off, and Porter's phrase for the principle is that the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do [2]. Trade-offs arise in part from the activities themselves: different positions require different product configurations, equipment, employee behavior, skills, and management systems, and many trade-offs reflect inflexibilities in machinery, people, or systems [2]. Fit among activities then makes the position hard to copy. First-order fit is simple consistency between each activity and the overall strategy, as at Vanguard, which aligns all activities with a low-cost strategy [2]. Porter argues that the most valuable fit is strategy-specific because it strengthens a position's uniqueness and amplifies trade-offs, and that a rival imitating only some activities without matching the whole system gains little and can decline; he cites Continental Lite's attempt to imitate Southwest [2]. He also argues that the growth imperative is hazardous to strategy, because efforts to grow blur uniqueness, create compromises, and reduce fit [2].

### Resources and Capabilities: The Inside-Out View

Barney's model asks which of a firm's resources can support sustained competitive advantage. A resource must have four attributes: it must be valuable, meaning it exploits opportunities or neutralizes threats; rare among current and potential competitors; imperfectly imitable; and without strategically equivalent substitutes that are valuable but neither rare nor imperfectly imitable [3]. Barney gives three reasons a resource can be imperfectly imitable: its acquisition depends on unique historical conditions, the link between the resource and the advantage is causally ambiguous, or the resource is socially complex, as with managers' interpersonal relations, a firm's culture, or its reputation with suppliers and customers [3]. His management-team example illustrates the substitutability test. A particular top management team may be valuable, rare, and imperfectly imitable, but if different teams are strategically equivalent and substitute teams are common or highly imitable, then a high-quality management team is not a source of sustained advantage [3]. The model also carries an explicit limit on managerial power: Barney assumes that managers cannot manipulate all attributes of their firms, and it is this limit that makes some resources imperfectly imitable [3].

The author's synthesis is that Barney's four tests and Porter's fit concept are complements. A system of activities is difficult to copy partly because the resources that run it are history-dependent or socially complex, and a rare resource is wasted if the activities around it do not use it.

### Dynamic Capabilities and the Boundary of Resource-Based Logic

Eisenhardt and Martin define dynamic capabilities as the organizational and strategic routines by which managers alter their resource base: they acquire and shed resources, integrate them, and recombine them to generate new value-creating strategies [5]. They argue that these routines are identifiable and specific, with strategic decision making, product development, and alliancing as examples, and that they show commonalities across firms, popularly termed best practice [5]. The consequence is equifinality: firms can reach similar capabilities by multiple paths, and in their analysis equifinality renders inimitability and immobility irrelevant to sustained advantage, which is a direct challenge to the traditional resource-based reasoning summarized above [5].

The form of a capability depends on market pace. In moderately dynamic markets, dynamic capabilities resemble traditional routines: detailed, analytic, stable processes with predictable outcomes. In high-velocity markets they are simple, experiential, and fragile, with unpredictable outcomes [5]. Two of their examples show what simple means. Yahoo's alliancing process consisted largely of a two-rule routine that set boundary conditions for managers forging alliances, and Intel managers allocated manufacturing capacity by a simple rule, margin per wafer start, while the company morphed into a microprocessor business [5]. Eisenhardt and Martin also separate two strategic logics: leverage, which uses capabilities to enhance existing resource configurations in pursuit of long-term advantage, and opportunity, which builds new configurations in pursuit of a series of temporary advantages [5]. Teece, Pisano, and Shuen, writing about rapid technological change, likewise place the source of wealth creation in internal technological, organizational, and managerial processes [4]. The author's synthesis is that the formulation question depends on the pace of the market: in stable settings the task is to build and defend a durable position, and in fast settings it is to build the process that renews advantage.

### Planning as a Process: Formal Analysis, Incrementalism, and Planned Emergence

Mintzberg and Waters caution that planning may be less creative than its label suggests. They argue that strategies appear not to be conceived in planning processes so much as elaborated from existing visions or copied from standard industry recipes, so that planning becomes programming, and that organizations plan not to be flexible but to realize specific intentions [1]. The later empirical literature qualifies this view without discarding it. Grant's case studies of the planning systems of eight of the world's largest oil companies described planned emergence: planning systems that coordinate decentralized strategy formulation within demanding performance targets and clear corporate guidelines [8]. Brews and Hunt concluded from 656 firms that formal planning and incrementalism both form part of good strategic planning, especially in unstable environments [7]. The author's synthesis is that planning is best understood as a coordination and learning mechanism, not as a prediction device. Its product is a shared set of assumptions, priorities, and commitments against which action can be compared.

### The Execution Gap: From Choice to Resource Commitment

Mankins and Steele describe the gap between strategy and performance as the result of breakdowns in planning and execution that senior management often cannot see [15]. In their survey, executives rated specific breakdowns, and the implied average losses of potential value included 7.5 percent from resources not being in the right place at the right time, 5.2 percent from poor communication of the strategy, 4.5 percent from poorly defined actions, and 4.1 percent from blurred accountabilities, among others [15]. They also describe the "venetian blinds" pattern, in which each year's plan projects modest first-year results and rapid improvement thereafter, the plan succeeds only partially, and the next year's plan again promises uninspiring first-year results followed by fast improvement, so that the successive forecasts viewed side by side resemble diagonally hung blinds [15]. Their report is that companies rarely track performance against long-term plans, which makes it hard to tell whether a shortfall came from the strategy, the plan, or the execution [15].

Their seven rules for closing the gap are: keep the strategy simple and concrete; debate assumptions, not forecasts; use a rigorous analytic framework such as profit pools; discuss resource deployments early; identify priorities clearly; monitor performance continuously against the plan; and reward and develop execution capabilities [15]. The rules are consultancy-derived and their evidential basis is discussed below, but they translate the kernel of diagnosis, policy, and coherent action into operating questions: what exactly will be done, by whom, with which resources, by when.

### Feedback Under Uncertainty: Assumptions, Inside Views, and Fit-Conserving Change

Kahneman and Lovallo's analysis of risk taking holds that overly optimistic forecasts result from adopting an inside view of the problem, which anchors predictions on plans and scenarios [14]. A strategic plan is built from the inside view by construction, so its projections inherit that bias. This is the cognitive reason the rule to debate assumptions, not forecasts [15], and to consult outside reference classes, has force.

Siggelkow adds a structural reason that feedback fails. He distinguishes internal fit, whether a firm's activities form a coherent configuration, from external fit, whether that configuration suits the environment [12]. Environmental change can leave internal fit intact while reducing external fit; he calls this fit-conserving change and argues that it poses a particularly difficult challenge, because no obvious internal misfit signals that anything is wrong [12]. Burgelman's study of Intel shows the other side of feedback: resource allocation by the firm's internal selection environment moved scarce manufacturing resources from memory to microprocessors before corporate strategy was officially changed [13]. Mintzberg and Waters make the corresponding general point that adding the concept of emergent strategy opens strategy making to learning, because the identification of emergent patterns, never intended, is often how managers come to change their deliberate strategies [1].

## Evidence

### Mintzberg and Waters (1985): Field Research on How Strategies Form

The paper rests on more than ten years of research into strategy formation, using the definition of strategy as a pattern in a stream of decisions [1]. The authors examined cases of organizations in different settings, among them the National Film Board of Canada, and derived the continuum from planned to imposed strategies described above [1]. Their findings are qualitative: perfectly deliberate strategies are unlikely because the three required conditions are demanding, virtually all real-world strategies have umbrella characteristics because no central leadership can totally pre-empt the discretion of others and none defers totally to others, and both rather deliberate and rather emergent strategies have been highly successful in their research [1]. The paper does not test performance differences between strategy types; the authors say it would be interesting to know how different types of strategies perform in various contexts [1]. It therefore supports the claim that strategy forms along a continuum, not the claim that one form outperforms another.

### Rumelt (1991) and McGahan and Porter (1997): Where Profit Variance Sits

Rumelt partitioned the total variance in rate of return among Federal Trade Commission Line of Business reporting units into industry factors, time factors, factors associated with the corporate parent, and business-specific factors, and distinguished stable from fluctuating effects [10]. His abstract reports negligible corporate effects, small stable industry effects, and very large stable business-unit effects, and concludes that the most important sources of economic rents are business-specific [10]. McGahan and Porter examined year, industry, corporate-parent, and business-specific effects on the profitability of U.S. public corporations within specific four-digit SIC categories and report 2, 19, 4, and 32 percent respectively of aggregate profitability variance [11]. By simple addition, the four named effects account for 57 percent of the variance (author's arithmetic); the abstract does not state the remainder. They also found that industry effects account for a smaller portion of profit variance in manufacturing but a larger portion in lodging and entertainment, services, wholesale and retail trade, and transportation, and that corporate-parent and industry effects have a negative covariance [11]. The two studies differ in samples and methods and in their estimate of the industry share, but they agree that business-specific effects are the largest component. Variance shares describe where differences in measured profitability lie; they do not by themselves identify which managerial choices produce the differences.

### Miller and Cardinal (1994) and George, Walker, and Monster (2019): Planning and Performance

Miller and Cardinal built a contingency model to explain inconsistent findings on planning and performance and tested it with meta-analytic data from 26 previously published studies [6]. Their abstract reports that strategic planning positively influences firm performance, that methods factors are primarily responsible for the inconsistencies in the earlier literature, and that the substantive contingency factors they examined, some frequently cited by earlier researchers, did not have a large impact [6]. George, Walker, and Monster performed a random-effects meta-analysis of 87 correlations from 31 empirical studies and report that strategic planning has a positive, moderate, and significant impact on organizational performance [9]. Their meta-regression found the impact strongest when performance is measured as effectiveness and when planning is measured as formal strategic planning, and the result held across private and public sectors and across U.S. and non-U.S. contexts [9]. Their conclusions add that planning is particularly potent for organizational effectiveness, meaning achievement of goals, but should not necessarily be undertaken in the hope of efficiency gains [9]. They also acknowledge that a meta-analysis of published articles might overestimate population effects because unpublished studies are not included [9]. Two meta-analyses twenty-five years apart (1994 and 2019), drawn from different bodies of studies, report the same sign of association, but neither establishes causation.

### Brews and Hunt (1999): Planning, Flexibility, and Time

Brews and Hunt analyzed the planning practices of 656 firms [7]. They report that formal planning and incrementalism both form part of good strategic planning, especially in unstable environments; that environment neither moderates the need for formal planning nor the direction of the planning-performance relationship, but does moderate planning capabilities and planning flexibility; that in unstable environments planning capabilities are far better developed and formal plans more amenable to change; and that the planning-performance relationship is moderated by planning duration, with at least four years of formal planning required before external performance associations are noted [7]. The duration finding matters for execution: a plan's benefit may depend on accumulated organizational skill at planning, not only on the content of any single plan.

### Grant (2003): Planning Systems at the Oil Majors

Grant conducted in-depth case studies of the planning systems of eight of the world's largest oil companies, firms facing volatile and unpredictable environments [8]. He found fundamental changes in the nature and role of strategic planning since the end of the 1970s, a process of planned emergence in which planning coordinated decentralized strategy formulation within demanding performance targets and corporate guidelines, and planning systems that fostered adaptation and responsiveness but showed limited innovation and analytical sophistication [8]. The study contradicts the presumption that environmental turbulence makes planning impossible, and it reports that the evidence from the corporate sector suggests reports of the demise of strategic planning are greatly exaggerated [8]. The sample is small and selected, so the result describes how a particular set of firms plan, not how planning performs across industries.

### Mankins and Steele (2005): The Strategy-to-Performance Gap

Mankins and Steele report research by the consultancy Marakon Associates, including a survey of executives, indicating that companies on average deliver 63 percent of the financial performance their strategies promise, and that more than one-third of the executives surveyed placed the figure below 50 percent [15]. The 37 percent average loss is attributed by the executives' importance ratings to specific breakdowns, for example 7.5 percent to inadequate resources and 5.2 percent to poor communication [15]. The retrieved text does not report the full sampling design, and the loss figures reflect managers' ratings, not measured counterfactuals, so the numbers are best read as the perceptions of informed practitioners. The article also reports that high-performing companies such as Cisco, Dow Chemical, and 3M boosted financial performance by 60 to 100 percent by following the rules, a claim that comes from the consultancy's own client experience and is not independently tested here [15].

### Siggelkow (2001): Liz Claiborne and Fit-Conserving Change

Siggelkow conducted a longitudinal case study of the fashion apparel company Liz Claiborne to illustrate his framework [12]. The company had built a system of tightly interconnected choices with high internal fit and, given the conditions of the time, high external fit: for example, it believed its merchandise had the greatest impact when presented as a collection, and it rejected orders from department stores unwilling to present the line that way [12]. In the early 1990s customer taste and demand, retailers' requirements, and available technology shifted; the company's choices remained internally consistent but became less appropriate to the environment, and a strong internal culture had created a belief in the organization's near invulnerability to environmental change [12]. Siggelkow concludes that incremental changes in a tightly coupled system rarely lead to the desired result, and that Liz Claiborne faced fit-conserving change [12]. A single case illustrates a mechanism; it does not measure how common the mechanism is.

### Burgelman (1994) and Kahneman and Lovallo (1993): Internal Selection and the Inside View

Burgelman's comparative study of Intel's strategic position in two semiconductor memory businesses and in microprocessors found that inertial forces caused Intel's distinctive competence to diverge from the evolving basis of competitive advantage in memory, and caused corporate strategy to diverge from the strategic actions of middle-level managers [13]. Intel's internal selection environment shifted the allocation of scarce manufacturing resources from memory to microprocessors before corporate strategy was officially changed [13]. Eisenhardt and Martin's reading of the same research identifies the simple margin-per-wafer-start production rule as the mechanism that determined resource allocation [5]. Kahneman and Lovallo's contribution is psychological. Their abstract reports that decision makers tend to treat problems as unique and isolate the current choice, and that overly optimistic forecasts result from the inside view, which anchors predictions on plans and scenarios [14]. Their paper illustrates the effect with an anecdote in which a project forecast was quietly dropped from debate and the project went on to completion some eight years later [14]. Together these sources describe a failure of feedback at two levels: the organization acts on a strategy that the plan has not yet recognized, and the plan itself is shaped by a forecasting bias.

## Implications

### For Senior Executives and Strategy Teams

The first implication is that the strategy document is a hypothesis about diagnosis, policy, and action, not a deliverable. The author's assessment is that executives should be able to state the diagnosis in plain language, name what the guiding policy rules out, and show which actions follow from the policy [16]. If the policy rules out nothing, Porter's argument applies: without trade-offs there would be no need for choice and thus no need for strategy [2]. The second implication concerns growth. Porter's warning that the growth imperative blurs uniqueness and reduces fit means that every proposed extension, new product, or new customer segment should be screened against the existing activity system and not only against its stand-alone economics [2]. Barney's four tests provide a second screen for capability investments: a proposed resource is worth building if it is valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and not easily substituted [3].

The third implication is about pace. Eisenhardt and Martin's distinction suggests different management styles for different markets [5]. In moderately dynamic markets, detailed analytic routines and long-horizon plans are suitable; in high-velocity markets the useful instruments are a few boundary and priority rules and fast learning from real-time knowledge [5]. The author's synthesis is that a firm operating in both kinds of market, as many diversified firms do, needs more than one planning style, and that applying a single corporate template to all businesses risks fitting none.

### For Boards and Owners

Boards cannot observe strategy directly; they observe plans, results, and the commentary between them. Mankins and Steele's venetian blinds pattern suggests a practical diagnostic: lay successive years' plans next to the realized results and see whether the forecasts repeat their shape [15]. The author's assessment is that a persistent pattern of hockey-stick forecasts followed by shortfalls is evidence about the planning process, not only about the market. The same article reports that executives rarely track performance against long-term plans, so a board that asks for that comparison obtains information management may not otherwise assemble [15]. Because Kahneman and Lovallo's inside-view bias applies to the plans boards receive, a board may also ask for an outside-view comparison, such as the track record of similar initiatives in similar firms [14]. For the resource commitments that follow a strategy, the connection to capital allocation is direct; the Resource Allocation and Capital Budgeting topic covers the mechanics.

The meta-analytic evidence supports requiring a planning process, with an important qualification. George and colleagues find a positive, moderate association between planning and performance, strongest for formal planning and for effectiveness, and Brews and Hunt find that associations with performance appear only after at least four years of formal planning [7][9]. The author's interpretation is that a board should evaluate a planning system over several cycles, not abandon it after one disappointing plan.

### For Corporate Centers and Business-Unit Leaders

Grant's planned emergence describes an operating arrangement that many multi-business firms use: the center sets demanding targets and clear guidelines, and the business units formulate their own strategies within them [8]. Mintzberg and Waters call the corresponding pattern a process or umbrella strategy and note that headquarters involvement in business strategies pulls them back toward the umbrella type [1]. Mankins and Steele's rules add specifics about the dialogue between center and unit: separate the debate about assumptions from the preparation of financial projections, use a shared framework such as profit pools, and discuss the level and timing of critical resource deployments early [15]. The Organizational Design and Decentralization topic covers where decision rights sit, and the Management Control Systems topic covers how targets and feedback are structured; this topic adds the requirement that the interaction be about assumptions and resources, not only about numbers.

### For Middle Managers and Operating Teams

Burgelman's Intel study and Mintzberg and Waters' unconnected strategies share an implication: strategy is partly made where resources are actually committed, which is often below the top [1][13]. Middle managers who allocate scarce capacity, choose which customers to serve, and decide which projects continue are enacting strategy whether or not it is documented. The author's synthesis is that leaders should make the simple prioritization rules explicit, as Intel's margin-per-wafer-start rule was, so that these everyday decisions point in a deliberate direction [5]. Siggelkow's case adds that people close to the work may be the first to see external-fit loss, so channels that carry such observations upward matter [12].

### For Investors and Analysts

For an outside analyst, the evidence suggests three kinds of reading. First, claims of competitive advantage can be tested against Barney's attributes and Porter's activity-fit logic: which resources are rare and hard to imitate, and which activities reinforce one another [2][3]. The Economic Moats topic develops the investor's version of this analysis. Second, a long record of plans compared with delivery is informative about management's forecasting discipline, in line with the venetian blinds diagnostic [15]. Third, a firm whose strategy is internally coherent and has been successful may be most exposed to fit-conserving change, because success reinforces the existing configuration [12]. This is an interpretation of Siggelkow's mechanism, not a tested valuation rule, and it should not be turned into a screening shortcut without further evidence.

### For Public-Sector and Nonprofit Leaders

George and colleagues report that the positive association between strategic planning and performance holds across private and public sectors and across U.S. and non-U.S. contexts, and that it is strongest when performance means effectiveness, that is, whether an organization achieves its goals [9]. The author's interpretation is that public and nonprofit leaders who adopt formal planning should expect the benefit to appear mainly as clearer goal attainment and should not promise efficiency gains as the justification. Mintzberg and Waters add a caution about organizations of experts: unconnected strategies tend to proliferate where professionals need considerable control over their own work, and many hospitals and universities appear to be little more than collections of personal strategies, with hardly any discernible central vision or umbrella [1]. In such settings the author's synthesis is that a planned strategy imposed from the center is the least likely type to take hold, and that an umbrella or process approach, in which leadership sets boundaries, targets, and the conditions for others to act, matches the structure of the work better [1][8].

### For the Design of the Strategy Process Itself

The evidence implies that the planning process is itself a managed system with inputs, owners, and a learning loop. Brews and Hunt's duration finding suggests that the process should be kept stable long enough to build skill, while their finding that formal plans in unstable environments are more amenable to change suggests that stability of process does not require rigidity of content [7]. Mankins and Steele's rules supply the operating cadence: agree on assumptions before projections, agree on resource timing before the plan is finalized, and compare results with the plan continuously so that assumptions are reset and resources reallocated [15]. Mintzberg and Waters' observation that emergent patterns often prompt managers to change their deliberate strategies implies a further design requirement, which is a routine for noticing and reviewing patterns that nobody planned [1]. The author's synthesis is that a strategy process with these elements treats the plan as the current best statement of intent and treats every review as a test of that statement against what the organization has actually done.

## Criticisms and Limits of the Evidence

Several limits apply to the claims above. First, the depth of reading differs by source. For Mintzberg and Waters, Porter, Barney, Eisenhardt and Martin, Siggelkow, and Mankins and Steele, substantial excerpts of the text were read; for Teece and colleagues, Miller and Cardinal, Brews and Hunt, Grant, George and colleagues, Rumelt (1991), McGahan and Porter, Burgelman, and Kahneman and Lovallo, claims here are limited to what the abstract, summary page, or short search-result excerpts report, and details of method beyond those pages were not verified. The Rumelt (Good Strategy/Bad Strategy) material comes from a published review, not from the book.

Second, the planning-performance literature measures planning in different ways, and its correlations do not show direction of causation: better-performing firms may plan more because they have slack to do so. George and colleagues acknowledge possible publication bias [9]. Third, variance decomposition studies apportion measured profit variance and do not identify causal mechanisms [10][11]. Fourth, the Mankins and Steele figures are executive perceptions from a consultancy, with sampling details not reported in the retrieved text [15]. Fifth, the case evidence (Liz Claiborne, Intel, the oil majors, the National Film Board) illustrates mechanisms in particular firms and cannot establish their frequency [1][8][12][13]. Finally, Eisenhardt and Martin's paper itself responds to the charge that dynamic capabilities are vague or tautological, which indicates that the concept's definition has been contested [5].
