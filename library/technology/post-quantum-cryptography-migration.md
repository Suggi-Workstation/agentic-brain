# Post-Quantum Cryptography Migration Requires Changing Protocols, Not Just Algorithms

Post-quantum cryptography migration replaces quantum-vulnerable public-key mechanisms in actual software, network handshakes, certificates, and signing workflows with deployable alternatives. The three finalized NIST standards supply a key-encapsulation mechanism and two signature families, but protecting a connection or artifact also requires compatible protocols, trust infrastructure, vendors, and tests; a hybrid key exchange alone does not make authentication post-quantum ([1] [2] [3] [10] [11]).

## Background

Public-key cryptography made it possible for parties without a prearranged secret to establish keys and verify signatures. In deployed systems, RSA, elliptic-curve key agreement, and elliptic-curve signatures appear in communication protocols, certificates, software update signatures, and hardware or service dependencies. A future cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer could defeat widely used public-key algorithms of those families; this is a conditional threat, not a claim that such a machine has already broken deployed systems. The joint CISA, NSA, and NIST migration factsheet identifies RSA, ECDH, and ECDSA as examples of products and protocols that will require change ([6]). This article addresses that engineering change, not the physics or resource estimates of quantum computing.

The time dimension differs for secrecy and authentication. An adversary can record traffic encrypted under vulnerable key establishment now and, if capable quantum hardware arrives while the contents are still valuable, try to recover it later. A signature on software or an archival record instead raises questions about how long its authenticity must remain verifiable and whether the verification chain can be renewed. CISA, NSA, and NIST explicitly call for risk assessment informed by critical data and its future exposure, rather than waiting for a single universal hardware date ([6]). The author's assessment is that these two lifetimes should be recorded separately in any migration inventory: a transport session's confidentiality horizon and an artifact's verification horizon are not interchangeable.

Earlier cryptographic transitions show why replacing a primitive in a specification is not the same as deploying it. Algorithms are embedded in libraries, firmware, protocols, interfaces, and certificates used by distinct operators. NIST's updated crypto-agility paper defines agility as the capability to replace and adapt algorithms across protocols, software, hardware, and infrastructure while preserving both security and operations ([5]). An organization that cannot identify its cryptographic dependencies cannot know what it has migrated. Its network edge might speak a new key exchange while an internal connector, hardware security module, or package-signing service retains an older signature scheme.

NIST began an international process to evaluate post-quantum public-key schemes and finalized its first three Federal Information Processing Standards in August 2024. FIPS 203 specifies ML-KEM for establishing shared secrets; FIPS 204 specifies ML-DSA signatures; FIPS 205 specifies stateless hash-based SLH-DSA signatures ([1] [2] [3]). They are standards for different functions, not three substitutes for one another. NIST continues to evaluate further schemes, and its publications list still identifies IR 8547's transition schedule as a draft as of this topic's preparation; a proposed transition milestone should not be represented here as a final universal deadline ([4] [14]). The engineering task can begin with finalized algorithms even while application profiles and additional standardization continue to evolve.

The sequence of protocol work also matters. An algorithm specification does not by itself tell a TLS client what bytes to send, how to derive a session key, or how to represent a signature in a certificate. RFC 9954, published in July 2026, describes a TLS 1.3 hybrid exchange construction; RFC 10024, published in August 2026, assigns specific hybrid ML-KEM and elliptic-curve groups; RFC 9881, published in October 2025, specifies ML-DSA algorithm identifiers and conventions for X.509 certificates and revocation lists ([9] [10] [11]). Their scopes differ: the hybrid exchange protects the establishment of a session secret, whereas certificate formats and verifiers govern identity assertions. Neither can be inferred from the other.

Deployment evidence shows different maturity levels. Google described experiments starting in 2016 and reported enabling ML-KEM by default in desktop Chrome's TLS 1.3 and QUIC in May 2024 ([13]). Cloudflare's October 2025 account reported widespread hybrid key agreement on its own client-facing traffic while describing public post-quantum certificates as a later, separate migration ([12]). These are operator observations, not a measurement of every network or an assertion that all end-to-end paths are protected. They illustrate that the deployment order may be key agreement first, authentication later, with legacy paths remaining between the two.

The domain boundary is practical. Quantum algorithms and quantum-hardware feasibility belong to the adjacent quantum-computing topic; general security governance is broader than this migration; and legal obligations vary by jurisdiction and deployment. Here the question is how to inventory cryptography, choose standardized primitives, make protocols and trust stores interoperable, and verify that the intended property survives the full path. A statement that a product "supports PQC" is too underspecified to answer that question without naming the role, algorithm, version, peer, and negotiated outcome ([6] [7] [9] [10]).

## Core Concepts

### Separate key establishment from signatures

ML-KEM is a key-encapsulation mechanism: one party publishes an encapsulation key; the peer generates a ciphertext and shared secret; the holder of the corresponding private decapsulation key derives the same secret. The secret may then feed a symmetric-key protocol. FIPS 203 specifies ML-KEM-512, ML-KEM-768, and ML-KEM-1024, in increasing order of security strength and decreasing performance; NIST SP 800-227 explains the relevant KEM definitions and application considerations ([1] [4]). A KEM is not a replacement for a digital signature. If a server negotiates an ML-KEM-based exchange but still authenticates through classical certificates, a future adversary's ability to forge classical signatures remains a distinct concern. The author's assessment is that migration records should have separate columns for confidentiality and authentication.

ML-DSA in FIPS 204 generates and verifies signatures; SLH-DSA in FIPS 205 is a stateless hash-based signature alternative with a different construction ([2] [3]). Signing a firmware release, a package, or a certificate introduces dependencies on public-key encoding, verification libraries, hardware key custody, and the lifetime of the signed artifact. RFC 9881 specifies ML-DSA's use in Internet X.509 public-key infrastructure, including identifiers and public-key and signature representations; that document specifies the pure ML-DSA variant for its certificate conventions ([11]). A signer's upgrade alone does not help if devices cannot parse or validate the resulting chain. Nor does adopting post-quantum signatures for certificates mean the negotiated session key is post-quantum protected.

There is no universal "strongest" selection independent of context. FIPS 203 Table 3 lists a 1,184-byte encapsulation key and 1,088-byte ciphertext for ML-KEM-768, compared with an 800-byte key and 768-byte ciphertext for ML-KEM-512. FIPS 204 Table 2 lists a 1,952-byte public key and 3,309-byte signature for ML-DSA-65 ([1] [2]). Those byte counts are primitive sizes, not a forecast of an entire handshake or certificate chain: protocol frames, multiple certificates, compression, and network conditions add their own costs. The author's assessment is that a deployment should choose a profile only after measuring its real message sizes and throughput on the target path, not by comparing a single signature in isolation.

### Inventory uses, owners, and lifetimes

A cryptographic inventory should identify where algorithms are used and what they protect. The joint factsheet calls for discovery across network protocols, server and endpoint applications and libraries, firmware and software updates, and CI/CD cryptographic dependencies; it cautions that tools may miss cryptography embedded inside vendor products and recommends asking suppliers directly ([6]). NIST's NCCoE discovery guide, a preliminary draft, demonstrates multiple discovery tools and describes correlating key, certificate, application, and network evidence rather than treating a simple list of algorithms as a complete roadmap ([7]). An inventory is a starting observation with known blind spots, not proof that every asset has been found.

For each use, an implementation-oriented record can hold the algorithm and parameter set, protocol and version, endpoint or artifact, generating and verifying party, key and certificate owner, hardware or library dependency, supported peers, and replacement route. This is the author's proposed schema, derived from the discovery and risk-prioritization tasks in the joint factsheet and NCCoE work ([6] [7]). Record the protected data's confidentiality lifetime or signature's verification lifetime, because a public website session and a long-retained encrypted archive present different transition priorities. Tie discovered network exchanges back to owners: a captured TLS cipher suite cannot alone identify a hidden signing operation inside a vendor's updater ([6] [7]).

Inventory methods are complementary. A network scanner can observe advertised or negotiated groups on reachable paths but misses dormant applications and offline signatures. Static analysis may locate API calls but not every runtime provider choice; library manifests identify package dependencies but not necessarily which component executes a cryptographic function. Certificate stores reveal issuers, algorithms, validity periods, and chains without proving that all devices accept replacements. These are reasoned limitations of each observation method, consistent with the NCCoE guide's multi-tool approach and the joint warning about undiscoverable embedded code ([6] [7]). Reconcile observations with vendor attestations and transaction tests; do not report a percentage of completed migration using an inventory whose denominator remains unknown.

### Hybrid deployment and downgrade boundaries

A post-quantum/traditional hybrid key exchange combines outputs from algorithms resting on different security assumptions. RFC 9954 describes a TLS 1.3 construction intended to preserve the session secret if at least one component remains secure, and treats the hybrid combination as a single negotiated key-exchange method within the handshake ([9]). RFC 10024 then defines X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768, and SecP384r1MLKEM1024 as specific TLS 1.3 groups ([10]). A valid hybrid construction is not obtained merely by putting two unconnected exchanges beside each other; RFC 10024 cautions that its security analysis relies on the TLS 1.3 transcript and cannot automatically be transferred to another protocol ([10]).

Hybridization is a transition mechanism, not a substitute for checking the result. A client may offer a hybrid group while the peer negotiates a classical fallback; an intermediate device might reject or alter a large handshake; and a separate proxy-to-origin connection may use a different suite. The author's assessment is that tests should capture the negotiated group and authentication chain at each real hop and treat a fallback as an explicit exception rather than silently counting the offered capability as protection. RFC 9954's binding of exchange components to TLS's negotiation and transcript is a protocol-level answer to mixing errors, but it does not guarantee that every deployment configures a hybrid group, disallows inappropriate fallback, or updates certificate signatures ([9] [10] [11]).

A conservative transition can retain traditional security against currently known attacks while adding a post-quantum component, provided the combination is implemented as specified. The fact that classical and post-quantum parts serve as independent protections does not mean all implementation risks vanish: bugs in parsing, randomness, key storage, or side-channel resistance can defeat an otherwise sound primitive. FIPS 203 requires safeguards for generated randomness and private decapsulation material, and NIST's updated crypto-agility guidance addresses changing algorithms without sacrificing operations ([1] [5]). The author's assessment is to test both the positive path and unsupported-peer behavior, including how the system surfaces any downgrade or failure to its operator.

### PKI, signatures, and long-lived artifacts

Public-key infrastructure binds identities to public keys using signed certificates and chains of trust. Moving a server or client from RSA or ECDSA credentials to ML-DSA affects certificate issuance, revocation lists, validation libraries, root programs, trust stores, hardware signing modules, and possibly the size of messages that transport chains. RFC 9881 supplies conventions for ML-DSA keys and signatures in X.509 certificates and certificate revocation lists; it does not, by itself, imply that every issuer, browser, or device accepts such certificates today ([11]). The author's assessment is that migration must test issuance and validation across each participating trust boundary, including archived objects that may be verified years later.

The certificate chain is not the only signature surface. A build pipeline may sign software packages, firmware images, container artifacts, or timestamps, and offline verifiers may have longer replacement cycles than web clients. CISA's inventory guidance explicitly includes firmware and software-update assets and code dependencies in development pipelines ([6]). A long-lived artifact also demands an archival validation design: record the signing algorithm and verification software, preserve evidence needed to validate the chain, and define a renewal method if the old signature is later considered unsafe. These last design steps are the author's operational inference from the signature function and the lifetime problem; they are not a statement that a particular archived signature has already failed ([2] [3] [6] [11]).

### Crypto agility means controlled change

An abstraction layer may help applications switch algorithms, but the protocol on the wire and the verifying peer still determine interoperability. NIST's CSWP 39 update defines crypto agility across software, hardware, firmware, protocols, and infrastructures; a library API alone is therefore narrower than full operational agility ([5]). Configuration should make algorithm selection observable, version-controlled, testable, and reversible where policy permits. The author's assessment is that a rollback plan must distinguish an availability recovery from a security downgrade: restoring service by reverting a critical path to a vulnerable key exchange can reinstate the long-horizon confidentiality exposure migration was intended to reduce ([5] [6] [9]).

The strongest hidden dependency often lies outside an application team's control: a managed service terminates TLS, an appliance fixes a firmware algorithm, or an HSM lacks a needed mechanism. The joint agencies advise explicitly discussing vendors' migration and testing roadmaps and include suppliers in the quantum-vulnerable inventory ([6]). A staged rollout can begin with supported endpoints, retain observed negotiation telemetry, and expand only when critical peers and artifact verifiers pass functional and performance tests. This is an engineering sequence inferred from the agencies' roadmap and NCCoE interoperability guidance, not evidence that any one sequence suits every organization ([6] [8]).

## Evidence

### Standards supply distinct primitives and protocol bindings

The strongest direct evidence for implementable building blocks is normative rather than a claim of field adoption. NIST's FIPS 203, 204, and 205, each finalized in August 2024, define an ML-KEM, ML-DSA, and SLH-DSA respectively; SP 800-227, finalized in September 2025, elaborates how KEMs establish secrets used with symmetric cryptography ([1] [2] [3] [4]). RFC 9954 provides a TLS 1.3 hybrid construction, RFC 10024 specifies three ML-KEM/ECDHE hybrid groups, and RFC 9881 defines ML-DSA representations for X.509 PKI ([9] [10] [11]). Method: compare the function and specified interfaces of these primary standards. Finding: migrating a TLS connection's key agreement and migrating the signed identity it authenticates are distinct implementation tasks. Limitation: publication of a standard establishes a defined method, not universal product support or secure configuration.

### NCCoE cryptographic-discovery demonstrations

NIST NCCoE's December 2023 SP 1800-38B preliminary draft reports a consortium's cryptographic-discovery workstream. Its method combines tools and representative use cases to find quantum-vulnerable public-key usage across products, connections, certificates, and applications, then places findings into an inventory relevant to risk assessment ([7]). The guide describes network discovery, static analysis, and other platform-specific observations and explains that discovered dependencies can exceed what an organization initially expects. Its useful empirical contribution is a demonstrated multi-tool architecture and concrete test workflows rather than a validated estimate of any enterprise's complete crypto footprint. As a preliminary draft based on selected collaborator environments, it cannot prove that a scanner finds cryptography hidden in every proprietary device; the agencies' separate factsheet explicitly flags that limitation ([6] [7]).

This case changes the practical question from "which approved algorithm should we buy?" to "what depends on the old one, and how would we know?" An organization can test an inventory against its own signing and networking use cases: whether a library call can be traced to an application, whether a certificate is bound to a specific endpoint, and whether a vendor's undocumented cryptographic module appears at all. The author's assessment is that sampling and owner sign-off should accompany automated discovery because a tool's empty result is not evidence of no cryptography. That assessment is motivated by the observed variety of discovery surfaces and stated blind spots, not by a measured false-negative rate ([6] [7]).

### NCCoE interoperability and performance experiments

The paired SP 1800-38C preliminary draft tested interoperability and performance of pre-standardized post-quantum and hybrid implementations in laboratory TLS 1.3, QUIC, SSH, X.509, and hardware contexts. For TLS, its method tested successful connections between collaborator clients and servers for supported algorithmic profiles, then measured handshake throughput or time under specified network conditions ([8]). The reported tests show both that compatible implementations could exchange keys in controlled settings and that hybrid key exchanges can reduce a heavily loaded server's maximum handshake throughput. One s2n-tls experiment reported a hybrid Kyber/P-256 handshake a few milliseconds slower than classical P-256 under the simulated conditions; the guide also discusses measurements under round-trip delay and packet loss ([8]).

The limits are material. The tests used draft protocol profiles and pre-standard versions of candidate algorithms before FIPS 203 and the later RFC 9954 and RFC 10024 existed. Their absolute timings and compatibility matrix cannot be transferred to a current production stack merely by renaming Kyber to ML-KEM. They do identify test dimensions worth retaining: handshake success between independent implementations, CPU-limited throughput, end-to-end latency with realistic round trips and loss, and the separate cost of authentication and certificate processing ([8] [9] [10]). The author's assessment is that performance acceptance criteria should be tied to target devices and full protocol paths, with the old and proposed profiles measured under the same workload.

### Internet operators' deployment observations

Google reported that Chrome's desktop TLS 1.3 and QUIC enabled ML-KEM by default in May 2024 and that Google servers also supported it; its account describes staged experiments dating to 2016 and preparatory work such as inventory, key rotation, and end-to-end testing ([13]). Method: a deployer's published record of implementation and rollout, not a randomized independent audit. Finding: a major browser and service operator could ship the key-establishment component before internet-wide post-quantum certificate adoption. Limitation: this record does not establish what cryptography every client, origin, or signed artifact uses; the visible negotiation on one path cannot certify another path ([13]).

Cloudflare's October 2025 state-of-the-internet report, drawing on traffic it handles, said roughly half of human-initiated requests at its edge used post-quantum key agreement by late 2025 while origin-server support was much lower. It described public post-quantum authentication certificates as a second, less mature migration ([12]). Method: an operator's measurement on its own network and discussion of its protocol deployments. Finding: negotiated hybrid key agreement can scale on some client-facing traffic while certificate adoption and edge-to-origin connections lag. Limitations: Cloudflare traffic is not the entire internet; client-to-edge protection does not imply edge-to-origin protection; and key-agreement telemetry cannot establish signature security. The author's assessment is that any adoption metric must specify both the sampled population and which cryptographic property it measures ([12]).

The independent sources converge on a narrower conclusion than "the internet is post-quantum secure." Standards now define implementable primitives and increasingly specific protocol bindings; laboratory work demonstrates potential interoperability and performance challenges; and two large operators report partial deployments of key agreement ([1] [2] [8] [9] [10] [12] [13]). None of these observations demonstrates complete migration of all application signatures, devices, vendors, and internal hops. A robust migration claim must therefore be falsifiable at each measured endpoint and artifact rather than inferred from a product feature or traffic aggregate. This concluding judgment is the author's synthesis of the distinct methods and limitations above.

## Implications

### For application and network engineers

Start with the highest-impact cryptographic uses, not a wholesale search-and-replace of strings named "RSA". Trace where each key is generated, negotiated, rotated, stored, and verified. If a service calls a library behind a load balancer, inspect the client-to-edge and edge-to-origin handshakes separately; if a peer still offers only a classical group, record the negotiated exception. Build a test matrix of peer implementations, TLS and other protocol versions, supported hybrid groups, certificate algorithms, packet sizes, error paths, and proxy behavior. RFC 10024's named TLS groups and RFC 9881's PKI representations provide concrete protocol targets, while NCCoE's controlled experiments demonstrate why interoperability has to be measured rather than presumed ([8] [10] [11]). These steps are the author's engineering application of the cited standards.

Prioritize confidentiality uses by how long captured data stays sensitive. A long-lived confidential archive sent over a vulnerable channel warrants attention even if the expected arrival date of capable quantum hardware is uncertain; a service handling only short-lived public data has a different exposure. The joint factsheet explicitly points to harvested data and criticality in prioritization ([6]). For integrity, identify signing keys and verifiers for firmware, software distributions, archives, and certificate chains; ensure the verifier migration does not lag the signer, and test rotation and recovery. The author's assessment is that prioritization should report which risk is reduced at each stage -- future decryption, future forgery, or present-day availability -- rather than claiming all three have been solved by a single KEM rollout ([1] [2] [6] [11]).

A test of the positive path is not enough. Confirm that peers reject malformed inputs safely, handle unsupported groups predictably, log actual negotiation, and preserve a way to diagnose unexpected fallback without treating it as success. FIPS 203's treatment of valid encodings and private key material and RFC 9954's transcript-bound construction emphasize correct implementation, not merely choosing a standard name ([1] [9]). The author's assessment is to separate staging and production policy: a lab may allow controlled classical fallback to establish compatibility, but production reporting must show where it still occurs. Document the reason and remediation owner for every exception rather than concealing gaps inside an aggregate "PQC enabled" label.

### For PKI and platform operators

Certificate issuance and verification are migration projects of their own. An operator should map which issuers, relying parties, HSMs, certificate stores, and revocation-processing components accept the target ML-DSA representation, then test an end-to-end chain. RFC 9881 specifies the necessary X.509 conventions but cannot guarantee an installed trust anchor or an application parser has been upgraded ([11]). Measure certificate and signature sizes at the actual path: FIPS 204's primitive-size table gives a baseline, while chains and transport framing determine operational overhead ([2]). The author's assessment is to treat a successful signing operation without independent verification on the oldest required client as an incomplete deployment.

Protocol upgrade schedules and artifact-renewal schedules may differ. A short-lived TLS certificate can be issued afresh after a change; a firmware image on an offline device may need an upgrade path for its boot-time verifier before it can safely consume a newly signed update. CISA's factsheet specifically directs discovery toward firmware and software-update assets, and NIST's crypto-agility guidance includes firmware and infrastructure rather than only application source code ([5] [6]). The author's assessment is that operators should maintain a signed-artifact registry linking each class of signature to a verifier population and retention period. This makes the risk of a stranded verifier explicit without pretending that the joint agencies prescribe that particular registry format.

### For suppliers and infrastructure owners

Ask a supplier for testable descriptions, not a "quantum-safe" badge. Which standardized algorithm and parameter set are implemented? Does the product support the final FIPS variant or an older experimental one? Which protocols and certificate formats work, and how are keys generated, protected, backed up, and rotated? What happens when a peer cannot negotiate the preferred hybrid group? The joint agencies call for discussing vendors' roadmaps and embedded cryptography, while the NCCoE drafts show both discovery gaps and the need for peer-to-peer interoperability tests ([6] [7] [8]). The author's assessment is to tie procurement acceptance to a reproducible negotiation or signature-verification test in the purchaser's environment, avoiding an unverified claim of end-to-end coverage.

Infrastructure with long replacement cycles, constrained processors, or unusual networking conditions deserves a separate pilot. A message-size increase can affect fragmentation or path middleboxes; a higher handshake cost can lower a busy server's connection throughput; offline HSMs may impose proprietary integration limits. FIPS 203 and 204 quantify primitive sizes, and NCCoE's pre-standard laboratory tests investigate connection and hardware scenarios, but neither supplies a universal penalty factor for all installations ([1] [2] [8]). The author's assessment is to measure on representative devices and routes, then choose rollout thresholds for latency, CPU, memory, and failure rate before adding more peers. This makes availability a constraint on the transition, not an excuse to ignore confidentiality or authenticity.

### For researchers and decision-makers

Do not collapse "standardized," "implemented," "negotiated," and "fully migrated" into one adoption number. A standard defines a scheme; a library implements it; two peers may negotiate it; a complete system still depends on certificate and artifact authentication and on every relevant hop. The FIPS and RFC documents establish the first part, while the Google and Cloudflare reports give bounded implementation and deployment observations ([1] [2] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13]). The author's assessment is that a useful migration dashboard should distinguish algorithm inventory coverage, peer compatibility, negotiated key exchange, post-quantum signing coverage, and residual classical exceptions. Each metric has a different denominator and a different security meaning.

Revisit guidance as standards and implementations mature. NIST's updated CSWP 39 offers a final operational crypto-agility framework, while NIST's listed transition report IR 8547 remains a draft; the distinction between final specifications and proposed timelines matters ([5] [14]). The author's assessment is to record document version and publication status with each technical decision, and to retest after changing the cryptographic library or protocol profile. This guards against retaining experimental encodings after final standards arrive or applying a laboratory performance number to unrelated hardware. The single worst failure would be to declare migration complete on the basis of offered hybrid key exchange while continuing to expose long-lived secrets through a classical hop or relying on unupgraded verifiers. Inventory at the use level, measure negotiation at every boundary, and verify signatures on actual recipients are the practical counterchecks ([6] [8] [10] [11] [12]).
