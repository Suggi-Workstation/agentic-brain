# AI Inference Systems -- Serving Trained Models Is Bounded by Memory Traffic, Scheduling, and Measurement Boundaries More Than by Peak Compute

An AI inference system turns a trained model into a service that answers requests within latency, cost, and energy limits. For large language models (LLMs) the work splits into a parallel prefill phase and a token-by-token decode phase that rereads the model weights and a growing key-value (KV) cache at every step, so memory bandwidth, memory capacity, batching policy, and scheduling often decide performance more than peak arithmetic throughput [2][5][9]. Continuous batching, paged KV memory, prefill-decode disaggregation, quantization, and speculative decoding each attack one of these bottlenecks, and measured energy per request varies by an order of magnitude depending on utilization and on what the measurement includes [4][6][7][8][9][11].

## Background

Training produces a model once; inference runs it every time a user asks something. Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell report three operator estimates of how that asymmetry shows up in practice: AWS estimated that inference makes up 80 to 90 percent of machine-learning cloud computing demand, a 2021 Meta publication attributed about one third of its internal machine-learning carbon footprint to inference, and a 2022 Google study attributed 60 percent of its machine-learning energy use to inference against 40 percent to training [10]. These are second-hand figures reported through that paper, but they explain why inference became a design problem of its own rather than an afterthought to training.

Google's Tensor Processing Unit is a documented example of a purpose-built inference accelerator that came from that pressure. Jouppi and colleagues describe a 2013 projection inside Google that people using voice search for three minutes a day with speech-recognition neural networks would require Google's datacenters to double to meet the computation demand [1]. Google designed, verified, built, and deployed the Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) in 15 months; it has served inference in Google datacenters since 2015 [1]. The chip centered on a 65,536-unit 8-bit multiply-accumulate matrix unit with a peak of 92 trillion operations per second and 28 MiB of software-managed on-chip memory [1]. Two findings from that work recur in the later papers cited here. First, four of the six production networks studied, which together represented 95 percent of Google's datacenter inference demand, were limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic [1]. Second, the binding constraint was a response-time limit, not throughput: application developers required a 99th-percentile response time of 7 ms for one multilayer perceptron workload, and that limit forced the contemporary CPU and GPU to run small batches far below their peak throughput [1].

Jouppi and colleagues also argued that inferences per second is a poor metric, because the same TPU ran one production network at 360,000 inferences per second and another at 4,700, and they called for a benchmark suite [1]. The MLPerf Inference benchmark answered that call in 2019. Its designers, drawn from more than 30 organizations, reported that over 100 organizations were building inference chips and that deployed systems spanned three orders of magnitude in power and five in performance [3]. MLPerf defined four scenarios (single-stream, multistream, server, and offline), fixed quality targets relative to a reference model, and made latency-bounded throughput the headline measure for datacenter inference [3].

The Transformer architecture made the memory problem sharper. Shazeer showed in 2019 that incremental Transformer decoding, where each new token must attend to every earlier token, is slow because each step reloads the large key and value tensors, and he proposed multi-query attention to shrink them [2]. As models grew past hundreds of billions of parameters, a cluster of systems papers between 2022 and 2024 defined the modern serving stack. Pope and colleagues analyzed how to partition 500-billion-parameter models across 64 TPU v4 chips for different latency and throughput targets [5]. Yu and colleagues introduced iteration-level scheduling in the Orca serving system [4]. Kwon and colleagues introduced PagedAttention and the vLLM engine to manage KV cache memory like operating-system pages [8]. Leviathan, Kalman, and Matias introduced speculative decoding, which produces several tokens per large-model step without changing the output distribution [6]. Frantar and colleagues showed that GPTQ could compress 175-billion-parameter models to 3 or 4 bits per weight with small accuracy loss [7]. Zhong and colleagues showed with DistServe that running prefill and decode on separate GPUs raises the request rate a cluster can serve within latency targets [9].

From 2025 the field moved toward measuring deployed systems rather than only benchmarks. Elsworth and colleagues published per-prompt energy, emissions, and water figures measured across Google's production fleet for Gemini Apps, which they describe as the first such measurement in a production environment, and argued that disagreement about what to include had made published estimates for similar tasks differ by an order of magnitude [11]. MLPerf added a DeepSeek-R1 reasoning benchmark in version 5.1 and, in version 6.0 (April 2026), an interactive scenario for it with tight per-token latency limits and the suite's first standard for speculative decoding [12][13]. Version 6.1 (September 2026) added end-to-end retrieval-augmented generation and edge agentic workloads, and MLCommons stated that a new API-centric benchmark, MLPerf Endpoints, will replace MLPerf Inference for datacenter systems [14]. In the author's reading, the trajectory runs from single-chip, single-model acceleration toward multi-node, multi-model services measured end to end.

## Core Concepts

### Prefill and decode are two different workloads

A generative Transformer request runs in two phases. In prefill, the model processes all input tokens at once and produces the first output token; in decode, it generates the remaining tokens one at a time, each step depending on the previous one [5]. Orca calls these the initiation and increment phases [4]. An N-parameter decoder-only model needs about 2N matrix-multiply floating-point operations per token in the forward pass, so compute per token is roughly fixed by model size [5]. What differs is how much data must move for that compute. Zhong and colleagues found that prefill of a 512-token prompt on a 13-billion-parameter model nearly saturates an NVIDIA A100's compute, while a decode step does similar memory input and output for only one new token and is therefore memory-bandwidth bound [9].

The phases also map to different user-visible metrics. Time to first token (TTFT) is the prefill duration; time per output token (TPOT) is the average time for each later token [9]. A chatbot needs a fast first token, while TPOT matters only until it exceeds human reading speed, which DistServe puts at about 250 words per minute; document summarization emphasizes low TPOT because outputs are long [9]. This suggests that a single serving configuration cannot be optimal for all requests, because the cost of a request depends on its input length, output length, and latency target.

### Arithmetic intensity: why decoding is starved for data

Accelerator performance depends on arithmetic intensity: how many operations a chip performs per byte fetched from memory. The original TPU needed about 1,350 operations per byte of weight memory to reach peak, compared with 13 for the contemporary Haswell CPU and 9 for the K80 GPU, which is why most production workloads sat on the memory-bound slope of its roofline [1]. Shazeer quantified the same problem for incremental attention: across n decoding steps, the ratio of memory access to arithmetic is on the order of n/d + 1/b, where n is sequence length, d is model dimension, and b is batch size [2]. When the batch is one or the sequence length approaches the model dimension, that ratio approaches one, while modern GPU and TPU hardware has roughly two orders of magnitude more compute capacity than memory bandwidth [2].

The 1/b term can be reduced by batching more sequences together, because one read of the weights then serves many requests [2]. Orca states the same principle as a design rule: perform as much computation as possible per round of model-parameter reads, because reading parameters from GPU global memory is a major bottleneck [4]. The n/d term comes from the KV cache, which is different for every request and is not amortized by batching [2]. Batch-1 generation on a 175-billion-parameter model is dominated by matrix-vector products that are limited by memory bandwidth, which is why reducing the bytes per weight speeds it up even when the arithmetic is unchanged [7].

### The KV cache is the dominant dynamic memory object

During decoding the model stores the attention keys and values of all previous tokens so it does not recompute them at each step; this store is the KV cache [11]. It grows with every generated token and must stay in accelerator memory. For OPT-13B, Kwon and colleagues calculate 800 KB of KV cache per token, or up to 1.6 GB for a single 2,048-token request; when serving a 13-billion-parameter model on a 40 GB A100, weights take about 65 percent of memory and the KV cache about 30 percent [8]. At larger scale the cache can exceed the model itself: for a 500-billion-parameter-class multi-head attention model with batch 512 and context length 2,048, Pope and colleagues compute a 3 TB KV cache, three times the size of the parameters [5]. Kwon and colleagues add a hardware trend: from the A100 to the H100, peak FLOPS more than doubled while maximum GPU memory stayed at 80 GB, so memory capacity becomes an increasing bottleneck [8].

Model architecture changes can shrink the cache. Multi-query attention keeps separate query heads but shares one key head and one value head across them, cutting KV size by a factor equal to the number of heads [2]. In Shazeer's translation experiments, amortized decoder time fell from 46 to 3.8 microseconds per token with a small quality loss (development-set BLEU 26.7 versus 26.5) [2]. Pope and colleagues report that multi-query attention, combined with a layout that shards the cache over the batch dimension, allows up to 32 times longer context lengths than multi-head attention under the same memory budget [5].

### Batching and iteration-level scheduling

Early serving systems scheduled whole requests: a batch started together and finished together. Yu and colleagues showed the cost of that design for generative models: a request that finishes early cannot return, and a request that arrives late must wait for the whole batch to complete [4]. Orca instead schedules at the granularity of a single model iteration, so a new request waits at most one iteration and a finished request returns immediately [4]. Because requests in a batch have different lengths, Orca uses selective batching: it batches the non-attention operations by flattening all tokens into one matrix and splits the batch only for attention, which has no weights to reuse [4]. DistServe describes current serving systems as using this technique under the name continuous batching [9].

Batching remains a latency-throughput trade-off. Pope and colleagues note that smaller batches lower latency but reduce model FLOPS utilization (MFU) and raise cost per token, and that the minimum generation latency they achieved was three times lower than the latency at batch size 512 [5]. Continuous batching also mixes a newly arriving prefill with ongoing decode steps in the same iteration, so a long prompt can delay every decoding request in the batch; DistServe calls this prefill-decode interference [9].

### Paged KV memory management

Before vLLM, serving systems reserved a contiguous memory region for each request sized for its maximum possible length. Kwon and colleagues measured that only 20.4 to 38.2 percent of KV cache memory in such systems held actual token states; the rest was lost to reservations for future tokens and to internal and external fragmentation [8]. PagedAttention stores the KV cache in fixed-size blocks that need not be contiguous, with a block table mapping each request's logical blocks to physical blocks, the way an operating system maps virtual pages to physical frames [8]. Blocks can be shared with reference counts and copy-on-write, so parallel samples and beam-search candidates share a prompt, and a common system prompt can be stored once [8]. When memory runs out, vLLM evicts all blocks of a request together and either swaps them to CPU memory or recomputes them later [8]. With this design, waste is limited to the last partially filled block, and memory utilization reached 96.3 percent [8].

### Parallelism, interconnects, and disaggregation

A model too large or too slow for one accelerator is partitioned. Pope and colleagues compare partitioning layouts on TPU v4, whose chips have 32 GiB of high-bandwidth memory at 1,200 GB/s and connect in a 3D torus at 270 GB/s; they find that communication time falls more slowly than compute time as chips are added and becomes the bottleneck at high chip counts [5]. Zhong and colleagues distinguish intra-operator parallelism, which splits each layer across GPUs and cuts execution time but needs fast links such as NVLink, from inter-operator (pipeline) parallelism, which splits layers into stages and raises the request rate a group of GPUs can sustain [9].

Disaggregation goes further by placing prefill and decode on different GPUs, so each phase gets its own batch size and parallelism [9]. Pope and colleagues had already described pipelining a batch-1 prefill server into a batch-64 decoding server for low-latency use [5]. The cost is moving the KV cache between phases: for OPT-66B, a 512-token request carries about 1.13 GB of KV cache, so 10 requests per second require about 11.3 GB/s, roughly 90 Gbps, of bandwidth between prefill and decode instances [9]. DistServe places the two phases where that traffic can use fast intra-node links [9].

### Quantization and reduced precision

Reduced numerical precision cuts both the bytes moved and the energy per operation. The original TPU used 8-bit integers partly because, citing Dally, an 8-bit integer multiply takes about 6 times less energy and area than a 16-bit floating-point multiply [1]. For LLMs, weight-only quantization targets the memory-bound decode phase. Pope and colleagues report that int8 weights reduced PaLM 540B generation time at batch 64 from 36.9 to 28.5 ms per token without noticeable quality loss [5]. GPTQ uses approximate second-order information about each layer to round weights to 3 or 4 bits: on OPT-175B, 4-bit GPTQ raised WikiText2 perplexity from 8.34 to 8.37, while simple round-to-nearest 4-bit quantization raised it to 10.54 and 3-bit round-to-nearest collapsed [7]. A 3-bit OPT-175B fits on one 80 GB A100 where 16-bit inference needs five, and per-token latency at batch size 1 fell from 230 ms on five GPUs to 71 ms on one [7]. Frantar and colleagues state the limits: the speedup comes from reduced memory movement rather than fewer computations, their kernels target batch-1 generation, and activations stay at 16 bits [7].

### Speculative decoding

Speculative decoding uses spare compute during memory-bound decoding. A small draft model proposes several tokens; the large target model scores all of them in one parallel pass; each draft token is accepted with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)), where p and q are the target and draft probabilities, and the first rejected token is resampled from an adjusted distribution [6]. Leviathan and colleagues prove that the output distribution is identical to sampling from the target model alone, and every target pass yields at least one token [6]. If alpha is the average acceptance rate and gamma the number of draft tokens, the expected tokens per target pass is (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / (1 - alpha) [6]. The method trades extra arithmetic, wasted on rejected tokens, for fewer serial target steps, so it helps only when compute is available [6]. MLPerf Inference 6.0 standardized one specific form: the DeepSeek-R1 interactive scenario permits EAGLE-style decoding with the model's official multi-token-prediction head, three speculative steps, and a ban on techniques that inflate acceptance rates [12].

### Latency targets and goodput

Because users experience tail latency, inference systems are judged on throughput within latency limits. DistServe defines per-GPU goodput as the maximum request rate a system serves while meeting a service-level objective (SLO) attainment goal, such as 90 percent of requests within TTFT and TPOT limits, divided by the number of GPUs [9]. MLPerf's server scenario sends queries with Poisson-distributed arrivals and reports the highest rate that keeps tail latency within the bound, and it requires enough queries to support a statistically confident tail estimate: 262,742 queries for a 99th-percentile bound [3]. The 2026 LLM tests encode current interactivity targets. The GPT-OSS 120B interactive scenario requires 99th-percentile TTFT of at most 2.0 seconds and TPOT of at most 15 ms, against 3.0 seconds and 80 ms in the server scenario [12].

## How Workload Shape Moves the Bottleneck

The table below is the author's synthesis of the cited sources. Each row pairs a workload shape with the constraint the sources identify as dominant and the techniques they show addressing it.

| Workload shape | Dominant constraint | Levers shown in the sources |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Interactive generation at small batch | Weight reads per token (memory bandwidth) | Weight quantization [5][7]; speculative decoding [6]; multi-query attention [2] |
| Large batch with long contexts | KV cache capacity and bandwidth | Multi-query attention and batch-sharded cache [2][5]; paged KV memory, swap, recompute [8] |
| Long prompts with strict first-token targets | Prefill compute and prefill-decode interference | Prefill-decode disaggregation [9]; chunked prefill as a trade-off [9] |
| Offline batch processing | Throughput and utilization | Maximize batch size; latency unconstrained [3][5] |
| Very large models across many chips | Interconnect communication | Partitioning layout chosen per phase [5]; inter-operator parallelism at high request rates [9] |
| Multi-step pipelines (retrieval, agents) | Several models and growing conversational history | End-to-end measurement of the whole pipeline [14] |

Hardware alone does not fix these constraints. MLPerf's designers observed that software frameworks and runtimes determine how much of a chip's potential is realized, and that some runtimes are built for particular hardware [3]. Google attributes part of its serving efficiency to its XLA compiler, Pallas kernels, and Pathways runtime, which compile model code written in JAX for its accelerators [11]. The original TPU used deterministic execution because it matched a 99th-percentile latency target better than the caches, out-of-order execution, and other average-case features of CPUs and GPUs [1].

Production services also include components that benchmarks omit. The Gemini measurement counts supporting models for scoring, ranking, classification, and prompt routing alongside the main LLM [11]. It counts idle machines held in reserve for traffic spikes and failover, which used 10 percent of per-prompt energy, and Google reports moving models among machines in near real time according to demand to limit that idle capacity [11]. DistServe notes a reliability risk in disaggregation: when one decode instance serves many prefill instances, a single fault can spread to many requests [9]. Observability in the Gemini study rests on per-machine power-supply telemetry mapped to job and machine identifiers [11]; the author's reading is that measuring a serving fleet is itself an engineering task.

## Evidence

### Production accelerator measurement: the TPU (2017)

Jouppi and colleagues compared the TPU with a contemporary Intel Haswell CPU and NVIDIA K80 GPU on six production networks (multilayer perceptrons, LSTMs, and convolutional networks) that represented 95 percent of Google's datacenter inference demand [1]. The method combined measured throughput under each application's real latency limit with roofline analysis and a performance model. The TPU was on average about 15 to 30 times faster than the GPU or CPU, with 30 to 80 times higher operations per watt [1]. Under the 7 ms 99th-percentile limit for one multilayer perceptron, the CPU and GPU could run batch 16 and reach only 42 and 37 percent of their maximum throughput, while the TPU ran batch 200 at 80 percent [1]. The model showed that memory, not compute, was the lever: using the K80's GDDR5 memory for the TPU's weight memory would have raised modeled performance 2.6 times (geometric mean) and 3.9 times (weighted mean), or 1.9 and 3.2 times after adding host-server overhead, while raising the clock rate without faster memory made almost no change [1]. The study also recorded a weakness: at 10 percent load the TPU drew 88 percent of its full-load power, worse energy proportionality than the CPU or GPU [1].

### Scheduling: Orca (2022)

Yu and colleagues evaluated Orca against NVIDIA's FasterTransformer serving GPT-3-style models from 13 to 341 billion parameters on A100 GPUs, using a synthetic Poisson request trace because no public production trace was available [4]. For the 175-billion-parameter model at the same median normalized latency, Orca sustained 36.9 times the throughput [4]. The gain came from iteration-level scheduling and selective batching; on a single machine, the execution engine alone performed similarly to or slightly worse than FasterTransformer, so the advantage lay in scheduling rather than kernels [4].

### Memory management: vLLM (2023)

Kwon and colleagues measured KV cache utilization in existing systems and found that only 20.4 to 38.2 percent of reserved memory held token states [8]. Using request lengths sampled from the ShareGPT and Alpaca datasets with synthetic Poisson arrivals, vLLM achieved 2 to 4 times the throughput of FasterTransformer and Orca at the same latency, and up to 22 times FasterTransformer's request rate on ShareGPT traces [8]. Sharing reduced memory use by 6.1 to 9.8 percent for parallel sampling and 37.6 to 55.2 percent for beam search on Alpaca [8]. The advantage shrank for OPT-175B on Alpaca, where the workload became compute-bound rather than memory-bound, and PagedAttention's attention kernel was 20 to 26 percent slower than FasterTransformer's [8].

### Disaggregation: DistServe (2024)

Zhong and colleagues first measured a 13-billion-parameter model on one A100 serving 512-token inputs and 64-token outputs: a colocated system met its latency targets at about 1.6 requests per second, a prefill-only GPU at 5.6, and a decode-only GPU at 10 [9]. Two prefill GPUs feeding one decode GPU served 3.3 requests per second per GPU, 2.1 times the colocated figure [9]. On a 32-GPU cluster serving OPT models from 13 to 175 billion parameters, DistServe served up to 7.4 times more requests, or met 12.6 times tighter SLOs, than state-of-the-art systems at over 90 percent SLO attainment; KV cache transfer accounted for less than 0.1 percent of total latency for OPT-175B [9]. The authors note that throughput-oriented offline workloads may favor chunked prefill instead [9].

### Speculative decoding (2023)

Leviathan and colleagues applied speculative decoding to an 11-billion-parameter T5-XXL model on one TPU v4 at batch size 1, using smaller T5 models as drafts [6]. For English-German translation with a 77-million-parameter draft, speed rose 3.4 times with greedy decoding and 2.6 times with standard sampling; for summarization, 3.1 and 2.3 times, with outputs identical in distribution to the baseline [6]. Measured acceptance rates for drafts about two orders of magnitude smaller than the target ranged from 0.5 to 0.9; even a bigram draft reached an acceptance rate of about 0.2 and a 1.25 times speedup [6].

### Quantization: GPTQ (2023)

Frantar and colleagues quantized the full OPT and BLOOM model families, up to 176 billion parameters, using 128 random 2,048-token text segments for calibration and a single A100; quantizing OPT-175B took 4.2 GPU hours [7]. Accuracy was measured mainly by perplexity, which they describe as particularly sensitive to quantization [7]. With custom kernels, 3-bit OPT-175B ran 3.24 times faster per token on A100s than 16-bit inference and 4.53 times faster on lower-bandwidth A6000 GPUs, while using one A100 instead of five [7]. The larger gain on the lower-bandwidth GPU fits the memory-bound explanation of decoding.

### Benchmarks: MLPerf Inference (2019-2026)

The first MLPerf Inference round received over 600 submissions from 14 organizations, of which 595 were cleared as valid, with a roughly 10,000-fold performance range between the smallest and largest systems [3]. Every system delivered less throughput in the latency-bounded server scenario than in the offline scenario; the reduction was 39 to 55 percent for neural machine translation, 3 to 35 percent (about 20 percent on average) for ResNet-50, and under 10 percent for MobileNet [3]. Operation counts also proved misleading: one object detector needed 175 times more operations per image than another but was only 50 to 60 times slower [3]. Auditing found about 40 issues among about 180 closed-division results [3]. Later rounds show the scale of change. MLPerf Inference 6.0 recorded a 30 percent rise in multi-node submissions and a largest system of 72 nodes and 288 accelerators [13]. In version 6.1 the largest system had 512 accelerators, and the best per-accelerator DeepSeek-R1 server result was 5.7 times that of one year earlier [14].

### Energy per request: two measurement approaches

Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell measured 88 models on 10 tasks, running 1,000 unbatched inferences per dataset ten times on A100 GPUs and recording GPU, CPU, and memory energy [10]. Mean energy per 1,000 inferences ranged from 0.002 kWh for text classification to 0.047 kWh for text generation and 2.9 kWh for image generation, a spread of more than 1,450 times; task type explained more variation than model size [10]. Multi-purpose generative models used far more energy than task-specific models on classification and extractive question answering, and output length drove energy, especially for decoder-only models [10].

Elsworth and colleagues measured Google's production fleet instead, counting active accelerators, host CPU and memory, idle reserve machines, and datacenter overhead [11]. The median Gemini Apps text prompt in May 2025 used 0.24 Wh: 0.14 Wh for accelerators, 0.06 Wh for host CPU and memory, 0.02 Wh for idle machines, and 0.02 Wh for overhead [11]. A narrower method counting only active accelerators in the most efficient datacenters gave 0.10 Wh, 2.4 times lower [11]. Between May 2024 and May 2025, energy per median prompt fell 33 times, of which the authors attribute 23 times to model improvements and 1.4 times to better machine utilization [11]. The two studies differ in method (unbatched open models on fixed hardware versus a batched production fleet with first-party telemetry) and are not directly comparable, which is the measurement-boundary point that Elsworth and colleagues make [11].

## Implications

### For teams building and operating serving systems

The evidence supports treating memory as the first-class resource. Weight bytes, KV cache bytes, and the bandwidth to move them set the cost of decoding [2][5][7]. In the cited studies, large gains came from changing what is stored and moved, or when work is scheduled, rather than from faster arithmetic: vLLM's attention kernel was slower than its baseline's yet the system delivered 2 to 4 times the throughput, and Orca's engine alone roughly matched its baseline while its scheduling raised throughput 36.9 times [4][8]. The evidence supports planning capacity in terms of goodput at a stated SLO rather than peak throughput: MLPerf found that unconstrained throughput has little bearing on latency-constrained throughput, and DistServe defines its optimization target as per-GPU goodput [3][9]. The author's assessment is that the choice among colocated continuous batching, chunked prefill, and disaggregation depends on traffic: DistServe's own analysis favors disaggregation for strict TTFT and TPOT targets and concedes that throughput-oriented offline work or single-GPU deployments may not benefit [9].

Operators also inherit costs outside the model. Idle reserve capacity for failover used 10 percent of per-prompt energy in Google's fleet, host CPUs and memory used 25 percent, and supporting routing and ranking models were part of the measured service [11]. A team that budgets only accelerator time underestimates both cost and energy. Disaggregated designs add network dependencies and new failure modes, such as a fault in one decode instance affecting many prefill instances [9].

Cost per token follows utilization. Pope and colleagues found that the lowest generation cost came at batch sizes larger than about 512, while lower latency required smaller batches and a higher cost per token [5]. A fleet that serves interactive and offline traffic with one configuration therefore pays for latency with utilization. The author's assessment is that separating latency-critical traffic from batch traffic, as MLPerf's distinct server and offline scenarios model, lets each class run at its own operating point [3][5].

### For application developers and product teams

Request shape is a cost lever that developers control. Output tokens are generated serially, one decode step each [5]; Luccioni and colleagues found that output length drove energy, and DistServe models total latency as TTFT plus TPOT times the number of generated tokens [9][10]. Long shared prefixes, such as system prompts reused across requests, can be stored once in paged systems, which raised throughput up to 3.58 times over Orca for a five-shot prompt workload in vLLM's experiments [8]. The author's assessment is that prompt design, output limits, and reuse of common prefixes belong in application design reviews, not only in infrastructure tuning; the consumer-side budgeting view is covered in the agent cost topic linked below.

Model choice matters as much as engineering. Luccioni and colleagues found that general-purpose generative models used orders of magnitude more energy than task-specific models on well-defined classification and extraction tasks and questioned deploying them where tasks are well defined [10]. Google reports that distilled serving models (Gemini Flash and Flash-Lite) let it run larger batches within the same latency goals, which raises utilization [11]. Routing simple requests to smaller models follows from both findings, though the cited sources measure the components rather than routing policies themselves.

### For hardware buyers and readers of benchmark claims

Peak operations per second is a weak guide to inference performance. The TPU paper showed memory bandwidth to be the larger lever, MLPerf showed operation counts and unconstrained throughput to be poor predictors, and GPTQ's larger speedup on a lower-bandwidth GPU shows that memory bandwidth, not FLOPS, governs batch-1 decoding [1][3][7]. Buyers should read benchmark results by scenario and latency bound. MLPerf deliberately publishes no summary score, because systems specialize and weighting tasks is subjective [3]. Per-accelerator records such as MLPerf 6.1's 5.7 times DeepSeek-R1 improvement are vendor-submitted, peer-reviewed results that do not by themselves normalize for power or price [14].

Energy behavior at partial load also matters. The original TPU drew 88 percent of its full-load power at 10 percent load, which Jouppi and colleagues identified as poor energy proportionality [1], and Google's later fleet measurement assigns 10 percent of per-prompt energy to idle reserve machines [11]. The author's assessment is that a buyer sizing accelerators for bursty or diurnal traffic should ask for power at low and intermediate load, not only at peak, because reserve capacity is part of every reliable deployment.

The benchmark rules themselves now track production practice. MLPerf 6.0 introduced a serving-style harness and an interactive scenario that permits a specified form of speculative decoding while forbidding manipulation of acceptance rates [12][13], and 6.1 reported that over half of submitters used an API-centric client-server harness [14]. Results from different rounds therefore measure partly different things, and comparisons across rounds should check which rules applied.

### For energy, sustainability, and infrastructure analysts

Per-request energy figures are only meaningful with their measurement boundary. The Gemini study's comprehensive figure was 2.4 times its narrow figure for the same prompts, and it cites published estimates for similar models differing by about six times because of method [11]. Analysts should ask whether a figure includes host systems, idle capacity, and facility overhead; whether it is a median or a mean (Google uses the median because the distribution is skewed by low-utilization models and long outputs); and whether workloads were batched [10][11]. Efficiency gains of the size Google reports, 33 times per median prompt in one year, can coexist with rising aggregate demand; the author's assessment is that per-prompt and fleet-level trends must be tracked separately, and the data-center economics topic linked below covers aggregate demand.

### For learners connecting this topic to other fields

Inference systems reuse classic computer-science ideas in a new setting. PagedAttention adapts operating-system virtual memory, speculative decoding adapts the speculative execution used in processors, and continuous batching applies fine-grained scheduling to a workload whose jobs grow during execution [4][6][8]. The recurring lesson across these papers, labeled here as the author's synthesis, is that the bottleneck moves with the workload: changing batch size, sequence length, model size, or latency target can move a system from compute-bound to memory-bound or communication-bound, so no single technique or chip is best for all inference.

## Criticisms and Limits of the Evidence

Much of the systems evidence rests on synthetic traffic. Orca used a synthesized Poisson trace because no public production trace existed, and vLLM and DistServe sampled request lengths from public datasets with synthetic arrivals [4][8][9]. Production traffic with bursts, long-tail lengths, and multi-turn sessions may change the measured gains.

Baselines move quickly. Orca's 36.9 times gain was measured against a request-level scheduler, and vLLM's and DistServe's gains against the specific systems and versions available when each paper was written [4][8][9]. Headline multiples describe the gap to a specific baseline at a specific time, not a permanent advantage.

Quality measurement for compression is narrow. GPTQ's results rely mainly on perplexity and a few zero-shot tasks, and its authors state that effects on secondary measures, including bias, were not studied [7]. MLPerf requires accuracy within 1 percent of a 32-bit reference for most tasks and set lower targets for mobile models that lost accuracy without retraining [3].

The production energy data is first-party and self-reported. Elsworth and colleagues are Google employees reporting Google's fleet; their emissions figures use market-based accounting that credits clean-energy purchases, and they attribute the low figures partly to in-situ measurement, frontier model efficiency, and batching at scale [11]. Independent replication with comparable access does not exist in the cited sources. Luccioni and colleagues' controlled measurements, conversely, used unbatched inference, which they chose to reflect variable deployment conditions; Elsworth and colleagues expect batch-size-1 benchmark constraints to lower accelerator utilization and raise energy per prompt [10][11].
