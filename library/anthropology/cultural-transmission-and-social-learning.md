# Cultural Transmission Persists Through Diverse Learning Networks, Not Copying Alone

Cultural continuity does not depend on a single channel or a passive transfer of complete traditions. Knowledge, norms, and skills persist because learners observe, participate, imitate, practice, receive feedback, and selectively attend to different models across the life course [2][7][17]. Distributed learning networks also create opportunities for retention, recombination, innovation, and cultural change [11][12].

## Background

Cultural anthropology has always needed an account of continuity: if culture is learned rather than genetically inherited, how do patterned practices remain recognizable across generations? Early ethnography documented enculturation, apprenticeship, ritual participation, child work, storytelling, and ordinary domestic interaction, but often treated acquisition as a background process rather than an object of measurement. Later research made the learning relation explicit by asking who transmits information, by what process, at what age, in which setting, and with what effect on the distribution of a trait [1][2][8][17]. This shift did not replace ethnographic interpretation with one formal model. It added a vocabulary for comparing pathways while preserving the anthropological requirement to examine locally meaningful practices.

A foundational quantitative distinction separates vertical transmission from parents to children, horizontal transmission among members of the same generation, and oblique transmission from members of an older generation other than the learner's parents. Cavalli-Sforza and colleagues showed theoretically that pathways can have different population-level consequences and observed that family transmission of religion and politics was associated with high stability and maintained variation [1]. These labels identify relations between learners and models, not learning mechanisms. A child may learn vertically by observing a parent, horizontally through play with peers, or obliquely through direct instruction from an older specialist. Conversely, imitation, participation, teaching, storytelling, and correction can each operate through more than one relational pathway [2].

The distinction between mode and process corrected two recurrent errors. One error was to infer teaching whenever knowledge persisted; a stable tradition can be learned through observation, repeated participation, and environmental structuring without an adult giving an explicit lesson. The opposite error was to equate the absence of classroom-like instruction with the absence of teaching. Functional definitions include a knowledgeable person modifying behavior so that another person can learn, which may involve tolerance, opportunity provision, attentional enhancement, evaluative feedback, or direct instruction [2][8][15]. Ethnographic work therefore treats pedagogy as a family of situated practices rather than a universal school lesson.

Research on cultural evolution added another distinction: social learning can transmit information faithfully without producing cumulative culture. Cumulative culture refers to a historical process in which retained modifications are combined or improved so that later repertoires exceed what an unaided individual could readily invent. It requires retention and innovation, while recombination can help generate further variants [5][11][17]. Researchers disagree about the threshold and evidence needed to demonstrate cumulative improvement. A tradition may be old, widespread, or complex without showing a sequence of socially transmitted beneficial modifications. Vaesen and Houkes therefore argue that generalized claims about human cumulativity require stronger historical and comparative tests based on plausible descent and measured directional improvement, not complexity alone [14].

Comparative primatology widened the field further. Whiten and eight colleagues synthesized 151 years of observation across seven long-term chimpanzee study groups and identified 39 behavior patterns that were customary or habitual in some communities but absent in others after evident ecological explanations were discounted [4]. This distributional evidence showed community-specific repertoires consistent with socially maintained traditions outside humans, while not directly observing the learning history of every variant [4]. Subsequent work focused the human comparison more narrowly: not culture versus no culture, but the fidelity, range, normativity, social organization, and cumulative consequences of learning [5][6]. Tennie and colleagues proposed that many chimpanzee traditions remain within a species-wide zone of latent solutions, while human learning more often copies processes, recruits teaching, and enforces conventional forms [5]. That proposal remains a framework to test, not a license to deny complex animal traditions.

Developmental and ethnographic research then challenged any simple opposition between a universal human learning psychology and cultural variation. Legare's 2019 review describes exploration, observation, participation, imitation, and instruction as complementary capacities, with their relative use shaped by local ecologies and socialization practices [17]. A seven-society experiment likewise found both continuity and diversity: general reliance on social information varied with population and age, while majority preference followed a pooled U-shaped age pattern with no detected cross-cultural heterogeneity in that trajectory [9]. Human social learning is therefore neither an empty mechanism filled entirely by local content nor a rigid instinct expressed identically everywhere.

The anthropology of cultural transmission now joins three scales. At the interactional scale, researchers observe attention, demonstration, correction, participation, and imitation. At the network scale, they identify parents, siblings, peers, grandparents, experts, prestigious people, and institutions as differently connected sources. At the historical scale, they ask how repeated learning changes the frequency, distribution, and complexity of practices over generations [2][3][11]. The author's synthesis is that no scale is sufficient alone. A transmission model without ethnography can misidentify what is being learned; an interaction without network context can miss why one model matters; and a history of artifacts without plausible learning processes can mistake similarity for inheritance.

## Core Concepts

### Culture Is Socially Inherited but Not Copied as a Complete Package

Social learning is learning influenced by observing or interacting with others or their products. Cultural transmission is the population process through which such learning contributes to the persistence or change of socially shared information. The concepts overlap but are not identical: one learning event need not create a stable tradition, and a cultural pattern is usually reproduced by many events distributed across people, places, and time [2][7][12]. This distinction prevents the metaphor of culture as a parcel passed intact from one mind to another.

What learners acquire can include motor routines, classifications, stories, tastes, moral expectations, roles, attentional habits, and judgments about who is entitled to act. Some elements can be demonstrated directly; others become intelligible only through participation in a relationship or institution. A novice may copy how to hold a tool while also learning who may use it, when its use is appropriate, and what counts as a competent performance. The author's synthesis is that transmission always involves selection: even faithful learners encounter partial models, variable contexts, and competing interpretations.

### Vertical, Horizontal, and Oblique Pathways Distribute Different Opportunities

Vertical transmission links parent and child. Its repeated, intimate character can support early acquisition and long continuity, but it does not imply exact reproduction. Parents differ, children attend selectively, and households remain connected to peers and wider communities [1][2]. In the Congo Basin studies of Aka and Bofi children, vertical learning was especially important before age five; between ages six and twelve, oblique and horizontal pathways became more prominent [2]. The pattern shows why a single survey of adult recall cannot represent the whole life course.

Horizontal transmission connects peers or members of the same generation. It can spread innovations, create age-specific practices, and give children access to tasks outside direct adult supervision. Peer learning includes watching, practicing together, correcting, teasing, and teaching, and it may be especially important where children spend substantial time in mixed-age groups [2][8]. Schniter and colleagues found that Tsimane peers were less frequently named than older relatives overall but were relatively more associated with modern and market-oriented skills, illustrating how a pathway's importance depends on the domain [10].

Oblique transmission connects a learner with older non-parental models, although studies vary in whether they reserve the term for unrelated adults or include grandparents and other older kin. It expands the accessible repertoire beyond the household and can connect learners with domain-specific knowledge held by grandparents, older kin, or other advisers [2][3][10]. Because definitions differ, comparisons should state who counts as an oblique model instead of treating the label as self-explanatory.

These pathways interact. Parents can create access to experts; siblings can transmit parental routines; peers can rehearse adult practices in play; and grandparents can carry difficult, low-strength, or declining skills [10]. The relevant unit is therefore often a learning network rather than a dyad. Network structure affects exposure, redundancy, and bottlenecks: information may persist because several people can demonstrate it, or disappear when expertise is concentrated in one inaccessible person [3][11].

### Observation, Participation, Imitation, and Emulation Are Distinct Processes

Observation provides information without requiring the model to organize a lesson. In many small-scale societies, children are present during productive and social activities and learn by watching before attempting simplified components themselves [2][8]. Observation is active: learners allocate attention, identify goals, compare performers, and decide when to practice. Calling it passive obscures the work of detecting relevant relations in a crowded environment.

Participation adds embodied feedback. Children who carry, fetch, sort, care for younger children, gather, prepare food, or make small tools encounter the material constraints and social consequences of a practice. Tasks can be scaled to competence, allowing legitimate contribution before mastery. The meta-ethnographic review of hunter-gatherer learning found recurrent combinations of observation, play, practice, and participation, with more direct instruction often appearing for difficult or hazardous skills [8]. Participation can transmit norms as well as techniques because others respond to the learner's timing, sharing, persistence, and errors.

Imitation usually refers to copying actions or procedures, while emulation refers to reproducing a result by discovering a method independently. The distinction matters for causally opaque tasks. If a learner cannot see why a sequence works, copying the process may preserve a solution that individual reasoning would discard; where causal relations are transparent, emulation may be more efficient [5][6][7]. High-fidelity imitation can therefore protect useful complexity, but indiscriminate copying also preserves irrelevant actions. The adaptive issue is not maximum fidelity in every situation but enough fidelity where hidden dependencies make improvisation costly.

Language and demonstration can reduce opacity. A model can direct attention, name a distinction, narrate an intention, or explain a failure that would be difficult to infer from outcomes alone. Yet verbal explanation is not the universal baseline of human learning. Aka and Bofi evidence shows teaching at early ages, but comparisons among observation, imitation, and instruction depend on operational definitions; reviews of hunter-gatherer subsistence learning document substantial variation in the timing and form of direct instruction [2][8]. Formal schooling represents one highly organized transmission institution, not the template against which all cultural learning should be judged.

### Teaching Includes More Than Explicit Instruction

A functional definition of teaching asks whether a knowledgeable individual modifies behavior in a way that helps a naive individual learn [15]. An inclusive taxonomy distinguishes social tolerance, opportunity provision, stimulus or local enhancement, evaluative feedback, and direct active teaching [8]. The definition separates teaching from learner-driven observation while recognizing forms that do not resemble classrooms.

Teaching is likely to vary with task difficulty, danger, opacity, learner age, and the relationship between learner and model. Direct intervention can be unnecessary when consequences are visible and mistakes are cheap, but valuable when errors are dangerous or when a sequence contains hidden steps. The meta-ethnographic review found that complex hunting and multicomponent manufacture were more often demonstrated or taught in later childhood and adolescence [8]. The author's synthesis is that teaching intensity should be predicted from the structure of the task and the social cost of access, not from a hierarchy that ranks cultures by the amount of verbal instruction they use.

Teaching itself can be culturally learned. In a conceptual Perspective, Brandl and colleagues argue that teaching practices show cross-cultural diversity, developmental plasticity, and historical elaboration [15]. They propose that dedicated settings, specialist instruction, professional educators, and didactic traditions can accumulate changes in how teaching is organized, while noting that stronger evidence is still needed [15]. On that account, teaching is both a mechanism of transmission and a transmitted practice: a society can change not only what it teaches but how, when, and by whom teaching is performed.

### Content and Model Biases Make Social Learning Selective

Learners cannot copy everyone or retain everything. Cultural-evolution research therefore distinguishes preferences based on the content of information, cues supplied by models, and the observed prevalence of variants. Content biases make some information memorable or attractive because of its form or subject. Model-based biases direct attention toward people perceived as knowledgeable, successful, prestigious, similar, older, or otherwise relevant. Frequency-dependent biases respond to how common a behavior appears [7][12][13]. These are analytical categories, not claims that every decision is unconscious or genetically fixed.

Conformity has a precise meaning: a disproportionate tendency to adopt the most frequent variant, beyond random copying. Copying one member of a majority is not enough to establish it. Conformist learning can stabilize group conventions and reduce the cost of individual trial, but it can also delay adoption of a superior rare option. Cross-cultural developmental evidence shows age variation in majority preference and demonstrates why population differences should be tested rather than assumed [9]. Normative sanctions can further stabilize practices by attaching approval, ridicule, exclusion, or moral consequence to performance [5].

Prestige bias refers to learning from people who receive freely conferred attention or deference, often because others treat them as competent or knowledgeable. It is distinct from dominance, direct success assessment, and simple age preference. Henrich and Broesch mapped whom Fijian villagers in three villages and two Yavusa nominated as models for fishing, yam growing, and medicinal-plant knowledge [3]. Nominations reflected perceived knowledge or success together with domain, village proximity, sex, age, and education; household effects varied by domain and specification, and genetic relatedness was not a robust predictor [3]. Jimenez and Mesoudi's review concludes that evidence for prestige-biased learning exists but that studies often operationalize prestige differently from the original theory and do not always separate prestige from competence, success, or age [13]. Prestige is therefore a conditional cue, not a universal guarantee of information quality.

Biases can interact and misfire. A prestigious person's behavior may spread outside the domain in which that person is competent; majority cues can amplify an initially arbitrary variant; memorable content can outrun accurate content. Boyd, Richerson, and Henrich emphasize that the same reliance on social information that supports cultural adaptation can also sustain maladaptive practices [12]. The author's assessment is that selective social learning should be evaluated by matching cue, domain, environment, and outcome rather than labeling a bias globally rational or irrational.

### Innovation and Fidelity Form a Productive Tension

Cultural persistence requires enough fidelity to retain useful organization, but adaptation requires variation. Innovation may arise through individual experimentation, error, recombination, borrowing, or deliberate redesign. Transmission then filters which variants remain visible and reproducible [7][11][12]. The author's synthesis is that too little fidelity can dissolve complex practices, while too much can preserve obsolete routines and suppress locally useful modification.

The tension is resolved differently across domains. Ritual and identity markers may reward exact conformity because coordination is itself the function. Subsistence techniques may tolerate variation but retain hidden safety constraints. Among Tsimane respondents, peers were relatively more often reported for modern and market-oriented skills, while grandparents were relatively more prominent for difficult, low-strength traditional skills such as storytelling and musical performance [10]. The same society can therefore be conservative and innovative at once because different networks and criteria govern different traits.

Cumulative culture is one possible result, not the default outcome of every transmission chain. It requires that improvements survive loss, that learners can access prior achievements, and that populations maintain enough connectivity and diversity for recombination. Migliano and Vinicius propose that multilevel networks balance these conditions: full interconnection may reduce loss while homogenizing traits, whereas excessive fragmentation can isolate clusters and erode collective memory [11]. Demographic scale alone does not prove accumulation; researchers must identify plausible descent and measured improvement rather than infer a ratchet from complexity alone [14]. The author's synthesis is that strong accounts should connect interaction, network structure, artifact sequence, and performance.

### Norms Are Learned Through Practice, Evaluation, and Participation

Norm transmission differs from skill transmission because the outcome is not merely an efficient action. Norms specify what is expected, permitted, prohibited, admirable, or shameful, and their force depends on shared interpretation and social response. Children can learn them through observation of consequences, correction, teasing, stories, and sanctions from adults or peers [2][5]. A copied action becomes normative when participants treat deviation as accountable, not simply uncommon.

Norms also organize learning itself. Expectations about children's autonomy, legitimate authority, gendered work, age grades, secrecy, and ownership determine who may observe whom and which questions are appropriate. These arrangements affect transmission without necessarily being stated as lessons. The author's synthesis is that a cultural transmission study must treat access rules as causal variables: knowing that a skill exists does not mean every learner can approach a model, handle the object, or receive correction.

### Cumulative Culture Is Distributed Across People and Institutions

No individual needs to understand an entire cultural system for it to persist. Migliano and Vinicius argue that skills can be distributed among people and social clusters while exchange and cooperation combine their contributions; in their account, multilevel foraging networks create collective or network memory, with mobility and intergroup ties increasing access to diverse information [11]. This shifts attention from one exceptional inventor to the social conditions that preserve and recombine partial expertise.

The author's synthesis is that institutions can extend network memory. Apprenticeship, ritual offices, archives, workshops, schools, professional associations, markets, and digital systems organize model access, standards, records, and incentives [11][15]. They may improve retention while narrowing acceptable variation. The author's assessment is that institutionalization changes the topology of transmission: it can make knowledge less dependent on one family, yet more dependent on gatekeepers, infrastructure, and authorized formats. Cultural durability therefore cannot be inferred from the longevity of a practice alone; researchers should identify the relations and material supports that reproduce it.

## Evidence

### Aka and Bofi Childhood Learning Shows Life-Course Change in Transmission

Hewlett and colleagues synthesized several age-specific studies and ethnographic observations concerning Aka and Bofi hunter-gatherer children and neighboring farming populations in Central Africa [2]. The underlying work used systematic behavioral observations, participant observation, open-ended and semi-structured interviews, questionnaires, and ethnographic comparison. It separated modes, meaning the people from whom children learned, from processes such as observation, imitation, and teaching. The authors reported that learning was early and rapid, predominantly vertical before age five, and increasingly horizontal and oblique from roughly ages six to twelve [2]. Preliminary video records showed teaching-like behavior at ten to twelve months. In a focal study of older children, observation accounted for 72 minutes per day, receiving instruction for 14, giving instruction for 5, and narrowly coded imitation for 3; the authors cautioned that the restrictive imitation definition likely missed many events [2].

The case rejects two simple models at once. It does not support a purely parent-to-child account across childhood, and it does not support the claim that hunter-gatherer children learn without any teaching. Instead, the learning network broadens with mobility, competence, and peer contact. The study's mixed methods also expose a measurement issue: what people say they learned, what ethnographers notice, and what time-sampled behavior records can yield different pictures. The evidence supports age- and domain-specific transmission, not a universal percentage for all knowledge.

### Meta-Ethnography Shows Observation and Teaching Are Complementary

Lew-Levy and colleagues screened 1,202 deduplicated records, selected 236 publications for an initial round of full-text reading, and retrieved another 340 full texts through references, reviews, and author contact before including 58 publications on hunter-gatherer children's acquisition of subsistence skills [8]. The review found recurring roles for observation, imitation, play, participation, practice, and teaching. Direct active teaching was described qualitatively as rare but appeared in difficult tasks, including complex tool manufacture and hunting, especially in later childhood and adolescence. The authors adopted an inclusive teaching taxonomy covering tolerance, opportunity provision, stimulus or local enhancement, evaluative feedback, and direct instruction [8].

Because the evidence is meta-ethnographic, it inherits uneven reporting across source texts. An ethnographer who did not set out to study learning may omit ordinary correction or observation, while a teaching-focused study may record it in detail. The review nevertheless improves on isolated anecdotes by comparing methods and domains. Its bounded conclusion is that autonomy and active learning can coexist with teaching; frequency, form, and timing vary with the skill and social setting [8].

### Three Fijian Villages Reveal Domain-Specific Model Selection

Henrich and Broesch asked residents of Teci, Dalomo, and Bukama, three Fijian villages belonging to two Yavusa, whom they would choose as advisers for fishing, yam cultivation, and medicinal-plant knowledge [3]. They analyzed the resulting nomination networks with individual and relational predictors. Centralization ranged from 16% to 41% across reported networks, and perceived within-domain success or knowledge strongly predicted nominations [3]. Domain, village proximity, sex, age, and education also mattered in some models. Same-household effects were domain- and specification-dependent, while genetic relatedness did not show the expected robust effect [3].

This is evidence for structured oblique learning opportunities, not a direct record of every learning event. Nomination methods can reveal whom people seek or regard as knowledgeable while missing unplanned observation and past transmission. The authors explicitly designed the approach to reduce retrospective claims about who had taught each item, but model nominations still measure a culturally interpreted learning network. The case supports adaptive selectivity while showing that prestige, competence, proximity, and access remain difficult to separate in real communities [3][13].

### Tsimane Skill Histories Show Kin, Gender, Age, and Domain Interact

Schniter, Kaplan, and Gurven collected retrospective transmitter nominations for five influence styles across a 92-skill inventory from 421 Tsimane forager-farmers in Bolivia [10]. Participants were asked about the sex-appropriate skills they possessed and could name relationship categories for people who taught, corrected, served as examples, encouraged, or discouraged them. Older same-sex relatives, especially parents, were the most frequently reported influences on traditional knowledge and skills. Grandparents were relatively prominent for difficult, low-strength activities in which experience offered a comparative advantage, including storytelling and musical performance. Same-generation peers were less frequent overall but relatively more associated with modern and market-oriented skills and with the small category of discouragement reports [10].

The study's contribution is not that one pathway dominates universally. It shows that reported transmitter categories depend jointly on kinship, sex, generational seniority, and skill type. Its retrospective reports cannot establish chronology, transmission speed, or the exact frequency or fidelity of observed learning events, but the large domain inventory demonstrates why conclusions drawn from one task can be misleading. The finding also qualifies broad claims that oblique transmission replaces vertical transmission in complex cultures: parents can remain central while grandparents, peers, and other nonparental kin carry distinct parts of the repertoire [10].

### Seven-Society Experiments Demonstrate Continuity and Variation

van Leeuwen and colleagues tested 605 children across seven opportunistic, unevenly sized society samples using one standardized choice-box task [9]. General reliance on social information decreased slightly with age in the pooled analysis and showed a population-by-age interaction. Among the 475 children who selected one of the demonstrated options, majority preference followed a pooled U-shaped age pattern, with no evidence of cross-cultural heterogeneity in that trajectory or an overall society difference after age was collapsed [9]. Demonstration order mattered: the odds of choosing the majority were 4.55 times higher when the majority demonstration came first, an effect included in the statistical models [9].

Experimental standardization permits cross-population comparison but simplifies the social world. Unfamiliar prerecorded peer models in a one-shot task do not reproduce kinship, expertise, moral stakes, or repeated apprenticeship. The value of the evidence is narrower: majority and social-information use are measurable, developmentally variable responses rather than assumptions that can be assigned to a culture as a whole. The samples were not nationally representative, and ethnography is needed to explain why a cue matters in one domain and not another [9].

### Comparative Puzzlebox Research Identifies a Sociocognitive Package

Dean and colleagues gave one capuchin population, eight chimpanzee groups, and eight groups of human children a puzzlebox with three sequential solution levels and increasingly valued rewards [6]. Children reached higher levels far more often. Their success covaried with verbal teaching, communication, action matching or observational learning, and prosocial behavior, whereas chimpanzees showed social learning at the first stage but not higher stages under the experimental conditions [6]. The action-matching measure could not distinguish imitation from object-movement reenactment or emulation. The method linked performance with observed interaction instead of merely noting a species difference.

The experiment supports the importance of a package of social processes for cumulative problem solving, but it does not prove that every historical accumulation requires every component. A laboratory apparatus, captive groups, reward structure, and short observation window differ from long apprenticeships and population networks. In a later six-generation basket task, Zwirner and Thornton found no significant treatment difference in the primary rice-carrying measure, while teaching improved basket robustness [16]. Teaching was useful in that simple task but not necessary for cumulative efficacy gains, reinforcing the need to treat mechanisms as conditional contributors rather than a fixed checklist [6][16].

### Chimpanzee Field Synthesis Shows Community-Specific Repertoires

Whiten and eight colleagues compared behavior inventories from seven long-term African chimpanzee study groups representing 151 years of observation [4]. They identified 39 tool-use, grooming, and courtship patterns that were customary or habitual in some communities and absent in others after researchers discounted evident ecological explanations. Each community displayed a distinctive repertoire [4]. The method used convergent long-term field knowledge rather than a single diffusion experiment.

Ecological exclusion cannot prove the learning history of every trait, and later studies continue to refine genetic, ecological, and social explanations. Even so, the synthesis made a decisive conceptual contribution by showing group-specific repertoires consistent with socially learned traditions outside humans. The comparative problem became explaining degrees of accumulation, fidelity, process copying, teaching, and normativity rather than drawing a categorical boundary around culture [4][5].

### Evidence for Cumulative Culture Is Strong but Definition-Dependent

Laboratory and comparative work shows that humans can diffuse increasingly difficult solutions within social groups, and developmental reviews identify imitation, language, teaching, participation, and innovation as mutually supporting processes [6][7][17]. Migliano and Vinicius argue that distributed network memory can preserve more knowledge than any one individual holds [11]. These findings support mechanisms capable of cumulative culture.

Historical proof remains harder. Vaesen and Houkes argue that researchers must distinguish social transmission from directional improvement, measure a stated outcome such as complexity, efficiency, or effectiveness, and establish a plausible relation by descent across observations [14]. Archaeological sequences can document change while leaving descent and reticulate transmission uncertain. The evidence therefore supports a careful claim: humans have demonstrable cases and proposed social conditions for cultural accumulation, while the extent, continuity, and adaptive direction of ratcheting must be demonstrated for particular traditions [11][14].

## Implications

### For Anthropological Research: Match the Claim to the Transmission Evidence

Researchers should specify the unit being transmitted, the relevant learner-model relation, the learning process, the observation period, and the outcome used to infer persistence. A report that children learn from elders does not identify whether the process is demonstration, storytelling, participation, or correction. A report of imitation does not establish whether the model is a parent, peer, specialist, or prestigious outsider. Separating these dimensions makes comparisons possible and prevents one vivid interaction from standing in for an entire cultural system [1][2].

Method triangulation is essential. Time allocation and focal observation can estimate frequencies; interviews reveal local concepts and remembered influences; network nominations identify accessible or valued models; experiments isolate responses to cues; material and historical records trace outcomes through time. Each method has characteristic blind spots [2][3][8][10]. The author's assessment is that disagreement among methods is often informative: it may show that explicit ideals, remembered lessons, and ordinary practice occupy different parts of the transmission system.

Researchers should also report age, gender, task, season, danger, opacity, and access. The Aka and Bofi evidence shows developmental change, while the Tsimane inventory shows domain-specific differences in retrospective transmitter reports [2][10]. Aggregating all knowledge into one vertical-versus-horizontal percentage can conceal the very specialization the study seeks to explain. The author's assessment is that a better design samples several domains and follows learners long enough to observe shifts in independence and network reach.

### For Cultural Preservation: Protect Relationships and Practice, Not Information Alone

Documenting a story, vocabulary, recipe, or tool sequence preserves evidence, but it does not recreate the social conditions of competent use. Knowledge may depend on access to land, materials, seasonal events, elders, peers, ritual authority, and repeated participation [2][8][10]. The author's synthesis is that archives alone cannot maintain a living repertoire and that preservation should map the learning ecology: who knows, who may teach, where practice occurs, what resources are required, and which relationships authorize correction.

The author's assessment is that interventions can unintentionally change the network they are meant to support. Moving instruction into a classroom may widen access while separating knowledge from environmental cues and cooperative tasks; recording restricted knowledge may violate local rules; and appointing one official expert may marginalize grandparents, peers, or less visible practitioners. These are hypothetical risks derived from evidence about situated learning and multilevel networks, not findings from evaluated preservation interventions [8][11]. They are reasons to involve knowledge holders in deciding format, access, sequence, and evaluation.

The author's assessment is that redundancy is a practical indicator of resilience. If only one person can perform a critical practice, continuity is vulnerable even when a high-quality recording exists. If several age groups practice and can correct one another, the system has more routes around loss. In principle, network analysis can identify concentrated expertise and missing ties, while ethnography is needed to determine whether creating new connections is culturally legitimate [3][10][11].

### For Education: Distinguish Schooling From the Full Human Learning Repertoire

Formal instruction is one powerful cultural technology, but it should not be treated as the natural baseline from which observation and participation deviate. Hunter-gatherer research documents children learning through close access to work, self-motivated practice, peer groups, and graded participation, with direct teaching concentrated where it adds value [2][8]. School design can use the same principles by making expert processes visible, providing authentic tasks, permitting safe peripheral contribution, and using feedback when learners reach a meaningful problem.

This implication does not romanticize minimal instruction. Complex symbolic systems, inaccessible expertise, and hazardous tasks may require explicit explanation and sequenced curricula. Dean and colleagues' experiment shows that communication, teaching, action matching or observational learning, and prosociality can jointly support higher-level solutions [6]. The design question is therefore not teaching versus discovery in the abstract. It is which information is opaque, which errors are productive, which are dangerous, and when a learner has enough context for explanation to become useful.

Peer learning should be treated as a transmission pathway with its own affordances, not merely a substitute when adults are absent. Peers can provide frequent practice, locally calibrated feedback, and access to emerging skills; they can also spread errors, exclusion, or risky norms. Tsimane respondents named same-generation transmitters relatively more often for modern and market-oriented skills than for traditional skills, although the retrospective design did not measure transmission speed [10]. Educators should identify what peers actually transmit and structure accountability without eliminating the autonomy that makes peer practice productive.

### For Organizations: Expertise, Prestige, and Access Must Be Kept Aligned

The author's assessment is that organizations depend on cultural transmission whenever routines exceed what new members can infer from manuals. Onboarding, apprenticeship, code review, laboratory practice, safety briefings, and informal mentoring all combine vertical-like authority relations, horizontal peer learning, and oblique access to specialists. The author's synthesis is that continuity improves when critical knowledge has more than one route and when novices can observe real work before being evaluated on independent performance.

Prestige can help learners find expertise when quality is hard to judge. The author's assessment is that the cue becomes dangerous when status travels across domains. Henrich and Broesch found domain-specific structure in Fijian model nominations, Jimenez and Mesoudi warn that studies often blur prestige with competence and success, and controlled online experiments show a preference for domain-specific prestige when it is available [3][13][18][19]. Organizations should therefore make expertise claims local and inspectable: identify which performances earned trust, permit comparison among models, and avoid treating general visibility as proof of technical judgment.

Conformity also has domain-specific value. Standardized procedures can reduce coordination and safety failures, while innovation is needed where environments change. A practical distinction is between a constraint whose rationale is opaque but safety-critical and a convention retained only because it is common. The former deserves high-fidelity transmission plus explanation when possible; the latter should remain open to controlled variation. This framework follows the documented tradeoff between cultural adaptation and socially amplified error [5][12].

### For Technology and Digital Media: Scale Changes Transmission, Not Human Selectivity

The author's synthesis is that digital platforms expand horizontal and oblique access beyond local communities and make popularity, success, and prestige cues visible at scale. Search, recommendation, follower counts, ratings, and engagement metrics alter which models are visible and which variants appear common; the older cultural-learning literature supplies the mechanism but does not by itself evaluate those platform designs [12][13].

Online experiments provide bounded evidence. In a real-time general-knowledge quiz with 269 starting participants, people used the number of prior copiers as a prestige cue when it indirectly tracked success and direct success information was unavailable [18]. In a second quiz with 397 starting participants, learners preferred prestige earned in the same topic over domain-general or cross-domain prestige, while still using broader prestige cues when better-matched cues were unavailable [19]. These experiments show selective use of online prestige information under controlled conditions, not that follower counts or recommendation systems generally identify expertise.

Evaluation should therefore distinguish content quality, source competence, social proof, and replication. A highly viewed explanation may be useful evidence that attention has converged, but not that the underlying claim is correct. A credential may be informative in one domain but irrelevant in another. Platforms and users could reduce cue mismatch by displaying provenance, domain-specific evidence, correction histories, and uncertainty rather than one undifferentiated popularity signal. This is the author's synthesis from model-bias research, not an evaluated claim that any single ranking design eliminates misinformation [3][12][13][18][19].

The author's assessment is that digital preservation also reproduces the archive-versus-practice problem. A video can show a sequence but may not transmit tactile feedback, material variation, access rules, or the social judgments that define competent performance. Remote learning becomes stronger when recordings are connected to practice, feedback, and communities capable of evaluating outcomes. Transmission fidelity is relational as well as informational.

### For Innovation Policy: Preserve Diversity Without Confusing Novelty With Improvement

Cumulative innovation depends on retaining prior solutions while generating and testing variants. Migliano and Vinicius argue that full interconnection can homogenize traits while excessive fragmentation can isolate clusters and lose collective memory; multilevel ties can combine local specialization with access to varied information [11]. The author's assessment is that organizations and public institutions can apply the principle by supporting exchanges across groups while preserving local experimentation and multiple independent lines of evidence.

Novelty is not automatically cumulative progress. A change becomes cumulative when it is retained, depends on prior achievements, and improves performance under a stated criterion [14]. Evaluation should name the outcome, compare against alternatives, and track whether later learners can reproduce the improvement. This prevents a complex succession of fashions from being mislabeled as an adaptive ratchet.

The author's assessment is that the same caution applies to heritage. Stability is not proof of optimality, and change is not proof of loss. A community may modify a practice to fit new materials, markets, or institutions while preserving its social function. Conversely, a visually similar performance may persist after the network of meaning and authority has changed. Anthropological evaluation should ask participants what continuity consists of and then compare that account with observed learning and use.

### For Reasoning About Culture: Reject Both Determinism and Individualism

Cultural transmission does not make people passive copies. Learners choose models, reinterpret demonstrations, combine sources, resist norms, and innovate. Yet individual agency does not imply that each person invents a repertoire independently. The evidence from life-course studies, model networks, cross-cultural experiments, and comparative cognition shows that available models and social access conditions structure what can be learned at acceptable cost [2][3][6][9][10].

This dual insight blocks two bad explanations. Cultural determinism treats a tradition as a force that reproduces identical persons; methodological individualism treats social outcomes as the sum of isolated choices made with complete information. A transmission account instead specifies how patterned environments shape attention and opportunity while leaving room for variation in uptake. The author's assessment is that this middle level is anthropology's distinctive contribution: it connects individual learning with relationships, institutions, and historical distributions.

The final implication is methodological humility. Labels such as vertical, prestige-biased, conformist, or cumulative are hypotheses about mechanisms, not decorations for any persistent practice. They earn explanatory value only when the relation, cue, process, and outcome are measured. The author's synthesis is that cultural transmission persists through diverse learning networks precisely because no single mechanism must carry the whole burden [1][2][14][17].
