# Urban Anthropology Shows That Cities Are Made Through Everyday Negotiation, Not Formal Plans Alone

Urban anthropology studies cities as lived cultural worlds in which residents, officials, objects, and institutions continually negotiate space, livelihood, recognition, and belonging. Its central finding is that a city is produced not only through demographic growth, markets, plans, and engineered systems, but also through everyday practices that connect formal rules with informal arrangements and make urban life possible or exclusionary [1][3][5][11].

## Background

Anthropologists did not begin with a separate theory of the city. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century fieldwork was commonly organized around villages, small-scale societies, colonial territories, kinship systems, and supposedly bounded cultures, even while migration, trade, imperial administration, and towns connected those research sites to wider worlds. Urban research became a distinct disciplinary problem when anthropologists asked whether methods developed through intensive local fieldwork could explain social life in large, mobile, internally differentiated settlements. Ulf Hannerz's synthesis described urban anthropology as a field that had grown rapidly but lacked coherence, and it drew from sociology, geography, and history to organize an anthropological account of urbanism [1]. Roger Sanjek's later review showed that the field had become global in subject matter and method rather than remaining a specialized study of Western cities or migrants detached from their places of origin [2].

This development produced a durable distinction between anthropology in the city and anthropology of the city. Research in the city uses an urban neighborhood, occupation, ethnic network, household, market, or institution as the location for a problem that might also be studied elsewhere. Research of the city asks how specifically urban scale, density, circulation, spatial division, infrastructure, and political economy configure those relations. The two approaches overlap. A neighborhood ethnography can reveal how citywide housing rules shape household strategies, while a comparative theory of cities remains empty unless grounded in what residents and institutions actually do. Hannerz sought conceptual unity without treating the city as a closed organism, and Low later identified the field's continuing concern with links between individual experience, sociopolitical and economic processes, and the cultural meaning of urban environments [1][3].

Urban anthropology also developed through a productive argument with urban sociology. The Chicago School treated the city as an ecological arrangement of zones, competition, mobility, density, and heterogeneous populations. Anthropologists adopted many of its questions but resisted explanations that made settlement form alone determine culture or social organization. Intensive observation showed that migrants did not simply lose community in the city; they could extend kinship, ritual, trade, ethnicity, and political ties across neighborhoods and between urban and rural sites. The anthropological contribution was not to deny demographic scale or structural inequality, but to examine how people interpreted and reorganized them through relationships, moral claims, and practical routines [1][2][3]. The existing sociology-demography topic on urbanization explains population concentration, segregation, neighborhood effects, and large-scale institutions; urban anthropology instead centers cross-cultural meaning, situated practice, and the experience of making a life within those structures.

The concept of informality became central after Keith Hart's study of Frafra migrants and low-income workers in Accra. Hart showed that price inflation, inadequate wages, and a labor surplus generated income practices that conventional employment categories did not capture. His article did not describe an unregulated residue outside the real economy. It asked whether activities treated as unemployment or underemployment possessed their own capacity to support urban and rural livelihoods, and it warned that importing Western categories could obstruct understanding of African cities [4]. The term subsequently traveled into development policy, labor statistics, planning, and public debate, sometimes becoming a label for everything from street vending to unauthorized housing. That expansion made the concept influential but also vulnerable to a misleading formal-versus-informal binary.

Later scholarship reframed informality as a relation rather than a sector. Ananya Roy argued that informality is a generalized mode of metropolitan urbanization and a state of exception through which rules are applied, suspended, or selectively recognized [5]. This perspective changes the question. Instead of asking which people or settlements lie outside law, it asks how authorities, landowners, firms, intermediaries, and residents produce variable legality and legitimacy. A luxury development granted an exception and a self-built settlement threatened with eviction may both depart from a formal plan, but their political protection differs. The analytical object is therefore not a fixed informal population; it is the process that makes some departures negotiable and others punishable [5][8][13].

At the same time, anthropologists expanded the meaning of infrastructure. Roads, water pipes, transit, electricity, drainage, and communications remained material systems, but ethnography showed that their operation depends on maintenance, brokerage, recognition, labor, and social relations. Nikhil Anand's work in Mumbai describes water distribution as a dynamic arrangement among residents, plumbers, politicians, engineers, and pipes; access is neither a simple technical output nor a stable legal entitlement [11]. The comparative collection edited by Anand, Akhil Gupta, and Hannah Appel similarly treats infrastructure as a site where promises of modernity meet fragile and sometimes violent relations among people, institutions, and materials [12]. This turn placed everyday breakdown, repair, waiting, connection, and disconnection at the center of urban analysis.

Research on public space, segregation, housing, and citizenship further widened the field. Setha Low's study of plazas in San Jose, Costa Rica, combined ethnography and history to show that public space is culturally shaped, physically encoded, and politically contested [10]. Teresa Caldeira connected fear, crime, fortified enclaves, segregation, and changing citizenship in Sao Paulo, demonstrating that walls and security practices reorganize encounters and ideas of the public [9]. James Holston traced how illegal residence, house building, and land conflict in Sao Paulo's peripheries became grounds for claims to rights, while also documenting the persistence of inequality and violence [8]. These works made the city legible as a cultural and political process: space is occupied, narrated, defended, serviced, classified, and made meaningful through unequal relations.

Urban anthropology now studies cities across the Global North and Global South without treating either as the universal model. Recent work in Nairobi, for example, analyzes how anti-urban ideologies and repeated demolition coexist with residents' efforts to create home and belonging across parks, railways, estates, roads, cemeteries, and dancehalls [15]. The author's synthesis is that the field's enduring question is not what a city is in the abstract, but how different actors make and contest urban life under particular histories. Comparison becomes useful when it preserves those histories rather than ranking cities by their resemblance to a single formal ideal [2][3][15].

## Core Concepts

### The city is a field of relations, not a bounded container

An urban field is composed of linked places, institutions, circulations, and social positions. A household may depend on rural land, remittances from another country, wholesale markets in another district, a neighborhood water broker, a municipal office, and a religious association at the same time. The geographic edge of the municipality does not necessarily mark the edge of the relationships that reproduce daily life. Hannerz therefore treated urbanism through networks, roles, and repertoires rather than assuming that one city contains one culture [1]. Low's review likewise located urban experience within migration, racism, deindustrialization, globalization, architecture, conflict, and metropolitan knowledge [3].

This relational view changes ethnographic design. A researcher can begin in one neighborhood but must follow the people, documents, goods, money, and infrastructure that explain what happens there. A street vendor's livelihood may require credit from kin, permission from an intermediary, customers commuting through a transit node, storage in a private home, and periodic negotiation with police. None of those relations is incidental to the apparently local activity. The author's synthesis is that the city is analytically urban when dense and overlapping relations create dependencies, opportunities, frictions, and classifications that cannot be understood from one site alone [1][3][4].

### Formality and informality are mutually produced

Formality refers to recognized rules, categories, offices, titles, contracts, plans, and procedures. Informality refers to practices or arrangements that are uncodified, incompletely recognized, selectively enforced, or organized through other institutions. The terms do not describe two separate cities. Hart's Accra workers combined wage employment, self-employment, household obligations, and activities missed by standard labor categories [4]. Roy later argued that metropolitan informality is not confined to poor settlements; state action and elite development also operate through exceptions, ambiguity, and selective legalization [5].

The crucial variables are authorization, enforcement, protection, and bargaining power. Two households may lack registered title but possess very different security because one has long residence, recognized utility connections, collective organization, political patrons, or an official promise of regularization. UN-Habitat therefore treats tenure security as a continuum that includes formal and informal arrangements and measures protection from involuntary removal rather than equating security with one document [13]. Likewise, informal work can occur inside formal enterprises, while formally registered workers can rely on household production and unrecorded exchanges. The ILO's statistical framework distinguishes informal employment from the legal status of the enterprise precisely because job and enterprise formality do not always coincide [14].

Informality is also governed. Street markets may have schedules, fees, territorial boundaries, seniority rules, and dispute procedures even when those institutions are not municipal law. Conversely, a formal rule may be weakly or selectively enforced. Calling informality an absence of order hides the question of whose order operates and who can invoke it. The author's assessment is that analysis should map each arrangement across at least four dimensions: the stated rule, actual practice, the authority that enforces or overlooks it, and the distribution of risk when recognition changes [4][5][13].

### Urban space is produced through use, meaning, and control

Space is not an empty surface on which society later acts. Streets, plazas, walls, transit stations, plots, markets, and homes organize visibility, movement, encounter, exclusion, memory, and claims to place. Low's plaza research shows that physical form encodes social and economic relations while public uses continually reinterpret that form [10]. Caldeira's Sao Paulo research shows the converse: security walls, fortified enclaves, and privatized orders do not merely reflect fear and inequality; they help reproduce segregation and transform public interaction [9].

The meaning of a place is plural. A plaza can be a ceremonial center, workplace, protest site, route, meeting place, tourist image, policing target, or refuge. Those meanings become consequential through schedules, design, surveillance, commerce, custom, and unequal authority. Users do not possess equal power to define appropriate conduct. An activity celebrated as culture in one context may be criminalized as disorder in another. The ethnographic task is to observe who uses the place, who avoids it, who is removed, what histories are invoked, and how physical changes alter the range of possible encounters [9][10].

Space is also temporal. Informal arrangements can be tolerated at one hour and prohibited at another; a street may function as transport corridor, market, religious route, and social gathering place across the day. Residents build attachments through repetition, while demolition, eviction, redevelopment, or infrastructure failure can interrupt those routines. Ng'weno's work on Nairobi follows urban life across diverse sites to show how home and belonging are produced despite ideologies that deny permanence to many residents [15]. The author's synthesis is that place-making is the cumulative alignment of memory, routine, access, recognition, and material form, not simply occupancy at one moment [10][15].

### Housing and tenure are social processes, not only legal statuses

Urban housing is often produced incrementally. Households obtain a claim, construct part of a dwelling, extend it as resources permit, connect to services through negotiated channels, and seek stronger recognition over time. Holston's research in Sao Paulo shows that illegal residence and house building can become practical and political foundations for a new language of citizenship [8]. UN-Habitat's tenure framework similarly rejects a simple titled-versus-illegal division and examines perceived and documented protection at household, settlement, and city levels [13].

A plot or dwelling can support several kinds of claim at once: occupancy, purchase, inheritance, customary allocation, rental, political promise, tax payment, service connection, or collective recognition. These claims may conflict, and their force depends on institutions capable of acknowledging them. Formal title can increase security, but title alone does not guarantee affordable services, protection from market displacement, or equal access to courts. Informal tenure can provide stability where social and political recognition is durable, but that durability may remain reversible [8][13].

Incremental construction also makes residents producers of the city. Labor invested in houses, paths, drainage, religious buildings, shops, and associations creates both material value and moral claims. Holston documents how peripheral residents turned experiences of self-building and exclusion into demands for rights, while Ng'weno shows Nairobi residents contesting demolition and anti-urban assumptions by continuing to build urban lives [8][15]. The author's assessment is that tenure should be studied as a sequence of recognition, investment, dispute, and adaptation rather than a checkbox applied after construction [8][13][15].

### People can function as infrastructure

Material systems never operate by hardware alone. They require people who coordinate, repair, translate, finance, queue, share information, enforce access, and improvise when formal provision fails. AbdouMaliq Simone's account of Johannesburg uses "people as infrastructure" to describe flexible combinations through which residents make livelihoods and services possible amid fragmented urban conditions [6]. The phrase does not imply that human labor can safely replace water systems, roads, or public obligations. It identifies relational capacities that conventional infrastructure inventories miss.

These capacities include trust, reputation, reciprocity, brokerage, and practical knowledge. A trader may know which transporter is reliable, a resident may know which official can clarify a record, and a neighborhood group may coordinate maintenance that no agency performs consistently. Such arrangements can be adaptive and productive, but they can also be exhausting, exclusionary, or coercive. Access through a broker may depend on payment, kinship, identity, or political loyalty. The author's synthesis is that social infrastructure should be evaluated by both what it enables and whose unpaid labor, exposure, or dependency sustains it [6][12].

Networks are especially important where institutions are intermittent. A rigid formal-versus-informal model treats a service as either provided or absent. Ethnography often finds variable states: water arrives at certain pressures, documents work in one office but not another, transport routes shift, and residents combine several partial systems. Anand's Mumbai research shows that flows depend on relations among residents, plumbers, engineers, politicians, and material networks [11]. This makes infrastructure a practice of ongoing coordination rather than a completed object [11][12].

### Infrastructure distributes recognition as well as resources

Pipes, roads, drains, electricity lines, and transit systems distribute water, mobility, sanitation, power, and access. They also classify residents by determining whose dwelling appears on a map, whose connection is legitimate, whose neighborhood receives maintenance, and whose use is described as theft or encroachment. Anand calls the negotiated recognition produced through Mumbai's water system "hydraulic citizenship": access is incremental, intermittent, and reversible, yet it can provide a basis for wider demands on the state [11].

Infrastructure therefore joins technical and political questions without reducing one to the other. Water pressure has material causes, but decisions about network extension, repair priority, billing, documentation, and legal connection shape who bears those failures. The Promise of Infrastructure emphasizes that material systems embody promises of equality and development while their breakdowns expose unequal relations [12]. Urban anthropology contributes by following the system into homes, offices, streets, maintenance work, and disputes, where design and social classification meet [11][12].

This approach does not treat every breakdown as intentional discrimination. Pipes corrode, budgets are finite, and demand fluctuates. The analytical requirement is to separate physical mechanism from institutional allocation and then study their interaction. The author's assessment is that an infrastructure claim is strongest when it documents the material pathway, the administrative decision, the affected relationships, and the consequences for everyday life [11][12].

### Citizenship is practiced through residence, service, and collective action

Legal citizenship is a formal status, but urban citizenship also appears in claims to inhabit, build, move, work, receive services, and participate in decisions. Holston shows that residents of Sao Paulo's peripheries developed rights claims through city struggles, especially illegal residence, house building, and land conflict [8]. Appadurai's study of the Mumbai alliance among SPARC, the National Slum Dwellers' Federation, and Mahila Milan examines how organized residents used enumeration, savings, knowledge exchange, and partnership to address poverty and negotiate with institutions [7].

These cases complicate a simple opposition between resistance and incorporation. Residents can challenge exclusion while seeking recognition from the same state that classifies them as illegal. An association may cooperate with officials on upgrading while contesting eviction. Informal practice can provide immediate survival and later support collective claims, but it can also normalize inadequate provision. The author's synthesis is that urban citizenship is a field of partial membership: rights, services, recognition, and voice do not necessarily arrive together [7][8][11].

### Belonging and exclusion are made in ordinary encounters

Belonging is not only an identity declared in words. It is reinforced when a person can remain in a place, use its facilities, recognize others, be addressed as a legitimate resident, and imagine a future there. Exclusion likewise operates through repeated signals: policing, walls, denial of services, insecure tenure, stigmatizing labels, inaccessible transport, or the assumption that a neighborhood is temporary. Caldeira's fortified enclaves show how fear and security practices reshape public life, while Ng'weno's Nairobi ethnography shows residents making home under repeated pressure of demolition and reconstruction [9][15].

Everyday power is visible in who can delay, authorize, connect, classify, or make another person wait. It includes officials and property owners but also brokers, association leaders, landlords, employers, security guards, and neighborhood authorities. Informal power may solve a problem quickly while making access personal and unequal. Formal authority may appear impersonal while being applied selectively. The author's assessment is that power should be traced through decisions and dependencies rather than inferred from organizational labels alone [5][8][9][11].

### Comparison must preserve difference in scale and history

Urban anthropology compares cases to identify mechanisms, not to arrange cities on a ladder from informal to modern. Accra's labor practices, Johannesburg's fragmented networks, Mumbai's water politics, Sao Paulo's peripheral citizenship, San Jose's plazas, and Nairobi's contested home-making emerge from different colonial histories, state forms, land regimes, and political economies [4][6][8][10][11][15]. A concept such as informality can travel only if the analyst states what is informal, relative to which rule, and with what consequence.

The same discipline applies to romanticization. Resident adaptation is evidence of skill and agency, but it does not prove that inadequate services or insecure tenure are desirable. Formalization can improve protection while also raising costs, exposing residents to taxation or eviction, or empowering intermediaries. The author's synthesis is that good comparison holds two questions together: what recurring relation does the concept reveal, and what local history changes its operation [2][3][5]?

## Evidence

### Accra: Hart's migrant study made hidden urban work analytically visible

Keith Hart's 1973 article originated in research on Frafra migrants from northern Ghana living in urban southern Ghana. Its method linked a defined migrant group with the income activities of Accra's low-income labor force rather than beginning from official employment categories. Hart observed that inflation, inadequate wages, and surplus labor encouraged complex combinations of earning and expenditure that standard economic analysis missed [4]. The evidence did not establish one timeless informal sector. It demonstrated that categories imported from Western labor markets could misclassify active livelihood strategies as passive unemployment.

The case matters methodologically because it began with what people did and then tested whether official categories described it. Wage income did not exhaust the household economy, and urban activity could remain connected to rural people and resources. The finding supports a relational view of informality: activities become informal relative to systems of enumeration, regulation, and employment classification [4]. Later ILO statistics preserve this distinction by measuring informal employment across enterprise types and employment arrangements rather than treating all informal workers as one occupation or place [14].

Hart also posed an unresolved evaluative question: did informal activity generate autonomous gains for poor households, or did it merely absorb people excluded from secure work [4]? The evidence permits neither celebration nor dismissal. An activity can create income while exposing workers to unstable demand, unsafe conditions, weak protection, or dependence on brokers. The author's assessment is that Hart's continuing contribution is an inference rule: observe the livelihood portfolio first, identify the governing categories second, and evaluate income, risk, autonomy, and distribution separately [4][14].

### Johannesburg: Simone identified coordination where institutions appeared fragmented

AbdouMaliq Simone's "People as Infrastructure" examined intersecting social fragments in Johannesburg. The article's central concept directs attention to flexible combinations of people, places, objects, and practices that enable residents to work and act in a city marked by fragmentation [6]. Rather than reading unstable arrangements only as institutional absence, the analysis asks how residents coordinate access, information, labor, and opportunity through changing relations.

The evidence is valuable because infrastructure is ordinarily counted as fixed assets: kilometers of road, pipes, buildings, vehicles, and network capacity. Simone's formulation adds the relational work that makes those assets usable or substitutes partially when they fail [6]. A person who links a customer, vehicle, supplier, document, and temporary workspace is not merely surviving around infrastructure; that coordination can itself become a condition of circulation. Yet the relation may be temporary and asymmetric, so a functioning outcome does not imply secure rights or equitable burden.

The limitation is built into the concept. Describing people as infrastructure can obscure the obligation of states or firms to provide safe material systems if the phrase is turned into praise for resilience. The Promise of Infrastructure helps constrain that reading by emphasizing both the generative and violent relations embedded in infrastructure [12]. The author's synthesis is that Simone's evidence supports a two-layer inventory: material capacity and relational capacity should be studied together, while costs and authority remain visible [6][12].

### Mumbai: organized knowledge and water connections turned residence into claims

Arjun Appadurai's study of deep democracy described an alliance among SPARC, the National Slum Dwellers' Federation, and Mahila Milan that developed from 1987 to address urban poverty in Mumbai [7]. The case followed organizations that used partnership rather than armed confrontation and built political capacity through practices such as enumeration, savings, and exchanges of knowledge. These practices mattered because residents generated information about settlements and needs that official systems often lacked or discounted. Knowledge production became part of negotiation rather than a neutral preliminary step [7].

The case shows that collective action need not begin with a single mass protest. Routine savings meetings, household counts, model building, visits between settlements, and repeated negotiation can create common categories and representatives. Those practices can increase residents' capacity to make claims in administrative forms while retaining knowledge grounded in everyday life. The evidence does not prove that partnership eliminates unequal power; it shows how organized residents can change what counts as credible urban knowledge [7].

Nikhil Anand's ethnography of Mumbai water infrastructure reveals a complementary process. Through extensive fieldwork in settlements, he traced water through relations among residents, plumbers, politicians, engineers, and roughly 3,000 miles of pipe. Legitimate service depended on recognition by city agencies, and that recognition was incremental, intermittent, and reversible [11]. Water access was therefore simultaneously material, administrative, and political. A connection could deliver a resource, classify a settlement, and open a path for claims to sanitation or education [11].

Together the cases show why formal institutions and informal practices cannot be studied separately. Residents negotiated through associations, technical workers, politicians, records, and pipes; public agencies depended on information and maintenance practices distributed beyond one office. The author's synthesis is that Mumbai demonstrates co-production without equality: residents help make systems function and become legible, but authorities retain disproportionate power to recognize, disconnect, or redefine their claims [7][11].

### Sao Paulo: self-building and fortified enclaves produced opposed forms of city-making

James Holston's Insurgent Citizenship combines comparative, ethnographic, and historical research on Sao Paulo's urban peripheries. It argues that Brazilian citizenship was formally inclusive in national membership while deeply unequal in the distribution of rights, and it traces how peripheral residents since the 1970s developed a new citizenship through illegal residence, house building, and land conflict [8]. The finding is not that illegality automatically produces democratic action. It is that experiences of building and defending urban residence created practical knowledge, collective organization, and claims to rights.

Holston's evidence also preserves contradiction. Democratic citizenship expanded while violence, injustice, and impunity intensified [8]. Residents could gain recognition in one domain and remain exposed in another. This prevents a linear account in which informal settlement progresses smoothly toward complete inclusion. It also shows why the house is more than a private asset: construction, documentation, neighborhood history, and conflict can become evidence in public arguments about membership [8][13].

Teresa Caldeira's City of Walls analyzes another trajectory in the same metropolis. Drawing on Sao Paulo and comparative material from Los Angeles, she connects fear, crime, police violence, segregation, fortified enclaves, and contradictory public space [9]. Walls, guarded compounds, and private orders gave affluent residents forms of control and proximity without common public life. Informality and exceptionalism therefore cannot be assigned only to poor residents. Urban elites also reorganize space through private governance and selective protection [5][9].

Read together, Holston and Caldeira reveal opposed uses of urban construction. Peripheral self-building can support claims to inclusion, while fortified building can withdraw resources and interaction into protected enclaves. Both alter streets, services, movement, and ideas of legitimate residence. The author's synthesis is that physical construction becomes political through the institutions and meanings attached to it; concrete alone neither democratizes nor excludes [8][9].

### San Jose: plaza ethnography connected form, use, and contested meaning

Setha Low centered On the Plaza on two public plazas in San Jose, Costa Rica, with comparisons elsewhere. Her method combined ethnography, history, literature, and personal narrative to examine everyday uses ranging from conversation and work to political rallies and leisure [10]. The study showed that cultural practice shapes public space while built form encodes social and economic relations. It also reconstructed conflicting European and Indigenous histories of the Latin American plaza and treated the plaza as politically contested rather than naturally public [10].

The evidence demonstrates why a map or design description is insufficient. A plan can identify paths, benches, monuments, and property boundaries, but it cannot by itself show who feels entitled to remain, which activities attract enforcement, how users interpret a monument, or how the site changes by time of day. Ethnographic observation adds sequences of use and participant meaning; historical evidence explains why the form and symbols carry authority [10].

The case also limits romantic accounts of public space. Co-presence does not guarantee equality, and lively use can include commerce, stigma, surveillance, conflict, and exclusion. The author's assessment is that a public-space claim should combine at least four kinds of evidence: physical arrangement, observed use, participants' interpretations, and the authority to permit or remove [9][10].

### Nairobi: long-term urban experience documented belonging under anti-urban rule

Bettina Ng'weno's No Place Like Home in a New City draws on decades of experience in rapidly changing Nairobi and follows rivers, cemeteries, parks, railways, housing estates, roads, and dancehalls. The book argues that anti-urban ideologies have repeatedly treated the city as a place that cannot become home for many residents, while everyday practices and creative resistance continue to produce belonging amid demolition and reconstruction [15]. Its multi-site movement is methodologically significant because home is not confined to a dwelling; it is assembled through routes, social spaces, memory, work, and claims across the city.

Nairobi adds a temporal dimension to the comparative evidence. A settlement or use may be physically present yet politically framed as temporary, out of place, or awaiting removal. Residents can counter that framing through age, burial, repeated use, association, narrative, and continued rebuilding [15]. The author's synthesis is that belonging should be investigated as a claim to duration: who is allowed to accumulate a past, maintain a present, and plan a future in the city [8][15].

### What the comparative evidence supports

Across Accra, Johannesburg, Mumbai, Sao Paulo, San Jose, and Nairobi, the methods differ: migrant and livelihood research, conceptual urban ethnography, organizational analysis, infrastructure fieldwork, historical comparison, public-space observation, and long-term multi-site interpretation [4][6][7][8][9][10][11][15]. No single case establishes a universal urban type. Their convergence is narrower and stronger: official categories incompletely describe urban practice; material systems operate through social relations; residence and service access shape citizenship; and spatial order is continually reproduced through unequal everyday decisions.

The cases also reveal recurring risks of inference. Observed adaptation does not prove preference, a durable informal institution does not prove fairness, formal recognition does not prove security, and a lively public space does not prove equal access. The author's assessment is that urban anthropology is most reliable when it preserves the chain from situated observation to institutional relation to bounded comparison, rather than using one vivid neighborhood as a metaphor for every city [2][3][5].

## Implications

### For research: begin with practices, then reconstruct the governing system

Researchers should not select residents into fixed formal and informal groups before observing how they work, build, connect, and claim. A stronger design starts with a practice such as vending, renting, water collection, commuting, construction, or public gathering, then identifies the rules, organizations, documents, material systems, and relationships that make it possible. Hart's Accra research and Anand's Mumbai infrastructure study both gained explanatory power by following activity across categories that official systems separated [4][11].

The design should sample positions as well as places. Residents, landlords, street workers, utility staff, engineers, association leaders, police, planners, brokers, and property developers can describe the same arrangement differently because they possess different authority and information. Interviews should be compared with observation, records, maps, and material traces. The existing brain topic on ethnographic methods explains the method in detail; the urban application adds the need to follow networks beyond one neighborhood and to connect micro-level encounters with citywide institutions.

Time must also be sampled. A one-time visit can mistake a temporary tolerance for a stable right or an exceptional service interruption for normal conditions. Repeated observation can show seasonal labor, election-period promises, construction phases, changing enforcement, and the cumulative effects of repair or demolition. The author's synthesis is that urban claims should specify both spatial scale and temporal window, because informality and recognition often change without a formal announcement [5][8][11][15].

### For civic and planning institutions: treat resident knowledge as evidence without shifting public burdens onto residents

Appadurai's Mumbai case shows that enumerations, savings practices, model building, and exchange among settlements can generate knowledge that public agencies lack [7]. Low's plaza study shows that observed use and cultural meaning reveal features that a physical plan cannot [10]. Institutions can therefore improve diagnosis by incorporating resident-produced maps, histories, service records, and accounts of everyday access. This is an evidentiary implication, not a claim that consultation automatically redistributes authority.

Participation can fail when agencies ask residents to supply unpaid labor, accept predetermined options, or validate a decision already made. Simone's relational infrastructure and Anand's water ethnography show why resident coordination is indispensable, but neither justifies replacing reliable public provision with permanent improvisation [6][11]. The author's assessment is that participation should be evaluated by decision rights, resources, feedback, and the ability to contest an outcome, not by attendance alone [7][11][12].

Formalization should likewise be treated as a bundle of changes rather than a universal cure. Registration may increase legal protection while raising rent, fees, taxes, or exposure to enforcement. Service connections may strengthen recognition but remain reversible. UN-Habitat's tenure continuum provides a more accurate diagnostic than a binary title count because it examines actual protection from removal and recognizes diverse arrangements [13]. Institutions should document which protection changes, for whom, at what cost, and with what appeal process. The point is analytical precision: the same intervention can reduce one risk and increase another.

### For infrastructure professionals: include social dependencies in system boundaries

Engineering analysis identifies flows, capacity, pressure, failure points, and maintenance requirements. Urban anthropology adds the social dependencies that determine whether those systems reach users. Anand's water network demonstrates that pipes operate through relations among residents, plumbers, politicians, and engineers, while The Promise of Infrastructure shows that systems embody political expectations as well as material performance [11][12]. A complete system map should therefore identify authorized and unauthorized connections, maintenance labor, information channels, complaint pathways, intermediary roles, and groups whose use is invisible in official demand estimates.

This does not mean substituting cultural explanation for technical diagnosis. Low pressure can result from pipe diameter, leakage, elevation, pumping, or demand; those mechanisms require engineering evidence. The anthropological question is how scarcity is allocated, interpreted, repaired, and converted into recognition or exclusion. The author's synthesis is that the two analyses meet at interfaces: meters, valves, documents, queues, maintenance visits, service counters, and household storage are points where physical and institutional systems act together [11][12].

Reliability should include the burden of coping. A network may meet an aggregate delivery target while particular households spend hours waiting, pay intermediaries, store unsafe water, or lose work when service timing shifts. Those adaptations can hide failure from administrative metrics. Ethnographic observation can reveal the labor and risk transferred to users, while technical measurement determines the material cause. Neither evidence type replaces the other.

### For organizations and investors: informal systems reveal both capability and hidden liabilities

Urban businesses often rely on informal distribution, home-based production, street commerce, flexible transport, brokers, and neighborhood credit. Hart's work shows why activity invisible to standard employment categories can remain economically significant [4], while the ILO documents informality across sectors and employment arrangements rather than only in unregistered firms [14]. An analyst who counts only licensed premises or payroll employees can therefore miss demand, labor, competition, and operational dependencies.

The same systems can conceal liabilities. Dependence on one broker, insecure premises, unprotected labor, intermittent utilities, or politically contingent permission can make revenue appear more durable than the underlying arrangement. Simone's people-as-infrastructure concept highlights coordination capacity, but its flexibility may rest on personal relations that do not scale or survive conflict [6]. The author's assessment is that commercial analysis should map who performs coordination, what authority protects the arrangement, how costs are distributed, and what would happen if recognition were withdrawn [4][6][11].

Formalization can alter an industry's economics unevenly. New licensing, tenure recognition, or infrastructure can reduce uncertainty for some participants while raising entry costs or displacing others. The relevant investment question is not whether informality will disappear, but how the boundary of recognized activity will move and which actors possess the relationships, assets, and legitimacy to adapt. This is a framework for investigation, not a forecast about any specific market [5][13][14].

### For civic organizations: build collective capacity without erasing internal inequality

The Mumbai alliance and Sao Paulo periphery cases show that savings groups, enumeration, neighborhood organization, and land struggles can transform private problems into collective claims [7][8]. Shared records can make service gaps visible, repeated meetings can stabilize representation, and exchanges between settlements can circulate tactics. These practices can create negotiating capacity before formal recognition arrives.

Organizations are not automatically representative. Leaders may control information, brokers may favor kin or clients, landlords and tenants may have conflicting interests, and women or migrants may perform work without equivalent authority. The same attention applied to the state should be applied internally: who sets categories, holds funds, speaks in negotiations, and can challenge a decision. The author's synthesis is that collective capacity is strongest when knowledge, decision rights, and accountability are distributed rather than concentrated in one indispensable intermediary [6][7].

### For public space: evaluate access through observed use and unequal exposure

A public designation does not establish equal publicness. Low's plazas and Caldeira's fortified city show that design, security, commerce, fear, and social classification influence who can remain and what conduct is accepted [9][10]. Evaluation should therefore examine use across time, participant groups, policing encounters, informal work, accessibility, and the meanings residents attach to the site.

Conflict is not necessarily evidence that public space has failed. Plazas and streets bring different claims into proximity, so disagreement can indicate active public life. The critical distinction is whether participants can appear, speak, work, and contest rules without disproportionate exposure to removal or violence. The author's assessment is that public space should be judged not only by order or footfall, but by the distribution of visibility, voice, risk, and duration [9][10].

### For cross-domain reasoning: keep urban anthropology distinct while making its connections explicit

Urban anthropology complements sociology-demography, political science, law, engineering, and planning, but it does not absorb them. Population concentration, segregation rates, housing supply, legal doctrine, infrastructure design, and municipal policy require methods beyond ethnography. Anthropology contributes evidence about how people experience, interpret, reproduce, and contest those systems across cultural settings. Its unit is the lived relation between institutions and everyday practice.

This boundary prevents two errors. One is to use an ethnographic case as a population estimate or engineering specification. The other is to treat demographic, legal, or technical measures as complete descriptions of urban life. The author's synthesis is that a strong urban explanation combines levels without collapsing them: measurement establishes scale, institutional analysis identifies rules and power, technical analysis traces material mechanism, and ethnography explains situated practice and meaning [3][11][12][14].

### A practical comparative framework

A disciplined comparison can proceed through six questions. First, what practice is being explained: work, residence, service access, mobility, public gathering, or political claim? Second, what formal rules and informal institutions organize it? Third, which material systems and spaces are required? Fourth, who can authorize, tolerate, broker, or interrupt it? Fifth, how are benefits, labor, risk, and recognition distributed? Sixth, what changes across time or between cases [4][5][8][11]?

The framework prevents informality from becoming a synonym for poverty or disorder. It also prevents resident agency from becoming a romantic substitute for secure rights and reliable systems. The final implication is analytical: cities are made through repeated negotiations whose outcomes become streets, houses, networks, boundaries, and expectations. Urban anthropology makes those negotiations visible and thereby explains why formally similar cities can be lived so differently [1][3][6][15].
