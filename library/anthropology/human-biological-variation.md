# Human Biological Variation Records Population History and Adaptation, Not Discrete Racial Types

Human biological variation consists of overlapping differences among individuals and populations produced by shared ancestry, migration, gene flow, drift, selection, development, and environment. Geographic structure is real and can support carefully bounded ancestry inference, but neither genetic nor phenotypic variation divides living humans into internally uniform, sharply separated racial types [1][2][3].

## Background

### From typology to populations

The study of human variation developed inside a discipline that once helped create and legitimize racial classifications. Early physical anthropology often treated selected measurements, visible traits, and continental labels as evidence for a few fixed human types. That procedure converted continuous and heterogeneous variation into boxes, then treated the boxes as if they were natural causes. The American Association of Biological Anthropologists, or AABA, now states that race does not accurately represent human biological variation, that human populations have never been biologically pure or fixed, and that biological anthropology must acknowledge the discipline's role in producing racial hierarchies [1]. The scientific correction is therefore both empirical and methodological: describe traits and populations as they are distributed, and do not make a social label stand in for an unmeasured biological process [1][2].

Twentieth-century evolutionary biology supplied a different unit of analysis. Population thinking asks how variant frequencies change through mutation, recombination, migration, gene flow, genetic drift, and natural selection. It expects individuals within a population to differ, boundaries to be porous, and populations to change through time. This replaced the typological question "Which race owns this trait?" with testable questions such as "How is this variant distributed?", "Which demographic history could produce that distribution?", and "Does an environmental pressure explain a pattern better than shared ancestry does?" The Race, Ethnicity, and Genetics Working Group summarized the resulting evidence in 2005: recent common ancestry and continuing gene flow have limited differentiation among human groups, while variation is clinal and heterogeneous across the genome [3].

Richard Lewontin's 1972 analysis made the shift quantitatively memorable. Using 17 blood-group, serum-protein, and red-cell-enzyme systems, he reported that 85.4 percent of diversity in his data occurred within local populations, 8.3 percent among populations assigned to the same race, and 6.3 percent among races [4]. Later scholarship has examined limitations in the marker set, sampling, weighting, and the distinction between partitioning diversity and classifying an individual. Edge, Ramachandran, and Rosenberg report that a reconstruction found minor calculation errors and placed the within-population component at approximately 86 percent rather than 85 percent [4]. These qualifications matter: information from many correlated loci can distinguish samples drawn from selected reference populations even when most diversity is within populations. They do not restore a small number of discrete, homogeneous, historically stable races. The durable result is that the conspicuous traits used in racial classification are not representative of the distribution of most human genetic variation [4].

### Genomics changed resolution, not the basic pattern

Genome-wide data made population structure observable at much finer scales. The 1000 Genomes Project reconstructed 2,504 genomes from 26 sampled populations and catalogued more than 88 million variants. It found that most common variants were shared, while rarer variants were more often restricted to closely related populations; individuals sampled from African-ancestry populations carried the greatest numbers of variant sites, consistent with deeper population history in Africa and bottlenecks associated with dispersal beyond it [5]. These results allow demographic inference and biomedical variant interpretation. The author's synthesis is that the project's named populations remain samples, not natural subdivisions of humanity.

Broader sampling has repeatedly revealed variation that narrower projects missed. Bergstrom and colleagues sequenced 929 genomes from 54 geographically, linguistically, and culturally diverse populations. They found variants absent from the 1000 Genomes resource that were common in sampled populations from southern Africa, central Africa, Oceania, and the Americas. They found no variant that was both private to and fixed across an entire continent or major geographic region, although some variants were fixed in small local samples [6]. Tishkoff and colleagues likewise found extensive structure within Africa by studying 121 African populations, four African American populations, and 60 non-African populations at 1,327 polymorphic nuclear markers: 848 microsatellites, 476 insertion-deletion markers, and three SNPs. In an Africa-only STRUCTURE analysis at K = 14, they inferred 14 ancestry clusters that correlated partly with geography and self-described linguistic or cultural affiliation, while admixture varied substantially among individuals [7]. The author's synthesis is that Africa cannot be represented adequately as one pole in a continental racial comparison.

Ancient DNA further changed the object of inference. Present-day populations are outcomes of migrations, mixtures, replacements, expansions, and bottlenecks; they are not unchanged stand-ins for ancestral populations. Ancient genomes permit some population relationships and allele-frequency changes to be observed closer to the times when they occurred. Reviews of the field document repeated admixture among modern and archaic humans and major Holocene population movements, including mixtures that reshaped the ancestry of present-day Europeans [13]. The historical record therefore contradicts a model of timeless continental populations evolving in isolation [1][13].

### The ethical problem is also a measurement problem

Population labels are necessary in some research because sampling, demography, environmental exposure, and unequal treatment can affect results. The error is to use a label without specifying what it measures. Race is ordinarily a social classification; ethnicity may refer to culture, history, language, or collective identity. Geography may denote birthplace, current residence, residential history, or where genealogical ancestors lived, so its time and spatial scale must be stated. Genealogical ancestry concerns biological relationships in a family tree. Genetic ancestry concerns the paths by which DNA was inherited through particular ancestors; in practice, shared genetic ancestry is inferred statistically from genetic similarity using specified data, methods, and often reference samples [2][3]. These variables can correlate in a particular population without becoming interchangeable [2][3][10].

The National Academies' 2023 consensus report recommends that genetics researchers not use race as a proxy for human genetic variation or assign genetic-ancestry labels from a person's race. It also recommends avoiding typological assumptions of homogeneity and fixed categories, measuring relevant environmental exposures directly when possible, applying a selected descriptor consistently, and disclosing how labels were selected, assigned, and used to group samples [2]. When an analysis measures genetic similarity rather than paths of inheritance, the report recommends describing the result as similarity rather than reifying an ancestry group [2]. This guidance does not require pretending that geography, ancestry, racism, or population history are biologically irrelevant. It requires matching the descriptor to the causal question. The author's synthesis is that accurate human-variation research begins by naming the process before naming the group: migration, relatedness, allele frequency, exposure, discrimination, diet, altitude, pathogen environment, or another measurable mechanism.

## Core Concepts

### Variation is a distribution, not an essence

A biological trait varies through a population as a distribution. For a genetic variant, researchers can estimate an allele frequency in a defined sample. For a phenotype such as stature, pigmentation, blood pressure, or oxygen saturation, they can estimate a distribution shaped by multiple biological and environmental inputs. A population average does not describe every member, and a difference between averages does not make the distributions nonoverlapping. These elementary facts are decisive because racial typology reverses them: it begins with categories, selects traits that appear to distinguish them, and then treats the observed difference as an essence of each category [1][3].

Most common genetic variants are shared across populations, but their frequencies can differ. Rare variants are more often geographically localized because many arose recently and have had less time to spread. The 1000 Genomes data demonstrate both patterns: extensive worldwide sharing of common variants and increasing geographic restriction as frequency declines; 86 percent of catalogued variants were confined to one continental grouping because the catalogue contains many rare variants [5]. A useful description therefore states the variant, frequency, sample, place, time, and uncertainty. "Present in population X" is usually too crude, because presence differs from frequency and the sampled population differs from every person assigned the same broad label.

Genotype is also not phenotype. A phenotype can reflect many loci, regulatory processes, development, nutrition, disease, physical environment, behavior, and social conditions. The same phenotype can arise through different genetic pathways, and the effect of a variant can depend on other variants or an environment. The AABA statement emphasizes that genome-environment interaction, local evolutionary change, and gene flow jointly produce human phenotypic diversity [1]. The implication is not that genes are unimportant. It is that visible similarity does not reveal a complete genealogy, and genetic similarity does not guarantee an identical phenotype.

### Mutation, drift, gene flow, and selection leave different signatures

Mutation introduces new sequence variation. Recombination reshuffles variants each generation. Genetic drift changes frequencies through the finite and partly random transmission of alleles, with stronger effects in smaller populations. Founder events and bottlenecks can reduce diversity when a subset gives rise to a later population. Gene flow moves variants through reproduction among populations. Natural selection changes the reproductive contribution of variants when they affect survival or reproduction in a particular environment. These processes operate together, so a geographic frequency difference is not automatically an adaptation [5][8].

Human dispersal illustrates their interaction. Individuals in sampled populations outside Africa carry fewer variant sites per genome on average than individuals in sampled African-ancestry populations, a pattern consistent with bottlenecks during dispersal beyond Africa [5]. Later mutations also produced many variants private to non-African regions, so non-African variation is not literally only a subset of variants observed within Africa [5][6]. Subsequent drift, local population expansion, and migration changed regional frequencies. Gene flow repeatedly reconnected populations and introduced ancestry from neighboring and, in some cases, archaic groups [5][6][13]. The resulting pattern is structured but reticulate: ancestry branches in models, yet actual population history repeatedly joins branches through admixture.

Selection must be distinguished from demography because both can create geographic differences. Coop and colleagues compared fine-scale HGDP and continental HapMap allele-frequency patterns against genome-wide structure and demographic simulations. They found enrichment of candidate selection signals, including loci near previously established adaptation candidates, but also showed that population history and gene flow strongly constrained the geographic distribution of putatively selected alleles; some extreme variants could be neutral outliers [8]. The author's synthesis is that adaptation is a locus- and environment-specific hypothesis, not a general explanation for every difference between population samples.

### Clines describe gradual change across geography

A cline is a gradual change in a trait or allele frequency across space. Isolation by distance can generate clines because people have historically reproduced more often with nearby than distant populations while migration connects adjacent groups. Environmental gradients can also generate clines when selection varies with latitude, altitude, pathogen burden, diet, or another exposure. Different traits need not share the same cline because they have different histories, genetic architectures, and selective pressures [1][3][8].

Skin pigmentation is an instructive case. Jablonski and Chaplin synthesized geographic, physiological, and genetic evidence that constitutive pigmentation tracks ultraviolet radiation through opposing selective pressures. Their model proposes that dark constitutive pigmentation under high ultraviolet exposure principally protected folate, while lower ultraviolet B exposure favored depigmentation that facilitated cutaneous vitamin D production [9]. They reviewed genetic evidence for independent routes to depigmentation and argued that tannable phenotypes evolved repeatedly, probably through independent mutations combined with gene flow [9]. Pigmentation therefore cannot serve as a map of total genomic similarity. Populations can have similar pigmentation because of similar selective environments without being each other's closest relatives, while neighboring populations can differ for particular pigmentation alleles without becoming separate biological types [1][9].

A cluster is not the opposite of a cline. Statistical methods can group sampled individuals by genetic similarity, especially when sampling locations are separated and many markers are combined. But the number, shape, and interpretation of clusters depend on who was sampled, which markers were measured, the model, and the requested number of clusters. More continuous sampling often reveals intermediate populations and gradients that sparse sampling concealed [3][7][10][14]. Clusters can be useful summaries of a dataset; they do not prove that nature contains the same fixed groups before the analyst chooses a sample and model.

### Population history is layered and local

"Out of Africa" names a major feature of modern human history, not a single clean split into continental branches. Genomic evidence supports deep diversity and structure within Africa, dispersals beyond Africa, population-size reductions outside Africa, and later gene flow among many populations [5][6][7]. Bergstrom and colleagues found deep but gradual separations within Africa, while Tishkoff and colleagues showed that linguistic, cultural, geographic, and genetic structure can align partially without being identical [6][7]. Broad labels such as African ancestry therefore compress many distinct histories.

Admixture is ordinary human history. It occurs when previously differentiated populations exchange genes and produces individuals whose genomes contain segments with different genealogical histories. The proportions inferred for an individual depend on the reference populations, markers, and statistical model. Tishkoff and colleagues found substantial person-to-person variation in the ancestry of African American and Cape Mixed Ancestry samples [7]. A group mean cannot substitute for measuring an individual, and present-day references are proxies rather than literal ancestral populations [7][10].

Ancient DNA shows why time depth matters. A present-day population can derive ancestry from several past populations, some of which no longer exist in unmixed form. Ancient genomes have revealed that present-day European variation reflects major contributions from hunter-gatherers, Near Eastern farmers, and Bronze Age steppe-associated populations, among other later movements [13]. Archaic introgression also supplied variants that later participated in adaptation. Introgression deserts and reduced Neanderthal ancestry on the X chromosome and in gene-rich and testis-expressed regions are consistent with purifying selection against many introgressed alleles [13]. Genetic ancestry is therefore a mosaic of genealogies across the genome, not a single percentage that captures every ancestor or identity.

### Ancestry inference is conditional statistical estimation

Genetic ancestry inference compares a target genome with selected reference samples under a statistical model. Its accuracy depends on the marker set, geographic distribution of variation, density and balance of sampling, reference populations, and model assumptions. Royal and colleagues note that the Human Genome Diversity Panel is geographically broad but sparse within regions, limiting fine-scale inference, and that no database samples all human diversity [10]. The output answers "similar to which references, at this scale, under this model?" rather than "what race is this person?"

Marker choice changes resolution. Mitochondrial DNA follows one maternal lineage and the Y chromosome, when present, follows one paternal lineage; neither represents the many other genealogical ancestors of a person. Autosomal markers sample ancestry across both parental lines but remain subject to recombination, finite marker coverage, and reference bias. Arrays designed from variants common in one population can measure another population unevenly. Whole-genome sequencing expands coverage, yet interpretation remains limited by who has been sampled and how populations were described [6][10][12].

Ancestry is also scale-dependent. A model designed to distinguish broad geographic regions can be accurate at that level while misleading about a country, community, or recent ancestor. Closely related populations can be difficult to separate, and an unsampled population may be represented as a mixture of the nearest available references. Confidence intervals and alternative models are therefore substantive results, not technical clutter [10]. The author's assessment is that an ancestry estimate without its reference panel, geographic scale, marker set, and uncertainty is not a complete scientific claim.

Genealogical ancestry and genetic ancestry diverge with time. Every person has a rapidly expanding number of genealogical ancestors, but recombination means that not every genealogical ancestor contributes a detectable DNA segment after enough generations. Conversely, a genetic segment may be assigned to a reference-defined component that represents shared population history rather than one identifiable ancestor. Cultural ancestry and identity add other relationships, including language, kinship, citizenship, community membership, and historical experience. Genetic evidence can inform but cannot adjudicate all of those relationships [1][2][10].

### Local adaptation concerns traits, environments, and trade-offs

Local adaptation occurs when selection favors a trait in one environment more than in another. Strong examples involve a measurable exposure, plausible mechanism, replicated genetic signal, and phenotype linked to performance or reproduction. Human pigmentation and ultraviolet radiation connect several of these criteria at a broad geographic scale, although the proposed folate mechanism and some genomic signatures retain evidential uncertainty [9]. Other researched examples include high-altitude physiology and lactase persistence [13], and malaria-resistance and pathogen-response loci [8][13], but each involves a distinct trait and history. They should not be bundled into a claim that whole populations are globally more or less adapted.

Adaptation can use new mutations, standing variation already present at low frequency, or introgressed variation from another population [8][13]. It can affect one major locus or many loci with small effects. Culture can modify the selective environment through food production, clothing, shelter, settlement, and movement [8][9]. Developmental plasticity can also alter growth, maturational tempo, and adult size within a lifetime and across early-life environments without changing inherited sequence [15]. A complete biological-anthropological account therefore asks whether an observed difference is genetic, developmental, environmental, cultural, or an interaction among them [1][8][9][13][15].

Every adaptation has boundaries. A trait advantageous under one exposure can be neutral or costly elsewhere, and rapid migration can produce mismatch between an inherited phenotype and a new environment. No adaptive finding licenses an inference about intelligence, moral worth, social rank, or the overall capacity of a population. The author's synthesis is that adaptation explains a relation among trait, environment, and history; typology incorrectly turns that local relation into a total description of persons.

### Sampling creates the map researchers later interpret

A dataset is not humanity. It records the samples recruited, consented, retained, sequenced, and assigned usable metadata. Popejoy and Fullerton reported that Need and Goldstein's 2009 analysis found 96 percent of GWAS sample instances were of European ancestry. Their August 2016 update found 19 percent were of non-European ancestry, with much of the increase coming from studies of Asian populations [11]. Their catalogue counts could reuse the same cohorts across studies and therefore were not a deduplicated census of unique participants [11]. Underrepresentation affects variant discovery, association power, imputation, interpretation, and the apparent geography of variation [5][11].

Sampling density also changes estimates of differentiation. Xing and colleagues added populations from regions underrepresented in earlier panels. In matched African, European, and Asian subsets, estimated global differentiation fell from 15.9 percent in HapMap II to 12.3 percent after the Affymetrix 6.0 populations were added and to 11.2 percent after a second broader panel was included; adding American and Polynesian samples yielded 11.3 percent [14]. Their SNP-versus-haplotype heterozygosity comparisons showed patterns that the authors said may reflect ascertainment bias because many assayed polymorphisms had historically been selected to maximize heterozygosity in European populations [14]. Apparent gaps can therefore reflect gaps in sampling and marker design as well as population history.

Reference design can embed those gaps in infrastructure. The Human Pangenome Project was created in part because a single linear reference cannot represent the structural and sequence diversity found across humans. Its 2022 plan targeted at least 350 reference-quality, haplotype-phased diploid genomes, while reporting an initial set of 47 participants and warning that samples from a location cannot be assumed to represent all variation in that region or an entire continent [12]. Better representation improves measurement, but it does not convert continental labels into biological essences.

### Race, ancestry, and biology operate at different levels

Rejecting biological racial types does not make race unreal. Race is socially real when classifications organize identity, exposure, opportunity, discrimination, and institutions. Those processes can produce biological consequences through stress, nutrition, pollution, access to care, violence, work, and other pathways. The AABA statement explicitly distinguishes the biological inaccuracy of racial categories from the real biological consequences of racism [1]. The existing sociology topic develops those institutional mechanisms at the social level; biological anthropology contributes the evolutionary and population account of variation.

The distinction prevents two errors. Biological essentialism attributes group outcomes to inherited racial nature without measuring causal mechanisms. Biological erasure refuses to study ancestry, population history, or unequal exposure because racial categories are not natural divisions. A stronger analysis measures relevant variables directly, identifies their level, and keeps social classification separate from genetic inference unless the relationship itself is being tested [2][3]. This preserves evidence about both human biological diversity and racialized inequality without using either to explain away the other.

## Evidence

### Diversity partitioning established the predominance of within-population variation

Lewontin assembled allele-frequency data for 17 blood-group, serum-protein, and red-cell-enzyme systems and used an entropy-based measure to partition diversity within populations, among populations within his racial grouping, and among those racial groups. His printed averages were 85.4 percent, 8.3 percent, and 6.3 percent, respectively [4]. The method addressed how variation in the selected data was apportioned at each grouping level. Lewontin acknowledged that his population choices were constrained by available data and chosen in part to maximize total and between-population diversity [4]. The analysis did not test every phenotype, use genome-wide sequence data, or ask how accurately many loci could classify a sample relative to chosen references.

The study's enduring empirical contribution is the predominance of within-population variation. Later debate correctly separated diversity partitioning from classification: small frequency differences across many loci can contain information about geographic ancestry even though selected visible traits do not define discrete, internally uniform races. Edge, Ramachandran, and Rosenberg's 2022 retrospective notes the small and historically assembled marker set, sampling and weighting limitations, and minor calculation errors; it reports that a reconstruction put the within-population component at approximately 86 percent rather than 85 percent [4]. Methodological correction refines the numerical result without supplying evidence for typological purity or hierarchy.

### The 1000 Genomes Project measured sharing, rare variation, and demographic history

The final phase of the 1000 Genomes Project combined low-coverage whole-genome sequencing, deep exome sequencing, and dense genotyping for 2,504 individuals from 26 populations. It catalogued 84.7 million single-nucleotide variants, 3.6 million short insertions and deletions, and about 60,000 structural variants, and reported coverage of more than 99 percent of single-nucleotide variants above one percent frequency for a variety of ancestries represented by the resource [5]. This was a reference-building study rather than a census of every population.

Its frequency results are central to interpreting human variation. Most common variants were shared across populations, while rare variants were more geographically restricted. African-ancestry samples contained more variant sites, as expected from the deeper history of modern-human populations in Africa and loss of diversity through non-African founder events [5]. Pairwise demographic analyses were consistent with shared human history beyond roughly 150,000 to 200,000 years ago. At the same time, the project showed that existing disease studies, dominated by European-ancestry samples, missed functionally relevant variation elsewhere [5]. Shared ancestry, geographic structure, and research bias are therefore simultaneous findings.

### Dense sampling within Africa revealed structure concealed by a continental label

Tishkoff and colleagues analyzed 1,327 polymorphic nuclear markers - 848 microsatellites, 476 indels, and three SNPs - in a combined dataset representing 121 African populations, four African American populations, and 60 non-African populations [7]. In the Africa-only STRUCTURE analysis at K = 14, the model inferred 14 ancestry clusters that correlated to varying degrees with geography, language, culture, and self-described ethnicity [7]. African and African American populations, except the Dogon sample, generally showed the highest within-population genetic diversity. Only 1.71 percent of variation was apportioned among the studied African populations [7].

The study also estimated admixed ancestry for sampled African American and Cape Mixed Ancestry individuals. Group averages showed contributions from several sources, but individual estimates varied widely; the Cape sample displayed particularly complex intercontinental admixture [7]. These findings demonstrate why "African" is inadequate as a single biological reference category. They also show that inferred clusters are historical summaries, not identities: alignment with language or ethnicity can reflect migration, marriage patterns, and shared history without making cultural categories genetic essences.

### High-coverage genomes found deep structure without fixed continental variants

Bergstrom and colleagues sequenced 929 genomes from 54 populations at an average depth of 35-fold after excluding nine cell-line samples with large-scale copy-number alterations. The study included groups underrepresented in earlier large sequencing projects and found about one million variants absent from the 1000 Genomes catalogue that reached at least 20 percent frequency in one sampled HGDP population, including variation in southern Africa, central Africa, Oceania, and the Americas [6]. Anthropologically informed sampling therefore changed the catalogue of what counted as common human variation.

The study found no variant that was both private to and fixed across an entire continent or major geographic region, although some variants were fixed in small local samples [6]. It reconstructed gradual population separations, complex variation within Africa, and contrasting hunter-gatherer and agriculturalist population-size histories during the last 10,000 years. Denisovan-related segments supported multiple source populations across living populations globally, but the authors did not find clear evidence for more than one Denisovan source within Oceanians [6]. The method demonstrates both the power and limit of population genomics: rare-variant sharing and haplotypes can recover fine historical structure, but broader sampling reveals connections and mixtures hidden by a few continental endpoints.

### Geography and adaptation studies separate selection from shared history

Coop and colleagues analyzed genome-wide allele frequencies in fine-scale HGDP and continental HapMap data, comparing extreme differentiation with genome-wide structure and demographic simulations. Their candidate loci included signals near genes previously linked to pigmentation, diet, and pathogen response, while their broader result showed that population relationships, migration, and drift strongly constrained the geographic distribution of putatively selected alleles [8]. The authors cautioned that some high-differentiation SNPs could come from the neutral tail and that the HGDP tag-SNP panel could miss selected sites.

This design matters because demographic history can mimic selection. A high-frequency allele in one sample can reflect drift, a founder event, or ancestry rather than an environmental advantage. Conversely, a beneficial allele can move through gene flow and appear in several populations. Mean genome-wide differentiation predicted the extremes, and the data contained no SNPs with very extreme frequency differences between closely related HGDP population pairs [8]. The result supports local adaptation while rejecting the assumption that every geographic difference is adaptive.

Jablonski and Chaplin provided an integrative synthesis and ultraviolet-mapping analysis of skin-pigmentation adaptation. They combined geographic ultraviolet-radiation data with physiological, epidemiological, comparative, and genetic evidence [9]. Their model described dark constitutive pigmentation under high ultraviolet exposure as protection principally against folate loss and depigmentation under low ultraviolet B as facilitating cutaneous vitamin D production [9]. They reviewed genetic evidence for independent routes to depigmentation and argued more cautiously that tannable phenotypes evolved repeatedly, probably through independent mutations and gene flow [9]. A visible trait historically used to define race is thus a poor proxy for total ancestry precisely because selection can repeatedly adjust it to local environments [1][9].

### Ancient DNA observed mixture and adaptation through time

Ancient-DNA research measures past genomes rather than inferring every event solely from living people. Llamas, Willerslev, and Orlando reviewed the transition from short, PCR-amplified mitochondrial targets to genome-wide high-throughput sequencing. They describe authentication through characteristic fragmentation and terminal C-to-T damage patterns together with independent mitochondrial, sex-chromosome, or autosomal contamination estimates; damage patterns alone are not conclusive [13]. Ancient genomes have tested population replacement, admixture, and selection across time, including archaic-modern human gene flow and major prehistoric mixtures contributing to present-day populations.

Ancient samples also show that adaptation can draw on admixture. The review describes Denisovan-like ancestry at EPAS1 in Tibetans and other introgressed immune and metabolic variants that may have aided later environmental responses [13]. It also reports introgression deserts, an approximately fivefold reduction in Neanderthal ancestry on the X chromosome, and reduced Neanderthal ancestry in gene-rich and testis-expressed regions. These depletion patterns were interpreted as evidence consistent with selection against many introgressed alleles [13]. The mixed outcomes reject a story of isolated, uniformly adapted races: populations exchanged genes, particular variants sometimes became useful in a new context, and different genomic regions followed different genealogies.

### Ancestry tests demonstrate dependence on references and scale

Royal and colleagues reviewed ancestry inference across uniparental markers, autosomal panels, genome-wide arrays, academic datasets, and commercial tests. They identified four determinants of accuracy: the true geographic distribution of variation, how that variation was surveyed, which populations served as references, and the statistical method [10]. The widely used Human Genome Diversity Panel sampled globally but not densely within each region, which limits fine-scale inference. Some ancestral populations cannot be sampled directly because they no longer exist as such [10].

These limitations are observable, not philosophical. A person from an unsampled population can be assigned as a mixture of available references; a sparse panel can miss local structure; and an array designed in one population can distort another. The review therefore calls ancestry estimation a probabilistic exercise and recommends explicit confidence, transparent reference data, and care about social interpretation [10]. The evidence supports bounded ancestry inference while contradicting deterministic readings of percentages.

### Sampling bias changes discovery and downstream performance

Popejoy and Fullerton updated the ancestry composition of the public GWAS Catalog. They reported that Need and Goldstein's 2009 analysis found 96 percent of GWAS sample instances were of European ancestry. Their August 2016 catalogue analysis found 19 percent were of non-European ancestry, with much of the increase coming from East, South, and Southeast Asian studies [11]. The catalogue could count reused cohorts more than once, so these were sample instances across studies rather than a deduplicated census of unique people [11].

The consequence is technical as well as distributive. Popejoy and Fullerton documented missed discovery opportunities, differing association strengths across populations, and false or ambiguous clinical variant interpretations produced by insufficiently diverse controls [11]. The 1000 Genomes Project found more disease-associated and ClinVar-listed variants in European-ancestry genomes than demography alone would predict and attributed the imbalance to study bias [5]. The Human Pangenome Project was designed to improve sequence and structural-variant representation, yet its authors warn that people sampled at one location cannot stand for a whole natural population or continent [12]. Better sampling narrows ignorance; it never eliminates the need to state who was sampled.

### Broader sampling changed estimates of global differentiation

Xing and colleagues genotyped 296 people from 13 populations underrepresented in earlier projects with the Affymetrix 6.0 array. They combined those samples with earlier data to analyze 246,554 autosomal SNPs in 850 people from 40 populations [14]. For the differentiation comparison, they repeatedly matched African, European, and Asian sample sizes to HapMap II. Estimated overall differentiation fell from 15.9 percent in HapMap II to 12.3 percent after the Affymetrix 6.0 populations were added and to 11.2 percent after another broad panel was included; adding American and Polynesian samples produced 11.3 percent [14]. The proportion of SNPs shared across the three matched continental groupings increased from 74.9 to 88.2 percent.

The design demonstrates that an estimate of differentiation depends on who and what is sampled. Geographically intermediate populations produced a more clinal picture and reduced the apparent gaps between widely separated endpoints. Xing and colleagues also found SNP-versus-haplotype heterozygosity patterns that they said may reflect, or could be caused by, SNP ascertainment bias because discovery panels historically favored variants highly heterozygous in European populations [14]. This was a stated possible explanation, not an experimental demonstration of the causal contribution of ascertainment.

### Developmental plasticity contributes to phenotypic variation

Kuzawa and Bragg reviewed evidence on environmental influences over human life-history traits rather than treating adult phenotype as a direct readout of inherited sequence [15]. Their synthesis examined nutrition and cues of mortality risk in relation to growth rate, maturational tempo, adult size, and reproductive scheduling across contemporary populations. They concluded that environmentally driven developmental plasticity accounts for substantial contemporary variation in these traits. They separately noted that genetic contributions to population variation in stature are especially likely at the extremes [15].

The timing of exposure matters. The reviewed evidence locates much nutrition-related variation in adult stature in fetal life, infancy, and early childhood, when growth is especially sensitive to maternal nutrition and early-life ecology [15]. Later nutrition still affects health and development, but it does not necessarily erase early growth deficits or produce the same effect on final adult size. This case complements the genomic studies: a population difference in stature or maturation can emerge through developmental response to conditions without requiring a corresponding group-specific genetic cause. It also shows why phenotype must be analyzed through measured exposure, age, development, and social context rather than assigned to a broad ancestry label.

## Implications

### For biological anthropology: study processes at the correct scale

The author's synthesis is that a biological-anthropological study should define its population operationally: recruitment location, inclusion criteria, time, sample size, kinship handling, and relevant social or linguistic context. It should then identify whether its claim concerns allele frequency, phenotype, genealogy, adaptation, exposure, or identity. This procedure responds to evidence that inferred structure appears at multiple scales and changes with sampling [6][7][10][14]. "Population" is a research unit whose boundaries need justification, not a neutral synonym for race.

Comparative work should preserve within-population distributions. Reporting only group means hides overlap, outliers, sex and age structure, recent admixture, and environmental heterogeneity. Maps should display gradients and uncertainty where the data support them rather than forcing every location into a solid color. Statistical clusters should be labeled as model outputs, with the chosen number of clusters and reference samples disclosed. The author's synthesis is that the worst analytical failure is to let a visualization turn a conditional result into an apparent natural boundary.

The author's synthesis is that adaptation claims require a demographic null model and a defined environment. A frequency difference should first be tested against population history, drift, gene flow, and ascertainment. A plausible adaptive account should identify the exposure, phenotype, mechanism, fitness-relevant consequence, uncertainty, and alternatives. Pigmentation research connects ultraviolet geography, physiology, and multiple genetic pathways while retaining uncertainty about parts of the folate mechanism and selection record [9]. A general racial explanation does not specify a trait mechanism or predict concordant variation across the genome [1][8].

Biocultural analysis adds development and social environment without treating culture as noise. Nutrition and early-life conditions can alter growth, maturational tempo, and adult size through developmental plasticity [15]. Diet, clothing, shelter, settlement, mobility, inequality, and pathogen exposure can also change environmental conditions, and some cultural practices can alter selection pressures over time [1][8][9][15]. These effects should be measured at the level where they operate. The result is not a choice between biology and culture but an account of how inherited variation, development, behavior, environment, and institutions interact over time [1][2][15].

### For ancestry inference: make the conditional question visible

An ancestry estimate should name the reference panel, marker or sequence coverage, statistical model, geographic resolution, and uncertainty. It should distinguish maternal, paternal, autosomal, genealogical, and genetic ancestry and state which relevant populations are missing. These disclosures follow from the determinants of accuracy identified by Royal and colleagues and from the National Academies' distinction among ancestry concepts [2][10]. A percentage without them creates false precision.

Researchers should avoid naming an inferred component as if it were a pure ancestral people. Terms such as "European," "West African," or "East Asian" usually identify similarity to selected contemporary reference samples collected within broad regions. They do not mean that the component was historically isolated, that every ancestor came from that region, or that the reference people are unchanged descendants of one ancient population [6][10][13]. Where possible, labels should use the specific sampling population and location rather than a continental abstraction.

Commercial ancestry tests compare a customer's markers with selected reference databases using different marker systems and statistical methods, so their results are probabilistic and method-dependent [10]. Missing ancestral populations can force a result into a composite of the available references [10]. The author's assessment is that an estimate should be treated as a versioned hypothesis about genetic similarity, not a certificate of culture, nationality, tribal membership, or race.

### For genetics and medicine: measure the variable that could cause the outcome

The National Academies recommends against using race as a proxy for human genetic variation and recommends direct measurement of relevant environmental exposures where possible [2]. The 2005 working-group review similarly argues for measuring detailed social, economic, environmental, biological, or genetic factors instead of using racial, ethnic, or ancestral labels as proxies [3]. If discrimination or access affects an outcome, those mechanisms should be studied rather than assigned to inherited racial biology [2][3].

Population history remains relevant because allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium, and reference-panel performance vary. Those differences affect variant discovery, imputation, fine mapping, and interpretation across represented and underrepresented populations [5][11][12]. They are reasons to recruit diverse participants and validate methods in intended populations, not reasons to apply a group average to an individual patient. The existing precision-medicine topic develops the clinical-utility gate: ancestry is not a substitute for genotype, and broad racial labels are not dosing algorithms.

Broader sampling can reveal variants absent from narrow resources and private alleles or structure hidden by sparse panels [6][7]. Inclusion should be conducted through ongoing partnerships with study participants and community experts so that relevant perspectives inform the selection and use of population descriptors [2]. The author's synthesis is that descriptor choice is only one part of research ethics and governance; safeguards cannot be inferred from genetic diversity alone.

A pangenome can reduce bias from a single linear reference and improve detection in structurally complex regions [12]. The authors of the 2022 project plan state that the initial resource cannot be comprehensive of global variation and that location-based samples cannot represent a region or continent [12]. The author's synthesis is that representation is not solved by accumulating a fixed quota of continental categories: projects must evaluate which variation remains missing, how samples were selected, and whether metadata describe the research question accurately. Genomic infrastructure should represent variation without pretending to enumerate a final set of human types.

### For public reasoning: hold unity and structure together

Two statements are simultaneously true. Humans share recent common ancestry and most genetic diversity occurs within populations; human genetic variation also has geographic structure that can be detected with sufficient markers [4][5][6]. Denying structure makes ancestry inference and population history unintelligible. Converting structure into races ignores clines, admixture, within-population diversity, and dependence on sampling. Sound reasoning keeps both levels visible.

Visible traits deserve special caution because attention is not a random sample of biology. Skin color, hair form, and facial morphology are socially salient, but their evolutionary histories differ and do not move as one package [1][9]. Selection can produce a steep or conspicuous geographic pattern at one trait while most of the genome remains weakly differentiated. The inference from appearance to total biological type is therefore not just ethically hazardous; it is statistically unrepresentative.

Classification and explanation must also remain separate. A model may classify a sample among its reference groups accurately by combining small frequency differences across many loci. That success does not establish that the reference groups are discrete natural races, that an individual has the average trait of a group, or that a group difference has a genetic cause [4][10]. Classification answers resemblance under a model; causal explanation requires additional evidence.

### For interdisciplinary boundaries: connect without collapsing

The author's synthesis is that biological anthropology explains how evolutionary, developmental, and demographic processes distribute human genetic and phenotypic variation; sociology explains how racial and ethnic categories are constructed, institutionalized, and connected to unequal life chances; medicine tests whether a measured difference changes a validated clinical decision; and genetics studies inheritance and molecular mechanisms. The sources reviewed here support cross-disciplinary analysis rather than a claim that one level can substitute for all others [1][2][3].

The distinction is practical. A study of skin-pigmentation adaptation belongs to biological anthropology when it concerns ultraviolet exposure, population history, and evolutionary mechanism [9]. A study of how skin color changes employment or housing outcomes concerns social classification and institutions. A clinical study must ask whether a specific biomarker improves care beyond ordinary variables. Using one level as a shortcut for another creates either genetic determinism or social erasure.

The author's synthesis is a four-question discipline for any claim about a human group. What exactly was measured? Which process could produce it? At what population, geographic, and historical scale does the evidence apply? What alternative explanation would change the conclusion? A claim that cannot answer those questions should not be converted into a statement about race, ancestry, adaptation, or individual capacity.

### A responsible description framework

The author's synthesis is a five-step description framework for human variation. First, identify the trait or variants rather than beginning with a group label. Second, define the sampled populations, dates, and recruitment. Third, report within- and between-population distributions with uncertainty. Fourth, test demography, environment, development, and social exposure at the appropriate level. Fifth, explain why each descriptor is necessary, how it was assigned, and what it does not mean [2][10].

This framework protects two forms of knowledge at once. It preserves the ability to reconstruct migrations, identify local adaptation, improve genomic resources, and investigate population-specific evidence. It also prevents those findings from being transformed into fixed racial taxonomies. The evidence supports variation that is geographically structured, extensively shared and clinal, altered by admixture, and sensitive to sampling [1][5][6][14]. The author's synthesis is that every description therefore needs an explicit time, place, sample, and purpose.
