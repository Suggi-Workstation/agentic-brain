# Human Biological Variation Records Population History and Adaptation, Not Discrete Racial Types

Human biological variation consists of overlapping differences among individuals and populations produced by shared ancestry, migration, gene flow, drift, selection, development, and environment. Geographic structure is real and can support carefully bounded ancestry inference, but neither genetic nor phenotypic variation divides living humans into internally uniform, sharply separated racial types [1][2][3].

## Background

### From typology to populations

The study of human variation developed inside a discipline that once helped create and legitimize racial classifications. Early physical anthropology often treated selected measurements, visible traits, and continental labels as evidence for a few fixed human types. That procedure converted continuous and heterogeneous variation into boxes, then treated the boxes as if they were natural causes. The American Association of Biological Anthropologists, or AABA, now states that race does not accurately represent human biological variation, that human populations have never been biologically pure or fixed, and that biological anthropology must acknowledge the discipline's role in producing racial hierarchies [1]. The scientific correction is therefore both empirical and methodological: describe traits and populations as they are distributed, and do not make a social label stand in for an unmeasured biological process [1][2].

Twentieth-century evolutionary biology supplied a different unit of analysis. Population thinking asks how variant frequencies change through mutation, recombination, migration, gene flow, genetic drift, and natural selection. It expects individuals within a population to differ, boundaries to be porous, and populations to change through time. This replaced the typological question "Which race owns this trait?" with testable questions such as "How is this variant distributed?", "Which demographic history could produce that distribution?", and "Does an environmental pressure explain a pattern better than shared ancestry does?" The Race, Ethnicity, and Genetics Working Group summarized the resulting evidence in 2005: recent common ancestry and continuing gene flow have limited differentiation among human groups, while variation is clinal and heterogeneous across the genome [3].

Richard Lewontin's 1972 analysis made the shift quantitatively memorable. Using 17 classical genetic systems, he estimated that 85.4 percent of the diversity in his data occurred within local populations, 8.3 percent among populations assigned to the same race, and 6.3 percent among races [4]. Later scholarship has examined limitations of the marker set, sampling, weighting, and the distinction between partitioning diversity and classifying an individual. Those qualifications matter: information from many correlated loci can distinguish samples drawn from selected reference populations even when most diversity is within populations. They do not restore a small number of discrete, homogeneous, historically stable races. The durable result is that the conspicuous traits used in racial classification are not representative of the distribution of most human genetic variation [4].

### Genomics changed resolution, not the basic pattern

Genome-wide data made population structure observable at much finer scales. The 1000 Genomes Project reconstructed 2,504 genomes from 26 sampled populations and catalogued more than 88 million variants. It found that most common variants were shared, while rarer variants were more often restricted to closely related populations; individuals sampled from African-ancestry populations carried the greatest numbers of variant sites, consistent with deeper population history in Africa and bottlenecks associated with dispersal beyond it [5]. These results allow demographic inference and biomedical variant interpretation, but the project's named populations remain samples, not natural subdivisions of humanity [5].

Broader sampling has repeatedly revealed variation that narrower projects missed. Bergstrom and colleagues sequenced 929 genomes from 54 geographically, linguistically, and culturally diverse populations. They found previously undocumented common variation in southern Africa, central Africa, Oceania, and the Americas, yet no common variant in their analysis was fixed between major geographic regions [6]. Tishkoff and colleagues likewise found extensive structure within Africa by studying 121 African populations, four African American populations, and 60 non-African populations at 1,327 markers. Their 14 inferred African ancestry clusters correlated partly with geography and self-described linguistic or cultural affiliation, while admixture varied substantially among individuals [7]. Africa cannot be represented adequately as one pole in a continental racial comparison [6][7].

Ancient DNA further changed the object of inference. Present-day populations are outcomes of migrations, mixtures, replacements, expansions, and bottlenecks; they are not unchanged stand-ins for ancestral populations. Ancient genomes permit some population relationships and allele-frequency changes to be observed closer to the times when they occurred. Reviews of the field document repeated admixture among modern and archaic humans and major Holocene population movements, including mixtures that reshaped the ancestry of present-day Europeans [13]. The historical record therefore contradicts a model of timeless continental populations evolving in isolation [1][13].

### The ethical problem is also a measurement problem

Population labels are necessary in some research because sampling, demography, environmental exposure, and unequal treatment can affect results. The error is to use a label without specifying what it measures. Race is ordinarily a social classification; ethnicity may refer to culture, history, language, or collective identity; geographic origin records place at a stated scale; genealogical ancestry concerns relationships among ancestors; and genetic ancestry is a statistical inference from selected markers and reference samples. These variables can correlate in a particular population without becoming interchangeable [2][3][10].

The National Academies' 2023 consensus report recommends that genetics researchers not use race as a proxy for human genetic variation, avoid typological assumptions of homogeneity and fixed categories, measure relevant environmental exposures directly when possible, and explain any necessary population descriptor [2]. This guidance does not require pretending that geography, ancestry, racism, or population history are biologically irrelevant. It requires matching the descriptor to the causal question. The author's synthesis is that accurate human-variation research begins by naming the process before naming the group: migration, relatedness, allele frequency, exposure, discrimination, diet, altitude, pathogen environment, or another measurable mechanism.

## Core Concepts

### Variation is a distribution, not an essence

A biological trait varies through a population as a distribution. For a genetic variant, researchers can estimate an allele frequency in a defined sample. For a phenotype such as stature, pigmentation, blood pressure, or oxygen saturation, they can estimate a distribution shaped by multiple biological and environmental inputs. A population average does not describe every member, and a difference between averages does not make the distributions nonoverlapping. These elementary facts are decisive because racial typology reverses them: it begins with categories, selects traits that appear to distinguish them, and then treats the observed difference as an essence of each category [1][3].

Most genetic variants are shared across more than one population, but their frequencies can differ. Rare variants are often geographically localized because they arose recently and have had less time to spread. Common variants tend to be older and more widely shared. The 1000 Genomes data demonstrate both patterns: extensive sharing of common variants and increasing geographic restriction as frequency declines [5]. A useful description therefore states the variant, frequency, sample, place, time, and uncertainty. "Present in population X" is usually too crude, because presence differs from frequency and the sampled population differs from every person assigned the same broad label.

Genotype is also not phenotype. A phenotype can reflect many loci, regulatory processes, development, nutrition, disease, physical environment, behavior, and social conditions. The same phenotype can arise through different genetic pathways, and the effect of a variant can depend on other variants or an environment. The AABA statement emphasizes that genome-environment interaction, local evolutionary change, and gene flow jointly produce human phenotypic diversity [1]. The implication is not that genes are unimportant. It is that visible similarity does not reveal a complete genealogy, and genetic similarity does not guarantee an identical phenotype.

### Mutation, drift, gene flow, and selection leave different signatures

Mutation introduces new sequence variation. Recombination reshuffles variants each generation. Genetic drift changes frequencies through the finite and partly random transmission of alleles, with stronger effects in smaller populations. Founder events and bottlenecks can reduce diversity when a subset gives rise to a later population. Gene flow moves variants through reproduction among populations. Natural selection changes the reproductive contribution of variants when they affect survival or reproduction in a particular environment. These processes operate together, so a geographic frequency difference is not automatically an adaptation [5][8].

Human dispersal illustrates their interaction. Populations outside Africa carry a subset of the diversity found within Africa because dispersal involved population bottlenecks and serial founder events. Subsequent drift, local population expansion, and migration changed regional frequencies. Gene flow repeatedly reconnected populations and introduced ancestry from neighboring and, in some cases, archaic groups [5][6][13]. The resulting pattern is structured but reticulate: ancestry branches in models, yet actual population history repeatedly joins branches through admixture.

Selection must be distinguished from demography because both can create geographic differences. Coop and colleagues compared allele frequencies across globally distributed populations and evaluated whether highly differentiated variants fit regional or global selection patterns. They found clear cases of adaptation but also showed that population history and gene flow strongly constrained the geographic distribution of selected alleles; many candidate signals could not be interpreted without demographic context [8]. The author's synthesis is that adaptation is a locus- and environment-specific hypothesis, not a general explanation for every difference between population samples.

### Clines describe gradual change across geography

A cline is a gradual change in a trait or allele frequency across space. Isolation by distance can generate clines because people have historically reproduced more often with nearby than distant populations while migration connects adjacent groups. Environmental gradients can also generate clines when selection varies with latitude, altitude, pathogen burden, diet, or another exposure. Different traits need not share the same cline because they have different histories, genetic architectures, and selective pressures [1][3][8].

Skin pigmentation is an instructive case. Jablonski and Chaplin synthesized geographic, physiological, and genetic evidence that constitutive pigmentation tracks ultraviolet radiation through opposing selective pressures: protection under high ultraviolet exposure and sufficient cutaneous vitamin D production under lower ultraviolet B exposure. They also documented that depigmentation and tanning capacities evolved through more than one genetic pathway [9]. Pigmentation therefore cannot serve as a map of total genomic similarity. Populations can have similar pigmentation because of similar selective environments without being each other's closest relatives, while neighboring populations can differ for particular pigmentation alleles without becoming separate biological types [1][9].

A cluster is not the opposite of a cline. Statistical methods can group sampled individuals by genetic similarity, especially when sampling locations are separated and many markers are combined. But the number, shape, and interpretation of clusters depend on who was sampled, which markers were measured, the model, and the requested number of clusters. More continuous sampling often reveals intermediate populations and gradients that sparse sampling concealed [10][14]. Clusters can be useful summaries of a dataset; they do not prove that nature contains the same fixed groups before the analyst chooses a sample and model.

### Population history is layered and local

"Out of Africa" names a major feature of modern human history, not a single clean split into continental branches. Genomic evidence supports deep diversity and structure within Africa, dispersals beyond Africa, population-size reductions outside Africa, and later gene flow among many populations [5][6][7]. Bergstrom and colleagues found deep but gradual separations within Africa, while Tishkoff and colleagues showed that linguistic, cultural, geographic, and genetic structure can align partially without being identical [6][7]. Broad labels such as African ancestry therefore compress many distinct histories.

Admixture is ordinary human history. It occurs when previously differentiated populations exchange genes and produces individuals whose genomes contain segments with different genealogical histories. The proportions inferred for an individual depend on the reference populations and time depth of the model. Tishkoff and colleagues found substantial person-to-person variation in the ancestry of African American and Cape Mixed Ancestry samples [7]. A group mean cannot substitute for measuring an individual, and present-day references are proxies rather than literal ancestral populations [7][10].

Ancient DNA shows why time depth matters. A present-day population can derive ancestry from several past populations, some of which no longer exist in unmixed form. Ancient genomes have revealed that present-day European variation reflects major contributions from hunter-gatherers, Near Eastern farmers, and Bronze Age steppe-associated populations, among other later movements [13]. Archaic introgression also supplied variants that later participated in adaptation, while selection removed many introgressed segments [13]. Genetic ancestry is therefore a mosaic of genealogies across the genome, not a single percentage that captures every ancestor or identity.

### Ancestry inference is conditional statistical estimation

Genetic ancestry inference compares a target genome with selected reference samples under a statistical model. Its accuracy depends on the marker set, geographic distribution of variation, density and balance of sampling, reference populations, model assumptions, and the historical question being asked. Royal and colleagues note that the Human Genome Diversity Panel is geographically broad but sparse within regions, limiting fine-scale inference, and that no database samples all human diversity [10]. The output answers "similar to which references, at this scale, under this model?" rather than "what race is this person?"

Marker choice changes resolution. Mitochondrial DNA follows one maternal lineage and the Y chromosome, when present, follows one paternal lineage; neither represents the many other genealogical ancestors of a person. Autosomal markers sample ancestry across both parental lines but remain subject to recombination, finite marker coverage, and reference bias. Arrays designed from variants common in one population can measure another population unevenly. Whole-genome sequencing expands coverage, yet interpretation remains limited by who has been sampled and how populations were described [6][10][12].

Ancestry is also scale-dependent. A model designed to distinguish broad geographic regions can be accurate at that level while misleading about a country, community, or recent ancestor. Closely related populations can be difficult to separate, and an unsampled population may be represented as a mixture of the nearest available references. Confidence intervals and alternative models are therefore substantive results, not technical clutter [10]. The author's assessment is that an ancestry estimate without its reference panel, geographic scale, marker set, and uncertainty is not a complete scientific claim.

Genealogical ancestry and genetic ancestry diverge with time. Every person has a rapidly expanding number of genealogical ancestors, but recombination means that not every genealogical ancestor contributes a detectable DNA segment after enough generations. Conversely, a genetic segment may be assigned to a reference-defined component that represents shared population history rather than one identifiable ancestor. Cultural ancestry and identity add other relationships, including language, kinship, citizenship, community membership, and historical experience. Genetic evidence can inform but cannot adjudicate all of those relationships [1][10].

### Local adaptation concerns traits, environments, and trade-offs

Local adaptation occurs when selection favors a trait in one environment more than in another. Strong examples involve a measurable exposure, plausible mechanism, replicated genetic signal, and phenotype linked to performance or reproduction. Human pigmentation and ultraviolet radiation satisfy several of these criteria at a broad geographic scale [9]. Other researched examples include high-altitude physiology, lactase persistence in dairying populations, malaria-related variants, and immune responses to regional pathogens, but each involves a distinct trait and history. They should not be bundled into a claim that whole populations are globally more or less adapted.

Adaptation can use new mutations, standing variation already present at low frequency, or introgressed variation from another population. It can affect one major locus or many loci with small effects. Culture can modify the selective environment through food production, clothing, shelter, medicine, settlement, and movement. Developmental plasticity and acclimatization can also alter phenotype within a lifetime without changing inherited sequence. A complete biological-anthropological account therefore asks whether an observed difference is genetic, developmental, environmental, cultural, or an interaction among them [1][8][9].

Every adaptation has boundaries. A trait advantageous under one exposure can be neutral or costly elsewhere, and rapid migration can produce mismatch between an inherited phenotype and a new environment. No adaptive finding licenses an inference about intelligence, moral worth, social rank, or the overall capacity of a population. The author's synthesis is that adaptation explains a relation among trait, environment, and history; typology incorrectly turns that local relation into a total description of persons.

### Sampling creates the map researchers later interpret

A dataset is not humanity. It records the people who were recruited, consented, retained, sequenced, and assigned usable metadata. Popejoy and Fullerton found that 96 percent of participants in genome-wide association studies were of European descent in a 2009 analysis; their 2016 update found improvement but continuing major imbalance, with much of the increase in non-European participation coming from Asian studies [11]. Underrepresentation affects variant discovery, association power, imputation, risk prediction, and the apparent geography of variation [5][11].

Sampling density also changes estimates of differentiation. Xing and colleagues added populations from regions underrepresented in earlier panels and showed that more uniform sampling reduced an estimate of global differentiation from about 16 percent in a HapMap-based comparison to about 11 percent in their broader dataset. Their analysis also demonstrated how arrays enriched for variants discovered in selected populations can produce ascertainment bias [14]. Apparent gaps can therefore reflect gaps in sampling as well as population history.

Reference design can embed those gaps in infrastructure. The Human Pangenome Project was created in part because a single linear reference cannot represent the structural and sequence diversity found across humans. Its design calls for hundreds of high-quality diploid genomes, while its authors explicitly warn that samples from a location cannot be assumed to represent all variation in that location or an entire continent [12]. Better representation improves measurement, but it does not convert continental labels into biological essences.

### Race, ancestry, and biology operate at different levels

Rejecting biological racial types does not make race unreal. Race is socially real when classifications organize identity, exposure, opportunity, discrimination, and institutions. Those processes can produce biological consequences through stress, nutrition, pollution, access to care, violence, work, and other pathways. The AABA statement explicitly distinguishes the biological inaccuracy of racial categories from the real biological consequences of racism [1]. The existing sociology topic develops those institutional mechanisms at the social level; biological anthropology contributes the evolutionary and population account of variation.

The distinction prevents two errors. Biological essentialism attributes group outcomes to inherited racial nature without measuring causal mechanisms. Biological erasure refuses to study ancestry, population history, or unequal exposure because racial categories are not natural divisions. A stronger analysis measures relevant variables directly, identifies their level, and keeps social classification separate from genetic inference unless the relationship itself is being tested [2][3]. This preserves evidence about both human biological diversity and racialized inequality without using either to explain away the other.

## Evidence

### Diversity partitioning established the predominance of within-population variation

Lewontin assembled allele-frequency data for 17 blood-group and protein systems and used an entropy-based measure to partition diversity within populations, among populations within his racial grouping, and among those racial groups. His averages were 85.4 percent, 8.3 percent, and 6.3 percent, respectively [4]. The method addressed an explicit question: how much randomly chosen genetic variation was apportioned at each grouping level. It did not test every phenotype, use genome-wide sequence data, or ask how accurately many loci could classify a sample relative to chosen references [4].

The study's enduring empirical contribution is the scale of within-population variation. Later debate correctly separated diversity partitioning from classification: small frequency differences across many loci can contain information about geographic ancestry even though no single locus and no small collection of visible traits defines discrete races. The 2022 retrospective on Lewontin's work also notes the limitations of his small and historically assembled marker set while explaining that later genomic evidence supported widespread admixture and the lack of natural racial boundaries [4]. Methodological criticism refines the conclusion; it does not justify typological purity or hierarchy.

### The 1000 Genomes Project measured sharing, rare variation, and demographic history

The final phase of the 1000 Genomes Project combined low-coverage whole-genome sequencing, deep exome sequencing, and dense genotyping for 2,504 individuals from 26 populations. It catalogued 84.7 million single-nucleotide variants, 3.6 million short insertions and deletions, and about 60,000 structural variants, and reported coverage of more than 99 percent of single-nucleotide variants above one percent frequency across several ancestries [5]. This was a reference-building study rather than a census of every population.

Its frequency results are central to interpreting human variation. Most common variants were shared across populations, while rare variants were more geographically restricted. African-ancestry samples contained more variant sites, as expected from the deeper history of modern-human populations in Africa and loss of diversity through non-African founder events [5]. Pairwise demographic analyses were consistent with shared human history beyond roughly 150,000 to 200,000 years ago. At the same time, the project showed that existing disease studies, dominated by European-ancestry samples, missed functionally relevant variation elsewhere [5]. Shared ancestry, geographic structure, and research bias are therefore simultaneous findings.

### Dense sampling within Africa revealed structure concealed by a continental label

Tishkoff and colleagues genotyped 1,327 nuclear microsatellite and insertion-deletion markers in samples representing 121 African populations, four African American populations, and 60 non-African populations. Their model-based analyses inferred 14 ancestry clusters within Africa that correlated to varying degrees with geography, language, culture, and self-described ethnicity [7]. African and African American populations generally showed high within-population diversity, and the proportion of variation among the studied African populations was small relative to variation within them [7].

The study also estimated admixed ancestry for sampled African American and Cape Mixed Ancestry individuals. Group averages showed contributions from several sources, but individual estimates varied widely; the Cape sample displayed particularly complex intercontinental admixture [7]. These findings demonstrate why "African" is inadequate as a single biological reference category. They also show that inferred clusters are historical summaries, not identities: alignment with language or ethnicity can reflect migration, marriage patterns, and shared history without making cultural categories genetic essences.

### High-coverage genomes found deep structure without fixed continental variants

Bergstrom and colleagues sequenced 929 genomes from 54 populations at an average depth of approximately 35-fold. Their sampling included groups underrepresented in earlier large sequencing projects, and their analysis found substantial previously undocumented common variation in southern Africa, central Africa, Oceania, and the Americas [6]. Anthropologically informed sampling therefore changed the catalogue of what counted as common human variation.

The same study found no common variant fixed between major geographic regions in its dataset. It reconstructed gradual population separations, complex variation within Africa, different recent population-size histories among hunter-gatherer and agriculturalist groups, and contributions from more than one Denisovan-related source to living populations [6]. The method demonstrates both the power and limit of population genomics: rare-variant sharing and haplotypes can recover fine historical structure, but broader sampling reveals connections and mixtures hidden by a few continental endpoints.

### Geography and adaptation studies separate selection from shared history

Coop and colleagues analyzed genome-wide allele frequencies across a geographically diverse panel and compared variants showing large frequency differences with models of regional and global adaptation. The study identified strong candidates for selection, including signals related to pigmentation, diet, and pathogen response, while finding that the spread of selected alleles was constrained by population relationships and migration [8]. Closely related populations generally showed fewer extreme differences, even when they occupied different environments, than a simple independent-adaptation model would predict [8].

This design matters because demographic history can mimic selection. A high-frequency allele in one sample can reflect drift, a founder event, or ancestry rather than an environmental advantage. Conversely, a beneficial allele can move through gene flow and appear in several populations. The authors therefore treated geography as both an environmental axis and a record of population history [8]. Their result supports local adaptation while rejecting the assumption that every geographic difference is adaptive.

Jablonski and Chaplin supplied a trait-specific test through skin pigmentation. They combined geographic ultraviolet-radiation data with physiology, comparative evidence, and pigmentation genetics. Their synthesis described two broad clines: darker constitutive pigmentation under high ultraviolet exposure and lighter pigmentation where lower ultraviolet B availability increased the constraint on cutaneous vitamin D production [9]. They also reviewed evidence for independent genetic routes to lighter pigmentation and repeated evolution of tanning responses [9]. A visible trait historically used to define race is thus a poor proxy for total ancestry precisely because selection repeatedly adjusted it to local environments.

### Ancient DNA observed mixture and adaptation through time

Ancient-DNA research measures past genomes rather than inferring every event solely from living people. Llamas, Willerslev, and Orlando reviewed technical developments that moved the field from contamination-prone mitochondrial fragments to genome-wide data with damage-based authentication. They showed how ancient genomes have tested population replacement, admixture, and selection directly across time [13]. The evidence includes archaic-modern human gene flow and major prehistoric mixtures that contributed to present-day populations.

Ancient samples also show that adaptation can draw on admixture. The review describes introgressed archaic variants that contributed to later environmental responses, alongside genome-wide evidence that many archaic segments were selected against [13]. These mixed outcomes reject a story of isolated, uniformly adapted races. Populations exchanged genes; particular variants sometimes became useful in a new context; and the rest of each genome continued to follow many genealogies.

### Ancestry tests demonstrate dependence on references and scale

Royal and colleagues reviewed ancestry inference across uniparental markers, autosomal panels, genome-wide arrays, academic datasets, and commercial tests. They identified four determinants of accuracy: the true geographic distribution of variation, how that variation was surveyed, which populations served as references, and the statistical method [10]. The widely used Human Genome Diversity Panel sampled globally but not densely within each region, which limits fine-scale inference. Some ancestral populations cannot be sampled directly because they no longer exist as such [10].

These limitations are observable, not philosophical. A person from an unsampled population can be assigned as a mixture of available references; a sparse panel can miss local structure; and an array designed in one population can distort another. The review therefore calls ancestry estimation a probabilistic exercise and recommends explicit confidence, transparent reference data, and care about social interpretation [10]. The evidence supports bounded ancestry inference while contradicting deterministic readings of percentages.

### Sampling bias changes discovery and downstream performance

Popejoy and Fullerton examined the ancestry composition of genome-wide association studies. Their review reported that a 2009 analysis found 96 percent of participants were of European descent; by 2016, non-European participation had risen to nearly 20 percent, but much of the increase came from studies in Asian populations and many populations remained severely underrepresented [11]. Their method analyzed the public GWAS catalogue rather than biological diversity directly, so its finding concerns the evidence base researchers created.

The consequence is technical as well as distributive. Association discovery, fine mapping, imputation, and interpretation all depend on allele frequencies and linkage patterns represented in data. The 1000 Genomes Project found more disease-associated and ClinVar-listed variants in European-ancestry genomes than demography alone would predict, which it attributed to research bias [5]. The Human Pangenome Project was designed to improve sequence and structural-variant representation, yet its authors warn that a set of people sampled at one location cannot stand for a whole natural population or continent [12]. Better sampling narrows ignorance; it never eliminates the need to state who was sampled.

## Implications

### For biological anthropology: study processes at the correct scale

A biological-anthropological study should define its population operationally: recruitment location, inclusion criteria, time, sample size, kinship handling, and relevant social or linguistic context. It should then identify whether its claim concerns allele frequency, phenotype, genealogy, adaptation, exposure, or identity. This procedure follows from evidence that structure appears at multiple scales and changes with sampling [6][7][10][14]. "Population" is a research unit whose boundaries need justification, not a neutral synonym for race.

Comparative work should preserve within-population distributions. Reporting only group means hides overlap, outliers, sex and age structure, recent admixture, and environmental heterogeneity. Maps should display gradients and uncertainty where the data support them rather than forcing every location into a solid color. Statistical clusters should be labeled as model outputs, with the chosen number of clusters and reference samples disclosed. The author's synthesis is that the worst analytical failure is to let a visualization turn a conditional result into an apparent natural boundary.

Adaptation claims require a demographic null model and a defined environment. A frequency difference should first be tested against population history, drift, and gene flow. A plausible adaptive account should identify the exposure, phenotype, mechanism, fitness-relevant consequence, and alternative explanations. Pigmentation research meets this standard by connecting ultraviolet geography, physiology, and multiple genetic pathways [9]. A general racial explanation does not, because it neither specifies the trait mechanism nor predicts concordant variation across the genome [1][8].

Biocultural analysis adds development and social environment without treating culture as noise. Diet, settlement, clothing, subsistence, inequality, pathogen exposure, and mobility can alter phenotype and selection. Their effects should be measured at the level where they operate. The result is not a choice between biology and culture but an account of how inherited variation, development, behavior, environment, and institutions interact over time [1][2].

### For ancestry inference: make the conditional question visible

An ancestry estimate should name the reference panel, marker or sequence coverage, model, geographic resolution, time depth, and uncertainty. It should distinguish maternal, paternal, autosomal, and genealogical ancestry. It should also state which relevant populations are missing. These disclosures follow directly from the determinants of accuracy identified by Royal and colleagues [10]. A percentage without them creates false precision.

Researchers should avoid naming an inferred component as if it were a pure ancestral people. Terms such as "European," "West African," or "East Asian" usually identify similarity to contemporary reference samples collected within broad regions. They do not mean that the component was historically isolated, that every ancestor came from that region, or that the reference people are unchanged descendants of one ancient population [6][10][13]. Where possible, labels should use the specific sampling population and location rather than a continental abstraction.

Consumers of commercial ancestry results should expect revisions when companies change databases and algorithms. Different services can return different estimates because they use different references, markers, smoothing rules, and reporting thresholds. A changed estimate need not indicate that the genome changed; it can indicate that the comparison set or model changed [10]. The author's assessment is that a responsible interpretation treats the estimate as a versioned hypothesis about genetic similarity, not a certificate of culture, nationality, tribal membership, or race.

### For genetics and medicine: measure the variable that could cause the outcome

Race should not be used as a shortcut for genotype, ancestry, environment, or treatment response. If a variant is clinically relevant, measure the variant when a validated test and action exist. If an exposure is relevant, measure the exposure. If discrimination or access affects an outcome, study those mechanisms rather than assigning their effects to inherited racial biology. This is the direct recommendation of the National Academies and the earlier genetics working group [2][3].

Population history remains relevant because allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium, and reference-panel performance vary. Those differences affect variant discovery, imputation, association confounding, and transfer of risk models. They are reasons to recruit diverse participants and validate methods in intended populations, not reasons to apply a group average to an individual patient [5][11][12]. The existing precision-medicine topic develops the clinical-utility gate: ancestry is not a substitute for genotype, and broad racial labels are not dosing algorithms.

Sampling equity improves both fairness and inference. Underrepresented populations contain variants and haplotypes absent from narrow resources, as the Bergstrom and Tishkoff studies show [6][7]. Inclusion should involve community partnership, consent, governance, return-of-results policies, data security, and benefit sharing, not only extraction of samples. The National Academies specifically recommends ongoing partnerships with participants and community experts when selecting and using population descriptors [2].

A pangenome can reduce bias from a single linear reference and improve detection in structurally complex regions, but representation is not solved by accumulating a fixed quota of continental categories [12]. Projects must evaluate which variation remains missing, how samples were selected, and whether metadata describe the research question accurately. The author's synthesis is that genomic infrastructure should represent variation without pretending to enumerate a final set of human types.

### For public reasoning: hold unity and structure together

Two statements are simultaneously true. Humans share recent common ancestry and most genetic diversity occurs within populations; human genetic variation also has geographic structure that can be detected with sufficient markers [4][5][6]. Denying structure makes ancestry inference and population history unintelligible. Converting structure into races ignores clines, admixture, within-population diversity, and dependence on sampling. Sound reasoning keeps both levels visible.

Visible traits deserve special caution because attention is not a random sample of biology. Skin color, hair form, and facial morphology are socially salient, but their evolutionary histories differ and do not move as one package [1][9]. Selection can produce a steep or conspicuous geographic pattern at one trait while most of the genome remains weakly differentiated. The inference from appearance to total biological type is therefore not just ethically hazardous; it is statistically unrepresentative.

Classification and explanation must also remain separate. A model may classify a sample among its reference groups accurately by combining small frequency differences across many loci. That success does not establish that the reference groups are discrete natural races, that an individual has the average trait of a group, or that a group difference has a genetic cause [4][10]. Classification answers resemblance under a model; causal explanation requires additional evidence.

### For interdisciplinary boundaries: connect without collapsing

Biological anthropology explains how evolutionary and demographic processes distribute human genetic and phenotypic variation. Sociology explains how racial and ethnic categories are constructed, institutionalized, and connected to unequal life chances. Medicine evaluates whether a measured difference changes a validated clinical decision. Genetics explains inheritance and molecular mechanisms. These domains overlap, but none can substitute for the others [1][2][3].

The distinction is practical. A study of skin-pigmentation adaptation belongs to biological anthropology when it concerns ultraviolet exposure, population history, and evolutionary mechanism [9]. A study of how skin color changes employment or housing outcomes concerns social classification and institutions. A clinical study must ask whether a specific biomarker improves care beyond ordinary variables. Using one level as a shortcut for another creates either genetic determinism or social erasure.

The author's synthesis is a four-question discipline for any claim about a human group. What exactly was measured? Which process could produce it? At what population, geographic, and historical scale does the evidence apply? What alternative explanation would change the conclusion? A claim that cannot answer those questions should not be converted into a statement about race, ancestry, adaptation, or individual capacity.

### A responsible description framework

A concise description of human variation can follow five steps. First, identify the trait or variants rather than beginning with a group label. Second, define the sampled populations, dates, and recruitment. Third, report within- and between-population distributions with uncertainty. Fourth, test demography, environment, development, and social exposure at the appropriate level. Fifth, explain why any descriptor is necessary and what it does not mean [2][10].

This framework protects two forms of knowledge at once. It preserves the ability to reconstruct migrations, identify local adaptation, improve genomic resources, and investigate population-specific evidence. It also prevents those findings from being transformed into fixed racial taxonomies. Human variation is structured enough to record history, continuous enough to cross every proposed racial boundary, and dynamic enough that every description requires a time, place, sample, and purpose [1][5][6].
