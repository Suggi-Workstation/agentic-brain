# Death, Burial, and Mortuary Archaeology -- Treatment of the Dead Supports Bounded Inferences About Living Societies, Not Direct Readings of Rank

Mortuary archaeology uses graves, bodies, monuments, and the traces of decomposition to study how past communities treated their dead, and from that treatment it infers kinship, mobility, health, belief, and inequality. A burial is a set of choices made by survivors, so a grave records what mourners did with a body and not a transparent portrait of the dead person's rank or life [1][2]. Ancient DNA, isotope analysis, and Bayesian dating have made these inferences far more precise, but the same studies show that claims about political hierarchy or universal symbolism usually outrun what a partial sample of the dead can support [5][6][9].

## Background

### Burial as deliberate practice

Burials survive archaeologically because people made decisions about bodies, places, and objects. Duday, Le Mort, and Tillier state the starting premise of the French field tradition directly: to understand a burial one must bear in mind that skeletons were once corpses, so the position of a skeleton at excavation may differ from the position in which it was deposited [1]. They also criticize a habit of treating the body as an accompaniment to the furnishings: bones are often published in appendices, dissociated from the archaeological analysis, which they call an epistemological aberration because the deceased, not the brooch or vessel, is the reason the tomb exists [1]. The burial record therefore has two layers that must be separated. One layer is the acts of the mourners. The other is the later transformation of the corpse, the grave, and the site.

### Anthropological roots and the search for rank

The anthropological roots of mortuary analysis lie in work on funeral ritual as a social process. Stevenson's review names Arnold van Gennep, who analysed funerals as rites of passage, and Metcalf and Huntington, authors of a comparative treatise on mortuary ritual, as influential anthropological treatments, and it cites Robert Hertz's study of the collective representation of death as the root of later work on the active roles of mourners [2]. In the Anglo-American world an explicit archaeology of death and burial became visible in the 1970s, within a positivist climate that shifted attention from discovering cultural beliefs to modelling past social systems from mortuary evidence [2]. The best-known formulation is the Saxe-Binford approach, often cited to justify the assumption that the energy expended on a grave corresponds to the social status the occupant held in life. Tainter developed energy-expenditure models for grave facilities, and statistical measures of rank built from tomb size, prestige goods, and abstract scales of object value were applied to cemeteries, including those of Predynastic Egypt [2]. Stevenson reports that the Saxe-Binford theory was subject to extensive critique soon after publication, by Braun, by Chapman and Randsborg, by Hodder, by Pader, and by Parker Pearson, among others [2]. Later work emphasized the active roles of mourners, funeral performance, social memory, and emotion, and a starting principle shared by many recent approaches is that funerals and their material correlates are better viewed in terms of social practice than direct social record [2].

### Archaeothanatology

In France a different tradition developed over roughly three decades, alongside the growth of rescue archaeology. It reconstructs attitudes toward death by analysing the human skeleton in the field and the acts linked to the management of the corpse [1]. Boulestin and Duday proposed the term archaeothanatology in 2005, because thanatology concerns both the biological and social components of death [1]. The approach distinguishes primary burials from secondary burials. A primary burial corresponds to what anthropologists call a simple funeral: a single ceremony in which a body, still anatomically intact, is placed in its final tomb, so that decomposition happens almost entirely at the place of burial. A secondary burial corresponds to a double funeral, in which the corpse decomposes in a temporary location and the bones are later transferred to the tomb [1]. Because the two are not always easy to tell apart, the approach requires evidence that the handling of dry bones was planned from the start before a deposit is called a secondary burial [1].

### Laboratory and statistical expansion

Several developments since the 1990s changed what mortuary data can answer. Routine Bayesian modelling of radiocarbon dates, implemented by the English Heritage scientific dating team from 1993 with the OxCal software, allowed burial monuments to be dated at a generational scale rather than within centuries of uncertainty [3]. Wood and colleagues' 1992 osteological paradox drew attention to the problem of inferring the health of a living population from the skeletons of those who died [4]. Ancient DNA grew from no published genome-wide data on ancient humans in 2009 to more than six thousand individuals by the time of the 2021 ethics guidelines [10]. Isotopic methods were extended to cremated remains once fully calcined bone was shown to preserve the original strontium isotope ratio [7]. These methods raised the resolution of mortuary inference, and they also created new obligations toward the remains being sampled. A virtual workshop in November 2020 of more than sixty archaeologists, anthropologists, curators, and geneticists from 31 countries produced five globally applicable guidelines for DNA research on human remains, published in 2021 [10].

## Core Concepts

### From corpse to skeleton: taphonomy and the original deposit

Funerary taphonomy covers all the processes that affect human remains after deposition: the preservation or loss of each skeletal element and its arrangement relative to the others [1]. Decomposition starts at death and proceeds in a predictable order, so the sequence in which joints fail is itself evidence. In temperate, relatively humid burial environments the joints that break down fastest are those of the hand, the distal foot, and the cervical vertebrae, while joints that bear heavy loads, such as the lumbar, lumbosacral, sacro-iliac, knee, ankle, and tarsal joints, persist longer. The hip, although load-bearing, also breaks down quickly because the femoral head sits in the socket without powerful retaining ligaments [1].

Two inferences follow from this chronology. First, the displacement of bones beyond the volume the body originally occupied proves that a void existed around the corpse during decomposition, for example inside a coffin, a chamber, or under a rigid leather cover. If instead the rib cage and pelvis retain their original volume and labile joints remain connected, sediment filled the space progressively and the body decayed in a filled space [1]. Second, the absence of anatomical connections does not by itself demonstrate a secondary deposit, because animals, water, or the collapse of a tomb long after deposition can disturb a primary burial [1]. Duday and colleagues apply this reasoning to Neolithic burials in France and the Levant and to the Middle Palaeolithic burial at Kebara, where the skull appears to have been removed after the cranio-cervical ligaments had decayed. They state that later human manipulation is possible there, but that it cannot be proved to have been planned as a secondary deposit [1]. A strongly contracted skeleton is often read as evidence of a bound or bagged corpse, but sediment pressure can close the angles between limb segments as ligaments decay, so binding is possible but difficult to prove [1].

Collective tombs add a terminological problem. Duday and colleagues distinguish individual burials, multiple burials (bodies deposited simultaneously, which generally indicates a catastrophe such as a massacre or plague), and collective burials (corpses deposited at different times in a structure built to be reopened), and they note that the non-individual types lack commonly accepted definitions [1].

### Dating burials: the taphonomy of the sample

Chronology is as dependent on taphonomy as skeletal analysis. Bayliss emphasizes that radiocarbon dating does not date deposits; it dates organic material. The stratigraphic order of deposits can constrain the calibration of radiocarbon dates only if the dated samples died and were deposited when the context formed, and Bayliss's rule is that all samples must be presumed residual, meaning older than the deposit in which they lie, unless there is evidence that they were fresh when deposited ("Guilty until proven innocent!") [3]. In the burial chamber at Wayland's Smithy I, the relative order of articulated and partially articulated skeletons can be inferred from their positions and expressed as a stratigraphic (Harris) matrix. Modelled in a Bayesian framework, one individual (ws15) died and was buried in 3600-3550 cal BC at 84% probability or 3545-3525 cal BC at 11% probability, and the first burial in the monument is estimated at 3610-3550 cal BC (83%) or 3545-3525 cal BC (12%) [3].

Bayliss also shows why visual inspection of plotted dates misleads. When a simulated settlement was presented to an audience of archaeologists, 91% estimated its start date inaccurately and all of them overestimated its duration, because radiocarbon measurements scatter around true ages. A model that simply assumed the dates came from a coherent, uniformly distributed phase of activity bracketed the true start and end [3]. Posterior estimates blend measurements with prior beliefs, so the responsibility for checking those beliefs rests with the archaeologist, and alternative models should be run as sensitivity analyses [3]. The statistical background is covered in `library/mathematics-statistics/bayesian-statistics.md`.

### Skeletons as biological records: osteology and the osteological paradox

Osteological analysis estimates sex and age at death from pelvic and cranial morphology, epiphyseal fusion, cranial suture closure, dental development, and dental attrition, and records non-metric traits and pathology [8]. Cremation limits all of this. Among 25 cremated individuals from Stonehenge only six possible females and nine possible males could be identified, and the remainder were unsexed [7]. Genetic sexing can now cross-check osteology, as with a Bell Beaker burial whose genetic sex was female although the body lay in the position customary for males [8]. The most discussed case is chamber grave Bj.581 at Birka, Sweden, excavated in 1878 and long assumed to be an archetypal high-status warrior grave of a man; osteology and ancient DNA showed that the individual was biologically female [12]. The authors use the case to ask what links archaeologists draw between buried individuals and the objects that accompany them, and they caution that this single, unusual grave cannot be made to bear an infinite burden of expectation [12].

The deeper difficulty is inference from a sample of the dead to a population of the living. McFadden and colleagues summarize the osteological paradox of Wood and colleagues (1992): archaeologists never observe the demographic and disease processes that produce a cemetery, and many different processes can yield indistinguishable skeletal data sets, especially with small samples [4]. Three issues drive this. Hidden heterogeneity means that individuals differ in susceptibility to disease and death. Selective mortality means that at each age the people who entered the cemetery are likely to have been sicker than the general population. Non-stationarity means that migration, fertility, and mortality fluctuate, so populations are neither stable nor stationary [4]. The practical result is that a skeletal lesion has no single meaning: it shows survival long enough to develop a lesion, whereas its absence can mean frailty, resilience, or lack of exposure. In the authors' words, it is possible for healthier populations to leave behind sicker looking skeletons [4].

Responses fall into several groups. Many studies add a brief caveat and then proceed with conventional interpretation, which the review identifies as the most common response. Others shift to comparative claims between sites or periods, which remain vulnerable if the groups differ in unobservable ways. Osteobiography, the study of individual lives, still depends on survivorship, and a skeptical minority withhold inference [4]. Quantitative responses include survival and hazards analysis, cumulative skeletal frailty indices, and a hazards-based index built separately for each site [4]. McFadden and colleagues propose agent-based modelling to simulate the unobserved processes. This is a proposal for future work, not a demonstrated resolution [4].

### Isotopes: diet and mobility

Strontium isotope ratios (87Sr/86Sr) in bone and teeth reflect the geology on which food was grown. Tooth enamel preserves a childhood signal, whereas the strontium in bone reflects an average of the foods eaten over roughly the last decade of life [7]. Interpretation requires a baseline of biologically available strontium, which does not map directly onto bedrock. Around Stonehenge the Wessex chalk gives modern plant values of 0.7074-0.7090 for at least 15 km in all directions, while the Ordovician and Silurian rocks of west Wales give 0.7095-0.7120 [7].

Isotopes are probabilistic evidence. Strontium values alone cannot distinguish places with similar geology, and a migrant who arrived more than about ten years before death would register as local in bone [7]. Oxygen isotopes in tooth enamel were too coarse to pinpoint origins at a regional level in the Bell Beaker cemeteries, although higher values indicate more maritime influence [8]. In cremated bone, carbon isotopes reflect the fuel of the pyre rather than diet, which allowed the Stonehenge team to argue that lower values in non-local individuals reflect wood from denser woodland [7]. No single isotopic system identifies where a person was born; each constrains the plausible set of origins.

### Ancient DNA and the reconstruction of kinship

Genome-wide ancient DNA allows researchers to estimate pairwise genetic relatedness, to classify relatives as first-, second-, or third-degree, to identify maternal and paternal lineages from mitochondrial and Y-chromosome haplogroups, and to measure inbreeding from runs of homozygosity, which reveal how closely related an individual's parents were [5][6]. At Hazleton North the pedigree was built by triangulating pairwise relatedness, uniparental markers, genetic sex, inbreeding, and age at death, and then validated against independent X-chromosome and shared-segment evidence [5].

Biological relatedness is not the same as kinship. Fowler distinguishes the pedigree, which is the set of people connected by biological descent, from the patriline, which is a social categorization, and uses the term elective kin for kinship not based on biological reproduction or marriage [9]. The same article argues that co-burial of a biological parent and offspring does not prove a nuclear family, because child-rearing may be distributed among several people and fostering is common, and it criticizes the use of the term nuclear family in archaeogenetic studies without good evidence [9]. The same article still regards it as reasonable to read a co-buried biological progenitor and offspring as parent and child, provided the relationship is not treated as exclusive [9]. Kinship terms attached to genetic results are interpretations layered on a biological finding. A separate study of the post-Roman cemetery at Worth Matravers, Dorset, sampled 18 of 26 excavated individuals, including everyone in one triple and three double burials. It found that biological kin were placed near one another, and also that unrelated individuals shared double or triple burials, which the authors say potentially highlights social kinship or cultural perceptions of kinship [11].

### Grave goods, wealth, and the limits of the mirror model

Stevenson identifies two assumptions that recur in status-based interpretation. The first is a direct link between an individual and the objects in the grave, as when copper chisels are taken to identify a woodworker. The second is that displayed belongings express the wealth, status, and identity of the deceased [2]. Against the first, some items are plausibly offerings from the surviving community, such as gifts or tributes; at Adaima, bracelets were too large for the infant arms on which they lay and some necklaces were overly long [2]. Against the second, identities are built from multiple relationships, including kinship, class, ethnicity, gender, age, and occupation, so it is unlikely that social identity will be mirrored directly by material culture [2]. Grave goods that are worn, repaired, broken, or incomplete may have moved from the living to the dead with their histories attached, which makes static scales of object value unstable [2]. A regional contrast supports the same caution against reading one meaning into burial. Stevenson contrasts Upper Egyptian graves, treated as arenas for display with rich grave furniture, with the graves of the Maadi groups in Lower Egypt, which held few objects and made the body the singular focus; she cautions that this does not make the Maadian communities less complex, since their management of death may have taken place away from the grave or in intangible or ephemeral ways [2]. The relation of gifts to burial is developed in `library/anthropology/gift-economies-and-reciprocity.md`.

Stevenson does not discard quantitative methods; she argues that they are a background against which qualitative evaluation of contexts proceeds [2]. The author's assessment is that grave wealth is best treated as one indicator among several, to be read alongside body treatment, grave location, and demographic selection, and not as a ranking score.

### Sampling, preservation, and who is missing

A cemetery is a selection of the dead. At Hazleton North, osteological analysis found a minimum of 41 individuals, and 35 yielded genome-wide data from 74 samples. Among 66 samples that passed authenticity criteria there were 31 genetic duplicates, which the authors take to show that the sampling captured a good fraction of those whose remains were recovered, although minimum-number estimates can underestimate the number present [5]. In the Bell Beaker cemeteries, young children are under-represented relative to expectation for a pre-industrial society, and the authors infer that communities selected which children received a burial in the communal cemetery; at one cemetery only three adult men were present against seven women, suggesting that some members were absent or ritually invisible [8]. At Hazleton North, 26 of 35 sampled individuals were genetically male, and none of the sampled adults were daughters of the lineage, so the authors infer that the remains of some women were treated in another way [5]. Cremation, looting, poor preservation, and the loss of skeletal collections impose further filters; the Predynastic cemetery at Gerzeh was one of the least plundered, with 88.3% of interments intact, but its bones fell apart on excavation and are no longer available for study [2]. Commingled remains, as in many collective tombs, make it difficult to assess frailty or lesion patterns because the analytical unit is no longer the individual [4].

### Stewardship, consent, and descendant communities

The 2021 guidelines of Alpaslan-Roodenberg and colleagues state that researchers must follow all regulations where they work and where the remains originated; prepare a detailed plan before any study; minimize damage to remains; make data available after publication so that findings can be critically re-examined; and engage stakeholders from the beginning [10]. The plan should state research questions, the techniques and expected impact on remains, the type of data, material sharing, the return of unused material, dissemination, and data storage, and researchers become stewards of the remains for the agreed purpose without transfer of ownership [10]. Minimizing damage matters especially because the petrous bone yields many times more DNA than other elements; researchers should sample no more than needed, document sampling, and report negative results [10].

The guidelines hold that ethical engagement is context-specific. In the United States the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act obliges institutions to consult and seek to transfer ancient Native American remains, and Australian laws seek repatriation of remains in some cases up to 40,000 years old, but the authors argue that an Indigenous-centred template that associates each ancient individual with a contemporary group does not fit every setting. In Mexico and Peru, where Indigenous heritage is embedded in national institutions, they describe government or heritage-body approval as potentially a robust form of engagement and a US template as potentially counterproductive. In Africa, South Asia, and West Eurasia they judge that using Indigenous identity or claims of local origin to decide who may permit research would probably be harmful, because it can contribute to conflict, discrimination, or xenophobia [10]. They also state that researchers must accept a negative answer if stakeholders are not collectively supportive, and that genetic data are inappropriate as an arbiter of identity [10]. Repatriation law and institutional practice are treated in `library/anthropology/museum-repatriation-cultural-heritage.md`.

## Evidence

### Hazleton North (England): a five-generation pedigree in a chambered tomb

Fowler and colleagues combined archaeological and genetic analyses of 35 individuals entombed about 5,700 years ago in the Hazleton North long cairn in Gloucestershire. Taphonomic observations showed varied treatment: bones of more than five individuals in the north chambered area had been gnawed by scavengers, which suggests exposure before deposition; cremated remains of an infant, a child, and an adult were placed in the north entrance; and remains in the south chambers were more commingled [5]. Twenty-seven individuals formed a single pedigree spanning five generations, about three times larger than the largest earlier ancient-DNA pedigrees [5]. All 15 intergenerational connections in the later generations ran through fathers (13) or stepfathers (2), a pattern the authors test with a two-sided binomial test (P = 0.000061). Twenty-six of 35 sampled individuals were male, and there were no adult daughters of the lineage against 14 adult sons [5]. One man had children with four women, and descendants of the women sorted spatially: all 12 individuals from two maternal sub-lineages lay in the south chambers, and 9 of 13 from the other two lay in the north [5].

The authors interpret this as patrilineal descent with virilocal burial, grouping of maternal sub-lineages into branches recognized in the tomb's design, and possible adoption of stepsons into the patriline, and they note that eight individuals were not close biological relatives. They also state that Hazleton North cannot be considered a template for all Neolithic chambered tombs [5]. In a later re-assessment Fowler treats patrilineal descent as one strand in the fabric of kinship, offers alternative readings of the stepsons (for example, inclusion through their mothers' placement in the tomb), and states that where the women born to the lineage were buried is not yet known [9]. Bayesian modelling of radiocarbon dates suggested that the tomb was in use for up to three generations, whereas the DNA documents five generations in the south chamber; the main text of the paper reports this as an additional insight without resolving the difference [5].

### Newgrange and the Irish tombs: one extreme genome, several readings

Cassidy and colleagues sequenced 44 genomes, 42 Neolithic and two Mesolithic, from remains sampled at 20 sites representing the major Irish Neolithic funerary traditions. In the most elaborately decorated recess of the Newgrange passage tomb, disarticulated unburnt bone included the cranial remains of an adult male (NG10). His genome carried long runs of homozygosity totalling a quarter of the genome, which identifies him as the child of a first-degree union; simulations could not distinguish full siblings from parent and child [6]. The authors argue that his parentage was very likely socially sanctioned given the setting of his remains, compare it with documented dynastic incest in Hawaii, the Inca empire, and ancient Egypt, and propose a hierarchical elite. They add that people from passage tombs up to 150 km apart were distant relatives (sixth degree or more), formed a distinct haplotype cluster, and had high d15N and depleted d13C values that the authors interpret as a diet rich in meat and animal products [6]. In the same study, a pair of neighbouring tombs differed in Y-haplogroup frequency (P = 0.035, Fisher's exact test), and an infant at Poulnabrone carried a trisomy of chromosome 21, which the authors describe as the earliest definitive case of Down syndrome [6]. The authors themselves note that denser sampling of Late Neolithic sites is needed to evaluate temporal drift as a cause of the clustering [6].

Fowler argues that this dynastic reading rests on a selective use of analogy, that access to large tombs may have been arranged on a rotating basis, and that the sample-to-minimum-number ratios at the passage tombs are low (for example 6 of 29 at the Carrowkeel tombs), so that relatedness could be missed [9]. Fowler cites an alternative reading by Carlin and colleagues, in which distantly related people interacted at a greater geographical scale than before, and concludes that genetic results of this kind bring us no closer to determining whether Neolithic communities were hierarchical [9]. Fowler is also the lead author of the Hazleton North study, so this is a critique from within the same research field and not from an independent group. The disagreement is over the interpretation of the same observations, not over the observations.

### Stonehenge: strontium in cremated bone

Excavations in 1919-26 recovered the cremated remains of up to 58 individuals at Stonehenge, which were re-buried in Aubrey Hole 7 and re-excavated in 2008; occipital fragments from at least 25 individuals, dated between 3180-2965 and 2565-2380 BC, were analysed [7]. Cremation destroys enamel, the standard material for strontium analysis, but fully calcined bone preserves the original 87Sr/86Sr ratio of the last decade or so of life. Ratios in the 25 individuals ranged from 0.7078 to 0.7118. Fifteen fell within the local chalk range, and ten (40%) did not, with the highest values pointing to considerably older rocks, such as those of southwest England and Wales, and the authors consider west Wales, the source of the bluestones more than 200 km away, the most plausible origin [7]. Non-local individuals also had lower d13C, which the authors read as pyre wood from denser woodland, so that some people were probably cremated elsewhere and their remains carried to the monument [7]. The authors state the limits of the data: strontium cannot separate places with similar values, long-term migrants would appear local, the 87Sr/86Sr values do not track radiocarbon date, and the interpretation of the strontium concentration difference needs further research [7].

### Irlbach and Alburg: two Bell Beaker cemeteries

Sjogren and colleagues integrated archaeology, osteology, DNA, and isotopes from two Late Copper Age cemeteries in Lower Bavaria, 17 km apart, with 24 and 18 burials of which 34 yielded genetic data. The burials date to roughly 2300-2150 BC on the basis of only four radiocarbon dates, whose calibrated ranges were too wide to refine the use-life, so relative dating relied on grave goods [8]. Nearly all burials were individual inhumations with a gendered body position (females crouched on the right side, males on the left), and goods were modest, with only one small copper dagger and few signs of social differentiation by prestige objects [8]. All males with sufficient data carried one Y-chromosome lineage, whereas 34 individuals carried 23 mitochondrial haplotypes, and six of eight isotopic outliers were women [8]. The authors infer patrilocal residence and female exogamy, note that the Y uniformity does not by itself imply close paternal kinship because the lineage arose centuries earlier, and propose a larger model of expansionist kinship institutions, including fostering and parallels with reconstructed Proto-Indo-European kinship [8]. That model is the authors' interpretation, and they call their case study a test case for future comparative work [8]. They also identify a selection problem that complicates any demographic claim: children are under-represented, the buried juveniles are mostly boys, and sex could not be determined for all 16 non-adults [8].

### Gerzeh (Egypt): two rich graves on different scales

Stevenson compared two graves from the Predynastic cemetery of Gerzeh. Grave 133 held over 600 beads and one of the most diverse bead assemblages in the cemetery, including lapis lazuli, obsidian, gold, carnelian, and shells from both the Red Sea and the Mediterranean, but only ten ceramic vessels (the cemetery average was eight) and a modest grave of 1.1 square metres. Grave 220 held 37 ceramic vessels and nothing else [2]. Stevenson notes that if grave 133 had been plundered of its exotic goods, as many Predynastic graves were, counts of pots or grave size would have hidden its significance, and a single wealth scale does not explain how the two assemblages differ qualitatively. Stevenson argues that they point to different pathways of power, one tied to local production and one to exchange networks and distant knowledge, and that models of social complexity should consider horizontal as well as vertical differentiation [2]. This reading is interpretive and the author states its limits: sex data are unavailable for Gerzeh, the human remains are no longer available for study, and the suggestions must remain tentative [2].

### Comparative cases

Fowler's comparison with the Gurgy cemetery in France and the Fralsegarden tomb in Sweden shows variation: half-siblings and maternal descent clusters occur at Hazleton North, only men had multiple reproductive partners at Fralsegarden, and no half-siblings were detected at Gurgy, as summarized from the original studies [9]. At each site the burial ground was used for about a century or less [9]. A larger pedigree study of four Avar-period cemeteries in present-day Hungary (424 individuals sampled, with pedigrees spanning nine generations and about 300 individuals) reported strict patrilineality with patrilocality and female exogamy, multiple reproductive partners, levirate unions, and no consanguinity [13]. Avar-period populations settled the Carpathian Basin from AD 567-568 for about 250 years [13]. The author's assessment is that this is a comparison with the Neolithic cases and not a test of them, because the society, period, and mortuary setting differ.

## Implications

### For excavators and curators of human remains

The evidence implies that field recording determines what can later be inferred. Archaeothanatology requires recording of the arrangement of each bone, the state of articulation, and the sediment relations while the grave is open, by people trained both in excavation and in human osteology [1]. Without that record a later analyst cannot distinguish a primary burial from a secondary deposit, a void from a filled space, or an ancient manipulation from modern disturbance. Archives can also repay revisiting. Archaeothanatological analysis of rediscovered photographs of thirteen burials excavated in 1960 and 1962 at the Mesolithic shell middens of Pocas de S. Bento and Arapouco in the Sado Valley, Portugal, led the authors to propose that some bodies were mummified before burial. For one burial (ARA1962, unknown 3) they argue that the combination of the body's position and preserved labile foot articulations suggests that it was wrapped and may have been desiccated through mummification before burial. It is a single case with an uncertain excavation context number, so they cannot say whether the practice was exceptional or common [14]. Dating should be designed around a model: Bayliss recommends simulating the chronology before submitting samples, choosing short-lived material demonstrably not residual, and running alternative models, and she reports that projects that select samples around the model produce more precise and cost-effective chronologies than those that graft a model onto existing dates [3]. For skeletal samples the osteological paradox implies that comparative statements about health should state their assumptions about frailty and selective mortality at the outset, and not invoke the paradox after an unexpected result [4].

### For museums, regulators, and descendant communities

For institutions that hold remains and for scientists who request samples, the guidelines convert general respect into specific procedures: a written plan understood by non-specialists, documented sampling, a timeline for returning unused material, negative-result reporting to prevent repeated destructive sampling, and data access sufficient for independent re-examination [10]. The guidelines also locate authority in stakeholders. Researchers must accept a negative answer, and they should not publish a result that cannot be shared in a way that avoids substantial harm to a stakeholder group, but they should not make publication depend on approval by groups outside the research team, a position the authors defend as a requirement of scientific independence [10]. The Hazleton North sampling shows the practical side of this regime, since the authors obtained permission from the museum that held the bones before sampling petrous bones and teeth [5]. Regulatory regimes differ: the Bell Beaker study reports that no permits were required in its German context [8], and the guidelines say that where regulations are insufficient researchers should adopt the higher standard in the guidelines [10]. The author's assessment is that a research design is incomplete if it treats the legal minimum as the ethical standard.

### For historians of inequality, kinship, and political organization

Researchers who use burials as data for large questions, such as the origin of hierarchy, should not treat a grave as a record of rank. Stevenson's Gerzeh comparison shows two graves that a single wealth scale cannot order [2], and Fowler's argument is that identifying descent traced in the placement of the dead does not identify a form of political organization, because the proportion of the community using a tomb and the meaning of tomb access are unknown [9]. At the same time, mortuary data can test specific questions that other sources cannot. The Hazleton North pedigree provides direct evidence that at least some Neolithic tombs were organized around kinship [5], the Stonehenge strontium data show long-distance movement of bodies or people [7], and the Bell Beaker cemeteries show that exogamy and a patrilineal pattern coexisted with modest grave goods [8]. The implication is a division of labour: mortuary evidence is strong for the mourners' practices and for biological relations among the buried, weaker for the beliefs of the dead and for the structure of the whole society.

### For readers of headline claims

Journalistic and governmental summaries have sometimes misrepresented ancient-DNA findings for political ends [10], and the author's assessment is that even accurate summaries compress a chain of inferences into one sentence. A reader can ask five questions of any such claim. What fraction of the burial population was sampled, compared with the minimum number of individuals [5][9]? How were the dated samples related to the burial event, and was uncertainty modelled [3]? Which statements are observations, such as a relatedness coefficient or a strontium ratio, and which are interpretations, such as a dynastic elite or an expansionist kinship system [6][8]? Does the author acknowledge alternative explanations, as the guidelines and several of the studies do [5][10]? Has the claim been tested in a second cemetery or region? Fowler observes that new analyses suggesting matrilineal descent at Catalhoyuk and a female-biased sex ratio in some tombs at Panoria in Spain appeared while the article was being drafted, and concludes that patrilineal descent may prove rare or widespread, so the key discipline is to avoid sweeping conclusions from sparse results [9].

### For students of bioarchaeology and health

For anyone using skeletal lesion frequencies as a health indicator, the main implication of the osteological paradox is that frequency data need an explicit model of who enters the sample. Indices of frailty, survival analysis, and eventually simulation make assumptions testable, but they do not remove the need for large, well-preserved samples, and McFadden and colleagues state that statistical methods are limited where preservation is poor and samples are small [4]. The practical posture they recommend is to integrate multiple lines of evidence, including isotopes, DNA, and archaeological context, and to treat uncertainty as a problem to be quantified and not merely acknowledged [4].

### For comparative research programmes

The case studies imply that the unit of progress is the comparison, not the single spectacular site. Fowler argues that kinship practices cannot be distinguished, and variation and change cannot be seen, without studying many sites from each sub-period of a region at high coverage and sampling as many individuals as possible at each site [9]. Bayliss makes a parallel point about dating: a precise date is just a number, and dates become meaningful only when they allow comparison between events, which is why about fifteen years of accumulated well-dated sites were needed before the impact of Bayesian chronology on English prehistory became apparent [3]. Taken together, the two arguments imply that one tomb, however well analysed, shows what a particular community did but not how typical it was, and the Hazleton North authors state this limit explicitly [5][9]. The author's assessment is that regional programmes combining DNA, isotopes, osteology, and Bayesian chronology at several sites per period are the design most likely to turn the present case studies into general knowledge about mortuary practice.

## Common Pitfalls

Several recurring errors are documented in the sources. Treating the absence of anatomical connections as proof of a secondary deposit ignores later disturbance [1]. Treating radiocarbon samples as contemporary with the context that contains them is, in Bayliss's account, the commonest source of error in chronological models [3]. Reading Y-chromosome uniformity as proof of close paternal kinship ignores the age of the lineage [8]. Equating biological relatedness with kinship, or the co-burial of a biological parent and child with a nuclear family, substitutes a genetic finding for a social one [9]. Inferring a burial's sex from its weapons or other grave goods, without skeletal or genetic sexing, can misassign the sex of the dead, as at Birka Bj.581 [12]. Using a single scale of wealth to rank graves obscures differences in kind [2]. Inferring population health from lesion frequency without considering selective mortality repeats the problem identified in the osteological paradox [4].

## Criticisms

The processual search for rank was subject to extensive critique soon after publication. Stevenson reports that Egyptologists generally acknowledged that debate without changing their quantitative socio-economic models, and her own objection is that burial form and content cannot be assumed to reflect individual status or to index social ranking [2]. The osteological paradox is more often cited than engaged: McFadden and colleagues report that it is frequently invoked superficially or as a post hoc explanation for contradictory results, and they contrast a pessimistic view that regards it as insurmountable with an optimistic view that regards it as a driver of better method [4]. Fowler reports that archaeogenetic claims of patrilineal descent have drawn concern, and responds by treating descent as one strand of kinship and by criticizing the terminology of nuclear family and of kinship-system categories [9]. The 2021 ethics guidelines themselves reject proposals that stakeholder groups outside the research team approve manuscripts before publication, citing earlier suggestions to that effect, so the allocation of authority between researchers and communities remains contested [10].
