# Political Anthropology -- Hierarchy Is Not Our Default Setting, It Had to Be Invented

Political anthropology examines how human societies organize power,
make collective decisions, and build or resist hierarchy. The central
finding of the field is that the institutions modern people take as
natural -- formal law, professional police, permanent leadership,
inherited wealth -- are recent inventions. For approximately 97% of
Homo sapiens history, humans lived in small, mobile, egalitarian bands
without formal leadership of any kind. The state, with its monopoly on
legitimate violence, has existed for roughly 0.3% of human history.
Hierarchy is not humanity's default political setting; it had to be
invented, and it is continuously contested.

## Background

Political anthropology emerged as a distinct subfield in the mid-20th
century, driven by the recognition that Western political categories
-- states, laws, elections, parties -- could not describe how most
human societies throughout history actually governed themselves. The
foundational question was straightforward: if you do not have a
government, a police force, or a written legal code, how do you
maintain order, resolve disputes, and make collective decisions?

The intellectual roots reach back to 19th-century evolutionary
anthropology. Lewis Henry Morgan's "Ancient Society" (1877) proposed
that human societies progressed through stages from "savagery" through
"barbarism" to "civilization," with political organization growing
more complex at each stage. This unilinear evolutionism was later
rejected for its ethnocentrism and its assumption that contemporary
hunter-gatherers were "living fossils" of earlier stages. But the core
insight -- that political organization takes fundamentally different
forms under different demographic, technological, and ecological
conditions -- survived the critique.

The modern framework was established in the 1960s and 1970s. Elman
Service's "Primitive Social Organization" (1962) and "Origins of the
State and Civilization" (1975) proposed a four-tier typology of
political integration: bands, tribes, chiefdoms, and states. Morton
Fried's "The Evolution of Political Society" (1967) offered a parallel
classification based on social relationships and hierarchy:
egalitarian, ranked, stratified, and state societies. Together, these
frameworks gave political anthropology a comparative vocabulary that
could be applied across cultures without imposing Western assumptions.

A critical breakthrough came with Marshall Sahlins' "Poor Man, Rich
Man, Big-man, Chief: Political Types in Melanesia and Polynesia"
(1963), which demonstrated that the difference between achieved
leadership (the Melanesian Big Man who earns authority through
generosity) and ascribed leadership (the Polynesian chief who inherits
it through genealogy) was not a difference of degree but of kind. The
two systems represented fundamentally different political logics.

Christopher Boehm's "Hierarchy in the Forest: The Evolution of
Egalitarian Behavior" (1999) added a further dimension: the concept of
"reverse dominance hierarchy." Boehm argued that egalitarian band
societies are not simply "without hierarchy" -- they actively suppress
it. Egalitarianism is not a passive absence of domination but an
active, collective political project maintained through leveling
mechanisms: gossip, ridicule, ostracism, and, in extreme cases,
assassination of would-be dominators.

## Core Concepts

### Elman Service's Four Levels of Political Integration

Service's typology remains the most widely taught framework in
political anthropology, despite decades of refinement and critique.
It classifies societies by their level of socio-cultural integration
-- essentially, how large and complex the political unit is and how
decisions get made within it.

**Bands** are the smallest and oldest form of political organization,
typically consisting of 20-50 people related by kinship. Bands are
associated with foraging (hunting-gathering) economies and are
characteristically egalitarian. There are no formal leadership
positions, no hereditary offices, and no specialized political roles.
Decisions are made by consensus. If someone attempts to dominate
others, the group deploys leveling mechanisms: ridicule, gossip,
ostracism, or simply walking away. Leadership, to the extent it
exists, is situational and based on skill -- the best hunter might
lead a hunt, the most experienced elder might mediate a dispute --
but this influence does not extend beyond the specific context and
carries no coercive authority. The Ju/'hoansi (!Kung San) of the
Kalahari, documented extensively by Richard Borshay Lee, are the
archetypal example, though hundreds of band societies have been
documented across every inhabited continent.

**Tribes** are larger, typically encompassing several hundred to a
few thousand people organized around kinship and shared culture.
Unlike bands, which are nomadic foragers, tribes are often associated
with horticulture or pastoralism. The key political innovation of the
tribe is the segmentary lineage system: society is organized into
nested kinship groups (lineages, clans) that provide a framework for
alliance and conflict. When two families dispute, their respective
lineages back them; when two lineages dispute, their clans back them.
This "pyramid of segments" can mobilize large numbers for warfare
while maintaining local autonomy in peacetime. Leadership in tribal
societies is typically achieved rather than ascribed: "Big Men" in
Melanesia, for instance, build influence through gift-giving, feast-
hosting, and oratory, but their authority depends entirely on
continued personal performance. No one owes them compliance by right
of birth or office. The position is not institutionalized -- when a
Big Man stops delivering, his followers drift away. Tribes have been
documented extensively in New Guinea, Amazonia, and precolonial North
America.

**Chiefdoms** represent a qualitative break: for the first time,
inequality becomes institutionalized. A chiefdom is a regional polity
comprising multiple communities under the authority of a hereditary
chief who holds an office that persists beyond the individual
incumbent. The chief controls redistribution: surplus goods flow from
producers to the chief, who then reallocates them for public works,
feasts, warfare, and personal prestige. Chiefly authority is often
buttressed by ideology -- in Polynesia, chiefs possessed mana, a
sacred power that legitimized their rank and made their persons
taboo. The office of chief is permanent; when the chief dies, a
successor is installed according to genealogical rules. This
institutional permanence is what separates chiefdoms from tribes: in
a tribe, when a Big Man dies, his influence dies with him; in a
chiefdom, the office survives. Chiefdoms vary enormously in scale,
from simple chiefdoms controlling a few villages to paramount
chiefdoms like precontact Hawaii, which integrated entire
archipelagoes under a single ruling lineage.

**States** are the most complex and recent form of political
organization. A state is a centralized political unit with a
monopoly on the legitimate use of force within a defined territory,
a bureaucratic administrative apparatus, a system of taxation, and
(formally or informally) a legal code. The key difference from
chiefdoms is not merely scale but structure: states have specialized
institutions -- courts, tax collectors, standing armies, record-
keepers -- that persist independently of any individual officeholder.
Social stratification becomes class-based rather than kinship-based.
The state emerged independently in at least six regions (Mesopotamia,
Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes) beginning
roughly 5,500 years ago, in each case following the development of
intensive agriculture and the capacity to generate, store, and
control surplus.

### Achieved vs. Ascribed Leadership -- The Melanesia/Polynesia Divide

Sahlins' (1963) comparison of Melanesian Big Men and Polynesian chiefs
illuminates the fundamental distinction between achieved and ascribed
leadership. The Melanesian Big Man builds his position from nothing:
he must work harder, produce more, and give more generously than
anyone else. His authority is a continuous performance that must be
renewed daily through gift exchange, feast sponsorship, and persuasive
speech. If he stops performing, his followers leave. The Polynesian
chief, by contrast, occupies an office that exists independently of
his personal qualities. His authority comes from genealogy, not
generosity. He has rights of call on the labor and produce of others
by virtue of his position, not his personality. Sahlins argued that
this distinction represents an evolutionary threshold: once political
authority is detached from personal performance and attached to an
office, the ceiling on political centralization is broken, and
states become possible.

### Reverse Dominance Hierarchy

Christopher Boehm's (1993, 1999) concept of reverse dominance
hierarchy provides a compelling explanation for why egalitarian
societies are egalitarian. The standard assumption had been that
egalitarianism represents a "baseline" -- the natural state of humans
before hierarchy developed. Boehm argued the opposite: the natural
state, observed in chimpanzees and bonobos (our closest relatives), is
dominance hierarchy. Alpha individuals claim priority access to
resources and mates. What makes humans distinctive is not the absence
of hierarchy but the collective capacity to resist it. In a reverse
dominance hierarchy, the subordinate majority bands together to
suppress would-be alphas. The result is an egalitarian social order
maintained through constant vigilance and active enforcement.
Leveling mechanisms are not optional cultural decorations; they are
the political machinery that keeps hierarchy at bay.

### Leveling Mechanisms

Leveling mechanisms are the concrete practices through which
egalitarian societies prevent the emergence of permanent inequality.
The most famous example is the !Kung practice of "insulting the meat,"
documented by Richard Lee. When a hunter returns with a large kill,
his bandmates do not praise him; they disparage the meat as scrawny,
tough, or barely worth eating. When Lee, confused by this behavior,
asked why they insulted a successful hunter's contribution, he was
told: "When a young man kills much meat, he comes to think of himself
as a chief or a big man, and he thinks of the rest of us as his
servants or inferiors. We refuse one who boasts, for someday his pride
will make him kill somebody. So we always speak of his meat as
worthless. This way we cool his heart and make him gentle."

Other leveling mechanisms include: gossip and ridicule to cut down
anyone who acts superior; demand sharing (anyone can demand a share of
another's resources, making accumulation impossible); mobility (if
unhappy, families or individuals simply leave the band and join
another); and, in extreme cases, sanctioned killing of persistently
aggressive individuals. These are not primitive reflexes but
sophisticated political technologies for maintaining an intentional
social order.

### The Origin of Hierarchy -- Agriculture or Ideology First?

A major debate in political anthropology concerns the causal sequence
linking agriculture, surplus, and hierarchy. The standard model,
associated with V. Gordon Childe's "Neolithic Revolution" concept,
holds that agriculture enabled surplus production, surplus enabled
wealth accumulation, and wealth accumulation enabled hierarchy.
In this view, hierarchy is an unintended consequence of technological
change: once people settled down and produced more than they could
immediately consume, inequality became possible, then inevitable.

The revisionist challenge, strengthened by discoveries at Gobekli
Tepe in southeastern Turkey (c. 9600 BCE), argues the opposite:
complex social organization requiring coordinated labor -- the
construction of monumental stone enclosures with carved megaliths --
emerged before agriculture, among hunter-gatherers. The Gobekli Tepe
builders were not farmers; they were foragers who somehow organized
the labor to quarry, transport, and erect multi-ton stone pillars.
Klaus Schmidt, the site's excavator, argued this inverts the standard
model: "First came the temple, then the city." In the revisionist
view, ideological and ritual imperatives drove the organizational
complexity that made agriculture possible, rather than agriculture
driving the organizational complexity that made hierarchy possible.
Graeber and Wengrow's "The Dawn of Everything" (2021) expands this
argument across multiple archaeological cases, contending that
prehistoric societies deliberately experimented with different
political forms -- oscillating seasonally between egalitarian and
hierarchical arrangements -- and that the linear-evolutionary model
obscures a richer, more contingent human story.

## Ethnographic Cases

### The Ju/'hoansi (!Kung San) -- The Egalitarian Baseline

The Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari Desert, studied extensively by Richard
Lee, are the most thoroughly documented egalitarian band society.
Living in groups of 20-40, they subsist on gathered plant foods
(which provide 60-80% of calories) and hunted game. Leadership is
informal and situational. Lee documented that when he asked who the
group's headman was, his informants found the question puzzling: "One
of us is headman, all of us are headmen." The leveling mechanism of
insulting the meat is just one element of a comprehensive egalitarian
political culture that includes demand sharing, gift exchange
obligations, and a deeply held ethos of humility. The Ju/'hoansi
demonstrate that political egalitarianism is not a fantasy or a
philosophical ideal -- it is a functioning, durable social system that
sustained human communities for the vast majority of our species'
existence.

### Melanesian Big Men -- Achieved Authority

In highland New Guinea and other parts of Melanesia, political
organization takes the form of Big Man systems. A Big Man builds
influence through the moka exchange cycle: he accumulates pigs, shell
valuables, and other goods, then gives them away in elaborate
ceremonial feasts. Recipients are obligated to reciprocate with
interest, creating spiraling cycles of competitive generosity. The
Big Man's authority is entirely personal and contingent. He has no
formal office, no coercive power, and no hereditary claim. His
followers are free agents who can shift their allegiance at any time.
The system produces a distinctive political psychology: ambition and
generosity are fused, and leadership is inseparable from debt.

### Polynesian Chiefdoms -- Ascribed Authority

Polynesian societies, from Tonga to Hawaii, represent the fullest
development of chiefdom organization. Rank was hereditary, sanctified
by mana, and reinforced by elaborate kapu (taboo) systems that
regulated everything from who could eat with whom to which foods were
permitted for which ranks. Hawaiian paramount chiefs (ali'i nui)
controlled land redistribution, organized warfare, and were treated
as semi-divine beings whose touch and shadow carried ritual danger.
The transition from achieved to ascribed leadership -- from the Big
Man who earns authority to the chief who inherits it -- marks one of
the most consequential political transformations in human history.

## Evidence

The empirical foundation of political anthropology rests on several
types of evidence.

**Ethnographic evidence** from living societies provides the most
direct data. Richard Lee's decades of fieldwork with the Ju/'hoansi
(1963-present) documented egalitarian decision-making, leveling
mechanisms, and the absence of coercive authority in meticulous
detail. Lee's "The Dobe Ju/'hoansi" (2003, now in its fourth edition)
is a landmark of ethnographic documentation. Similarly, Marshall
Sahlins' fieldwork in Polynesia and Melanesia, synthesized in "Stone
Age Economics" (1972), provided the comparative basis for
distinguishing achieved from ascribed leadership. These are not
speculative reconstructions but direct observations of living
political systems.

**Archaeological evidence** reveals the deep timeline of political
organization. The Gobekli Tepe excavations (Schmidt, 1995-2014)
demonstrate that monumental construction requiring coordinated labor
occurred c. 9600 BCE, before the domestication of plants and animals
in the region. The site's T-shaped pillars, some weighing over 10
tons, were quarried, transported, and erected by hunter-gatherers
using stone tools. This challenges the assumption that complex
organization requires agriculture. At Catalhoyuk in Anatolia
(c. 7100-5700 BCE), thousands of people lived in densely packed
houses without evidence of centralized authority -- no public
buildings, no administrative structures, no elite residences --
suggesting that large, sedentary populations do not inevitably
produce hierarchy.

**Cross-cultural statistical analysis** provides systematic evidence.
The Standard Cross-Cultural Sample (Murdock and White, 1969) and the
Ethnographic Atlas (Murdock, 1967) code hundreds of societies across
dozens of variables, allowing researchers to test hypotheses about
the correlates of political complexity. These databases have
consistently confirmed correlations between: agricultural intensity
and political hierarchy; population density and the presence of
hereditary offices; and settlement size and the complexity of dispute
resolution mechanisms. However, they also reveal significant
exceptions -- societies that break the expected patterns -- which
caution against deterministic models.

**Comparative primatology** provides an evolutionary baseline. Among
chimpanzees, dominance hierarchies are pronounced: alpha males
achieve their position through aggression and coalition-building and
enjoy priority access to food and mates. Among bonobos, female
coalitions suppress male aggression and maintain a more egalitarian
social order. Neither species, however, exhibits the systematic,
culturally transmitted leveling mechanisms found in human band
societies. This suggests that the human capacity for deliberate,
collective suppression of hierarchy is an evolutionary novelty, not
a phylogenetic inheritance from a common ancestor.

**Experimental evidence** from economic games conducted across
cultures further supports the claim that political organization
shapes psychological dispositions. Henrich et al. (2001, 2005), in
a landmark study across 15 small-scale societies, found that
behavior in the Ultimatum Game and Dictator Game varies dramatically
by social structure. Societies with higher levels of market
integration and cooperative food production made fairer offers and
were more willing to punish unfair behavior, even at a cost to
themselves. This suggests that norms of fairness and the willingness
to enforce them -- both central to egalitarian political order -- are
culturally evolved, not genetically hardwired, and they vary
systematically with the political-economic structure of the society.

## Implications

**For understanding the modern state:** The institutions contemporary
societies treat as permanent features of political life -- formal law,
career politicians, professional police, standing armies, taxation
systems -- are historically anomalous. The state is not the
culmination of an inevitable evolutionary process but one possible
outcome among many, contingent on specific ecological, demographic,
and historical conditions. Political anthropology reveals that
stateless societies are not failed states; they are alternative
political configurations that solved the problems of order,
cooperation, and conflict through different means.

**For inequality debates:** If hierarchy is not natural but invented,
then inequality is not a fact of nature but a political choice. Boehm's
reverse dominance hierarchy concept suggests that egalitarianism
requires constant political work -- leveling mechanisms, institutional
checks, cultural norms that punish aggrandizement. When those
mechanisms weaken, hierarchy reasserts itself. This has direct
relevance to modern debates about economic inequality, as it suggests
that inequality is not self-correcting and that egalitarian outcomes
require active institutional maintenance.

**For development and governance:** International development policy
often assumes that state institutions are the universal template for
political organization. Political anthropology challenges this
assumption. Postcolonial states in Melanesia, for instance, struggle
with governance models imported from Westminster or Washington that
conflict with indigenous political logics grounded in Big Man
dynamics. Understanding the political anthropology of a region is not
an academic luxury; it is a practical prerequisite for effective
institution-building.

**For organizational design:** The distinction between achieved and
ascribed leadership, between situational authority and permanent
office, has direct implications for how organizations are structured.
The Big Man model -- leadership earned through contribution,
vulnerable to follower exit -- resembles meritocratic ideals in
modern organizations. The chiefdom model -- leadership as office,
independent of individual performance -- resembles bureaucratic
hierarchy. Recognizing these patterns helps diagnose organizational
dysfunctions: when does an organization need to prevent the
crystallization of permanent hierarchy, and when does it need the
stability of institutionalized roles?

**For understanding ourselves:** Political anthropology's deepest
implication may be psychological. Modern humans carry the cognitive
and emotional equipment of 200,000+ years of band-level living. Our
intuitive sense of fairness, our resentment of domination, our
preference for face-to-face accountability -- these are not abstract
philosophical commitments but evolved political instincts shaped in
a world without states, police, or professional politicians. When
modern political institutions feel alienating or illegitimate, it may
be because they violate expectations deeply embedded in our evolved
political psychology.
