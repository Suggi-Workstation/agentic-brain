# Market Structures -- How the Shape of Competition Determines Prices, Profits, and Power

Market structure is the anatomy of competition: the number of sellers in a
market, the degree to which their products are substitutes, and the barriers
that keep outsiders from entering. These features together determine whether
a firm is a price taker with no market power at all, or a price maker that
can hold prices above marginal cost for decades. Understanding the spectrum
from perfect competition to monopoly explains why some industries generate
persistent profits while others grind toward zero, and why that difference
matters to investors, managers, and policymakers alike.

## Background

The study of market structure is older than economics itself as a formal
discipline, but its mathematical foundation dates to Augustin Cournot's 1838
model of two mineral-water sellers, the first formal treatment of how a small
number of rivals interact. Cournot showed that a market with a single seller
sets the highest price, a duopoly a lower one, and that price falls toward
cost as the number of sellers grows. The idea that the number and relative
size of firms shapes price did not become the organizing principle of an
entire field until a century later. In 1933 Edward Chamberlin published The
Theory of Monopolistic Competition and Joan Robinson published The Economics
of Imperfect Competition in the same year, two independent attacks on the
same target: the textbook dichotomy between pure competition and pure
monopoly. Their shared claim was that most real markets lie in between, and
that product differentiation gives even a small firm a measure of price
discretion that the pure-competition model denies. Chamberlin in particular
showed how many small differentiated sellers could each face a downward
sloping demand curve, and how free entry would nonetheless drive their
profits to zero in the long run, leaving firms producing below efficient
scale. This gap between the efficient scale and the output the market
supports became known as excess capacity and remains the signature
prediction of monopolistic competition.

From these foundations grew the Structure-Conduct-Performance paradigm,
built at Harvard by Edward Mason in the 1930s and formalized by his student
Joe S. Bain in the 1950s. Bain's Barriers to New Competition (1956)
established the cross-industry empirical program that dominated industrial
organization for two decades: measure an industry's structure (concentration,
barriers to entry, product differentiation), then test whether structure
predicts conduct (pricing, collusion, advertising) and performance (profit
rates above the competitive norm). The paradigm supplied the intellectual
scaffolding for the antitrust enforcement of the postwar decades, and its
key empirical finding, that concentrated industries earned persistently
higher profits, was read as evidence that structure drives outcomes.

The paradigm came under sustained attack in the 1970s. Harold Demsetz argued
in 1973 that the SCP reading reversed the causality: if superior firms win
market share and earn higher profits through efficiency, concentration and
profitability will be correlated even in a competitive market. Structure, on
this view, is an outcome of the competitive process, not an exogenous
determinant of it. William Baumol, John Panzar, and Robert Willig pressed the
point further with their 1982 theory of contestable markets: if entry and
exit are costless, even a market with a single incumbent is disciplined by
the threat of hit-and-run entry, and the number of firms tells us nothing
about competitive outcomes. What matters is not how many firms are in the
market but how expensive it is to get in and out, which in practice means the
size of sunk costs. Meanwhile the field's center of gravity shifted from
cross-industry statistics to game theory, synthesized by Jean Tirole's 1988
treatise, which modeled oligopoly explicitly as strategic interaction rather
than as a statistical regularity. Market structure became less a fixed box
and more a setting in which firms play repeated games.

The most recent chapter is empirical. Beginning with the production-function
markup methods of the 2010s, economists can now estimate the wedge between
price and marginal cost for individual firms across entire economies, which
transformed market structure from a microeconomic taxonomy into a
macroeconomic variable. The finding that economy-wide markups have risen
sharply since 1980 made the structure of markets a first-order explanation
for wage stagnation, declining business dynamism, and the falling labor
share, and it returned the field to questions Bain would have recognized,
now answered with firm-level data he could not have imagined.

## Core Concepts

Market structure is organized around a spectrum. At one end sits perfect
competition, a theoretical benchmark in which no firm has any power over
price; at the other sits monopoly, in which a single firm is the market. Two
intermediate structures, monopolistic competition and oligopoly, describe
most of the observable economy. Each structure is defined by four features:
the number and size distribution of sellers, the degree of product
differentiation, the height of barriers to entry, and the resulting extent of
pricing power.

### Perfect Competition -- The Benchmark

Perfect competition requires four conditions that almost never hold
together: many small sellers and buyers, none large enough to affect the
market price; a homogeneous product, so that buyers regard every seller's
output as identical; perfect information about prices and quality; and free
entry and exit. When these hold, each firm is a price taker facing a
horizontal demand curve: it can sell any quantity it wants at the market
price and nothing at any higher price. The firm maximizes profit by
producing where price equals marginal cost, and because free entry erodes
any short-run profit, long-run equilibrium occurs at the minimum of the
average cost curve. Perfect competition is therefore both allocatively
efficient (price equals marginal cost, so the last unit produced is worth
exactly what it costs society) and productively efficient (output is
produced at minimum average cost). Economic profit is zero in the long run.
Commodity markets such as wheat and corn and wholesale foreign exchange come
closest to the model, but the model's role is normative: it is the yardstick
against which every other structure is measured, and the reference point for
computing the welfare losses that market power imposes.

### Monopoly -- The Price Maker and the Deadweight Loss

A monopoly is a market with a single seller of a product with no close
substitutes, protected by barriers that block entry. The monopolist faces
the entire market demand curve, which slopes downward, so marginal revenue
lies below price: selling one more unit requires cutting the price on every
unit, not just the last one. The monopolist therefore produces where
marginal revenue equals marginal cost and charges the higher price read off
the demand curve at that quantity. The result is a price above marginal
cost, a quantity below the competitive level, and a deadweight loss: the
triangle of trades that would benefit both buyer and seller at a price
between cost and the monopoly price, but that never happens. The standard
measure of this power is the Lerner index, the ratio (price minus marginal
cost) divided by price, which equals the inverse of the firm's demand
elasticity: the less elastic the demand a firm faces, the more it can mark
up price over cost. Monopolies also fail productive efficiency, since
nothing forces production at minimum average cost, and the absence of
competitive pressure can blunt the incentive to innovate or serve customers
well. The structural sources of monopoly matter for policy. A natural
monopoly arises when economies of scale are so large that one firm supplies
the whole market at lower average cost than two firms could, as with local
water or electricity distribution. Legal monopolies rest on patents,
copyrights, or exclusive franchises. A long-running counterargument holds
that monopoly profits fund the innovation that patents and scale make
possible. The author's assessment is that the static deadweight loss is
real, while the dynamic case is context-dependent: a patent-protected
pharmaceutical is not the same thing as a protected utility, and policy
should treat them differently.

### Monopolistic Competition -- Differentiation and Excess Capacity

Chamberlin's intermediate case combines many firms, easy entry, and
differentiated products. Each restaurant, salon, or clothing label sells a
close but imperfect substitute for its rivals' products, which gives each a
downward-sloping demand curve and a small measure of price discretion: a
firm that raises its price loses some customers, not all of them, unlike a
wheat farmer who would lose every customer. In the short run the firm
behaves like a small monopolist, setting marginal revenue equal to marginal
cost and charging a price above it. But entry is free, and profits attract
imitators. Each entrant draws away customers, shifting each incumbent's
demand curve leftward until it is just tangent to the average cost curve. At
that tangency the firm earns zero economic profit, but the tangency sits on
the falling portion of the cost curve, to the left of the efficient scale.
This is excess capacity: the market supports more firms than efficiency
would dictate, each producing too little, each with spare capacity, each
still charging a price above marginal cost. Consumers pay a small
deadweight loss, and in return they get variety, location, and brand
distinction. Because differentiation is the only source of profit,
monopolistically competitive firms compete heavily through advertising and
non-price means, a pattern the perfectly competitive firm has no reason to
imitate.

### Oligopoly -- Interdependence, Collusion, and Game Theory

Oligopoly is the structure in which a few large firms account for most or
all of a market's output, as Boeing and Airbus do in large commercial
aircraft and Coca-Cola and Pepsi do in soft drinks. Its defining feature is
mutual interdependence: each firm is large enough that its pricing and
output decisions visibly affect its rivals, so every decision must
anticipate the others' reactions. This makes oligopoly the natural home of
game theory, and the formal models fall into two families. Cournot
competition has firms choose quantities and the market price clears the
total; Bertrand competition has firms choose prices, and the striking
result is that two firms with identical products undercut each other all the
way to marginal cost, the competitive outcome. Which model applies depends
on whether firms can commit to quantities (capacity, factories) or must
react in prices. Oligopolists face two opposing temptations: to collude,
jointly restricting output and pricing like a monopoly, or to defect from a
collusive arrangement and grab market share with a price cut. Formal
collusion, the cartel, is illegal in the United States and the European
Union, but the French detergent case described in the Evidence section shows
how durable the temptation is. Tacit coordination without explicit
agreement, price leadership, and mutual forbearance blur the legal line and
are precisely what antitrust authorities scrutinize. Because the outcome of
oligopoly is indeterminate, between the competitive and monopoly poles, the
same structure can deliver brutal price wars or comfortable parallel
pricing, which is why concentration alone is an incomplete guide to market
power.

### Barriers to Entry -- What Protects Market Power

Barriers to entry are the moat that converts short-run pricing power into
long-run profit. Bain's classic taxonomy identified three: economies of
scale, so large that an entrant must enter at big scale or suffer a cost
disadvantage; product differentiation and brand loyalty, which force the
entrant to outspend incumbents on advertising; and absolute cost advantages,
from patents to proprietary technology to control of essential inputs. To
these the modern literature adds network effects, where a product's value
rises with the number of its users and the installed base itself becomes the
barrier; switching costs, which lock customers into an incumbent's
ecosystem; and government-created barriers such as licenses, permits, and
regulatory exclusivity. The contestable markets critique sharpened the
concept: only sunk costs, expenditures that cannot be recovered on exit,
are a true barrier, because only they deter the hit-and-run entrant who
enters when prices are high and leaves when they fall. Fixed costs that are
recoverable, like resalable equipment, do not deter entry the same way.
Barriers, not the number of incumbent firms, are the deepest determinant of
whether an industry's profits persist.

### Measuring Concentration -- CR4 and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index

Because structure matters, economists and regulators need measures of it.
The simplest is the concentration ratio, the combined market share of the
largest four or eight firms; a CR4 above 60 percent is conventionally read
as moderate to high concentration. The ratio's weakness is that it ignores
the distribution among the top firms and everything below the fourth.
The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) fixes this by summing the squares of
every firm's market share, on a scale from near zero to 10,000. Squaring
weights large firms heavily: four equal firms of 25 percent each produce an
HHI of 2,500, while a market of ten equal firms scores 1,000. The US
Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission classify markets
with HHI below 1,000 as unconcentrated, between 1,000 and 1,800 as
moderately concentrated, and above 1,800 as highly concentrated. The HHI
also predicts the competitive effect of a merger mechanically: combining two
firms with shares a and b raises the index by 2ab. The index is a
screening device, not a verdict. The author's assessment follows the modern
literature: concentration is an outcome of the competitive process, and a
high HHI with low entry barriers, the contestable case, may be far more
competitive than a moderate HHI protected by sunk costs.

### Price Discrimination -- Extracting Willingness to Pay

A firm with market power can do more than set a single price: it can charge
different prices to different buyers. Arthur Pigou's taxonomy from The
Economics of Welfare (1920) remains standard. First-degree, or perfect,
discrimination charges each buyer exactly her maximum willingness to pay,
capturing the entire consumer surplus as profit and, in theory, restoring
allocative efficiency because every profitable trade occurs, though all the
gains go to the seller. Second-degree discrimination charges different
prices for different quantities or versions, as with volume discounts,
loyalty tiers, or deliberately degraded products, letting customers
self-select by intensity of demand. Third-degree discrimination segments
buyers by observable characteristics, student or senior fares and
geographic pricing being the classic cases. Discrimination requires three
conditions: market power, the ability to prevent resale, and information
that separates high-value from low-value buyers. It is ubiquitous in
practice and its welfare effects are genuinely ambiguous, as the Evidence
section details.

### Contestability -- When Potential Entry Disciplines Incumbents

The contestable markets theory of Baumol, Panzar, and Willig (1982) makes
the sharpest modern claim about structure: what disciplines an incumbent is
not the number of rivals inside the market but the ease with which rivals
outside can enter and exit. In a perfectly contestable market, with zero
entry and exit costs, even a monopolist must price at average cost, because
any profit invites a hit-and-run entrant who undercuts, harvests, and
leaves without loss before the incumbent can respond. The theory's policy
influence peaked in the deregulation debates of the late 1970s and 1980s,
where it suggested that removing regulatory barriers could substitute for
breaking up concentrated incumbents, and its core insight survives even
though perfectly contestable markets are rare: the credible threat of entry
is a form of competition, and sunk costs are the variable that measures
whether the threat is credible. This is why the practical analysis of any
market must ask not only who is in it, but who could be.

## Evidence

The strongest empirical case that market structure determines real
outcomes comes from the modern markup literature. De Loecker, Eeckhout, and
Unger (2020) estimated firm-level markups for the entire US economy from
1955 to 2016 using the production approach, which infers the wedge between
price and marginal cost from firm-level revenue and input expenditure data
without assuming anything about the form of competition. They document a
remarkable pattern. Markups were stable, even slightly declining, from 1955
to 1980, then rose steadily: the average markup went from 21 percent above
marginal cost in 1980 to 61 percent by 2016. More striking than the level is
the distribution: the median firm's markup did not change at all, while the
upper tail rose sharply, with the 90th percentile roughly doubling. The
economy-wide rise is driven by a minority of firms that gained substantial
pricing power while most firms gained none. Profitability tells the same
story: the average profit rate rose from about 1 percent in 1980 to 8
percent in 2016, an increase too large to be explained by overhead costs
alone. The authors show the rise occurred mostly within industries rather
than through sectoral shifts, and they link it to a set of secular trends:
the falling labor share, rising profit share, declining business dynamism,
slower labor flows, and lower participation. In a companion exercise they
argue that both technological change and weakened competitive pressure
contributed, and that the net welfare effect of the rise has been negative.

The pattern is global. De Loecker and Eeckhout's study of over 70,000
firms in 134 countries finds the worldwide average markup rose from about
1.15 in 1980 to about 1.6 in 2016, with the largest increases in North
America and Europe, much smaller ones in Asia, and flat or falling markups
in Latin American emerging economies. Notably, the mechanism differs across
regions: in the United States, about two-thirds of the rise came from
reallocation, the shift of market share toward high-markup superstar firms,
while in Europe no such reallocation appears, even though aggregate markups
rose similarly. The same aggregate can mask very different competitive
processes, a caution against reading any single number as the whole story.

Contestability, the theory that potential entry disciplines incumbents,
found an empirical test bed in the airline industry. After the deregulation
of US domestic routes in the late 1970s removed the regulatory barrier to
entry, carriers could deploy aircraft on new routes with little sunk cost
beyond marketing, and the threat of such entry kept fares on many
concentrated routes below what the number of incumbent carriers alone would
have predicted. Studies in the deregulation era found that fares were lower
on routes exposed to potential entry even where few carriers actually
operated, the pattern contestability predicts, though later work qualified
the finding once frequent-flyer programs and airport slot constraints
rebuilt entry barriers. The episode illustrates both the power and the
limits of the theory: entry threats discipline pricing only while sunk
costs stay low, and incumbents respond by investing precisely in the
switching costs and slot scarcity that restore their protection.

The older cross-industry evidence anticipated these results with cruder
tools. Bain's 1950s studies of US manufacturing industries found that
industries with high concentration among the top firms earned persistently
higher profit rates than unconcentrated ones, which he interpreted as
structure driving performance. Demsetz (1973) answered that the same
correlation is consistent with the efficiency hypothesis: superior firms
both win share and earn profits, so concentration and profitability are
joint outcomes of the competitive process rather than proof of its failure.
The exchange established the enduring lesson of the literature, that
correlation between structure and profits must be interpreted with a causal
theory, a lesson the modern markup literature addresses with firm-level
methods that measure the price-cost wedge directly.

On the welfare consequences of one specific market-power practice, the
evidence is genuinely mixed. Schmalensee's 1981 analysis of third-degree
price discrimination showed that the practice can raise or lower output and
welfare depending on the curvature of demand in each segment. Pigou had
already established the benchmark result: when total output is unchanged,
discrimination transfers surplus from consumers to the monopolist and
lowers total welfare. The modern two-sided market literature complicates
the picture further, showing that on platforms, charging sellers
differently can expand participation on both sides of the market and raise
total welfare. Price discrimination is therefore not uniformly villainous,
which is why policy treats it case by case.

In policy practice, market structure is operationalized through screening
thresholds. The 2023 US Merger Guidelines, issued jointly by the Department
of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission, create a structural
presumption that a merger may substantially lessen competition when the
post-merger HHI exceeds 1,800 and the merger increases the index by more
than 100 points, with a parallel presumption when the merged firm's share
exceeds 30 percent and the HHI change exceeds 100. The 2023 guidelines
lowered the highly-concentrated threshold from 2,500, the level used since
the 2010 guidelines, to 1,800, signaling stricter enforcement. These are
rebuttable presumptions that frame, but do not end, the economic analysis.

A single case illustrates why the structure of a market matters concretely.
In the French detergent market between 1997 and 2004, four firms, Procter
and Gamble, Henkel, Unilever, and Colgate-Palmolive, controlled about 90
percent of sales. European competition officials documented secret meetings
in out-of-the-way cafes in Paris whose explicit goal was to stamp out
competition and fix prices, in effect converting an oligopoly into a cartel
that priced like a monopoly. The case is a textbook demonstration of the
core prediction of oligopoly theory, that a small number of interdependent
firms face a standing temptation to collude, and of why structure-based
monitoring exists.

## Implications

For investors and analysts, market structure is the root cause of the most
important question in valuation: whether a firm's profits will persist.
Economic theory says competitive industries converge to zero economic
profit in the long run, so any forecast that assumes permanently high
margins is implicitly a claim about market structure, about barriers to
entry, differentiation, switching costs, or scale advantages that protect
the firm's pricing power. Analysts who map an industry's structure before
forecasting margins, counting the effective competitors, estimating
concentration with CR4 or HHI, and auditing the durability of the entry
barriers, are testing the moat rather than assuming it. The markup evidence
adds urgency: because pricing power has become more concentrated in a
smaller set of firms while most firms gained none, averages mislead, and
the relevant question is which side of the distribution a given firm sits
on. The same evidence also identifies the principal risk to such forecasts:
competition itself, whether from new entrants drawn by high profits, from
regulators drawn by high concentration, or from substitutes that redefine
the market boundary.

For policymakers, market structure is the object of competition policy.
The HHI thresholds of the merger guidelines turn the theoretical spectrum
into an operational screen, and the 2023 tightening of those thresholds
shows that the policy view of acceptable concentration can shift within a
decade. The structure-versus-conduct debate has direct regulatory
consequences: if structure drives conduct, the remedy for market power is
structural, breakups and blocked mergers, while if conduct and entry
conditions are what matter, the remedy is behavioral rules and the removal
of entry barriers. The contestability tradition points to a third lever,
regulatory design that lowers sunk costs and eases entry, as with the
deregulation experiments of the late twentieth century. Natural monopolies
pose a distinct problem, since their structure is efficient, and are
typically addressed with price regulation or public provision rather than
fragmentation. The static-dynamic trade-off runs through all of it:
blocking a merger preserves today's competition but may sacrifice scale
that funds tomorrow's innovation, and no general rule settles where the
line falls.

For managers and firms, market structure is partly chosen, not merely
inhabited. Differentiation, branding, switching costs, and network effects
are investments in a more favorable structure, and the monopolistic
competition model shows their double edge: they buy pricing power today at
the price of attracting imitators tomorrow. In oligopoly, strategy is
inherently interactive, and the game-theoretic models of Cournot and
Bertrand translate into practical commitments, building capacity that
signals aggressive quantity intentions, or pricing rules that signal
forbearance. The antitrust constraint is part of the environment, not an
external nuisance: conduct that converts parallel behavior into collusion
is illegal in most jurisdictions, and the line between legitimate
strategic signaling and unlawful coordination is where corporate counsel
earn their keep.

For workers, the same forces operate on the other side of the market.
Market power in product markets suppresses output and employment, and
monopsony power, the mirror-image case of one or a few employers that Joan
Robinson analyzed alongside imperfect competition in 1933, lets firms hold
wages below workers' marginal product. The markup evidence shows that as
pricing power rose, the labor share of income fell, which is the
distributional signature of market power: a larger slice of each dollar of
sales going to owners and a smaller slice to wages. Labor economists
increasingly treat employer concentration in local labor markets as a
structural problem parallel to product-market concentration, and the policy
remedies are parallel too: reduce entry barriers, enforce competition, and
limit the accumulation of buyer power. Market structure is thus not only an
industrial-organization question but a distributional one, and part of why
the topic has moved from the margins of microeconomics to the center of
macroeconomic debate.

At the macroeconomic level, the modern markup evidence elevates market
structure to a first-order variable. De Loecker, Eeckhout, and Unger tie
the rise in market power to the falling labor share, wage stagnation, and
the decline of business dynamism, and their cross-country work shows the
pattern is not uniquely American. Within this brain, the topic connects
directly to three neighbors. Game theory supplies the formal machinery
that replaced the SCP paradigm and remains the language in which oligopoly
is analyzed. Inflation dynamics intersects through price-setting: firms
with market power set prices as markups over marginal cost, and who holds
pricing power shapes how cost shocks pass through to consumers. Trade and
comparative advantage intersects through competition: import penetration
is one of the few forces that reliably disciplines domestic market power,
which is one reason openness and competition policy are complements rather
than substitutes. Market structure is not one more microeconomic topic; it
is the mechanism through which micro-level power becomes macro-level
outcomes.

The macroeconomic link also runs through monetary policy. Inflation
analysis treats the price level as built up from markups over costs, and
the degree of pricing power determines the pass-through: where markups are
thick and competition thin, cost shocks flow faster into consumer prices
and disinflation takes longer. The author's assessment is that the markup
literature has made market structure impossible to ignore in macroeconomic
policy, because it offers a unifying explanation for otherwise puzzling
trends: profits rising while wages stagnate, productivity dispersing while
dynamism falls, and inflation responding more sluggishly to slack than
standard models predict.

For consumers, market structure sets the terms of daily life. The
difference between a competitive and a concentrated market is the
difference between prices near cost and prices far above it, between real
choice and the appearance of choice among brands owned by the same few
firms, and between innovation aimed at winning customers and innovation
aimed at defending position. The French detergent case is a reminder that
the line between oligopoly and cartel is policed, not automatic.

## Common Pitfalls

The most common error is conflating concentration with market power. A
high HHI means few firms, not weak competition: if entry is cheap and sunk
costs are low, even a concentrated market can be disciplined by the threat
of entry, while a moderately concentrated market protected by sunk costs
can be uncompetitive. The second is treating the four structures as
exhaustive boxes rather than a spectrum, which misses the fact that most
real markets blur boundaries and that the same industry can shift along the
spectrum as technology lowers entry costs or consolidates rivals. The third
is confusing the short run with the long run: pricing power observed today
may be eroding profits in tomorrow's equilibrium as entry responds, and
monopolistic competition predicts exactly this life cycle of profits. The
fourth is confusing accounting profit with economic profit: a monopolist
that earns only a normal return on its capital after paying for its
monopoly position has no economic profit, which is why observed profit
rates must be benchmarked against the cost of capital. The fifth is
treating structure as exogenous, the original SCP error: firms invest to
shape structure, and superior firms become large, so the direction of
causation must be argued, not assumed. The sixth is over-applying the
static benchmark: the deadweight loss triangle is measured at a moment in
time and says nothing about whether today's market power funds the
innovation that tomorrow's consumers will enjoy.
