# Elasticity Determines Incidence Only After Market Structure and Adjustment Are Made Explicit

Elasticity measures how strongly quantities respond to prices, while incidence traces who ultimately gains or loses when a tax, tariff, subsidy, wage mandate, or price control changes an equilibrium. In a competitive benchmark, the less elastic side of a market bears more of a tax and captures more of a subsidy, but that rule is local and conditional: market power, demand curvature, evasion, salience, input-output links, adjustment time, and rationing can all change observed pass-through and welfare. [1][2][3][5][6][8][12]

## Background

The intellectual origin of elasticity lies in the effort to convert the directional laws of demand and supply into quantitative statements. A downward-sloping demand curve says that a higher price reduces quantity demanded, but it does not say whether a 1 percent price increase reduces quantity by 0.1 percent or 10 percent. Price elasticity of demand answers that question by dividing the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage change in price; price elasticity of supply applies the same logic to quantity supplied. Demand elasticity is negative by sign convention because price and quantity move in opposite directions, but it is commonly reported in absolute value when classifying demand as elastic, unit elastic, or inelastic. [1]

Alfred Marshall gave elasticity an explicit role in partial-equilibrium price analysis. In *Principles of Economics*, he defined demand as more or less elastic according to how much quantity changes for a given price change and tied market statements to a specified time and set of conditions. Modern measurement preserves that discipline: productive capacity, habits, contracts, inventories, and substitutes can be fixed in the short run but adjustable in the long run. The result is not one permanent elasticity for a good. Elasticity belongs to a defined market, price, population, and horizon. A household may have few immediate alternatives to gasoline, while vehicle choice, residence, commuting, and production technology create more adjustment over years. OpenStax summarizes the general implication: demand and supply often, though not always, become more elastic over longer horizons. [1][13]

Tax incidence developed from the recognition that the person who sends money to the government need not be the person whose welfare falls. A per-unit tax creates a wedge between the price paid by a buyer and the net price received by a seller. Buyers can respond by purchasing less, sellers by producing less or leaving, firms by changing inputs or markups, workers by changing labor supply, and capital by moving across sectors or countries. Those responses change equilibrium prices, wages, rents, and returns. Fullerton and Metcalf therefore define incidence as the allocation of economic burden through higher product prices, lower wages, or lower returns to factors, and review partial-equilibrium, general-equilibrium, dynamic, and lifetime approaches to measuring it. [2]

The simplest competitive model supplied three durable benchmark propositions. First, statutory remittance and economic burden are conceptually distinct. Second, if the only change is which side remits an otherwise identical, fully enforced and fully salient tax, the same buyer-seller wedge and equilibrium result. Third, the less price-responsive side bears more because it has fewer alternatives. A nearly vertical demand curve lets sellers raise the buyer price with little quantity loss; a nearly vertical supply curve leaves sellers with little ability to withdraw and forces more adjustment into their net price. [1][2]

Harberger extended incidence beyond one market. His 1962 corporate-tax model divided the analyzed economy into corporate and noncorporate sectors using capital and labor, then traced how a tax on corporate capital caused factors to reallocate until returns and wages again cleared both sectors. The model demonstrated why a sectoral tax can affect owners and workers outside the taxed sector and why general-equilibrium incidence depends on substitution in production and consumption. Its competitive baseline fixes aggregate factor supplies, omits the government and rest-of-world sectors, and derives first-order comparative-static results, although the paper also presents discrete examples and an extension involving monopoly. These are model choices rather than universal facts. [4]

Later research weakened the idea that relative elasticities alone are sufficient. Weyl and Fabinger generalized incidence to imperfect competition, where pass-through depends on market conduct, marginal-cost response, and the curvature of demand as well as ordinary demand and supply elasticities. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft showed that consumers can respond differently to a tax and to an equally large posted-price change when the tax is less salient. Kopczuk, Marion, Muehlegger, and Slemrod showed that the point of legal collection can matter when evasion opportunities differ across supply-chain stages. These findings do not erase the competitive benchmark. They identify conditions that must hold before its invariance and elasticity rules can be applied literally. [3][5][6]

Policy analysis also widened from taxes to economically similar wedges and constraints. A tariff taxes an imported good and can propagate through imported inputs. [7][8] A subsidy places a negative wedge between the amount a buyer pays and the amount a seller receives. [2][3][9] A minimum wage is a statutory floor in a labor market, while rent control is a ceiling whose shortage can require non-price allocation. [11][12] Each policy changes at least one combination of prices, quantities, participation, or allocation. The common analytical problem is to identify the margin constrained by policy, measure the relevant responses, and distinguish transfers among groups from net losses in total surplus. [2][8][9][11][12]

## Core Concepts

### Point, Arc, and Policy Elasticities

For a differentiable demand function Q(P), point elasticity at a particular price and quantity is `(dQ/dP) * (P/Q)`. It is a local derivative: it describes the percentage quantity response to an infinitesimal percentage price change around one point. Point elasticity can change along a straight demand curve even when its slope is constant because the ratio P/Q changes. Marshall expressed the same proportional logic in defining unit elasticity; OpenStax illustrates the distinction by showing different elasticities on different portions of a constant-slope demand curve. [1][13]

Arc elasticity measures a discrete move between two observations. The midpoint formula divides the quantity change by average quantity and the price change by average price, then forms their ratio. Using averages makes the calculated elasticity the same whether the analyst describes the movement from the first point to the second or in reverse. [1] The author's assessment is that the midpoint measure is useful for a material tax or price change but, as an interval average, should not be read as the local response at every point when behavior is nonlinear. Point and arc elasticities answer local and interval questions rather than estimate one timeless constant.

A policy elasticity requires an additional distinction. A tax-inclusive price may change because the posted producer price changed, because the tax changed, or because both changed in equilibrium. If consumers do not fully attend to a separately displayed tax, the quantity response to a tax can differ from the response to an equal posted-price change. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft therefore estimate both tax and price elasticities and show that incidence and excess-burden formulas need both when salience is incomplete. [5]

### The Competitive Tax Wedge

Let `Pc` be the price paid by consumers, `Pp` the price received by producers, and `t` a per-unit tax. The wedge is `Pc - Pp = t`. A tax reduces the traded quantity from the no-tax equilibrium until demand at `Pc` equals supply at `Pp`. The government collects `t` times the post-tax quantity. That revenue is a transfer from private parties to the public sector, not itself a deadweight loss. [1][2]

For a small tax introduced into a competitive market, with positive supply elasticity `Es` and the absolute value of demand elasticity `Ed`, the local consumer share of the price wedge is `Es / (Es + Ed)` and the producer share is `Ed / (Es + Ed)`. Equivalently, the local pass-through rate to the consumer price is `dPc/dt = Es / (Es + Ed)`. Weyl and Fabinger write the same competitive relation as `1 / (1 + Ed/Es)`. These formulas encode the less-elastic-side rule. If supply is much more elastic than demand, producers can readily withdraw and consumers bear most of the tax. If demand is much more elastic than supply, buyers can readily leave and producers bear more through a lower net price. [1][2][3]

The formulas also show why elasticity is relative. Calling demand inelastic because `Ed` is below one does not by itself determine incidence; supply may be even less elastic. They are local formulas, so applying one local value to a large tax assumes that response and market structure do not change materially over the policy interval. Weyl and Fabinger show that finite-change incidence uses the quantity-weighted average pass-through over the tax range. Estimating that average requires the observed pass-through path or a model that supplies it; one point elasticity is insufficient. [2][3]

Statutory incidence concerns who is legally responsible for remittance. Economic incidence concerns changes in welfare after all price and quantity responses. Under the canonical competitive assumptions, moving the remittance obligation from seller to buyer changes the accounting route but not the equilibrium wedge. That invariance can fail when the legal point of collection changes evasion opportunities, compliance costs, enforcement, or salience. Under imperfect competition, neutrality also requires that market conduct not depend directly on which side physically remits. The benchmark is therefore a conditional result, not a claim that administration never matters. [2][3][5][6]

### Specific and Ad Valorem Taxes

The formulas above use a specific, or per-unit, tax: `t` is a fixed currency amount for each unit. An ad valorem tax instead applies a percentage to value. Under Fullerton and Metcalf's tax-exclusive convention, a net producer price `p` becomes a gross consumer price `p(1 + tau)`. The money wedge per unit is therefore `p * tau`, which changes when the net price changes. A quoted percentage is also ambiguous unless the denominator is stated: a tax-inclusive rate divides tax by the gross price, while a tax-exclusive rate divides it by the net price. [2]

This distinction matters whenever price and markup move with the policy. The author's assessment is that an ad valorem rate cannot be inserted mechanically as a fixed `t` in a per-unit pass-through formula. The analyst must specify the tax base, denominator, and whether the response is to a unit cost or a proportional wedge, then apply a model consistent with market conduct and demand curvature. [2][3]

### Taxes, Subsidies, and Pass-Through

Pass-through measures how much a policy-induced cost or benefit appears in a market price. For a unit tax, a consumer-price pass-through of 1 means the consumer price rises by the full tax; 0 means no increase; a value between 0 and 1 divides the price effect; and a value above 1 is over-shifting. The complementary change in the producer's net price completes the wedge under the simple model, but observed welfare incidence can also include quantity changes, fixed costs, entry, quality, and effects in other markets. [2][3]

A subsidy reverses the wedge. If the government pays `s` per unit, the total amount received by the seller can exceed the buyer's out-of-pocket price by `s`. In a competitive local benchmark, the less elastic side captures more of the subsidy just as it bears more of a tax. Economic incidence must be measured from market prices and welfare, not inferred from the named recipient. Pless and van Benthem's preferred California estimates make the point: host-owned buyers received 78 percent pass-through, statistically below complete pass-through, while third-party-owned customers received 153 percent, statistically above complete pass-through. The authors relate the over-shifting result to imperfect competition and sufficiently convex demand, while describing their curvature estimates as noisy and the exact predicted range as uncertain. [9]

Pass-through is not identical to incidence in every model. A price change transfers surplus on units still traded, while the quantity response removes trades and changes producer costs and consumer value. Under market power, the pre-policy price already exceeds marginal cost. A tax can change both the price-cost wedge and output; a subsidy can alter entry or product variety. Weyl and Fabinger show that demand curvature and conduct determine how marginal cost changes map into price, so over-shifting can occur without violating optimization. [3][9]

### Short-Run and Long-Run Incidence

Short-run elasticities hold some adjustment margins fixed. A landlord cannot instantly construct housing, a refinery cannot instantly change capacity, a worker may be bound by a contract, and a household cannot instantly replace a vehicle or move. Long-run elasticities permit more substitution, entry, exit, capital reallocation, relocation, and technology change. A tax burden observed immediately can therefore migrate over time as quantities and factor prices adjust. [1][2]

Payroll-tax incidence can move into wages as labor markets adjust, but the mechanism and horizon must be identified rather than assumed. Fullerton and Metcalf report the standard incidence result that employees can bear payroll taxes through lower net wages, while Gruber's Chile study provides a multi-year institutional example discussed below. Corporate-tax models likewise allow investment and capital accumulation to change future wages and returns. Harberger's model illustrates cross-sector factor reallocation while explicitly focusing on long-run equilibrium rather than estimating a transition path. [2][4][10]

The author's synthesis is that every incidence claim should name at least two horizons: the adjustment window in the data and the horizon relevant to the decision. A one-month retail pass-through estimate can be correct and still differ from a five-year incidence estimate because inventories, contracts, sourcing, entry, and household substitution have not completed. This is not an empirical nuisance to average away; it is part of the causal mechanism. [1][2]

### Capitalization into Asset Prices

Some future tax or subsidy streams are capitalized into the current price of a durable asset. Fullerton and Metcalf describe the property-tax case: holding public services and amenities constant, buyers can bid a stream of higher future property taxes into a lower current property value, while a positive fiscal surplus can raise value. The current owner can therefore bear a burden at the announcement or sale even if later owners make the statutory payments. Empirical work does not imply a universal capitalization rate; the review notes continuing disagreement over its degree and the difficulty of separating taxes from associated services. [2]

Capitalization changes the timing and identity of incidence. A recurring charge can be reflected once in an asset-price revaluation, while a temporary or unexpected policy may be capitalized only partly. Anticipation also matters: the price can move before the first payment when buyers expect the policy. The author's assessment is that incidence studies involving land, housing, licenses, or other durable claims should report both the payment flow and any asset-price change, while avoiding double counting the same expected burden. [2]

### Annual, Lifetime, and Cohort Incidence

An annual income table and a lifetime-incidence model answer different distributional questions. A household can have low current income while consuming from past savings, or high current income during a temporary earnings peak. Fullerton and Metcalf note that classifying consumption-tax burdens by annual income can bias the analysis toward regressivity if consumption follows lifetime resources, although the reviewed lifetime estimates do not imply that this bias always dominates. Lifetime studies construct histories of earnings, transfers, inheritances, saving, consumption, and bequests before assigning cumulative burdens. [2]

Dynamic analysis can also separate old from new capital and current from future generations. A reform can capitalize an unexpected loss into assets already owned while changing saving, investment, wages, and taxes for later cohorts. Social-insurance underfunding can transfer resources between current and future generations even if one year's accounts balance. The author's assessment is that a distributional claim should state whether groups are defined by current income, lifetime resources, birth cohort, or factor ownership; those classifications are not interchangeable. [2]

### Deadweight Loss and the Role of Elasticity

A tax transfers revenue to government and usually reduces quantity below the no-tax competitive level. Under linear curves and no pre-existing distortion, the familiar approximation to deadweight loss is one half times the per-unit tax times the reduction in quantity. The triangle represents mutually beneficial trades whose buyer value exceeded seller cost but not by enough to cover the tax wedge. The more responsive demand and supply are, the larger the quantity response to a given small tax and, other conditions equal, the larger the efficiency loss. [2]

This approximation has strict boundaries. It assumes the pre-tax competitive quantity is the relevant efficient benchmark, treats quality and entry consistently, and excludes other distortions. If an activity creates an unpriced external cost, a corrective tax can move quantity toward rather than away from the social optimum. If a market already has monopoly underproduction, a tax and the existing markup interact. If consumers ignore a nonsalient tax, little observed quantity change does not alone prove zero welfare loss: with income effects, unchanged demand for the taxed good can still distort allocation elsewhere in the budget. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft show that this particular channel depends on income effects and derive incidence and efficiency formulas that separate responses to tax-inclusive prices from responses to taxes themselves. [3][5]

Tax revenue, producer loss, consumer loss, and deadweight loss must not be added indiscriminately. In a standard surplus calculation that credits public receipts dollar for dollar, revenue offsets part of private surplus loss; a different valuation or use of public funds changes that accounting. Net social loss counts real resource, allocation, and behavioral costs under the stated welfare weights and externalities. A distributional evaluation can still care greatly about transfers even when aggregate surplus is unchanged. Incidence and efficiency are related but separate questions. [2][7][8]

The elasticity used for welfare analysis must also match the experiment. Marshallian, or uncompensated, demand includes income effects; Hicksian, or compensated, demand holds utility constant and isolates substitution. Fullerton and Metcalf show that a partial-equilibrium calculation that ignores revenue use may employ an uncompensated elasticity, while a general-equilibrium experiment returning revenue lump-sum uses a compensated elasticity because the specified rebate removes the relevant income effect. Equivalent variation is measured against compensated demand. The correct choice therefore follows from the counterfactual, not from a generic elasticity label. [2]

### Revenue Use and Incidence Experiments

No tax-incidence estimate is complete until it states what happens to the revenue. Fullerton and Metcalf distinguish three experiments. Absolute incidence assumes that government holds the proceeds, requiring the analyst also to consider debt. Balanced-budget incidence spends the revenue, so results depend on who receives the spending and what is purchased. Differential incidence uses the proceeds to reduce another tax, so the result includes the incidence of the tax being replaced. [2]

These closures can yield different distributions even when the same market tax and pass-through are observed. A lump-sum rebate to exactly the households bearing the burden can neutralize some income effects; spending on another population or reducing a distortionary tax changes prices and welfare differently. Subsidy analysis has the mirror obligation to identify the financing tax or spending reduction. The author's assessment is that reports should present the market-side wedge and the public-budget closure separately so that a transfer is neither omitted nor counted twice. [2]

### Imperfect Competition, Curvature, and Market Power

Under imperfect competition, firms choose prices or quantities while recognizing that their actions affect the market. A tax changes marginal cost, but the price response depends on how the firm's optimal markup changes as quantity changes. Demand elasticity at one point does not fully describe this response; the curvature of demand and the conduct of rivals also matter. In Weyl and Fabinger's symmetric framework, if `rho` is pass-through, `theta` is conduct, and `I` is the ratio of consumer to producer welfare burden, then `I = rho / [1 - (1 - theta)rho]`. Pass-through and incidence are therefore not identical except in special cases. [3]

Over-shifting illustrates the difference. A one-dollar tax can raise price by more than one dollar, or a one-dollar subsidy can lower price by more than one dollar, when the policy moves the firm to a portion of demand where the optimal markup changes sufficiently. Pless and van Benthem use subsidy over-shifting as evidence relevant to market power after ruling out alternative explanations and testing demand curvature. An observed pass-through above 100 percent is therefore not proof that arithmetic failed; it is evidence that a competitive constant-markup interpretation may be wrong. [3][9]

The author's synthesis is that concentration alone does not determine pass-through. Competition mode, product differentiation, capacity, entry, multi-product pricing, and the shape of marginal cost can generate different outcomes for markets with similar concentration. The practical implication is to estimate pass-through and its heterogeneity rather than assign a rate from an industry label. [3]

### Input-Output Links and General Equilibrium

A policy imposed at one transaction can travel through a production network. A tariff collected at the border raises the buyer's cost of an imported final good, but a tariff on steel, semiconductors, or machinery also raises costs for domestic downstream producers. In the 2018 tariff evidence, firms absorbed some higher input costs in lower margins, raised some producer prices, redirected trade, and lost imported varieties. The final modeled burden can reach consumers, firms, factors, and regions that never remit the tariff. [7][8]

Fajgelbaum and coauthors combine estimated trade responses with a general-equilibrium model containing sectoral specialization and input-output relationships. That method distinguishes the direct loss to consumers and firms buying imports from tariff revenue, gains to protected producers, retaliatory effects, and regional exposure. Harberger's older two-sector model makes the same conceptual move in simpler form: factor mobility transmits a sectoral tax into economy-wide returns. General equilibrium is required when the policy is large enough, the input is widely used enough, or factor reallocation is central enough that holding other markets fixed would omit important burden channels. [4][8]

### Wage Mandates and Price Controls as Incidence Problems

A minimum wage is a statutory floor on the price of labor rather than a tax. Its observed incidence can appear in hourly pay, employment, full-time-equivalent hours, worker composition, entry or incumbency, and sectoral reallocation. Cengiz and coauthors measure these margins; their study does not identify product prices, profits, automation, hiring standards, or monopsony as adjustment mechanisms. The competitive diagram predicts disemployment from a binding floor, but an empirical magnitude cannot be assigned without a design, a supported policy range, and the relevant market structure. [11][14]

A binding price ceiling suppresses monetary price adjustment and creates excess demand. Incidence then migrates into non-price margins such as queues or lotteries, search costs, side payments, reduced maintenance, quality differences, mobility lock-in, or misallocation across buyers. Glaeser and Luttmer discuss these mechanisms and estimate allocation differences; they do not separately identify every non-price margin. Their model shows that if scarce controlled apartments do not go to the highest-valuing users, misallocation can create welfare costs in addition to the textbook undersupply triangle. Price-control incidence therefore cannot be read solely from the regulated price. [12]

The author's synthesis is that taxes, subsidies, wage floors, and price ceilings should be analyzed with one common sequence: identify the wedge or constraint, identify every feasible response margin, estimate the relevant elasticities and pass-through over a stated horizon, trace linked markets, and separate distributional transfers from net resource losses. The policy labels differ, but the equilibrium discipline is the same. [2][3][12]

## Evidence

### Salience Separates Tax Responses from Price Responses

Chetty, Looney, and Kroft use two complementary designs. In a three-week grocery-store intervention, tax-inclusive shelf labels were posted for roughly 750 taxable products in three selected product groups. Scanner data analyzed with difference-in-differences and triple-difference comparisons show that demand for treated products fell by about 8 percent; the detailed level estimate is 7.6 percent and the log specification is 10.1 percent. The product groups and store were not randomized, and the authors identify a possible Hawthorne or short-run norm effect as a limitation. [5]

Their second design uses state alcohol-tax changes from 1970 through 2003 and finds that excise taxes included in posted prices reduced consumption more than sales taxes added at the register. Their welfare framework then uses separately estimated tax and price elasticities. If consumers fully optimize over tax-inclusive prices, the two responses should coincide after pass-through is accounted for. The evidence supports underreaction to less salient taxes and implies that statutory design can affect economic incidence when it changes attention. The limitation is equally important: the designs identify behavior in the studied retail and alcohol settings and do not establish one salience parameter for every tax or population. [5]

### Collection Point Tests the Evasion Boundary of Invariance

Kopczuk, Marion, Muehlegger, and Slemrod use state-month variation from 1986 through 2006 in the supply-chain stage responsible for remitting diesel taxes. In their main specification, moving from retailer remittance to supplier or distributor remittance raises the tax-price interaction by 0.209 (standard error 0.052) and 0.100 (0.056), respectively; the distributor estimate is marginal at `p = 0.073`, and the supplier-distributor difference is marginal at `p = 0.081`. Estimated supplier-remitting tax-inclusive pass-through is 1.089 per tax dollar and is not statistically different from full pass-through (`p = 0.14`). [6]

The design is valuable because it studies a change in statutory administration while the economic object remains a state diesel tax. The authors find higher reported taxable gallons under upstream collection and describe the revenue evidence as suggesting, not proving, evasion as the mechanism. The collection regime is not randomly assigned, unmeasured joint determinants cannot be ruled out, and the main incidence analysis observes retail rather than every supply-chain price. Timing, wholesale-price, concentration, and crude-oil checks strengthen the interpretation without converting it into a randomized design. The result preserves the fully enforced benchmark while showing why its qualifications must be tested. [6]

### Two Tariff Studies Show Direct and Network Incidence

Amiti, Redding, and Weinstein analyze the 2018 U.S. tariff waves using monthly product-country import prices and quantities, producer prices, and product varieties. They find almost complete pass-through into domestic prices of imported goods, with little estimated reduction in prices received by foreign exporters. Their conventional welfare calculation reaches a $1.4 billion monthly U.S. real-income loss by the end of 2018. They separately document trade redirection, lost imported varieties, and domestic producer-price effects; the $1.4 billion estimate excludes lost variety, fixed supply-chain reorganization costs, and policy-uncertainty costs and is therefore described as conservative. [7]

Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy, and Khandelwal use a different empirical and modeling route. They estimate import and retaliatory tariff effects on prices and quantities, then embed the elasticities in a short-run general-equilibrium model with sectoral and regional linkages. Targeted before-duty import prices did not fall, implying complete pass-through to duty-inclusive prices. At annual 2016 prices, they estimate a $51 billion loss to U.S. consumers and firms buying imports, equal to 0.27 percent of GDP. Estimated producer gains of $9.4 billion and tariff revenue of $34.3 billion reduce the modeled aggregate real-income loss to $7.2 billion, or 0.04 percent of GDP. The aggregate loss is not statistically significant at conventional levels, and the authors cannot reject zero. The model assumes perfect competition, flexible prices and intermediate-input adjustment, short-run immobility of primary factors across sectors and regions, and unitary input-output elasticities. [8]

The figures answer different accounting questions and should not be substituted for one another. Gross losses to import buyers, government revenue, producer gains, retaliation, and net real-income loss are separate quantities. Agreement on near-complete border pass-through is strengthened by the distinct methods, while the general-equilibrium decomposition demonstrates why border incidence is not the final distribution across households, firms, factors, and regions. [7][8]

### Subsidy Pass-Through Reveals Heterogeneous Market Conduct

Pless and van Benthem match California Solar Initiative transactions from 2010 through the second quarter of 2013 to sharp rebate changes across utilities and time. They compare host-owned purchases with third-party-owned leases or power-purchase agreements, constructing third-party prices from roughly 2,000 transcribed contracts because reported program costs were unreliable. Their preferred estimates are 78 percent pass-through for host-owned systems and 153 percent for third-party-owned systems. The first is statistically incomplete and the second statistically exceeds 100 percent. The estimates are local to California market conditions. [9]

This case supplies three lessons. A subsidy legally paid to a firm need not remain with that firm. Pass-through can differ across contractual forms for what the authors call virtually the same product. A rate above one can be an economically meaningful response under imperfect competition rather than sampling noise by definition. The authors rule out several alternative explanations and find demand curvature consistent with over-shifting, but their curvature estimates are noisy, imprecise, and not statistically significant; the exact predicted price range therefore remains uncertain. The evidence does not imply that every subsidy is over-shifted. [9]

### Payroll Taxes Show a Wage Margin

Gruber studies Chile's social-security privatization using plant-level data from a census of manufacturing establishments with more than 10 employees from 1979 through 1986. The average payroll-tax rate in his sample fell from about 30 percent to 5 percent over six years; the main regressions compare 1979-1980 with 1984-1985. Multiple estimators address mechanically correlated measurement error in plant tax rates and wages. He reports strong evidence that the tax reduction was fully shifted into wages, with no employment effect in that setting. [10]

This finding illustrates multi-year labor-market incidence: a tax nominally paid by firms can be borne by workers through lower wages. It is not a universal payroll-tax coefficient. The reform combined pension privatization with other financing changes and a mandated nominal-wage increase, and Chile entered a severe recession during the period. Gruber also warns that a highly inflationary environment may differ from other settings and that downward wage rigidity could make tax cuts shift differently from tax increases. The study's value is institutional specificity, not permission to assume full wage shifting under other labor-market conditions. [10]

### Minimum-Wage Evidence Identifies Where Jobs Move in the Distribution

Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer study 138 prominent state minimum-wage changes between 1979 and 2016 with a difference-in-differences event study by wage bin. The average event raised the real minimum wage 10.1 percent and initially covered 8.6 percent of workers. Over five years, missing jobs below the new minimum equaled 1.8 percent of pre-treatment employment and excess jobs at or above it equaled 2.1 percent. Affected employment rose by a statistically insignificant 2.8 percent (standard error 2.9 percent), while affected hourly wages rose 6.8 percent (1.0 percent). [11]

The wage-bin method is useful because movements high in the wage distribution can act as a falsification check rather than being attributed automatically to the policy. The paper reports some evidence of reduced tradable-sector employment, but those sector estimates use a smaller event sample and are imprecise and statistically insignificant. The studied minimum-to-median ratios span roughly 37 to 59 percent; the authors state that effects above that range remain open. A 2021 U.S. Department of Labor CLEAR review rated the study's causal evidence low because it found that the compared groups were not shown to be sufficiently similar before intervention. The estimates should therefore be reported as study findings with design uncertainty, not a universal zero-employment result. [11][14]

### Rent Control Exposes Allocation Costs Hidden by Price Data

Glaeser and Luttmer compare demographic housing-consumption patterns in rent-controlled New York City with free-market metropolitan areas. Their baseline uses the 1990 Census 5 percent Public Use Micro Sample: 10,000 New York renter households in buildings with at least five units and 20,600 control households from 103 metropolitan areas without rent control. After correcting for sampling error, they estimate that 20.9 percent of New York renters occupy apartments with a different number of rooms than predicted by the free-market allocation, with a standard error of 1.4 percentage points. [12]

The empirical design identifies an allocation margin beyond supply, but its interpretation is conditional. Placebo applications find statistically significant apparent misallocation in Hartford, Chicago, New York small-building renters, and New York owners; the authors conclude that their identifying assumptions are not strictly true, though New York's baseline estimate is substantially larger. The comparison between misallocation and undersupply is a theoretical result under stated allocation assumptions, not a direct dollar estimate from the New York data. The evidence therefore supports a non-price allocation problem while requiring caution about magnitude and causality. [12]

### What the Combined Evidence Establishes

The evidence supports a hierarchy rather than one universal formula. Relative demand and supply elasticities organize incidence in competitive, fully enforced, salient markets. Salience and evasion explain why legal design can matter. Demand curvature and conduct explain why pass-through can be incomplete or excessive under market power. Input-output and factor links explain why border or sector prices are not final incidence. Wage-bin and allocation evidence show why quantity and non-price margins must be observed rather than inferred. [2][3][5][6][8][11][12]

The studies also illustrate different identification strategies and their limits: a nonrandom price-label intervention with difference-in-differences controls; nonrandom collection-regime changes with timing and falsification checks; product-level tariff timing under exogeneity assumptions; local transaction-level subsidy discontinuities; a large institutional payroll reform with multiple estimators; wage-bin event studies disputed by an official evidence review; and cross-market allocation comparisons whose placebo tests reject the assumptions when interpreted strictly. None is assumption-free. A useful incidence estimate states the counterfactual, affected population, policy range, horizon, price concept, margins observed, and material threats to identification. [5][6][7][8][9][10][11][12][14]

## Implications

### For Policy Design

The first implication is to begin with the objective and the counterfactual. A revenue tax, corrective tax, tariff, subsidy, wage floor, and price ceiling may all create wedges, but they target different problems. If an activity has an external cost, reduced quantity can be a benefit rather than deadweight loss. If the objective is redistribution, who receives or bears surplus matters even when aggregate efficiency effects are modest. If the objective is industrial protection, input costs and retaliation must be counted alongside gains to protected producers. [2][8]

The second implication is to identify the least elastic margin before forecasting distribution, without confusing it with legal remittance. A narrow tax base with readily available substitutes can produce large avoidance and little revenue, while a broader base can reduce substitution among taxed forms but still affect the overall activity. Subsidy pass-through likewise depends on relative responses and market conduct. These are positive predictions conditional on a model, not a general instruction to tax or subsidize the least responsive group. [1][2][3][9]

Administration belongs inside incidence analysis. Collecting a tax from fewer upstream firms can reduce evasion and change pass-through; displaying a tax separately can reduce salience and change demand. These choices can also affect compliance cost, transparency, and enforcement concentration. The author's synthesis is that legal design should be evaluated through a chain: remittance point, visibility, compliance behavior, price response, quantity response, revenue, and distribution. The canonical invariance result is the baseline against which each departure is tested. [5][6]

Time should be explicit in legislation and evaluation. Pass-through measured before inventories, contracts, sourcing, entry, or asset prices adjust can differ from later incidence. Gruber's Chile evidence shows a payroll-tax change reflected in wages over a multi-year comparison, while property-tax theory shows how expected recurring payments can be capitalized into asset values. The author's synthesis is that an impact statement should report immediate, transitional, and long-run incidence separately whenever adjustment margins differ. [1][2][10]

### For Tax and Tariff Analysis

A tax analysis should report at least five objects: the change in the buyer's gross price, the seller's net price, quantity, government revenue, and total surplus. It should then trace wages, rents, profits, intermediate-input costs, and entry where relevant. This prevents two common mistakes: treating revenue as a social loss and treating border or checkout pass-through as the final burden. [2][7][8]

Tariff analysis especially requires input-output accounting. A tariff on final consumption can raise household prices directly; a tariff on intermediates changes the cost structure of domestic production. Downstream firms may raise prices, compress margins, change suppliers, or reduce output. Retaliation can reduce export demand in different sectors and regions. The author's synthesis is that claims that foreigners, importers, consumers, or producers "pay the tariff" are incomplete unless they name the price boundary and horizon. The 2018 evidence found little foreign-export-price adjustment at the border, but the domestic distribution still required a network and general-equilibrium model. [7][8]

Deadweight-loss estimates should be stress-tested against elasticity uncertainty. Because the quantity response enters the efficiency calculation, a narrow confidence interval around a tax rate does not create a narrow confidence interval around welfare. A robust report should show results across credible elasticities, nonlinear pass-through, and alternative counterfactuals, while separating corrective benefits from ordinary excess burden. The author's synthesis is that the worst error is false precision from one borrowed elasticity whose market definition or horizon does not match the policy. [2][3][5]

### For Subsidies and Corrective Policy

The author's assessment is that a subsidy should be evaluated by who receives the price benefit, how much additional activity it induces, and whether that activity creates the intended social value. High pass-through can help target consumers but does not by itself establish cost-effectiveness or additional adoption. A low rate is consistent with seller capture, constrained supply, or delayed adjustment, but distinguishing among them requires evidence beyond pass-through alone. An over-shifted rate can reveal market conduct that a competitive diagram misses, as in the California solar setting. [2][3][9]

The author's synthesis is that corrective policies require a distinction between the taxed or subsidized proxy and the underlying externality. Solar-adoption pass-through, for example, measures price incidence in the studied market; it is not itself an estimate of emissions effects, learning spillovers, grid value, or distributional objectives. The existing externalities topic develops instrument choice; incidence analysis adds who gains, who pays, and how the market transforms the nominal incentive into prices and quantities. [9]

The author's synthesis is to pair every subsidy with a counterfactual adoption estimate and a pass-through estimate. The first measures additional quantity; the second measures distribution between buyers and sellers. Neither alone measures social benefit. A program can pass through fully but induce little additional activity, or induce activity while transferring most funds to inframarginal participants. [2][9]

### For Wage Mandates and Price Controls

Minimum-wage analysis should not reduce incidence to one employment count. At minimum, it should distinguish hourly wages, employment, full-time-equivalent work, the location of jobs across wage bins, worker composition, entry or incumbency, and sector. Cengiz and coauthors measure those margins; their tradable-sector point estimates are imprecise and statistically insignificant, and the CLEAR review raises an additional comparability concern. Claims about prices, profits, automation, turnover, or monopsony require separate evidence. [11][14]

The author's synthesis is that a mandate should be evaluated against the local wage distribution, the supported policy range, and market structure. Cengiz and coauthors study minimum-to-median ratios of roughly 37 to 59 percent and explicitly leave effects above that range open; they do not identify employer power as the reason for their average result. Extrapolation beyond observed support requires a separate model, evidence, and uncertainty bounds. [11][14]

Price ceilings require measurement of allocation as well as supply. Stable controlled rents do not reveal who receives scarce units. Glaeser and Luttmer estimate room, bedroom, and maintenance-pattern differences and discuss search, mobility, and side-payment mechanisms, but they do not separately estimate every access cost. The author's synthesis is that every ceiling evaluation should specify the rationing mechanism that replaces price and report the identifying assumptions behind any allocation estimate. [12]

### For Businesses and Investors

The author's assessment is that a business should treat elasticity as a measured response for a segment, channel, location, and horizon rather than as a product label. Tax or input-cost pass-through should be estimated from price and volume changes while testing mix, quality, competitor response, and macroeconomic demand as alternative explanations. A firm that appears to pass through a tax may be serving a shrinking customer base, while one that initially absorbs a cost may reprice after contracts renew. [1][3]

Input-output exposure should be mapped beyond direct purchases. The relevant questions are which suppliers face the policy, how quickly inputs can be substituted, whether competitors share the exposure, and whether customers can switch. A common industry cost shock can raise market prices without proving firm-specific pricing power. Conversely, a firm with unique substitutes or long-term contracts may absorb a shock that industry averages suggest should pass through. The author's synthesis is to separate market pass-through from company capture: both affect profits, but they are not the same variable. [3][7][8]

The author's assessment is that investors should translate tax incidence into cash-flow and risk scenarios only after the economic channels are specified. A corporate tax, payroll tax, tariff, or environmental charge can reach revenue, input cost, wages, investment, working capital, and competitive position on different schedules. A valuation should not assign the statutory bill entirely to current profit if equilibrium prices and factor costs will adjust, nor assume full recovery from customers without evidence on demand and competition. [2][3][7][8]

### A Practical Incidence Protocol

The author's synthesis is a nine-step protocol. First, define the market narrowly enough that buyers, sellers, product, geography, and horizon are explicit. Second, state the statutory rule and the economic wedge. Third, identify whether the policy is marginal or large enough to move elasticities and market structure. Fourth, estimate point or arc elasticities appropriate to that scale. Fifth, test salience, evasion, compliance, and enforcement assumptions. [1][5][6]

Sixth, diagnose competition, demand curvature, marginal cost, and entry so that pass-through is not inferred from competitive formulas where they do not apply. Seventh, trace input-output, labor, capital, and regional links when the affected market is not economically isolated. Eighth, separate gross transfers, government revenue, corrective benefits, and deadweight loss. Ninth, report immediate, transitional, and long-run results with uncertainty and observations that would falsify the model. [2][3][4][8]

This protocol preserves the simple insight without overselling it. Relative elasticity remains the first answer to who bears a competitive-market wedge. It is not the last answer when institutions change attention or evasion, firms possess market power, policy propagates through production networks, or controlled prices replace exchange with rationing. Incidence is therefore an equilibrium claim supported by a specified model and identified evidence, not an accounting label attached to the party named in law. [2][3][5][6][12]
