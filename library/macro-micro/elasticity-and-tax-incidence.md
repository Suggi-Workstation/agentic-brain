# Elasticity Determines Incidence Only After Market Structure and Adjustment Are Made Explicit

Elasticity measures how strongly quantities respond to prices, while incidence traces who ultimately gains or loses when a tax, tariff, subsidy, wage mandate, or price control changes an equilibrium. In a competitive benchmark, the less elastic side of a market bears more of a tax and captures more of a subsidy, but that rule is local and conditional: market power, demand curvature, evasion, salience, input-output links, adjustment time, and rationing can all change observed pass-through and welfare. [1][2][3]

## Background

The intellectual origin of elasticity lies in the effort to convert the directional laws of demand and supply into quantitative statements. A downward-sloping demand curve says that a higher price reduces quantity demanded, but it does not say whether a 1 percent price increase reduces quantity by 0.1 percent or 10 percent. Price elasticity of demand answers that question by dividing the percentage change in quantity demanded by the percentage change in price; price elasticity of supply applies the same logic to quantity supplied. Demand elasticity is negative by sign convention because price and quantity move in opposite directions, but it is commonly reported in absolute value when classifying demand as elastic, unit elastic, or inelastic. [1]

Alfred Marshall introduced elasticity into a broader system of partial-equilibrium price analysis. That system separates shifts of demand or supply from movements along a curve and treats time as part of the model: productive capacity, habits, contracts, inventories, and substitutes can be fixed in the short run but adjustable in the long run. The result is not one permanent elasticity for a good. Elasticity belongs to a defined market, price, population, and horizon. A household may have few immediate alternatives to gasoline, while vehicle choice, residence, commuting, and production technology create more adjustment over years. OpenStax summarizes the general implication: demand and supply often, though not always, become more elastic over longer horizons. [1]

Tax incidence developed from the recognition that the person who sends money to the government need not be the person whose welfare falls. A per-unit tax creates a wedge between the price paid by a buyer and the net price received by a seller. Buyers can respond by purchasing less, sellers by producing less or leaving, firms by changing inputs or markups, workers by changing labor supply, and capital by moving across sectors or countries. Those responses change equilibrium prices, wages, rents, and returns. Fullerton and Metcalf therefore define incidence as the allocation of economic burden through higher product prices, lower wages, or lower returns to factors, and review partial-equilibrium, general-equilibrium, dynamic, and lifetime approaches to measuring it. [2]

The simplest competitive model supplied three durable benchmark propositions. First, statutory remittance and economic burden are conceptually distinct. Second, if the only change is which side remits an otherwise identical, fully enforced and fully salient tax, the same buyer-seller wedge and equilibrium result. Third, the less price-responsive side bears more because it has fewer alternatives. A nearly vertical demand curve lets sellers raise the buyer price with little quantity loss; a nearly vertical supply curve leaves sellers with little ability to withdraw and forces more adjustment into their net price. [1][2]

Harberger extended incidence beyond one market. His 1962 corporate-tax model divided a closed economy into corporate and noncorporate sectors using capital and labor, then traced how a tax on corporate capital caused factors to reallocate until returns and wages again cleared both sectors. The model demonstrated why a sectoral tax can affect owners and workers outside the taxed sector and why general-equilibrium incidence depends on substitution in production and consumption. It also made its limitations visible: fixed factor supplies, perfect competition, a closed economy, and local comparative statics are assumptions rather than universal facts. [4]

Later research weakened the idea that relative elasticities alone are sufficient. Weyl and Fabinger generalized incidence to imperfect competition, where pass-through depends on market conduct, marginal-cost response, and the curvature of demand as well as ordinary demand and supply elasticities. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft showed that consumers can respond differently to a tax and to an equally large posted-price change when the tax is less salient. Kopczuk, Marion, Muehlegger, and Slemrod showed that the point of legal collection can matter when evasion opportunities differ across supply-chain stages. These findings do not erase the competitive benchmark. They identify conditions that must hold before its invariance and elasticity rules can be applied literally. [3][5][6]

Policy analysis also widened from taxes to economically similar wedges. A tariff taxes an imported good and can propagate through imported inputs. A subsidy places a negative wedge between the amount a buyer pays and the amount a seller receives. A minimum wage is a price floor in a labor market, while rent control is a price ceiling in housing. Each policy changes prices, quantities, participation, and possibly allocation by non-price mechanisms. The common analytical problem is to identify the margin constrained by policy, measure the relevant responses, and distinguish transfers among groups from net losses in total surplus. [1][8][9][11][12]

## Core Concepts

### Point, Arc, and Policy Elasticities

For a demand function Q(P), point elasticity at a particular price and quantity is `(dQ/dP) * (P/Q)`. It is a local derivative: it describes the percentage quantity response to an infinitesimal percentage price change around one point. Point elasticity can change along a straight demand curve even when its slope is constant because the ratio P/Q changes. OpenStax illustrates this distinction by showing different elasticities on different portions of a constant-slope demand curve. [1]

Arc elasticity measures a discrete move between two observations. The midpoint formula divides the quantity change by average quantity and the price change by average price, then forms their ratio. Using averages makes the calculated elasticity the same whether the analyst describes the movement from the first point to the second or in reverse. Arc elasticity is useful for a material tax or price change, but it averages behavior over an interval and can conceal nonlinear responses within that interval. Point and arc elasticities are therefore not rival estimates of one timeless constant; they answer local and interval questions. [1]

A policy elasticity requires an additional distinction. A tax-inclusive price may change because the posted producer price changed, because the tax changed, or because both changed in equilibrium. If consumers do not fully attend to a separately displayed tax, the quantity response to a tax can differ from the response to an equal posted-price change. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft therefore estimate both tax and price elasticities and show that incidence and excess-burden formulas need both when salience is incomplete. [5]

### The Competitive Tax Wedge

Let `Pc` be the price paid by consumers, `Pp` the price received by producers, and `t` a per-unit tax. The wedge is `Pc - Pp = t`. A tax reduces the traded quantity from the no-tax equilibrium until demand at `Pc` equals supply at `Pp`. The government collects `t` times the post-tax quantity. That revenue is a transfer from private parties to the public sector, not itself a deadweight loss. [1][2]

For a small tax introduced into a competitive market, with positive supply elasticity `Es` and the absolute value of demand elasticity `Ed`, the local consumer share of the price wedge is `Es / (Es + Ed)` and the producer share is `Ed / (Es + Ed)`. Equivalently, the local pass-through rate to the consumer price is `dPc/dt = Es / (Es + Ed)`. These formulas encode the less-elastic-side rule. If supply is much more elastic than demand, producers can readily withdraw and consumers bear most of the tax. If demand is much more elastic than supply, buyers can readily leave and producers bear more through a lower net price. [1][2]

The formulas also show why elasticity is relative. Calling demand inelastic because `Ed` is below one does not by itself determine incidence; supply may be even less elastic. They are local formulas, so applying them to a large tax assumes that elasticities and market structure do not change materially over the policy interval. For a large discrete change, the analyst needs the entire path of demand, supply, and pass-through or an explicit structural model. [2][3]

Statutory incidence concerns who is legally responsible for remittance. Economic incidence concerns changes in welfare after all price and quantity responses. Under the canonical competitive assumptions, moving the remittance obligation from seller to buyer changes the accounting route but not the equilibrium wedge. That invariance can fail when the legal point of collection changes evasion, compliance cost, enforcement, salience, liquidity, or bargaining. The benchmark is therefore a conditional neutrality result, not a claim that administration never matters. [2][5][6]

### Taxes, Subsidies, and Pass-Through

Pass-through measures how much a policy-induced cost or benefit appears in a market price. For a unit tax, a consumer-price pass-through of 1 means the consumer price rises by the full tax; 0 means no increase; a value between 0 and 1 divides the price effect; and a value above 1 is over-shifting. The complementary change in the producer's net price completes the wedge under the simple model, but observed welfare incidence can also include quantity changes, fixed costs, entry, quality, and effects in other markets. [2][3]

A subsidy reverses the wedge. If the government pays `s` per unit, the total amount received by the seller can exceed the buyer's out-of-pocket price by `s`. In a competitive local benchmark, the less elastic side captures more of the subsidy just as it bears more of a tax. Economic incidence must be measured from market prices and welfare, not inferred from the named recipient. Pless and van Benthem's California solar evidence makes the point: pass-through was nearly complete for buyers and more than complete for lessees, a pattern their model and tests associate with imperfect competition and demand curvature. [9]

Pass-through is not identical to incidence in every model. A price change transfers surplus on units still traded, while the quantity response removes trades and changes producer costs and consumer value. Under market power, the pre-policy price already exceeds marginal cost. A tax can change both the price-cost wedge and output; a subsidy can alter entry or product variety. Weyl and Fabinger show that demand curvature and conduct determine how marginal cost changes map into price, so over-shifting can occur without violating optimization. [3][9]

### Short-Run and Long-Run Incidence

Short-run elasticities hold some adjustment margins fixed. A landlord cannot instantly construct housing, a refinery cannot instantly change capacity, a worker may be bound by a contract, and a household cannot instantly replace a vehicle or move. Long-run elasticities permit more substitution, entry, exit, capital reallocation, relocation, and technology change. A tax burden observed immediately can therefore migrate over time as quantities and factor prices adjust. [1][2]

This timing distinction is especially important for factor taxes. A payroll tax can initially raise an employer's labor cost when nominal wages are sticky, but subsequent bargaining or turnover can shift the burden into wages. A corporate tax can initially reduce after-tax profits in taxed firms, then change investment, capital location, wages, and prices. Fullerton and Metcalf distinguish static partial-equilibrium results from dynamic models in which capital accumulation changes future wages and returns; Harberger's model illustrates cross-sector factor reallocation even without an explicit time path. [2][4]

The author's synthesis is that every incidence claim should name at least two horizons: the adjustment window in the data and the horizon relevant to the decision. A one-month retail pass-through estimate can be correct and still differ from a five-year incidence estimate because inventories, contracts, sourcing, entry, and household substitution have not completed. This is not an empirical nuisance to average away; it is part of the causal mechanism. [1][2]

### Deadweight Loss and the Role of Elasticity

A tax transfers revenue to government and usually reduces quantity below the no-tax competitive level. Under linear curves and no pre-existing distortion, the familiar approximation to deadweight loss is one half times the per-unit tax times the reduction in quantity. The triangle represents mutually beneficial trades whose buyer value exceeded seller cost but not by enough to cover the tax wedge. The more responsive demand and supply are, the larger the quantity response to a given small tax and, other conditions equal, the larger the efficiency loss. [1][2]

This approximation has strict boundaries. It assumes the pre-tax competitive quantity is the relevant efficient benchmark, treats quality and entry consistently, and excludes other distortions. If an activity creates an unpriced external cost, a corrective tax can move quantity toward rather than away from the social optimum. If a market already has monopoly underproduction, a tax and the existing markup interact. If consumers ignore a nonsalient tax, little observed quantity change does not prove zero welfare loss because budget-allocation errors can remain. Chetty, Looney, and Kroft derive incidence and efficiency formulas that separate responses to tax-inclusive prices from responses to taxes themselves. [3][5]

Tax revenue, producer loss, consumer loss, and deadweight loss must not be added indiscriminately. Consumer and producer surplus can fall partly because resources are transferred to government. Net social loss subtracts revenue and counts real resource, allocation, and behavioral costs, subject to the welfare weights and externalities in the model. A distributional evaluation can still care greatly about transfers even when aggregate surplus is unchanged. Incidence and efficiency are related but separate questions. [2][8]

### Imperfect Competition, Curvature, and Market Power

Under imperfect competition, firms choose prices or quantities while recognizing that their actions affect the market. A tax changes marginal cost, but the price response depends on how the firm's optimal markup changes as quantity changes. Demand elasticity at one point does not fully describe this response; the curvature of demand and the conduct of rivals also matter. Weyl and Fabinger extend competitive incidence principles to a general imperfectly competitive framework precisely because the simple elasticity split is incomplete in these markets. [3]

Over-shifting illustrates the difference. A one-dollar tax can raise price by more than one dollar, or a one-dollar subsidy can lower price by more than one dollar, when the policy moves the firm to a portion of demand where the optimal markup changes sufficiently. Pless and van Benthem use subsidy over-shifting as evidence relevant to market power after ruling out alternative explanations and testing demand curvature. An observed pass-through above 100 percent is therefore not proof that arithmetic failed; it is evidence that a competitive constant-markup interpretation may be wrong. [3][9]

Market concentration alone does not determine pass-through. Competition mode, product differentiation, capacity, entry, multi-product pricing, and the shape of marginal cost all matter. A concentrated market can exhibit low, complete, or excessive pass-through under different demand and conduct conditions. The practical implication is to estimate pass-through and its heterogeneity rather than assign it from an industry label. [3]

### Input-Output Links and General Equilibrium

A policy imposed at one transaction can travel through a production network. A tariff collected at the border raises the buyer's cost of an imported final good, but a tariff on steel, semiconductors, or machinery also raises costs for domestic downstream producers. Those producers can absorb the cost in margins, change suppliers, reduce output, alter quality, or pass some cost to their customers. The final burden can reach consumers, workers, owners, suppliers, and regions that never remit the tariff. [7][8]

Fajgelbaum and coauthors combine estimated trade responses with a general-equilibrium model containing sectoral specialization and input-output relationships. That method distinguishes the direct loss to consumers and firms buying imports from tariff revenue, gains to protected producers, retaliatory effects, and regional exposure. Harberger's older two-sector model makes the same conceptual move in simpler form: factor mobility transmits a sectoral tax into economy-wide returns. General equilibrium is required when the policy is large enough, the input is widely used enough, or factor reallocation is central enough that holding other markets fixed would omit important burden channels. [4][8]

### Wage Mandates and Price Controls as Incidence Problems

A minimum wage is a statutory floor on the price of labor rather than a tax. Its incidence can appear in higher hourly pay, fewer jobs or hours, changed hiring standards, lower turnover, higher product prices, lower profits, or substitution among workers and technologies. Which margin dominates depends on labor demand, labor supply, monopsony power, product-market pass-through, and the policy's level relative to the wage distribution. The competitive diagram predicts disemployment from a binding floor, but it does not determine the magnitude without elasticities and market structure. [1][11]

A binding price ceiling suppresses monetary price adjustment and creates excess demand. Incidence then migrates into non-price margins: queues, search, favoritism, reduced maintenance, lower quality, black markets, or misallocation across buyers. Glaeser and Luttmer show that if scarce controlled apartments do not go to the highest-valuing users, misallocation can create welfare costs in addition to the textbook undersupply triangle. Price-control incidence therefore cannot be read solely from the regulated price. [12]

The author's synthesis is that taxes, subsidies, wage floors, and price ceilings should be analyzed with one common sequence: identify the wedge or constraint, identify every feasible response margin, estimate the relevant elasticities and pass-through over a stated horizon, trace linked markets, and separate distributional transfers from net resource losses. The policy labels differ, but the equilibrium discipline is the same. [2][3][12]

## Evidence

### Salience Separates Tax Responses from Price Responses

Chetty, Looney, and Kroft use two complementary designs. In a three-week grocery-store field experiment, tax-inclusive shelf labels were posted for 750 taxable products; scanner data show that demand for treated products fell by 8 percent. In observational state data, increases in alcohol excise taxes included in posted prices reduced consumption more than sales taxes added at the register. The method distinguishes a tax change from an equally visible posted-price change instead of assuming consumers process them identically. [5]

Their welfare framework then uses separately estimated tax and price elasticities. This is a direct test of a canonical assumption: if consumers fully optimize over tax-inclusive prices, the two responses should coincide after pass-through is accounted for. The evidence supports underreaction to less salient taxes and implies that statutory design can affect economic incidence when it changes attention. The limitation is equally important: the result identifies behavior in the studied retail and alcohol settings and does not establish one salience parameter for every tax or population. [5]

### Collection Point Tests the Evasion Boundary of Invariance

Kopczuk, Marion, Muehlegger, and Slemrod exploit variation across U.S. states and over time in the supply-chain stage responsible for remitting diesel taxes. Moving collection upstream from retailers to distributors or prime suppliers substantially increased pass-through to retail diesel prices. Tax revenue also rose under upstream collection, supporting evasion as the mechanism rather than a purely mechanical relabeling of the payer. [6]

The design is valuable because it studies a change in statutory administration while the economic object remains a state diesel tax. Under the simplest invariance model, the collection point should not change final incidence. It does when enforcement and evasion opportunities differ. This finding does not overturn the benchmark for a fully enforced, equally salient tax; it shows why those qualifications must be tested before legal remittance is declared irrelevant. [6]

### Two Tariff Studies Show Direct and Network Incidence

Amiti, Redding, and Weinstein analyze the 2018 U.S. tariff waves using detailed import prices, quantities, producer prices, and product varieties. They find complete pass-through into domestic prices of imported goods, meaning foreign exporters did not materially lower pre-tariff prices in their estimates. Their calculations imply a reduction in U.S. real income of $1.4 billion per month by the end of 2018, alongside supply-chain adjustment and lost import varieties. The method identifies price effects at the product and country level and then accounts for welfare channels. [7]

Fajgelbaum, Goldberg, Kennedy, and Khandelwal use a different empirical and modeling route. They estimate import and retaliatory tariff effects on prices and quantities, then embed the elasticities in a general-equilibrium model with sectoral and regional linkages. Targeted import prices did not fall, again implying complete tariff pass-through at the border. They estimate $51 billion in losses to U.S. consumers and firms buying imports, while tariff revenue and gains to domestic producers reduce the modeled aggregate real-income loss to $7.2 billion. [8]

The figures answer different accounting questions and should not be substituted for one another. Gross losses to import buyers, government revenue, producer gains, retaliation, and net real-income loss are separate quantities. Agreement on near-complete border pass-through is strengthened by the distinct methods, while the general-equilibrium decomposition demonstrates why border incidence is not the final distribution across households, firms, factors, and regions. [7][8]

### Subsidy Pass-Through Reveals Heterogeneous Market Conduct

Pless and van Benthem match California solar-system transactions to subsidy variation and compare owner-purchased with third-party-owned or leased systems. Buyers captured nearly the full subsidy, while lessees received more-than-complete pass-through. The authors formalize over-shifting as a test for market power, examine demand curvature, and rule out alternative explanations within their setting. [9]

This case supplies three lessons. A subsidy legally paid to a firm need not remain with that firm. Pass-through can differ across contractual forms for the same physical product. A rate above one can be an economically meaningful response under imperfect competition rather than sampling noise by definition. The evidence does not imply that every subsidy is over-shifted; it shows that incidence must be estimated in the actual market and contract environment. [9]

### Payroll Taxes Show a Wage Margin

Gruber studies Chile's social-security privatization, which reduced the average payroll tax rate in the manufacturing sample from about 30 percent to 5 percent over six years. Using a census of manufacturing firms and multiple estimators designed to address measurement error in firm tax rates and wages, he finds strong evidence that the tax reduction was fully shifted into wages, with no employment effect in that setting. [10]

This finding illustrates long-run labor-market incidence: a tax nominally paid by firms can be borne by workers through lower wages. It is not a universal payroll-tax coefficient. The episode involved a large reform, a particular labor market, and data whose firm-specific tax variation could contain measurement error, a limitation the paper addresses but cannot make irrelevant. Its value is causal and institutional specificity, not permission to assume full wage shifting wherever wages are rigid, informality is high, benefits are valued differently, or labor demand and supply differ. [10]

### Minimum-Wage Evidence Identifies Where Jobs Move in the Distribution

Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer study 138 prominent state minimum-wage changes between 1979 and 2016. Their difference-in-differences bunching method compares missing jobs below a new minimum with excess jobs at or just above it across wage bins. They find that the overall number of low-wage jobs remained essentially unchanged over five years following the increases studied, average earnings rose, and modest spillovers appeared above the new floor; they also find some evidence of reduced employment in tradable sectors. [11]

The wage-bin method is important because a broad employment regression can attribute unrelated movements higher in the distribution to the minimum wage. The result also shows why one elasticity need not describe all sectors: firms facing local demand, product-market power, or nontradable services can adjust differently from firms exposed to external competition. The study estimates average effects over its historical policy range; it does not prove that any minimum wage at any level has zero employment cost. [11]

### Rent Control Exposes Allocation Costs Hidden by Price Data

Glaeser and Luttmer compare housing consumption patterns across demographic groups in rent-controlled and free-market locations to test whether controlled apartments are allocated differently from market housing. They find an economically and statistically significant degree of misallocation in New York City. Their framework shows that welfare losses from assigning scarce apartments to lower-valuing rather than higher-valuing users can exceed the conventional undersupply loss. [12]

The empirical design does not reduce rent control to one supply elasticity. It identifies a separate allocation margin created when a binding ceiling prevents price from clearing the market. The case demonstrates a general rule for price controls: observed rents can be stable by law while search time, match quality, maintenance, mobility, side payments, and access bear the adjustment. A price-only incidence calculation would miss those burdens. [12]

### What the Combined Evidence Establishes

The evidence supports a hierarchy rather than one universal formula. Relative demand and supply elasticities organize incidence in competitive, fully enforced, salient markets. Salience and evasion explain why legal design can matter. Demand curvature and conduct explain why pass-through can be incomplete or excessive under market power. Input-output and factor links explain why border or sector prices are not final incidence. Wage-bin and allocation evidence show why quantity and non-price margins must be observed rather than inferred. [2][3][5][6][8][11][12]

The studies also illustrate credible identification strategies: randomized display of tax-inclusive prices, policy variation across jurisdictions and time, product-level tariff timing, transaction-level subsidy schedules, a large institutional payroll reform, wage-distribution bunching, and cross-market allocation comparisons. None is assumption-free. A useful incidence estimate states the counterfactual, affected population, policy range, horizon, price concept, and margins observed. Without those elements, an elasticity applied to a diagram is a hypothesis rather than an empirical result. [5][6][7][8][9][10][11][12]

## Implications

### For Policy Design

The first implication is to begin with the objective and the counterfactual. A revenue tax, corrective tax, tariff, subsidy, wage floor, and price ceiling may all create wedges, but they target different problems. If an activity has an external cost, reduced quantity can be a benefit rather than deadweight loss. If the objective is redistribution, who receives or bears surplus matters even when aggregate efficiency effects are modest. If the objective is industrial protection, input costs and retaliation must be counted alongside gains to protected producers. [2][8]

The second implication is to design around the least elastic margin without confusing it with legal remittance. A narrow tax base with readily available substitutes can produce large avoidance and little revenue. A broad base can reduce substitution among taxed forms while still affecting the overall activity. A subsidy paid to suppliers can be captured by buyers if supply is elastic, captured by sellers if buyers can readily enter or exit, or over-shifted when market power and curvature produce that response. The correct forecast uses market responses, not the name printed on the statute. [1][2][3][9]

Administration belongs inside incidence analysis. Collecting a tax from fewer upstream firms can reduce evasion and change pass-through; displaying a tax separately can reduce salience and change demand. These choices can also affect compliance cost, transparency, and enforcement concentration. The author's synthesis is that legal design should be evaluated through a chain: remittance point, visibility, compliance behavior, price response, quantity response, revenue, and distribution. The canonical invariance result is the baseline against which each departure is tested. [5][6]

Time should be explicit in legislation and evaluation. A temporary tariff can be absorbed from inventories before contracts and sourcing change. A permanent payroll tax can shift into wages through bargaining and turnover. A subsidy can induce entry, learning, or capitalization into asset prices. The author's synthesis is that an impact statement should report immediate, transitional, and long-run incidence separately whenever adjustment margins differ. One number without a horizon invites a false contradiction between studies that observe different stages of the same response. [1][2]

### For Tax and Tariff Analysis

A tax analysis should report at least five objects: the change in the buyer's gross price, the seller's net price, quantity, government revenue, and total surplus. It should then trace wages, rents, profits, intermediate-input costs, and entry where relevant. This prevents two common mistakes: treating revenue as a social loss and treating border or checkout pass-through as the final burden. [2][7][8]

Tariff analysis especially requires input-output accounting. A tariff on final consumption can raise household prices directly; a tariff on intermediates changes the cost structure of domestic production. Downstream firms may raise prices, compress margins, change suppliers, or reduce output. Retaliation can reduce export demand in different sectors and regions. The author's synthesis is that claims that foreigners, importers, consumers, or producers "pay the tariff" are incomplete unless they name the price boundary and horizon. The 2018 evidence found little foreign-export-price adjustment at the border, but the domestic distribution still required a network and general-equilibrium model. [7][8]

Deadweight-loss estimates should be stress-tested against elasticity uncertainty. Because the quantity response enters the efficiency calculation, a narrow confidence interval around a tax rate does not create a narrow confidence interval around welfare. A robust report should show results across credible elasticities, nonlinear pass-through, and alternative counterfactuals, while separating corrective benefits from ordinary excess burden. The author's synthesis is that the worst error is false precision from one borrowed elasticity whose market definition or horizon does not match the policy. [2][3][5]

### For Subsidies and Corrective Policy

A subsidy should be evaluated by who receives the price benefit, how much additional activity it induces, and whether that activity creates the intended social value. A high pass-through rate can help target consumers but does not prove cost-effectiveness; much spending may go to transactions that would have occurred without the subsidy. A low rate can indicate seller capture, constrained supply, or adjustment delay. An over-shifted rate can reveal market conduct that a competitive diagram misses. [3][9]

Corrective policies also require a distinction between the taxed or subsidized proxy and the underlying externality. A tax on fuel is not identical to a tax on emissions if vehicles, locations, or users create different damages. A solar subsidy supports adoption but may be a coarse instrument for emissions, learning spillovers, grid value, or distributional objectives. The existing externalities topic develops instrument choice; incidence analysis adds who gains, who pays, and how the market transforms the nominal incentive into actual prices and quantities. [9]

The author's synthesis is to pair every subsidy with a counterfactual adoption estimate and a pass-through estimate. The first measures additional quantity; the second measures distribution between buyers and sellers. Neither alone measures social benefit. A program can pass through fully but induce little additional activity, or induce activity while transferring most funds to inframarginal participants. [2][9]

### For Wage Mandates and Price Controls

Minimum-wage analysis should avoid reducing incidence to an employment count. The relevant outcomes include hourly wages, hours, employment, hiring, separations, worker composition, turnover, prices, profits, automation, and sectoral relocation. Cengiz and coauthors' wage-bin design shows that the location of job changes within the distribution matters, while their tradable-sector result warns that product-market exposure changes adjustment. [11]

The author's synthesis is that a mandate should be evaluated against the local wage distribution and market structure. A modest floor in a labor market with employer power can raise wages with little employment loss; a much higher floor or a highly trade-exposed sector can produce different responses. Historical average effects should not be extrapolated beyond the observed policy range without a structural argument and uncertainty bounds. [11]

Price ceilings require measurement of allocation as well as supply. Stable controlled rents do not show the cost of waiting, restricted mobility, match quality, deferred maintenance, or exclusion of newcomers. Glaeser and Luttmer's method demonstrates how demographic consumption patterns can reveal misallocation that the regulated price conceals. The author's synthesis is that every ceiling evaluation should specify the rationing mechanism that replaces price. If no mechanism is named, the analysis has omitted the process that determines access. [12]

### For Businesses and Investors

For a business, elasticity is not a product label; it is a measured response for a segment, channel, location, and horizon. Tax or input-cost pass-through should be estimated from realized price and volume changes while separating mix, quality, competitor response, and macroeconomic demand. A firm that appears able to pass through a tax may be harvesting a shrinking customer base, while a firm that initially absorbs a cost may later reprice after contracts renew. [1][3]

Input-output exposure should be mapped beyond direct purchases. The relevant questions are which suppliers face the policy, how quickly inputs can be substituted, whether competitors share the exposure, and whether customers can switch. A common industry cost shock can raise market prices without proving firm-specific pricing power. Conversely, a firm with unique substitutes or long-term contracts may absorb a shock that industry averages suggest should pass through. The author's synthesis is to separate market pass-through from company capture: both affect profits, but they are not the same variable. [3][7][8]

For investors, tax incidence is a cash-flow and discount-rate problem, but the analysis remains economic before it becomes financial. A corporate, payroll, tariff, or environmental tax can reach revenue, input cost, wages, capital expenditure, working capital, and terminal competitive position on different schedules. A valuation should not assign the statutory tax bill entirely to current profit if equilibrium prices and factor costs will adjust, nor assume full recovery from customers without evidence on demand and competition. [2][3][4]

### A Practical Incidence Protocol

The author's synthesis is a nine-step protocol. First, define the market narrowly enough that buyers, sellers, product, geography, and horizon are explicit. Second, state the statutory rule and the economic wedge. Third, identify whether the policy is marginal or large enough to move elasticities and market structure. Fourth, estimate point or arc elasticities appropriate to that scale. Fifth, test salience, evasion, compliance, and enforcement assumptions. [1][5][6]

Sixth, diagnose competition, demand curvature, marginal cost, and entry so that pass-through is not inferred from competitive formulas where they do not apply. Seventh, trace input-output, labor, capital, and regional links when the affected market is not economically isolated. Eighth, separate gross transfers, government revenue, corrective benefits, and deadweight loss. Ninth, report immediate, transitional, and long-run results with uncertainty and observations that would falsify the model. [2][3][4][8]

This protocol preserves the simple insight without overselling it. Relative elasticity remains the first answer to who bears a competitive-market wedge. It is not the last answer when institutions change attention or evasion, firms possess market power, policy propagates through production networks, or controlled prices replace exchange with rationing. Incidence is therefore an equilibrium claim supported by a specified model and identified evidence, not an accounting label attached to the party named in law. [2][3][5][6][12]
