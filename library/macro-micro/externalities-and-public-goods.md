# Externalities and Public Goods -- Market Prices Miss Social Costs and Benefits

Externalities arise when an action changes other people's welfare without an equivalent price entering the decision, while public goods combine shared consumption with weak or impossible exclusion. In both cases, observed market prices omit part of the relevant social value, so efficient analysis must identify the missing cost or benefit, the information and transaction costs of correcting it, and who gains or loses under each remedy. [1][2][3]

## Background

Modern analysis of externalities grew from welfare economics. Arthur Pigou distinguished the private net product considered by an individual or firm from the social net product that includes effects on other people. When the two diverge, a competitive price can guide the decision-maker toward an output that does not maximize total welfare; Pigou therefore examined taxes, bounties, legal rules, information, and public provision as possible responses rather than treating every spillover as a case for one automatic instrument. [1]

Pollution supplies the standard negative case. A producer that pays for fuel, labor, and machinery but not for harm imposed by its emissions faces a marginal private cost below the marginal social cost. The private optimum therefore permits more of the harmful activity than an optimum based on all affected parties' costs. Positive spillovers reverse the wedge: when research, vaccination, or another action benefits people who do not pay the originator, marginal private benefit is below marginal social benefit, and the decentralized quantity tends to be too low. This description is conditional on the effect being technologically real, uncompensated, and relevant at the margin; a transfer through market prices alone is not necessarily an external cost. [1][8]

Paul Samuelson supplied the canonical theory of collective consumption in 1954. He defined a collective consumption good as one whose use by one person does not subtract from the amount available to another. For a private good, different people's quantities add horizontally because a unit consumed by one person cannot also be consumed by another. For a pure public good, each person experiences the same supplied quantity, and efficient provision requires the vertical sum of individual marginal willingness to pay to equal marginal cost. Samuelson also stressed the preference-revelation problem: each beneficiary has an incentive to understate interest in a jointly consumed good and let others finance it. [3]

Contemporary usage adds nonexcludability to nonrivalry. Nonrivalry means an additional user's consumption does not materially reduce another user's opportunity to consume; nonexcludability means preventing an unpaying user is technically, legally, or economically difficult. A pure public good has both properties, but many important goods are impure. A road is largely nonrival when empty but rival when congested; a digital standard can be widely shared yet remain excludable through licenses or access controls; a fishery can be hard to exclude users from while every catch reduces what remains. Elinor Ostrom's work made these distinctions operational by comparing public goods, toll or club goods, common-pool resources, and private goods rather than forcing all shared resources into one category. [5]

Ronald Coase changed the policy question from "who caused the harm?" to "which feasible institutional arrangement yields the highest total product?" In a hypothetical world with well-defined rights and costless bargaining, affected parties can negotiate toward the higher-valued use regardless of the initial assignment of liability, although that assignment still determines the distribution of wealth. Coase's larger point concerned the real world: bargaining, measurement, enforcement, organization, litigation, and government administration all have costs. Courts, firms, markets, and regulators are alternative institutions whose total effects must be compared, not ideal solutions judged against a frictionless market. [2]

Instrument choice became more explicit in Martin Weitzman's comparison of prices and quantities under uncertainty. A tax fixes the marginal price of an external effect but allows the resulting quantity to vary; a quota or cap fixes quantity but allows marginal compliance cost to vary. Under Weitzman's benchmark, their relative expected welfare depends on the slopes of marginal benefit and marginal cost when the regulator must act before uncertain costs are known. The implication is not that taxes always dominate caps or vice versa. It is that information about damages, abatement costs, enforcement, and the consequences of quantity error determines which uncertainty society should bear. [4]

Ostrom's field and experimental program challenged a second false binary: that shared resources must be either privatized or administered by a central government. Her Nobel lecture reports evidence that users sometimes construct durable, polycentric rules for public goods and common-pool resources. Communication, monitoring, graduated sanctions, legitimate local rule-making, and nested institutions can support cooperation, while isolated anonymous users are more likely to overharvest. These findings do not abolish the free-rider problem; they show that institutions can change incentives, information, trust, and enforcement costs. [5]

The late twentieth century turned these theories into measurable policy experiments. The U.S. Acid Rain Program placed a national cap on sulfur dioxide emissions from large power plants and allowed allowances to trade, combining a binding aggregate quantity with decentralized choices about where and how to abate. Research, vaccination, infrastructure, and digital networks supplied different positive-spillover cases in which private returns, geographic reach, congestion, and compatibility varied. The resulting evidence supports a mechanism-first approach: classify the missing price, identify who is affected and at what scale, then compare feasible remedies and their distributional consequences. [6][7][9][10][11][12][13]

## Core Concepts

### The Marginal Wedge Between Private and Social Value

An externality is defined at the margin. For a negative production externality, marginal social cost equals marginal private cost plus marginal external damage. A competitive producer normally expands output until price equals marginal private cost, but the social optimum equates marginal benefit with the larger marginal social cost. The units between those two quantities create less benefit than total cost. A corrective tax equal to marginal external damage at the efficient quantity can, under the benchmark assumptions, align the producer's private condition with the social one. [1][8]

For a positive externality, marginal social benefit equals marginal private benefit plus marginal external benefit. The private decision-maker stops when personal benefit equals personal cost, even though additional units may generate benefits for others. A subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit can align the private and social conditions in the same benchmark model. Research spillovers and infection reduction after vaccination are examples because some benefits accrue to other firms, households, or communities rather than solely to the actor paying for the activity. [9][10][11]

The distinction between average and marginal effects is essential. A large total social benefit does not imply that the next unit has the same benefit, and an average damage estimate is not automatically the efficient tax rate. Marginal external damage can vary by location, time, technology, and recipient. Knittel and Sandler show the practical consequence in transportation: vehicle emissions and responses to gasoline prices differ across vehicles, so a uniform gasoline tax is only an indirect proxy for the underlying emissions externality. In many sample years, more than 75 percent of emissions-related deadweight loss remained even under their optimal second-best uniform tax. [8]

Externalities must also be distinguished from pecuniary effects. If a new competitor lowers an incumbent's price, the incumbent loses but consumers gain through the same market transaction; this redistribution through prices is not by itself a technological externality. By contrast, smoke that damages health or knowledge that raises another firm's productivity affects real opportunities outside the priced exchange. The author's synthesis is that analysts should identify a physical, informational, or behavioral transmission channel before labeling an unwanted effect an externality. [1][2]

### Public Goods, Club Goods, Commons, and Private Goods

The two dimensions of rivalry and exclusion produce four useful ideal types. Private goods are rival and excludable: consuming a meal removes it from others, and the seller can withhold it from nonpayers. Pure public goods are nonrival and nonexcludable: once supplied, an additional beneficiary does not appreciably reduce anyone else's consumption and cannot feasibly be excluded. Toll or club goods are nonrival over a range but excludable, as with a subscription database or uncongested members-only network. Common-pool resources are rival but difficult to exclude, as with a fish stock or groundwater basin. [3][5]

These categories describe properties, not moral judgments or ownership labels. A publicly financed service can be rival and excludable, while a privately produced protocol or body of knowledge can have public-good features. Technology, law, congestion, and institutional design can move a good between categories. Encryption can make digital access excludable; tolling can convert open road access into a club arrangement; congestion can make an initially nonrival facility rival; patents can make knowledge legally excludable for a period even though one person's use of an idea does not physically consume it. [5][12][13]

Public goods create a free-rider problem because a person who can enjoy the supplied quantity without paying has an incentive to conceal willingness to pay. Samuelson's efficient condition adds beneficiaries' marginal rates of substitution vertically until their combined willingness to pay equals marginal cost. A market seller, however, cannot generally charge each beneficiary according to the unobserved value received when exclusion is impossible. Private voluntary supply may therefore fall below the Samuelson quantity even when the sum of benefits exceeds cost. [3]

Underprovision is a tendency, not a universal outcome. A donor with unusually high value may finance a public good for everyone; firms can bundle a shared benefit with an excludable product; advertising, reputation, reciprocity, repeated interaction, or selective benefits can support contributions. Ostrom's evidence shows that communication, monitoring, and user-created sanctions can also sustain cooperation. The important question is not whether free riding exists in theory, but which institutional features make contribution, exclusion, monitoring, and enforcement feasible in the specific group. [5][14]

Common-pool resources require a different diagnosis. Their problem is not mainly that too little is supplied but that too much may be extracted because each user receives the private benefit of extraction while sharing depletion costs with others. A rule suited to a pure public good can therefore be wrong for a commons. Charging nothing for an uncongested public signal can be efficient, while charging for a congested road or limiting a fish catch can preserve scarce capacity. The author's synthesis is that remedy selection should begin with rivalry at the relevant load level and exclusion at the relevant institutional cost. [5][12]

### Pigouvian Taxes and Subsidies

A Pigouvian tax places a price on marginal external harm; a Pigouvian subsidy rewards marginal external benefit. Their attraction is decentralization. Once the price is set, households and firms compare it with their own marginal costs and benefits, revealing dispersed information through behavior rather than following one prescribed technology. Tax revenue can finance compensation, public goods, or reductions in other taxes, while subsidy finance requires revenue from somewhere else. None of those distributional choices follows automatically from the efficiency condition. [1][8]

The benchmark requires demanding information. The authority must estimate the external effect at the margin, observe or proxy the relevant activity, collect the tax or pay the subsidy, and update the rate as conditions change. A tax on a correlated input can be badly targeted when harm varies independently of that input. A subsidy can pay for inframarginal activity that would have occurred anyway, attract strategic relabeling, or transfer resources to parties with high bargaining power. Administrative cost, tax-system distortions, and distribution can move the preferred rate away from the textbook first best. [8]

Incidence is separate from statutory liability. A pollution tax legally imposed on producers can be passed to consumers, workers, landowners, or suppliers depending on relative elasticities and market structure. Revenue recycling can offset some household burdens, but a corrective tax does not itself compensate the particular people harmed. The same tax rate can also impose different burdens across income groups or places. A complete proposal therefore specifies the tax base, rate path, monitoring rule, expected behavioral response, revenue use, and distributional adjustment rather than presenting the marginal-damage estimate as the whole policy. [1][8]

### Direct Regulation and Standards

Direct regulation restricts technologies, emissions rates, locations, or conduct. It can be preferable when harms are difficult to reverse, when monitoring a prohibited action is easier than measuring continuous damage, when a minimum safety standard is required, or when administrative and bargaining costs make decentralized adjustment unreliable. Coase recognized regulation as one possible institution while warning that uniform rules can be inappropriate across heterogeneous cases and that government action has its own operating costs. [2]

Standards can provide quantity certainty at the regulated unit but may sacrifice cost-effectiveness. If firms have different abatement costs, a uniform technology mandate can force an expensive reduction at one source while leaving a cheaper reduction unused elsewhere. Performance standards preserve more flexibility than technology standards, but regulators still need reliable measurement and enforcement. Distribution can also differ from a tax: compliance costs may be embedded in product prices, while no public revenue is created for rebates or complementary investment. [2][4][6]

The correct comparison is not "regulation versus no regulation." Property, contract, and nuisance law already define permitted actions and liabilities, and market exchange operates inside those rules. The comparison is among alternative legal and administrative designs, each with measurement error, compliance cost, strategic behavior, and enforcement risk. The author's synthesis is that direct rules are strongest where the prohibited margin is observable and catastrophic error must be bounded, and weakest where heterogeneous actors can find many low-cost adjustments that a regulator cannot specify in advance. [2][4]

### Property Rights, Liability, and Bargaining

Coasean bargaining can internalize a spillover when affected rights are clear, parties are few enough to negotiate, information is usable, agreements are enforceable, and strategic holdout is limited. If a factory's noise harms one neighboring clinic, either side might pay for quieter equipment, altered hours, insulation, or relocation depending on which adjustment costs least. Under the zero-transaction-cost benchmark, the initial right affects who pays and receives compensation but not the efficient use. [2]

Real cases depart from the benchmark. Air pollution can affect millions of people whose injuries and valuations differ; future victims may not be represented; causal attribution may be disputed; and bargaining can be defeated by holdouts, free riders, legal expense, or power asymmetry. Clear rights remain valuable because they identify standing and the object of exchange, but assignment is not distributionally neutral. Giving the polluter a right to emit and giving residents a right to clean air can produce the same efficient quantity only under the frictionless theorem, while wealth, bargaining power, and the legitimacy of the outcome differ. [2]

Firms, associations, and local governments can be understood as devices for lowering repeated transaction costs. A condominium association writes common rules instead of renegotiating every noise or maintenance spillover; a firm brings interacting activities under one authority; a watershed group coordinates users who share a resource. Ostrom's work adds that durable governance usually requires boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, and recognized authority to make local rules. [2][5]

### Cap-and-Trade and Tradable Rights

Cap-and-trade fixes an aggregate quantity and divides it into transferable allowances. Sources with low abatement costs reduce more and sell allowances; sources with high costs buy them. If monitoring is accurate, trading is competitive, and transactions are not obstructed, allowance prices tend to equalize marginal abatement costs and achieve the cap at lower total compliance cost than source-by-source uniform requirements. Banking can shift reductions across time, while a declining cap can tighten the aggregate constraint. [6][7]

The cap determines environmental quantity; allowance allocation determines wealth and political incidence. Free allocation can transfer scarcity rents to incumbents without changing the marginal incentive to emit, while auctions create public revenue. Spatially heterogeneous damage complicates a uniform trading ratio: moving a ton of emissions from a sparsely populated location to a densely populated one can lower compliance cost but raise health damage. Chan and coauthors document exactly this tension in the Acid Rain Program, where trading saved compliance cost relative to a uniform performance standard but produced greater estimated health damages than their no-trade counterfactual because allowances moved toward eastern plants near larger exposed populations. [7]

Prices and quantities therefore solve different information problems. A tax limits marginal compliance cost but leaves emissions uncertain; a cap limits emissions but leaves allowance price uncertain. Hybrid designs can add price floors, ceilings, reserves, or differentiated trading ratios, but each added feature demands information and governance. Weitzman's framework supplies the central test: compare the welfare consequences of being wrong about quantity with those of being wrong about marginal cost. [4]

### Public Provision, Procurement, and User Finance

Public provision can address a public good when exclusion is infeasible and broad compulsory finance can aggregate resources. Provision does not require government production: a public authority can finance and specify a service while contracting with private or nonprofit suppliers. The public-good problem remains informational because willingness to pay is not directly revealed, and political processes can overprovide visible projects, underprovide maintenance, or distribute benefits toward organized groups. Samuelson's preference-revelation problem therefore applies to public budgeting as well as private markets. [3][12]

Infrastructure illustrates the mixed nature of provision. Glaeser and Poterba identify externalities, natural monopoly, and capital-market limits as distinct rationales for public involvement, while emphasizing project-level cost-benefit analysis and economy-wide spillovers. User fees can reveal demand and ration congested capacity when beneficiaries are identifiable. General taxation is more defensible where benefits are broad, exclusion is costly, or marginal cost is well below the average cost needed to finance a natural monopoly. The source of finance and the efficient price need not be identical: an access charge, usage price, subsidy, or tax transfer may be combined to cover fixed cost without encouraging inefficient use. [12]

### Research, Vaccination, Infrastructure, and Digital Networks

Research produces private returns through patents, products, and process improvements, but knowledge can also raise other firms' productivity. Because inventors cannot capture every downstream use, positive knowledge spillovers can justify research subsidies, prizes, public laboratories, procurement, or limited intellectual-property rights. Stronger exclusion raises private appropriability but can restrict diffusion and follow-on innovation; support should therefore target the spillover mechanism rather than assume that every research expenditure has the same social return. [9]

Vaccination combines private protection with external protection when it reduces transmission. The magnitude and reach depend on the pathogen, vaccine, population, and contact network. White's influenza study and Freedman and coauthors' COVID-19 study both find indirect benefits, but the latter finds that measured spillovers reached household contacts and not schoolmates in its setting. That boundary matters: a uniform mandate or subsidy justified by an assumed population-wide externality can be mistargeted when protection is concentrated among close contacts. [10][11]

Infrastructure can shift category as it fills. An empty road or broadband backbone has spare capacity; congestion makes each additional user impose delay or quality costs on others. Tolls and congestion prices can internalize use costs where collection is feasible, while subsidies may be justified for network links whose benefits extend beyond direct users. Project appraisal must count induced demand, land-use change, complementary investment, maintenance, and distribution rather than label all infrastructure a pure public good. [12]

Digital networks often exhibit positive adoption effects: a user's value rises when more compatible users, applications, or complementary products join. Katz and Shapiro show that these feedbacks can produce standardization, expectations-driven adoption, and tipping between incompatible systems. Such networks are not necessarily nonexcludable public goods; platform owners can charge access, restrict interoperability, or control complements. A policy that subsidizes adoption may accelerate useful coordination, but incompatibility and market power can also lock users into an inferior standard. The author's synthesis is that interoperability, portability, and open standards can sometimes target the external adoption effect more directly than permanent demand subsidies. [13]

## Evidence

### The Acid Rain Program Shows Both the Power and the Limits of Trading

The U.S. Acid Rain Program is a large-scale test of quantity regulation with transferable rights. The Environmental Protection Agency reports that the program, together with later power-sector rules and changes in the energy sector, helped deliver annual sulfur dioxide reductions exceeding 95 percent and nitrogen oxide reductions exceeding 89 percent. Wet sulfate deposition fell by more than 70 percent between the 1989-1991 and 2020-2022 periods. The attribution is deliberately qualified because the official source identifies the program alongside subsequent regulation and sector change rather than assigning every reduction to allowance trading alone. [6]

Chan, Chupp, Cropper, and Muller compare 2002 compliance under the program with a uniform performance standard designed to achieve the same aggregate sulfur dioxide emissions. Using plant-level compliance costs for non-New Source Performance Standards coal units, they estimate annual savings of approximately $240 million in 1995 dollars. The method holds aggregate emissions constant and asks whether trading reallocated abatement toward cheaper sources, which directly tests cost-effectiveness rather than comparing the program with uncontrolled emissions. [7]

The same study models health damage from the observed geographic emissions pattern and compares it with a no-trade counterfactual based on each unit's allowance allocation and bank drawdown. It estimates that observed trading produced $2.4 billion more health damage in 2000 dollars than the no-trade pattern because allowances moved from plants west of the Mississippi toward eastern plants with larger exposed populations. The finding does not show that the program's total benefits were negative; it shows that equal tons were not equal in damage and that a national cap optimized aggregate quantity and compliance cost without automatically optimizing location. [7]

This case supports three bounded conclusions. Tradable allowances can reveal heterogeneous abatement costs and reduce the cost of a fixed aggregate target. Continuous emissions measurement and enforceable allowance surrender preserve cap integrity. A uniform trading ratio can nevertheless create spatial distribution and health consequences when damages vary by source. The author's synthesis is that a cap-and-trade design should test both the cost market and the damage geography before treating one allowance as socially identical everywhere. [6][7]

### Transportation Shows Why an Indirect Tax Can Miss the Harm

Knittel and Sandler study personal transportation using variation in vehicle emissions and driving responses to fuel prices. The policy problem is second-best: directly taxing each vehicle's emissions may be difficult, so gasoline is taxed as a correlated input. Their empirical analysis finds that dirtier vehicles respond more strongly to fuel prices in absolute value, making the welfare-maximizing uniform gasoline tax differ from a naive tax based on average emissions per gallon. [8]

Even after optimizing that uniform tax, the authors find that in many sample years more than 75 percent of emissions-related deadweight loss remains. The result does not refute corrective pricing. It demonstrates that a proxy tax loses precision when the external harm and behavioral elasticity vary across agents. Direct emissions measurement, differentiated charges, inspection rules, or combinations of taxes and standards can outperform a uniform input tax if their information and administration costs are not too high. [8]

### Firm-Level Research Data Reveal a Persistent Social-Private Return Gap

Lucking, Bloom, and Van Reenen extend earlier U.S. firm-level analysis through 2015 and update measures of proximity in technology and product markets. Their design separates positive knowledge spillovers, through which nearby firms benefit from another firm's research, from product-market rivalry, through which innovation can take business from competitors. They report that the magnitude of research spillovers remained broadly similar to that observed in the mid-1980s. [9]

Their estimated ratio of marginal social to private returns rose from roughly three to roughly four, with no evidence that the divergence had narrowed. This is evidence for a persistent positive spillover, not proof that every project merits subsidy or that research has no business-stealing effect. The policy-relevant result is the wedge: firms deciding on research from private returns alone can invest less than the level justified by net social returns, while targeting requires evidence about which research fields, firms, and diffusion channels create the spillover. [9]

### Vaccination Evidence Measures Reach Rather Than Assuming It

White uses pre-existing state vaccination rates interacted with annual variation in influenza-vaccine quality as a natural experiment. The estimates indicate that a one-percentage-point increase in the U.S. influenza vaccination rate produces 1,134 fewer deaths and 8.9 million fewer work hours lost to illness annually when scaled nationally. Mortality benefits concentrate among people age 75 and older, while much of the effect is attributable to vaccination of people under 75, which identifies an external protection channel rather than only private benefit to recipients. [10]

White also studies health-care-worker vaccination mandates, a setting in which contact with vulnerable patients makes potential external benefit large. The reported reductions in influenza diagnoses in affected counties are consistent with substantial spillovers from targeted vaccination. The study's natural-experiment structure is important because it links changes in coverage to outcomes rather than valuing externality solely through an epidemiological assumption. Its estimates remain specific to influenza, periods, and populations studied. [10]

Freedman, Sacks, Simon, and Wing use nearly universal microdata from one state and the six-month eligibility gap between 12- and 11-year-olds to separate direct and indirect COVID-19 vaccine effects. In the published American Economic Journal: Applied Economics version, vaccination reduces cases by about 80 percent for recipients. Protection spills to household contacts with an estimated indirect effect about three-fourths as large as the direct effect, but the study does not find indirect effects extending to schoolmates. [11]

Together, the studies reject two shortcuts. It is wrong to count only the recipient's benefit when transmission falls. It is also wrong to presume that every vaccine creates the same population-wide externality. The measured network boundary determines whether a subsidy, targeted mandate, outreach program, or public purchase addresses the relevant spillover. [10][11]

### Collective-Governance Evidence Qualifies the Free-Rider Prediction

Ostrom's Nobel lecture synthesizes laboratory, field-experimental, and observational work on public goods and common-pool resources. In anonymous settings, participants tend to overharvest common resources relative to cooperative outcomes. When face-to-face communication is possible, participants can assess trustworthiness, coordinate rules, and increase cooperation; experiments also find that subjects spend resources to sanction free riders. In field evidence reported in the lecture, monitoring among 178 forest user groups is strongly associated with foresters' assessments of forest density even after controlling for whether monitoring is formally organized. [5]

These findings do not establish that informal governance always succeeds. Ostrom compares sustained regimes with failures and emphasizes multiple interacting variables, including boundaries, rules, monitoring, sanctions, communication, and nested authority. The evidence instead rejects institutional fatalism: neither universal privatization nor universal central command follows from the existence of a collective-action problem. The feasible solution depends on group size, repeat interaction, local knowledge, monitoring cost, and recognition of rule-making authority. [5]

## Implications

### Diagnose the Missing Price Before Choosing the Remedy

The first practical implication is procedural. An analyst should specify the action, affected parties, direction of the spillover, physical or informational transmission channel, geographic and temporal reach, and marginal rather than average effect. The analysis should then ask whether the effect is already priced through contract, property, reputation, insurance, or another institution. Only the residual uncompensated effect is the externality requiring further comparison. [1][2]

The second step is to classify the good at the relevant load. Nonrivalry and nonexcludability support a public-good diagnosis; rivalry with costly exclusion indicates a common-pool problem; excludability with spare capacity indicates a club good; rivalry plus feasible exclusion indicates an ordinary private good. Classification can change over time and scale. A road at midnight and the same road at rush hour are economically different goods, as are open scientific knowledge and a patented implementation. [3][5][12]

The third step is institutional comparison. A Pigouvian price needs a measurable marginal effect and a collectible base. Bargaining needs defined rights, few enough parties, enforceability, and manageable strategic behavior. Direct regulation needs an observable rule and competent enforcement. Cap-and-trade needs a defensible cap, accurate monitoring, a functioning allowance market, and attention to spatial damages. Public provision needs preference aggregation, procurement discipline, and finance. Community governance needs boundaries, repeat interaction, monitoring, sanctions, and legitimate local authority. [2][4][5][6][7][8][12]

The author's synthesis is a least-total-cost rule: choose the arrangement that minimizes the sum of residual external damage, abatement or provision cost, information error, transaction and administration cost, enforcement failure, and unacceptable distributional harm. This rule rejects both market romanticism and regulatory romanticism because it places real markets, governments, firms, courts, and associations on the same comparative basis. [2]

### Make Distribution Explicit Rather Than Hiding It Inside Efficiency

An efficient quantity does not determine an equitable allocation. The initial assignment of property rights changes who pays under bargaining. Free allowance allocation transfers scarcity rents differently from auctions. A corrective tax raises revenue without automatically compensating the people harmed. A subsidy requires financing and may reward actors for activity they would have undertaken anyway. Public provision financed from general taxation can spread costs broadly even when benefits are local. [1][2][7][8][12]

Distribution can also affect feasibility and behavior. Households with limited liquidity may be unable to respond to a tax by purchasing cleaner equipment; workers and regions tied to an emitting industry can bear transition costs; a road toll can improve travel time while burdening travelers without schedule or route alternatives. These concerns do not make the externality disappear. They require separate instruments such as targeted rebates, transition assistance, access guarantees, phased implementation, or investment in substitutes, evaluated without weakening the marginal signal that corrects the spillover. This is the author's synthesis from the separation between efficiency conditions and rights, revenue, and incidence in the source frameworks. [1][2][7][12]

Geography is part of distribution. The Acid Rain evidence shows that an equal-ton trading system can move emissions toward more exposed populations while lowering compliance cost. Vaccination evidence shows that indirect protection can be concentrated within households rather than diffuse across a whole school. Research spillovers can cross firm and national boundaries. A credible assessment should map recipients and harms rather than stop at an aggregate net-benefit total. [7][9][11]

### Apply Prices and Quantities According to the Error Society Can Bear

For pollution, the central design choice is which uncertainty to constrain. A tax provides a known marginal incentive but an uncertain emissions quantity. A cap guarantees an aggregate quantity if monitoring and enforcement work but allows the allowance price and compliance burden to vary. When incremental damage changes slowly with quantity and abatement cost is uncertain and steep, a price can avoid extreme compliance cost. When crossing a quantity threshold creates severe damage, a cap or direct prohibition can bound the risk more reliably. [4]

Hybrid policies can combine strengths: a cap with an allowance price ceiling or floor, a tax with an emissions performance backstop, or trading ratios that reflect location. Complexity is not free. Each feature creates new data, governance, and strategic requirements. The author's synthesis is that a hybrid should be added only when it addresses a named failure mode whose expected cost exceeds the added administrative and gaming cost. [4][7]

Monitoring is the non-negotiable foundation. A tax on unobserved emissions, a cap without verified measurement, or a standard without enforcement changes legal text but not necessarily behavior. The Acid Rain Program's measured allowance surrender made an aggregate cap credible, while Knittel and Sandler's results show what is lost when policy can reach only an imperfect proxy. Investment in measurement can therefore be part of the welfare-improving intervention rather than overhead external to it. [6][8]

### Target Positive Spillovers Without Paying Indiscriminately

Research support should distinguish knowledge spillovers from private appropriation and business stealing. Firm-level evidence of a social-private return gap supports intervention at the portfolio level, but a subsidy tied simply to reported spending can finance inframarginal or low-spillover projects. Competitive grants, prizes, public research, tax credits, procurement, and intellectual-property rules shift different risks between taxpayers and innovators. Evaluation should measure additional research induced, diffusion, follow-on use, and displaced activity rather than total spending alone. [9]

Vaccination policy should value direct and indirect benefits separately. A recipient may accept vaccination based on private protection even without subsidy; the external component provides the efficiency rationale for additional support. White's influenza results support substantial external value, especially across age groups and in health-care settings. Freedman and coauthors show why reach must be measured: household spillovers can be large even when school-level spillovers are not detected. The appropriate geographic and institutional target may therefore be a household, workplace, care facility, or high-contact group rather than an undifferentiated population. [10][11]

Subsidies are not the only tool for positive externalities. Information can correct mistaken private beliefs; convenient delivery can reduce access cost; mandates can address high-risk settings when voluntary uptake leaves material harm; public purchase can spread fixed development cost; and liability or professional standards can change organizational incentives. The author's synthesis is that the least coercive effective option is preferred when expected results are equal, but effectiveness must be demonstrated against the actual transmission mechanism rather than assumed from the policy label. [2][10][11]

### Treat Infrastructure as a Portfolio of Mixed Goods

Infrastructure should not receive a blanket public-good presumption. A local road, national highway, water network, sewer, grid connection, and broadband backbone differ in exclusion, congestion, fixed cost, natural-monopoly conditions, and spillover reach. Glaeser and Poterba's framework requires project-level cost-benefit analysis extended to economy-wide externalities, not a spending target justified by the word "infrastructure." [12]

User prices are useful when they reveal demand and ration scarce capacity. Congestion charges make travelers consider delay imposed on others; connection or access fees can finance excludable networks; metering can reduce waste. Yet charging average cost per use can suppress efficient consumption when marginal cost is low and fixed cost is high. Two-part tariffs, public subsidies, or general revenues may finance the fixed component while marginal-use prices address congestion or operating cost. [12]

Project appraisal should include the counterfactual. A new transit line may reduce road congestion, but only to the extent that it changes driving; a new road can alter routes and land use; broadband can create adoption spillovers while also strengthening a platform's market power. The author's synthesis is that benefits should be traced through behavior and networks, while transfers in land value or market share should not be double counted as new social surplus. [12][13]

### Separate Useful Digital Network Effects From Lock-In

A digital network becomes more valuable when additional compatible users or complements join, but private platforms can capture part of that value through access fees, advertising, or control of complements. Katz and Shapiro's analysis shows why expectations and installed base influence adoption and why incompatible systems can tip toward one standard. The same positive feedback that supports coordination can also protect an incumbent after a technically better alternative appears. [13]

The policy problem is therefore not identical to financing a pure public good. Subsidizing one proprietary network can accelerate scale while entrenching a private gatekeeper. Interoperability, data portability, open interfaces, standard-setting, multi-homing, and support for shared protocols can preserve the coordination benefit while lowering switching costs. These measures can also weaken investment incentives or introduce security and governance costs, so their desirability depends on the architecture. The author's synthesis is to ask whether the missing benefit arises from another user, a complementary product, compatibility, or open knowledge, then target that margin rather than the platform's total size. [13]

### Implications for Firms, Investors, and Decision-Makers

For firms, externalities create regulatory, contractual, and reputational exposures that ordinary accounting can omit. A process with low private cost may face future taxes, permit requirements, liability, or community opposition when social costs become measurable. A research program can create uncaptured value for suppliers, competitors, or standards ecosystems. A platform's network effect can be a moat, but the same incompatibility that protects it can invite interoperability mandates. These are contingent business effects grounded in the mechanisms above, not a claim that every externality will be regulated. [2][7][9][13]

For investors, the useful question is not whether a company is "green" or "social." It is whether cash flows depend on an unpriced harm or uncompensated benefit, whether measurement is improving, which institution is likely to internalize the wedge, and who will bear the incidence. A pollution-intensive asset can lose value through taxes, standards, permit scarcity, litigation, or technology substitution. An innovator can gain through subsidies or procurement but lose exclusivity through diffusion rules. The author's synthesis is that scenario analysis should map each remedy to prices, quantities, capital expenditure, demand, and distribution rather than attach one generic externality discount. [1][2][7][9]

For public decision-makers, evaluation should be falsifiable. Before implementation, state the expected marginal effect, behavioral response, administrative cost, distribution, and monitoring indicator. After implementation, compare outcomes with a credible counterfactual and separate the instrument's effect from concurrent regulation, technology, and macroeconomic change. The EPA's qualified attribution, the plant-level Acid Rain counterfactual, the transportation heterogeneity study, and the age-eligibility vaccine design illustrate increasing levels of evidentiary discipline. [6][7][8][11]

The durable conclusion is narrow but powerful. Market prices coordinate private costs and benefits well only when rights, prices, and contracts encompass the relevant effects. Where they do not, the remedy is not automatically government, bargaining, taxation, or public provision; it is the feasible institution that best incorporates missing social value after accounting for information, transaction, enforcement, and distributional costs. [1][2][3][5]
