# Advertising and Consumer Culture -- Advertising Shapes Desire Beyond Informing Choice

Advertising can inform people about products, prices, and availability, but much of its cultural power comes from doing more: attaching goods to identities, emotions, relationships, and imagined futures. "Manufacturing desire" is therefore best treated as a critical interpretation of how advertising shapes the settings in which preferences develop, not as a claim that audiences have no agency or that every purchase is caused by an advertisement ([5] [6] [17]).

## Background

Advertising long predates industrial capitalism. Notices about goods, services, and events existed in ancient and early modern societies, while printed newspapers expanded the scale of commercial announcements. Raymond Williams argued that the historically important transition was not from no advertising to advertising, but from particular notices to an institutional system of commercial information and persuasion. In his account, this system became integrated with capitalist business organization and with the financing of newspapers, magazines, and broadcasting. That history matters because contemporary advertising remains both a sales practice and part of the economic infrastructure through which culture circulates ([6]).

Industrialization, urban markets, branded packaged goods, and mass media enlarged the problem that advertising could solve. Producers had to make distant buyers recognize and distinguish goods that were no longer purchased only from known local sellers. Advertising could communicate availability and attributes, reduce search costs, create familiarity, differentiate similar products, and support distribution. It could also cultivate favorable associations that exceeded the product's functional properties. These informational and persuasive functions coexist; treating all advertising as either useful information or pure manipulation misses the mixed purposes of actual campaigns. A meta-meta-analysis covering 44 prior meta-analyses, more than 1,700 primary studies, and more than 2.4 million participants found a positive average advertising effect, but effects varied by inputs and outcomes. That variation is incompatible with a universal account in which any exposure mechanically produces desire ([17]).

Edward Bernays was one influential pioneer in the professionalization of public relations, not the sole father of modern advertising or public relations. Ivy Lee and other publicity practitioners also helped develop the field before and alongside him. Bernays's distinctive contribution was to present the public relations counsel as a professional who used research, social psychology, symbols, news, and organized events to shape public response. His 1928 book "Propaganda" defended the organized management of opinion as a feature of mass democracy and described commercial, political, and civic applications. The book is evidence of Bernays's program and self-understanding; it is not independent proof that practitioners possessed the comprehensive control he sometimes implied ([1] [20]).

The phrase "engineering of consent" must be dated precisely. Bernays used it as the title of an article published in March 1947 in volume 250 of The Annals of the American Academy of Political and Social Science. There he defined an engineering approach as action based on thorough knowledge of a situation and the application of scientific principles and tested practices to obtaining support for ideas and programs. A later edited book titled "The Engineering of Consent" appeared in 1955. The 1947 article, rather than the later book, is the primary origin for the formulation discussed here ([2]).

Bernays's 1929 "Torches of Freedom" event for the American Tobacco Company became a canonical story about connecting cigarettes with women's emancipation. The event was deliberately staged around women smoking during New York's Easter parade, and it received press coverage. The stronger story -- that credulous newspapers celebrated it nationwide and that the event itself decisively changed women's smoking behavior -- relies heavily on Bernays's retrospective self-presentation. Vanessa Murphree's examination of contemporary coverage found significant but less celebratory reporting and concluded that the event's reach and persuasive impact were probably smaller than Bernays later suggested. The case therefore documents symbolic staging and publicity, but it does not establish a quantified causal effect on smoking adoption ([3]).

Ernest Dichter extended psychological market research after arriving in the United States in 1938. His early work included a 1939 study for the Compton agency on Ivory soap and a 1939 automobile study involving Chrysler's Plymouth line. He used nondirective depth interviews and interpreted products as carrying meanings beyond price and utility. After work for the J. Stirling Getchell agency and CBS, he founded the Institute for Motivational Research in 1946. Dichter's approach helped make the consumer's motives and symbolic associations an explicit object of research, although its psychoanalytic interpretations were not equivalent to controlled causal evidence ([4]).

Campaign histories require narrower attribution than popular retellings often provide. Archival and scholarly accounts connect Dichter's research to Ivory, Plymouth, Esso, and Mattel. A 1959 study for Mattel examined children's and mothers' responses to Barbie and informed recommendations for presenting the doll. Harvard Business School's archival guide says Dichter's work supplied building blocks for the Esso tiger campaign and Barbie's introduction. ExxonMobil's own history, however, says an advertising copywriter coined "Put a Tiger in Your Tank" in 1959. The evidence supports saying that Dichter contributed consumer research to campaigns and product positioning; it does not justify crediting him alone with every slogan or commercial outcome associated with those brands ([4] [19] [21]).

Postwar critics approached the same development from economics and cultural studies. In "The Affluent Society," published in 1958, John Kenneth Galbraith called the relation between expanding production and induced wants the "dependence effect." His argument was that wants at the margin of affluence are not always independent inputs to production: emulation, advertising, and salesmanship can help create the wants that production then satisfies. This challenged a strong version of consumer sovereignty, but it did not show that all wants are artificial or that innovation never answers latent needs. The dependence effect is an economic and normative argument about preference formation, not a laboratory law applying uniformly to every consumer ([5]).

Williams's "Advertising: The Magic System" supplied a complementary cultural account. He wrote it for "The Long Revolution," which appeared in 1961, but withdrew it from that book; part appeared in New Left Review in July-August 1960, and a full version was collected in 1980. Williams argued that modern advertising had moved beyond announcements and into the teaching of social and personal values. His paradox was that consumer society was not materialist enough: the material object was rarely allowed to be enough on its own, so beer, appliances, and other goods were validated by associations with sociability, masculinity, modernity, or status. Calling this a "magic system" described the organized transfer of social meaning to commodities; it did not allege literal magic or deny that goods have use value ([6]).

Digital advertising changed the scale, speed, and observability of these processes. Search engines, social platforms, ad exchanges, data brokers, and publishers can infer audiences from behavior and select advertisements in milliseconds. The shift did not abolish mass advertising or invent symbolic persuasion, but it made segmentation, experimentation, and individualized delivery routine. It also tied media finance to data processing. This is the contemporary setting in which older questions about manufactured desire -- who defines relevance, which identities are made salient, and whose interests shape the communication environment -- acquire new technical and regulatory forms ([13] [18]).

## Core Concepts

### Manufactured Desire as a Bounded Claim

The strongest defensible version of the manufactured-desire thesis is not that firms implant arbitrary wants into passive minds. It is that advertising can alter attention, salience, beliefs, affect, perceived norms, and the symbolic meanings attached to available choices. Those changes can affect attitudes and behavior, but they interact with prior needs, resources, social relationships, product experience, competing messages, and the recipient's motivation and ability to think. The thesis concerns influence over preference formation, not total authorship of preferences ([7] [17]).

This distinction separates three functions that often occur in the same advertisement. An informational function tells a potential buyer that a product exists, what it costs, where it is sold, or how it performs. A persuasive function selects and frames information to increase a favorable response. A symbolic function links the product to meanings such as competence, care, rebellion, belonging, or prestige. A fourth function, prominent online, is allocative: targeting systems decide which person sees which message and when. These functions are analytically distinct even when one creative execution performs all four ([6] [11] [13]).

"Manufacture" should also be separated from coercion. An advertisement normally changes the choice environment rather than removing choice. It may make one goal vivid, offer a social script, or repeatedly pair a brand with positive imagery. Recipients may accept, reinterpret, ignore, mock, or resist it. Interpretation: advertising power is most plausibly understood as cumulative and probabilistic. The cultural environment can make some desires easier to imagine and express without determining what every individual will want ([7] [17]).

### Engineering Consent and the Creation of Public Contexts

Bernays's engineering metaphor joined research, planning, media relations, symbols, authorities, and staged events. Instead of relying only on a paid claim from a seller, a campaign could create an event that became news, enlist organizations or experts whose statements appeared independent, and place the product or cause inside a broader public issue. In "Propaganda," Bernays openly described the coordination of channels and group affiliations as a way to reach publics through existing social structures ([1]).

The method matters for advertising because source and setting alter how a message is processed. A claim in a conventional display ad is visibly interested speech. The same claim embedded in news, an expert statement, a social cause, entertainment, or an influencer's routine may appear to arise from a less interested context. This does not make every public relations event deceptive. It does mean that analysis should identify the sponsor, intermediary, financial relationship, and degree of editorial independence rather than evaluate only the visible words ([1] [12]).

The "Torches of Freedom" episode illustrates both the method and its evidentiary limit. Cigarettes were connected to an already meaningful political vocabulary of freedom, and the connection was presented as an event rather than merely as product copy. Yet contemporary press evidence does not support the later legend of overwhelming, uncritical media acceptance. The case shows an attempt to engineer context; it does not prove that context can be engineered with predictable results ([3]).

### The Dependence Effect and Consumer Sovereignty

Galbraith's dependence effect attacks a circular welfare argument. If greater production is justified because it satisfies urgent wants, but the production-and-sales system helps create those wants, then observed demand alone cannot establish the independent urgency of the output. Advertising is central because firms spend resources not only to make goods but also to form or redirect demand for them. Galbraith's concern was strongest at the affluent margin, where additional wants may be more susceptible to emulation and salesmanship than needs for food, shelter, or safety ([5]).

The concept should not be converted into a binary classification of "real" and "false" needs. Culture shapes language, taste, fashion, leisure, and technology even without commercial advertising. A desire can be socially learned and still matter to the person who has it. New products can reveal useful possibilities that buyers could not previously name. The relevant questions are narrower: how much of a preference is responsive to marketing, what information and alternatives were available, whether the persuasive process was transparent, and whether satisfying the induced want improves welfare ([5]).

Interpretation: the dependence effect is most useful as a challenge to treating purchases as complete evidence of autonomous preference. It directs attention upstream to the institutions that structure visibility and meaning. It does not by itself measure advertising's causal contribution, distinguish beneficial discovery from exploitative inducement, or establish that a regulator knows a consumer's welfare better than the consumer ([5]).

### Advertising as a Magic System

Williams's magic-system concept explains why functional comparison is only one part of advertising. Modern advertisements often move between a material object and a social scene: a vehicle becomes independence, a beverage becomes friendship, or a device becomes creative competence. The product can contribute to the represented activity, but the advertisement compresses a complex social value into an obtainable commodity. The symbolic promise can therefore exceed what the object alone can deliver ([6]).

This process is cultural rather than merely individual. Advertisements draw on shared codes about class, gender, age, family, beauty, achievement, and authenticity. Their meanings depend on conventions already circulating in film, music, sport, news, and everyday life. Advertising also feeds revised images back into those domains. A campaign does not invent the entire code; it selects, intensifies, and commercially anchors elements of it ([6]).

The framework avoids a common mistake: assuming that an audience must literally believe an explicit promise for an advertisement to work. A viewer can know that a perfume does not cause romance while still learning which moods, bodies, and situations the brand represents. Familiarity, emotional association, and social legibility can operate without assent to a factual proposition. At the same time, symbolic interpretation alone cannot demonstrate sales effects. It explains the content and cultural function of an appeal; behavioral evidence is still needed to establish what the appeal caused ([6] [17]).

### Motivational Research and the Strategy of Desire

Dichter's motivational research sought meanings that respondents might not articulate in a direct survey. Depth interviews invited extended accounts, while projective and interpretive techniques treated hesitation, metaphor, fantasy, and product personification as evidence about motives. Products were analyzed as psychological and social objects, not only bundles of attributes. That orientation anticipated later qualitative consumer research by making consumption experience and identity central objects of inquiry ([4]).

The approach generated useful hypotheses, but its historical prominence should not be confused with strong validation. Psychoanalytic interpretations can be difficult to falsify, small interview samples may not generalize, and a compelling explanation after the fact may not predict response to a campaign. A research report can influence creative strategy without proving that the inferred unconscious motive caused purchasing. The appropriate evidence chain distinguishes interview observation, analyst interpretation, campaign recommendation, actual deployment, and measured outcome ([4]).

The campaign record demonstrates why attribution matters. The 1939 Ivory work and Plymouth study are documented parts of Dichter's early career, and the Institute dates to 1946. The 1959 Mattel report documents involvement with Barbie. Esso's tiger imagery is connected to motivational research in archival summaries, while the slogan's wording is credited by ExxonMobil to an advertising copywriter. A careful history describes those contributions separately rather than turning Dichter into the sole author of a portfolio of famous campaigns ([4] [19] [21]).

### Elaboration Likelihood as a Continuum

Richard Petty and John Cacioppo established the Elaboration Likelihood Model through work in the early 1980s, later synthesizing it in their 1986 book. The model organizes persuasion by the likelihood that a recipient will elaborate on issue-relevant information. Under relatively high elaboration, responses depend more on scrutiny of arguments and on the thoughts those arguments generate. Under relatively low elaboration, recipients may rely more on simple cues, affect, familiarity, source characteristics, or decision rules that require less issue-relevant thought ([7] [8]).

"Central" and "peripheral" are endpoints on an elaboration continuum, not boxes into which products permanently fall. A car can receive cursory processing from a habitual buyer; a snack can receive extensive scrutiny from someone with an allergy. Motivation, personal relevance, accountability, knowledge, distraction, time, repetition, and message comprehensibility can all move processing. The same feature can also perform different roles. Source expertise might be a simple positive cue under low elaboration, evidence relevant to an argument under greater elaboration, or a factor that changes how much thinking occurs ([7] [8]).

The model therefore does not license the claim that most advertising simply "bypasses" reason. Some advertising encourages comparison; some works through affect or cues; and some combines routes. Nor does low elaboration imply unconscious processing. A recipient can consciously notice a celebrity or a popularity cue without carefully evaluating the product case. Attitudes based on careful thought tend, under the model, to be more persistent and predictive than attitudes based on less thought, but this is a conditional tendency rather than an invariant property of every exposure ([7] [8]).

For analysis, ELM suggests examining the recipient and situation, not labeling a creative execution in isolation. Ask what ability and motivation the audience had, which elements were processed as arguments or cues, what thoughts were generated, and whether the outcome was immediate attitude, delayed recall, choice, or behavior. These distinctions prevent the central/peripheral vocabulary from becoming an unsupported synonym for rational/irrational or expensive/cheap products ([7] [8]).

### Identity, the Extended Self, and Self-Congruity

Russell Belk's extended-self framework proposes that possessions can contribute to and reflect identity. It is broader than the claim that buyers select brands matching a fixed self-image: people may invest objects, places, experiences, collections, and other possessions with autobiographical and social meaning. Goods can help maintain continuity, display affiliations, support roles, or embody relationships. Belk presented converging conceptual and empirical evidence, not a claim that every possession becomes part of every owner's self ([9]).

Self-congruity research tests a related but narrower proposition: evaluations and choices are associated with perceived fit between a consumer's self-concept and a brand or user image. A meta-analysis of 46 empirical articles and 262 effect sizes estimated a weighted mean association of r = .31 across attitudes, intentions, and behavior. Moderation by the type of self-motive, brand personality, abstraction, elaboration, and impression formation shows that identity fit is neither uniform nor context-free ([10]).

These findings provide firmer support for identity advertising than slogans such as "people buy who they want to be." Products can participate in identity, and perceived self-brand fit has a moderate association with consumer outcomes. They do not show that functional attributes cease to matter, that identity is produced only by marketers, or that an advertisement can assign any identity to any product. Interpretation: identity advertising is most credible when it joins an existing symbolic resource, audience self-understanding, and plausible consumption practice ([9] [10]).

### Personalization, Influencers, and Relevance

Personalized advertising changes message content or delivery based on information about a recipient. Its persuasive advantage is not automatic. A 2025 meta-analysis synthesized 53 experimental studies, 114 effect sizes, and 11,786 participants. Personalized advertisements were, on average, more persuasive than nonpersonalized controls across overall persuasion, attitudes, and behavioral intentions. Meta-analytic structural equation modeling indicated that perceived relevance, not perceived intrusiveness, mediated the overall advantage. Studies using actual information for personalization had a positive average effect, whereas imagined, scenario-based personalization did not show a significant effect ([11]).

This result supports a relevance mechanism, not a general claim that more data always produces more persuasion. Personalization may fail when the inference is wrong, the message exposes unexpected surveillance, or relevance does not compensate for distrust. The study measured experimental effects on persuasion outcomes; it did not prove that every targeting system improves sales or welfare. Darmody and Zwick add a critical interpretation: marketers may present "hyper-relevance" as consumer empowerment even when it requires persistent surveillance and algorithmic shaping of choice contexts. Their analysis concerns the industry's justificatory logic, not a measured universal state of consumer consciousness ([18]).

Influencer advertising embeds commercial communication in a person's content, reputation, and follower relationship. A 2025 meta-analysis restricted its main database to experiments, combining 71 papers, 135 studies, and 571 effect sizes. It found positive effects on engagement and purchase intention and found influencers relatively more effective than brand posts, virtual influencers, and celebrities in the included comparisons. Credibility and attractiveness mediated responses, while influencer size, message, product, platform, and follower characteristics moderated effects ([12]).

The experimental restriction strengthens causal inference within the studied settings, but important caveats remain. Many studies use hypothetical influencers and participants with no prior relationship to them, purchase intention is not purchase behavior, and sponsorship disclosure, audience composition, and platform design vary. Influencer evidence therefore supports conditional causal effects; it does not justify saying that aspiration always bypasses critical evaluation ([12]).

### RTB and the Data Infrastructure of Advertising

Real-time bidding, or RTB, is one part of programmatic advertising. When a page or app requests an advertisement, a publisher and intermediaries can send a bid request describing the placement and information used to evaluate the audience. Potential buyers respond, an auction is resolved, and an advertisement is delivered, often within milliseconds. The UK Information Commissioner's Office reported that bid-related information could range from device and broad location data to browsing interests and inferred health-related interests ([13]).

The ICO also warned that a single website visit and auction could result in personal data being seen by hundreds of organizations. Its 2019 investigation identified concerns about lawful basis, special-category data, transparency, data-supply chains, security, retention, and inadequate data-protection impact assessments. "Could" and "hundreds" are important qualifiers: the report describes the potential distribution in an ecosystem, not a finding that every impression sends every field to the same number of recipients ([13]).

RTB separates persuasion from infrastructure. The visible creative may be ordinary, while the consequential operation occurs in profiling, audience selection, auctioning, measurement, and feedback. Interpretation: contemporary manufactured desire includes not only what a message means but also how systems allocate attention and learn from response. That broader frame should not collapse all programmatic advertising into RTB or assume that profiling can infer a person's inner life with certainty ([13]).

## Evidence

### Historical Cases: Evidence of Strategy, Not Automatic Effect

Bernays's writings provide primary evidence that an influential practitioner advocated planned management of public opinion, group-based communication, and symbolic action. His 1947 article documents the "engineering of consent" formulation and its procedural ambitions. These sources establish doctrine and professional practice, but their claims about effectiveness are interested claims by the practitioner himself. They should be read alongside independent historical evidence rather than as outcome evaluations ([1] [2]).

Murphree's study of the "Torches of Freedom" march demonstrates the value of that distinction. She searched historical newspapers and compared contemporary reporting with Bernays's later narrative. The event was staged and did receive meaningful coverage, but some coverage recognized its publicity character, and the reception was less uniformly celebratory than the standard story implies. The study does not show that the campaign had no influence; it shows that attributing a broad change in women's smoking to this event exceeds the available evidence ([3]).

The Dichter archive similarly supports claims about research activity more strongly than claims about market impact. The chronology of the Ivory and Plymouth studies, the 1946 Institute, and the 1959 Mattel report is documented. These materials show advertisers using depth interviews and symbolic interpretations in strategy. Unless a campaign also has controlled outcome data or a credible comparison, sales success cannot be assigned to a particular psychological insight alone. Product changes, price, distribution, competing campaigns, and economic conditions are alternative explanations ([4] [19]).

### ELM Experiments and Accumulated Tests

Petty, Cacioppo, and Goldman tested personal relevance, argument quality, and source expertise in a 1981 experiment. One hundred forty-five undergraduates heard or read advocacy for comprehensive senior examinations. High relevance meant the policy would begin the following year and could affect participants; low relevance meant implementation in ten years. Messages contained strong or weak arguments and came from a source presented as high or low in expertise. Another 18 students served as unexposed controls ([7]).

The key result was an interaction. Under high relevance, attitude change depended primarily on argument quality. Under low relevance, source expertise had greater influence. This supports the proposition that personal involvement can change which message features carry persuasive weight. It does not prove two sealed mental systems, and its student sample and artificial issue constrain generalization. The manipulation also concerned an institutional policy, not a commercial product, although later advertising studies extended the framework ([7]).

Carpenter's 2015 meta-analysis examined the ELM prediction that strong arguments should exceed weak arguments more under central than peripheral processing. The series of meta-analyses included k = 134 effects and was generally consistent with the predicted interaction. Effects were smaller in pre-post designs than in post-test-only designs, and induction-strength assessments did not generally track effect size. The result supports the argument-quality by processing prediction while also showing that design features matter; it is not evidence that every named peripheral cue works in every context ([8]).

### Identity and Self-Congruity Evidence

Belk's 1988 article assembled evidence across possessions, places, experiences, gifts, loss, care, and disposition to argue that possessions can contribute to identity. Its contribution is theoretical synthesis supported by multiple research streams, not a single randomized estimate of an advertisement's effect. It establishes a mechanism through which consumption may carry identity meaning and identifies consequences for consumer behavior ([9]).

Aguirre-Rodriguez, Bosnjak, and Sirgy supplied quantitative evidence for the related self-congruity proposition. Their meta-analysis included 46 empirical articles and 262 effect sizes in which congruity predicted pre- or post-purchase outcomes. The weighted mean was r = .31, approximately 10 percent of variance in the included outcomes. Because the evidence combined correlations and varied designs, it supports a robust association rather than proving that advertising-induced congruity causes every downstream behavior. The moderators show why product, self-motive, and processing conditions cannot be ignored ([10]).

Together, these sources support a limited version of identity advertising: possessions can be identity-relevant, and perceived fit between self and brand is reliably related to consumer response. The evidence does not support the universal claim that consumers buy identities instead of products. Functional performance, price, access, habit, and identity can operate jointly ([9] [10]).

### Experimental Evidence on Personalized Advertising

The 2025 personalized-advertising meta-analysis is especially relevant because it admitted experimental comparisons of personalized and nonpersonalized messages. Across 53 studies and 11,786 participants, the authors extracted 114 effect sizes and used a three-level model to address dependent estimates. The synthesis found an overall persuasive advantage for personalization and positive effects for attitudes and behavioral intentions ([11]).

Its mediation analysis is central to the manufactured-desire question. Perceived relevance explained the personalization advantage, whereas perceived intrusiveness did not mediate the aggregate effect. Actual use of participant information produced a significant positive average effect of d = 0.28, while imagined personalization produced a nonsignificant estimate. This suggests that personalization works partly because recipients connect a message to interests or preferences that feel applicable, not simply because a system signals that it knows them ([11]).

The limits are as informative as the result. Fifty-three studies are not fifty-three deployed ad systems; outcomes and manipulations varied, behavioral intentions are not purchases, and an aggregate mediation result does not mean intrusiveness never matters. The evidence supports a modest, conditional persuasive effect and a relevance pathway. It does not establish that collecting additional personal data always increases effectiveness or that personalization creates the underlying preference rather than matching it ([11]).

### Experimental Evidence on Influencers

Barari, Eisend, and Jain's influencer meta-analysis focused on experimental studies that compared social media influencers with other endorsement forms. Its 71 papers yielded 135 experiments and 571 effect sizes. Influencers positively affected engagement and purchase intention in the combined evidence and outperformed brand posts, virtual influencers, and celebrities in the relevant comparisons. A meta-analytic structural equation model linked effects to perceived credibility and attractiveness ([12]).

The moderation results complicate any simple rule that a larger following produces greater influence. Smaller and medium-sized influencers were more effective for engagement in the authors' analysis, while larger influencers had more impact on purchase intention. Message, product, platform, follower, and influencer characteristics also changed effects. These findings make influencer marketing a testable form of source and identity persuasion rather than evidence of an irresistible parasocial mechanism ([12]).

Causal language should stay within the experimental contrast. Experiments support effects of the stimuli and conditions studied, but many simplify an evolving platform environment and use hypothetical endorsers. Commercial campaigns also involve selection: firms choose influencers and audiences rather than randomizing every relationship. Field sales data, disclosure effects, long-term trust, and repeated sponsorship remain distinct outcomes ([12]).

### Young People and Advertising Literacy

Packer and colleagues searched ten databases for studies published from 2010 onward involving participants aged 6 to 17, advertising exposure, objectively measured understanding or attitudes, and a comparison condition. Thirty-nine studies from 38 articles met the review criteria; nine studies with attitudinal outcomes entered meta-analysis. Advertising for unhealthy products produced more favorable brand or product attitudes than neutral or no-ad controls, with an overall standardized mean difference of 0.36 in the format analysis. Significant effects appeared in both digital and nondigital formats ([16]).

Greater advertising understanding did not protect attitudes in the available evidence, and critical reasoning continued developing through adolescence. The review could not meta-analyze the effect on understanding or determine a precise age threshold, so it does not prove equal vulnerability at every age. It does show that recognition of selling intent is not a complete defense against affective or embedded advertising and supports including adolescents, not only young children, in policy analysis ([16]).

### Infrastructure Scale, Data Flows, and Business Dependence

The ICO's RTB investigation supplies regulatory evidence about the data system behind targeted advertising. It mapped participants and bid requests, found that auctions happen in milliseconds, and reported that a single visit could expose personal data to hundreds of organizations. The ICO concluded that the scale and opacity created serious risks involving consent, special-category data, proportionality, transparency, security, and accountability. These are findings about processing practices and legal compliance, not proof that the resulting ads changed a particular purchase ([13]).

Alphabet's fiscal-year 2025 Form 10-K provides a separate measure of economic scale. The filing reports Google advertising revenue of $294.691 billion and Alphabet consolidated revenue of $402.836 billion, both in millions in the source table. Dividing the former by the latter yields 73.154 percent. The ratio shows that advertising remained the dominant reported revenue source for Alphabet in 2025; it does not show what portion came from behavioral targeting, RTB, or any single persuasion mechanism ([14]).

The broader effectiveness literature also counsels against equating revenue with individual susceptibility. The advertising meta-meta-analysis estimated an average effect around .20 across prior syntheses, with different inputs affecting cognition, emotion, attitudes, memory, and behavior differently. Advertising is effective on average, but response is heterogeneous and causal attribution is difficult outside experiments. The evidence supports influence, not omnipotence ([17]).

## Implications

### For Consumers and Citizens

Consumers should treat preference as formed within an environment, not discovered in isolation. Family, peers, culture, experience, price, design, and advertising can all make goals and options salient. Recognizing that fact need not produce cynicism about every desire. It supports a practical question: what part of an appeal concerns the product's likely use, and what part asks the buyer to purchase status, reassurance, belonging, rebellion, or self-repair? Williams's framework is useful because it directs attention to the social promise attached to the object ([6]).

ELM suggests that defense cannot consist only of checking factual claims. A person may accurately recognize an advertisement while still responding to familiarity, source credibility, humor, aesthetics, or social proof. Useful friction includes delaying high-stakes purchases, comparing alternatives in a different setting, identifying who paid for the message, and restating the offer without its imagery or endorser. These practices increase the opportunity for elaboration; they do not guarantee a "correct" preference ([7] [8]).

Personalization adds an inference problem. A relevant message may feel useful because it fits an existing interest, yet the recipient often cannot see which data, categories, exclusions, or auction participants produced it. Consumers can inspect ad explanations where platforms provide them, restrict permissions, clear or separate profiles, and use contextual or subscription-supported services when available. These steps reduce some data flows but cannot create complete visibility into an ecosystem the ICO described as complex and opaque ([11] [13]).

For citizens, sponsorship literacy matters beyond commerce. Bernays's approach shows how interested communication can enter news and public culture through events, experts, organizations, and causes. Influencer marketing adds commercial messages that may resemble ordinary social content. The appropriate response is not to assume that all mediated speech is covert advertising, but to ask who initiated, paid for, distributed, and benefited from the communication. That inquiry preserves the distinction between evidence about a claim and cues supplied by its setting ([1] [12]).

### For Media Literacy and Education

Interpretation: media education should cover four layers: claim, symbol, source, and system. Claim analysis tests accuracy and omitted comparison. Symbol analysis asks which identity, emotion, or social relation has been attached to the product. Source analysis identifies the sponsor and intermediaries. System analysis asks why this recipient saw this message, what data may have guided selection, and how response is measured. This framework joins the informational, cultural, and infrastructural dimensions of advertising ([6] [13]).

Teaching only that "ads try to sell" is insufficient, especially for young people. The 2022 systematic review found that greater understanding did not reliably protect brand or product attitudes and that both digital and nondigital advertising affected attitudes. Embedded formats are difficult because commercial content can arrive through games, entertainment, creators, or peer-like communication rather than a separated commercial break. Education should use actual examples and practice identifying persuasion under realistic conditions, while avoiding the unsupported promise that literacy alone neutralizes effects ([16]).

ELM also cautions educators against presenting central processing as morally good and peripheral processing as a defect. People cannot scrutinize every low-stakes message, and expertise can be a rational shortcut when the source is genuinely accountable. The educational goal is calibrated attention: recognize when a decision has material consequences, when a cue is substituting for evidence, and when additional information would change the choice. It is also important to teach that central and peripheral processing can coexist ([7] [8]).

### For Advertisers, Influencers, and Publishers

For practitioners, the evidence favors relevance and credibility over the fantasy of hidden control. Personalized advertising had a positive aggregate effect mediated by perceived relevance, but imagined personalization did not. Influencer effects depended on credibility, attractiveness, audience, platform, message, product, and influencer size. These results imply that context and fit matter more than applying a universal psychological trick ([11] [12]).

Ethical practice requires more than effectiveness. A campaign can produce attention while exploiting a sensitive inference, disguising sponsorship, or making a product carry a social promise it cannot reasonably support. Advertisers should specify the intended mechanism, collect only data necessary for that purpose, test for heterogeneous and adverse effects, label commercial relationships, and distinguish purchase intention from verified behavior. Researchers should preregister experiments where feasible and report null and negative outcomes so that campaign folklore does not substitute for evidence ([11] [12] [13]).

Publishers face a structural conflict because advertising finances content while data-intensive delivery may expose audiences to risks. The ICO's report shows why contractual promises among intermediaries are not enough if participants cannot map who receives bid-request data, why it is retained, or what lawful basis applies. Publishers can reduce exposure by limiting intermediaries, fields, retention, and sensitive categories; choosing contextual delivery where appropriate; and auditing the actual supply chain rather than relying only on interface consent language ([13]).

Influencers occupy both source and medium roles. Disclosure can make the commercial relationship legible, but legibility alone does not erase credibility or identity effects. Practitioners should avoid claims that exceed evidence, make sponsorship conspicuous at the point of exposure, and account for young audiences who may recognize a creator yet incompletely process the commercial arrangement. The experimental literature supports influence under some conditions; it does not create an ethical entitlement to exploit every condition that increases response ([12] [16]).

### For Platforms and Competition

Alphabet's 2025 figures demonstrate why advertising design is not a peripheral feature of the platform economy. Google advertising generated $294.691 billion, or 73.154 percent of Alphabet's $402.836 billion in consolidated revenue. This concentration of revenue does not prove that a particular product decision sacrifices users to advertisers, but it identifies a durable incentive to improve ad inventory, relevance, measurement, and conversion ([14]).

Interpretation: platform analysis should examine both message-level persuasion and market architecture. Control over identity, audience data, auctions, attribution, and measurement can shape which sellers can reach which publics and what publishers earn. A platform may simultaneously provide useful discovery, fund services, and create privacy or competition risks. Evaluating only whether an individual advertisement is truthful misses these allocation and governance effects.

The RTB evidence also cautions against equating personalization with a private dialogue between one publisher and one advertiser. An impression can involve a chain of exchanges and service providers. Accountability therefore requires data-flow maps, role definitions, access controls, deletion rules, and tests of whether downstream use matches the original purpose. These are organizational obligations, not tasks an individual can solve by becoming more media literate ([13]).

### For Regulators

The European Union's Digital Services Act provides precise examples of advertising rules. Article 26(1) requires online platforms to make each advertisement identifiable and disclose on whose behalf it is presented, who paid when different, and meaningful information about the main targeting parameters. Article 26(3) prohibits online platforms from presenting advertisements based on profiling that uses the special categories of personal data identified in GDPR Article 9(1). This is a prohibition tied to profiling with specified sensitive categories, not a ban on all personalized advertising ([15]).

Article 28(2) separately prohibits an online platform from presenting profiling-based advertisements using the recipient's personal data when the platform is aware with reasonable certainty that the recipient is a minor. The provision does not require platforms to collect additional personal data solely to determine age. The DSA entered into general application on February 17, 2024, although designated very large platforms and search engines had earlier obligations. Precision matters: saying merely that the DSA "bans ads to minors" overstates both the covered actors and the prohibited practice ([15]).

These rules address transparency, sensitive-data profiling, and minors, but they do not resolve every question raised by manufactured desire. An ad can be transparent and lawful while still linking a product to status or insecurity. Conversely, symbolic persuasion is not necessarily deceptive or harmful. Regulation needs differentiated tools: truth-in-advertising enforcement for claims, sponsorship disclosure for source opacity, data protection for processing, child protections for developmental vulnerability, competition rules for market power, and research access for systemic evaluation ([15]).

Interpretation: outcome measurement should also be proportionate to the claim. A regulator assessing privacy risk needs data-flow and lawful-basis evidence; one assessing deceptive claims needs substantiation; one assessing effects on young people needs developmental and behavioral evidence. Company revenue, cultural interpretation, an attitude experiment, and a field sales experiment answer different questions. Treating them as interchangeable produces either exaggerated alarm or false reassurance.

### For Research and Cultural Analysis

Researchers should maintain an evidence hierarchy without dismissing forms of evidence that answer different questions. Primary campaign files establish what practitioners did and believed. Historical press analysis tests later legends. Qualitative interviews reveal meanings and generate hypotheses. Experiments estimate causal effects under specified conditions. Meta-analyses summarize patterns and heterogeneity. Filings and regulatory investigations document economic scale and data infrastructure. Cultural criticism explains how advertisements organize symbols and social values ([3] [6] [11] [14]).

The strongest conclusion supported across these sources is conditional. Advertising can inform, persuade, and shape symbolic meaning; identity fit, personalization, source credibility, and elaboration conditions are associated with or cause measurable responses in bounded settings. Effects vary, and evidence does not support a model of passive consumers whose wants are wholly created by advertisers. Interpretation: "manufactured desire" remains useful when it names institutional influence over the formation, expression, and commercial allocation of wants, provided it is not used as a substitute for mechanism-specific evidence.

That interpretation preserves the topic's cultural significance. Advertising is a public archive of the selves, relationships, fears, and futures that institutions believe can be made commercially salient. Studying it as pop culture reveals not only what products were sold but which meanings were repeatedly attached to consumption. The critical task is to connect those meanings to evidence about processing, behavior, infrastructure, and law rather than treating a vivid campaign anecdote as proof of total social control.
