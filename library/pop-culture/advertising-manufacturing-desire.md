# Advertising and Consumer Culture -- How Ads Manufacture Desire Instead of Informing Choice

Advertising is not merely a commercial information service that connects products to willing buyers. It is a system for manufacturing desire -- a deliberate psychological apparatus that creates dissatisfaction, associates products with identity and aspiration, and transforms citizens into consumers. From Edward Bernays's application of Freudian psychoanalysis to commercial persuasion in the 1920s, through Ernest Dichter's motivational research in the postwar boom, to the algorithmic micro-targeting of the surveillance capitalism era, advertising has evolved from selling functional goods to selling aspirational selves. Understanding how advertising works as a cultural and psychological force is essential for understanding modern consumer culture and the commercial persuasion engine that shapes identity, norms, and values.

## Background

The history of advertising as a system for manufacturing desire begins not with the first newspaper advertisement but with the convergence of industrial mass production, urbanization, and mass media in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. When factories could produce more goods than populations needed, the central economic problem shifted from supply to demand. Producers needed to create consumers, not merely serve them. This structural shift -- from scarcity to surplus -- is the material condition that made modern advertising necessary.

The intellectual breakthrough came from Edward Bernays, Sigmund Freud's nephew, who is widely credited as the father of public relations and modern advertising. Bernays served on the U.S. Committee on Public Information (the Creel Committee) during World War I, where he witnessed the power of systematic propaganda to mobilize public opinion. After the war, he asked whether the same techniques could be applied to peacetime commercial purposes. In his 1928 book "Propaganda," Bernays argued that the "conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society" and that those who manipulate this "unseen mechanism" constitute "an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country" (Bernays, 1928). Bernays developed what he called "the engineering of consent" -- the application of scientific principles and psychological insights to the task of getting people to support ideas, programs, and products (Bernays, 1947).

Because the word "propaganda" had acquired pejorative connotations after its wartime associations, Bernays promoted the term "public relations" as a more palatable label for the same set of techniques. Drawing on his uncle's theories of the unconscious, Bernays argued that effective persuasion must appeal not to the rational mind but to hidden desires, fears, and aspirations. His approach shifted advertising from informing consumers about product attributes to associating products with deeper emotional needs -- status, belonging, sexual attractiveness, freedom, power (The Conversation, 2015).

The post-World War II era deepened this trajectory. The unprecedented prosperity of the 1950s created an affluent society in which basic needs were largely met, raising a new question: how do you sell goods to people who already have enough? The economist John Kenneth Galbraith provided the most influential answer in "The Affluent Society" (1958). Galbraith coined the "dependence effect" -- the proposition that "as a society becomes increasingly affluent, wants are increasingly created by the process by which they are satisfied." In other words, production creates the wants it then satisfies. Advertising, in Galbraith's analysis, does not respond to pre-existing demand; it synthesizes demand that would not otherwise exist (Galbraith, 1958; Dutt, 2008).

The same era saw the rise of motivational research, pioneered by Ernest Dichter, an Austrian-born psychologist who fled Nazi-occupied Vienna in 1938 and established the Institute for Motivational Research in the United States. Dichter applied Freudian psychoanalytic techniques -- depth interviews, projective tests, focus groups -- to uncover the unconscious motivations behind consumer behavior. His 1960 book "The Strategy of Desire" argued that "many of our daily decisions are governed by motivations over which we have no control and of which we are often quite unaware" (Dichter, 1960). Dichter promised the "mobilisation and manipulation of human needs as they exist in the consumer" and shaped campaigns for Ivory soap, Exxon, Chrysler, and the introduction of the Barbie doll (Wikipedia, "Ernest Dichter").

Dichter's work drew sharp criticism. Vance Packard's "The Hidden Persuaders" (1957) popularized the concern that advertisers were using depth psychology to manipulate consumers without their awareness. While Packard's warnings about subliminal advertising were later shown to be exaggerated -- the famous James Vicary "Eat Popcorn / Drink Coca-Cola" experiment was almost certainly a hoax -- his broader critique of motivational research as manipulative resonated widely (datafield.dev, 2025).

The cultural critique of advertising extended beyond psychology to ideology. Raymond Williams, in his 1960 essay "Advertising: The Magic System," argued that advertising performs an anthropological function: it invests consumer goods with symbolic meaning, associating their purchase with the satisfaction of fundamental human needs -- belonging, status, love, freedom -- that the goods themselves cannot deliver. Williams located advertising within a tradition of magical thinking rather than rational information exchange, which explains its persistence despite widespread consumer awareness that advertising makes exaggerated claims (Williams, 1960). The Frankfurt School philosophers Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno had earlier developed the concept of the "culture industry" in "Dialectic of Enlightenment" (1944), arguing that the system of mass media, entertainment, and advertising provides manufactured desires and their commercial satisfaction as substitutes for political consciousness (datafield.dev, 2025).

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries transformed advertising again through digital technology. The rise of Google in 1998 and Facebook in 2004 created platforms that held vast quantities of user data and could deliver highly targeted advertisements. Shoshana Zuboff's "The Age of Surveillance Capitalism" (2019) analyzed this transformation as a new economic order that "unilaterally claims human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioral data." The surplus behavioral data, fed into machine intelligence systems, is fabricated into "prediction products" that anticipate what consumers will do now, soon, and later. These products are traded in "behavioral futures markets" where advertisers place bets on future consumer behavior (Zuboff, 2019). Advertising in this era is no longer broadcast to mass audiences but algorithmically targeted to individuals based on their predicted psychological profiles, behavioral patterns, and vulnerability to specific persuasive appeals.

## Core Concepts

### The Engineering of Consent

Edward Bernays's "engineering of consent" is the foundational concept of modern advertising as manufactured desire. Bernays defined it as "the use of an engineering approach -- that is, action based only on thorough knowledge of the situation and on the application of scientific principles and tried practices to the task of getting people to support ideas and programs" (Bernays, 1947). The concept rests on a Freudian premise: humans are fundamentally irrational beings motivated by unconscious desires, and persuasion must target these hidden drives rather than appealing to reason. Bernays applied this framework to commercial products by linking them to emotional needs. He convinced women to smoke by rebranding cigarettes as "torches of freedom," associating a product with the women's suffrage movement. He promoted Dixie cups by founding a committee to scare the public about the unsanitary nature of reusable glasses. In each case, the strategy was not to describe the product but to create a psychological context in which the product became the solution to an induced anxiety or the symbol of an aspirational identity (The Conversation, 2015).

### The Dependence Effect

Galbraith's dependence effect is the economic theory that explains why advertising is structurally necessary in an affluent society. The argument is counterintuitive: as societies grow wealthier and basic needs are satisfied, the wants that remain to be fulfilled are increasingly created by the very process of production and marketing that satisfies them. "Wants can be synthesized by advertising, catalyzed by salesmanship, and shaped by the discreet manipulations of the persuasion industry," Galbraith wrote (Galbraith, 1958). The dependence effect inverts the classical economic assumption that production responds to consumer demand. In an affluent society, production creates the demand it then meets. Advertising is the mechanism of this creation. The implication is that consumer sovereignty -- the idea that the market faithfully serves pre-existing preferences -- is an illusion in conditions of abundance. Consumers believe they are exercising free choice, but their preferences have been manufactured upstream by the advertising apparatus (Dutt, 2008).

This concept has been debated by economists. Austrian-school critics argue that entrepreneurs discover latent wants rather than create them, and that Galbraith underestimates the genuinely innovative role of producers like Steve Jobs, who created products for needs consumers could not have articulated. The debate turns on whether advertising creates desires that would not otherwise exist or merely activates and channels desires that are already present. The evidence suggests both processes operate, but the dependence effect captures something real about affluent economies: the marginal goods sold by advertising are not responding to urgent, independently felt needs (Independent.org, 2011).

### The Magic System

Raymond Williams's concept of advertising as a "magic system" provides the cultural-studies framework for understanding how advertising works as meaning-making rather than information transfer. Williams argued that advertising does not merely sell products; it sells "a way of seeing the world, a philosophy of fulfillment through acquisition, a promise that the gap between who you are and who you want to be can be closed by what you buy" (Williams, 1960). The "magic" is the symbolic investment of material goods with meanings they do not inherently possess. A car is not transportation; it is freedom. A perfume is not a scent; it is sexual desirability. A brand of jeans is not denim; it is an identity marker signaling membership in a social group. The magic system explains why consumers continue to respond to advertising even when they know rationally that the claims are exaggerated: the appeal operates at the level of symbolic meaning, not factual persuasion. Advertising constructs a social world in which specific products function as identity markers, and consumers participate in this world by purchasing the markers that signal who they are or wish to be (datafield.dev, 2025).

### Motivational Research and the Strategy of Desire

Ernest Dichter's motivational research operationalized the Freudian insights that Bernays had introduced theoretically. Dichter's method was the depth interview -- an open-ended, psychoanalytically informed conversation designed to uncover the latent, unconscious motivations behind consumer choices. His key insight was that products carried "psychic content" -- symbolic meanings that connected to deep psychological needs. A cigarette lighter, Dichter found, was not merely a tool for ignition but a symbol of "the desire for mastery and power" (Dichter, 1964). Bathing had an erotic element -- "one of the few occasions when the Puritanical American was allowed to caress himself or herself" -- which led to the Ivory soap campaign "Be Smart and Get a Fresh Start" (Wikipedia, "Ernest Dichter"). Dichter's "strategy of desire" was essentially Freud's pleasure principle applied to consumer goods: by freeing the id from the constraints of Puritanical restraint, advertising could give consumers "moral permission" to enjoy products. Dichter believed he was building a "stable society" by creating a common identity through shared consumption. His critics saw something darker: the systematic exploitation of unconscious vulnerabilities for commercial gain (Psychology Today, 2022).

### The Elaboration Likelihood Model

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), developed by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo in 1986, provides the cognitive-psychology framework for understanding how advertising messages are processed. ELM proposes two routes to persuasion. The central route operates when consumers are highly motivated and able to carefully process a message: they evaluate the strength of arguments, the quality of evidence, and the logic of claims. Attitudes formed through the central route are durable, resistant to change, and predictive of behavior. The peripheral route operates when consumers lack motivation or cognitive capacity: they rely on surface cues -- source attractiveness, celebrity endorsement, pleasant music, humor, message length, social consensus. Attitudes formed through the peripheral route are weaker, more volatile, and less predictive of behavior (Petty and Cacioppo, 1986).

For advertising, the ELM explains the strategic distinction between high-involvement and low-involvement products. High-involvement products (cars, insurance, enterprise software) reward central-route design: detailed comparisons, specification tables, third-party test data. Low-involvement products (snack foods, toothpaste, streaming subscriptions) reward peripheral-route design: emotional music, attractive spokespeople, repetition-based familiarity. The two routes are not a binary but endpoints of a continuum. A consumer scrolling through social media may elaborate on a headline while ignoring the rest of an ad, or notice a brand logo while ignoring the headline entirely. The critical insight for understanding manufactured desire is that most advertising is designed for the peripheral route -- it bypasses critical evaluation and associates products with positive affect through cues rather than arguments (Simply Psychology, "ELM"; yukaichou.com, 2026).

### Identity Advertising and the Aspirational Self

The dominant mode of modern advertising, traceable from Bernays through David Ogilvy's formulation that "people don't buy products, they buy who they want to be," is identity advertising. Identity advertising does not sell a product's functional attributes; it sells an aspirational identity state. It creates social worlds in which specific products function as identity markers -- signals of who you are and where you belong. The consumer is not purchasing a better product but "an aspirational position in a social hierarchy that advertising had constructed" (datafield.dev, 2025). This mechanism operates through a psychological inversion: instead of desire being an internal impulse toward meaning or accomplishment, desire becomes an externalized demand imposed by the market. The individual no longer asks "What do I truly want?" but "What should I want in order to be socially legible, respectable, enviable, or happy?" Advertising continuously destabilizes self-perception, provoking micro-doses of anxiety: Am I successful enough? Do I look sophisticated enough? Does my phone betray economic stagnation? The objective is not to create satisfaction but to stimulate a chronic sense of insufficiency, ensuring that the emotional economy remains perpetually imbalanced and receptive to new stimuli (faelbropinionandculture.blogspot.com, 2025).

Influencer marketing is the purest modern expression of identity advertising. An influencer does not argue that a product is good; they model a lifestyle in which the product appears naturally. The message is not "buy this" but "this is what life looks like for people like the person you want to be." The desire is manufactured not through argument but through aspiration, and aspiration bypasses critical evaluation entirely (moresapien.org, 2025).

### Surveillance Capitalism and Algorithmic Targeting

Shoshana Zuboff's analysis of surveillance capitalism describes the most recent evolution of manufactured desire. In Zuboff's framework, surveillance capitalism "unilaterally claims human experience as free raw material for translation into behavioral data." The surplus behavioral data is fed into machine intelligence systems that fabricate "prediction products" designed to forecast what consumers will feel, think, and do. These prediction products are traded in "behavioral futures markets" where advertisers buy probabilistic information about future consumer behavior (Zuboff, 2019).

The shift from broadcast advertising to algorithmic targeting is qualitative, not merely quantitative. Mass broadcast advertising addressed undifferentiated audiences with generalized appeals. Algorithmic advertising addresses individuals with messages tailored to their predicted psychological states, behavioral patterns, and momentary vulnerabilities. The data harvesters build profiles that include age, health, interests, sex, political leanings, income, and browsing behavior for billions of users, then sell the ability to match these profiles against the audiences advertisers wish to reach (Magellan Investment Partners, 2022). Programmatic advertising -- automated, real-time bidding on ad placements -- places ads in milliseconds with no human intervention. The decisions that were once made by human marketers are now made by machine learning models trained on behavioral surplus (ResearchGate, 2025).

Darmody and Zwick (2020) identify a paradox at the heart of this system: digital marketing brings about "unprecedented levels of consumer empowerment and autonomy and total control over and manipulation of consumer decision-making." This contradiction is resolved through the concept of "relevance" -- the claim that algorithmic targeting serves consumers by showing them only what they want to see. In this framing, manipulation and empowerment become indistinguishable: the algorithmically curated world is presented as a personalized service rather than a form of control (Darmody and Zwick, 2020).

## Evidence

### The "Torches of Freedom" Campaign (1929)

The most documented case of advertising manufacturing desire through identity association is Bernays's 1929 campaign for the American Tobacco Company. In the 1920s, smoking was considered socially unacceptable for women -- it was associated with loose morals and was banned in many public spaces. George Washington Hill, president of American Tobacco, hired Bernays to break this taboo and expand the cigarette market to women. Bernays did not attempt to argue that cigarettes were good for women. Instead, he consulted psychoanalyst A.A. Brill, who told him that "some women regard cigarettes as symbols of freedom" -- that smoking represented a sublimation of oral frustration and a claim to equality with men. Bernays staged an event at the 1929 Easter Parade in New York City in which fashionable young women lit cigarettes in public, which Bernays had instructed reporters to call "torches of freedom." The campaign framed cigarette smoking as an act of feminist liberation, associating a commercial product with the women's rights movement. The campaign is documented as helping to shift social attitudes toward women's smoking from taboo to acceptability (The Conversation, 2015; Wikipedia, "The Engineering of Consent"). The case demonstrates the core mechanism: advertising did not inform women about cigarettes; it manufactured a desire by associating the product with an aspirational identity (independent, liberated woman) that the product itself could not deliver.

### Dichter's Motivational Research Campaigns

Ernest Dichter's work provides multiple documented cases of advertising rooted in unconscious motivation rather than functional attributes. For Ivory soap, Dichter's depth interviews revealed that bathing carried an erotic and psychological-cleansing dimension -- it was "one of the few occasions when the Puritanical American was allowed to caress himself or herself." This insight produced the slogan "Be Smart and Get a Fresh Start with Ivory Soap," which reframed soap from a hygiene product to a psychological renewal ritual. For Exxon, Dichter discovered that consumers associated motor vehicles with power, leading to the campaign "Put a tiger in your tank" -- a slogan that connected gasoline with the symbolic power of a wild animal rather than its functional properties as fuel. For Chrysler, Dichter was hired to help sell Plymouth cars by uncovering the emotional meanings consumers attached to automobile ownership (Wikipedia, "Ernest Dichter"; ebrary.net, 2025). Time magazine profiled Dichter as "the first to apply to advertising the really scientific psychology." These cases demonstrate that the strategy of desire was not theoretical but operationalized across major American brands, with measurable commercial impact. Dichter's Institute for Motivational Research conducted thousands of projects for hundreds of companies over four decades, making motivational research a standard practice in the advertising industry.

### The Elaboration Likelihood Model Experiments

The ELM is supported by a substantial body of experimental evidence. In a foundational study, Petty, Cacioppo, and Goldman (1981) demonstrated that personal involvement moderates which route to persuasion is effective. Participants who were highly involved with a product issue were persuaded by the quality of arguments (central route), while low-involvement participants were persuaded by peripheral cues such as the number of arguments or the attractiveness of the source. The experiment showed that the same message could produce attitude change through different mechanisms depending on the audience's motivation to process it. A meta-analysis by Carpenter (2015) confirmed the ELM's argument quality by processing type predictions across multiple studies, establishing the model's empirical robustness (Carpenter, 2015; Simply Psychology, "ELM"). The ELM evidence is directly relevant to advertising because it explains why most advertising -- designed for low-involvement processing -- relies on peripheral cues rather than argument quality. The implication is that the peripheral route, which produces weaker and more volatile attitudes, is the primary mechanism through which mass-market advertising operates. Consumers form attitudes toward brands not through careful evaluation but through accumulated associations with attractive imagery, celebrity endorsements, and emotional music.

### Zuboff's Documentation of Behavioral Surplus

Zuboff's research documents the discovery of behavioral surplus at Google in 2001-2002 as a pivotal moment in the evolution of advertising. Google's initial search service operated on a reinvestment cycle: behavioral data from users improved search results, which served users, who generated more data. The breakthrough came when Google discovered that the surplus behavioral data -- more than was needed for service improvement -- could be fabricated into prediction products and sold to advertisers. In 2001, Google's net revenues jumped to $86 million, a 400 percent increase over 2000, and the company turned its first profit. By 2002, revenues reached $347 million, then $1.5 billion in 2003, and $3.5 billion in 2004 -- a 3,590 percent increase in less than four years. By 2016, 89 percent of Alphabet's revenues derived from Google's targeted advertising programs (Zuboff, 2019). This evidence demonstrates that the economic engine of the digital advertising industry is not the advertisement itself but the behavioral data infrastructure that enables prediction and targeting. The scale is extraordinary: Google processes over 40,000 search queries per second, more than 3.5 billion per day. The behavioral surplus extracted from these interactions constitutes the raw material for a new kind of marketplace -- behavioral futures markets -- where advertisers buy probabilistic predictions about what consumers will do.

### The Influencer Marketing Phenomenon

The rise of influencer marketing in the 2010s provides contemporary evidence for the identity advertising mechanism. Research on digital influencers and algorithms on Instagram (Cotter, 2019) documents how influencers negotiate visibility with platform algorithms, modeling lifestyles in which products appear as natural elements rather than advertised objects. The influencer economy operates on the principle that aspiration is more persuasive than argument: a follower does not evaluate a product's features but observes the lifestyle of a person they wish to emulate and purchases the products that appear in that lifestyle. The global influencer market has grown into a multi-billion-dollar industry, demonstrating the commercial viability of manufacturing desire through identity modeling rather than functional persuasion (ResearchGate, 2025; moresapien.org, 2025). This case also illustrates the continuity between Bernays's twentieth-century techniques and twenty-first-century digital marketing: both operate by associating products with aspirational identities, but the digital version benefits from algorithmic amplification and granular audience segmentation.

## Implications

### For Consumers and Citizens

The primary implication of advertising as a system for manufacturing desire is that consumer preferences in affluent societies are, to a significant degree, not autonomous. The dependence effect means that many of the wants consumers experience as their own have been synthesized by the advertising apparatus upstream of their awareness. This does not mean that all consumption is coerced or that consumers lack agency. It means that the information environment in which preferences form is systematically shaped by commercial interests with the resources and expertise to influence those preferences at an unconscious level. The practical consequence is a structural asymmetry: the advertising industry spends hundreds of billions of dollars annually studying and targeting consumer psychology, while individual consumers spend essentially nothing studying the techniques being used on them.

For citizens, the implication extends beyond consumption to democratic participation. Bernays himself acknowledged that the engineering of consent could be "subverted" and used "for antidemocratic purposes" by demagogues with "as much success as those who employ them for socially desirable ends" (Bernays, 1947). The techniques developed for commercial advertising -- emotional appeals, identity association, peripheral-route processing -- are the same techniques used in political propaganda and disinformation campaigns. The pop-culture domain's propaganda topic documents how computational propaganda weaponizes these techniques for political ends. The commercial advertising infrastructure thus serves as a training ground and technology platform for persuasion techniques that can be redirected toward political manipulation. A citizenry that cannot recognize manufactured desire in commercial contexts is unlikely to recognize it in political contexts.

The deeper issue is what the manufactured-desire system does to the quality of human aspiration. When desire is systematically externalized -- when the question "What do I truly want?" is replaced by "What should I want to be socially legible?" -- the capacity for intrinsic motivation atrophies. The advertising apparatus does not merely add new wants to a pre-existing set; it reshapes the mechanism by which wants are generated. Consumers trained from childhood to derive their desires from commercial signals may lose the ability to distinguish between wants that originate from their own values and wants that have been implanted by the persuasion industry. This is not a claim about brainwashing or coercion -- consumers retain agency and can resist specific appeals -- but about the structural environment in which preference formation occurs. In a culture saturated with advertising, the default source of desire is commercial, and opting out of that default requires conscious, sustained effort that most people have neither the time nor the training to maintain.

### For Policymakers and Regulators

The evolution from broadcast advertising to algorithmic micro-targeting has created regulatory challenges that existing frameworks were not designed to address. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), introduced in 2018, established consent requirements for data collection. The Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act further constrain how platforms can combine user data for advertising and prohibit targeting ads at minors or based on ethnicity, sexual orientation, or political views. In the United States, the Competition and Transparency in Digital Advertising Act proposes to break up companies that play multiple roles in the digital advertising supply chain (Magellan Investment Partners, 2022).

The regulatory challenge is that algorithmic advertising operates as what Georgetown Law researchers call a "wicked problem" -- a subject of conflicting narratives where the same system is simultaneously the embodiment of surveillance capitalism and a legitimate commercial service that funds free online content (Georgetown Law, 2025). Regulation must balance the privacy and autonomy interests of consumers against the economic value of the advertising-funded internet. The dependence effect suggests that consumer preferences shaped by advertising do not provide a legitimate basis for evaluating whether advertising serves consumer welfare -- the preferences themselves are the product of the system being evaluated. This creates a measurement problem: how do regulators assess consumer harm when the consumers do not experience the harm as harm?

### For Media Literacy and Education

The third implication concerns the need for media literacy that addresses the psychological mechanisms of advertising rather than merely its factual claims. Traditional media literacy emphasizes critical evaluation of advertising claims -- recognizing exaggeration, identifying bias, checking sources. This approach targets the central route to persuasion. But the evidence from the ELM and from the history of manufactured desire shows that most advertising operates through the peripheral route, bypassing critical evaluation entirely. A consumer who can identify exaggerated claims may still be susceptible to identity advertising that associates products with aspirational selves through imagery, music, and social context rather than explicit claims.

Effective media literacy for the advertising age must therefore address the symbolic and identity dimensions of advertising. Consumers need to understand that advertising works not by lying about products but by constructing social worlds in which products function as identity markers. They need to recognize the dependence effect -- that in an affluent society, many of their wants are manufactured by the system that satisfies them. And they need to understand the surveillance capitalism dimension: that their behavioral data is being continuously extracted and used to predict and shape their behavior, often without their awareness. The pop-culture domain's internet culture and memetics topic provides related analysis of how digital environments shape what people share, believe, and amplify. Media literacy that addresses only the content of advertising while ignoring its psychological and structural mechanisms is insufficient for the task of empowering consumers in an environment designed to manufacture their desires.

The educational challenge is compounded by the fact that advertising funds the media ecosystem through which media literacy itself is typically propagated. News organizations, educational platforms, and social media -- the channels through which critical perspectives on advertising would reach audiences -- are themselves dependent on advertising revenue. This creates a structural conflict of interest: the institutions best positioned to educate consumers about manufactured desire are economically dependent on the system they would critique. The result is that criticism of advertising tends to be marginalized within mainstream media channels and relegated to academic publications, independent journalism, and niche platforms that lack the reach to counterbalance the advertising apparatus. The asymmetry between the resources of the advertising industry and the resources available for critical media education is itself a structural feature of the system, not an accident.
