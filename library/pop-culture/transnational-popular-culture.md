# Cross-Border Popular Culture Is Reinterpreted Through Unequal Gates

Popular culture does not cross borders as an unchanged object moving from one national container to another. Films, television, music, games, formats, celebrities, and internet genres travel through unequal systems of selection, translation, localization, recommendation, and audience interpretation, so wider circulation can diversify cultural exchange while also concentrating power in a few languages, markets, and platforms [1][2][7].

## Background

Modern arguments about transnational popular culture developed from a dispute over whether cross-border media produce cultural sameness or cultural difference. Cultural-imperialism approaches emphasized the reach of large Western, especially United States, media industries and the risk that imported commodities would displace local production. Arjun Appadurai reframed the problem as a tension between homogenization and heterogenization: metropolitan forms can travel widely, but receiving societies also indigenize them. His model treats global cultural traffic as disjunctive because the movements of people, media, technologies, capital, and political ideas do not proceed at the same speed or in the same direction [1]. This made a simple one-way sender-receiver model inadequate without denying that some senders possess far greater resources than others.

The author's historiographic synthesis identifies a subsequent shift from counting imports to studying reception. Joseph Straubhaar's cultural-proximity work argued that audiences commonly prefer programs that are linguistically and culturally relevant when such choices are available. Later work with Antonio La Pastina showed why proximity cannot be reduced to national origin: telenovelas moved from national products to regional and global exports, while viewers responded to language, genre, religion, class, regional values, and other overlapping forms of familiarity [3][4]. A Brazilian audience can therefore find an imported Mexican melodrama more meaningful than a domestic program, and a genre can cross a language boundary because its emotional or narrative conventions are recognizable. Distribution establishes access, not a uniform reading.

The author's historiographic synthesis also treats research on East Asian flows as a correction to accounts that equated globalization with Americanization. Koichi Iwabuchi used interviews with producers, promoters, and consumers to examine Japanese popular culture in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, and Japan. His account shows Japan repackaging Western popular forms for regional markets while Japanese television, music, anime, and characters acquired different resonances in different places [5]. The case establishes two points. First, cultural centers can be regional rather than singularly global. Second, a non-Western counter-flow can challenge one hierarchy while reproducing another, because regional familiarity, colonial memory, and production power remain uneven [5].

Hybridity became a common term for the mixtures created by such movement, but Marwan Kraidy warned against treating every mixture as evidence of equality or resistance. A hybrid work may join local language, imported genre, global production technique, and diasporic performance while still being financed, distributed, and valued through institutions controlled elsewhere. Kraidy's intercontextual approach therefore links hybridity to hegemony and political economy [2]. The author's operationalization of that approach asks who can mix, whose contribution is named, which mixture is marketable, and which actors retain control. Cultural combination is observable; its political meaning must be demonstrated rather than celebrated in advance [2].

Digital distribution accelerated circulation without abolishing geography. Using a February 2021 catalog snapshot, Amanda Lotz, Oliver Eklund, and Stuart Soroka found substantial cross-national variation in Netflix libraries, even though the interface and corporate service appear global. Their comparison of 17 national libraries found that United States titles generally represented about 40 percent of a library, while the remaining titles came from roughly 80 countries. Domestic titles averaged 7.7 percent, falling to 3.8 percent when three outlier libraries were excluded; productions originating in the 17 sampled national markets accounted for 95 percent of Netflix commissions, although commissioned titles were generally distributed across multiple libraries [6]. A platform can therefore widen access to foreign content and remain highly selective about where it commissions, licenses, promotes, and localizes.

The Korean Wave made this platform-mediated phase especially visible. Dal Yong Jin, Sangjoon Lee, and Seok-Kyeong Hong describe a historical movement from Western-dominated flows, through regional counter-flows such as Bollywood and telenovelas, toward a platform era in which local content can become globally available almost simultaneously. They stress that the convergence of Korean popular culture and global digital platforms does not mean the end of United States hegemony [8]. Yoon's audience studies separately document the work of diasporic communities and fan translators in circulating and interpreting K-pop [9][10]. The author's synthesis is that creators, entertainment firms, audiences, translators, and subscription platforms all participate in the flow, while their control over production, distribution, and interpretation remains unequal [6][8][9][10].

The author's synthesis treats transnational popular culture as a chain of transformations rather than a border crossing alone. The relevant object includes the artifact, its production context, its translation and localization, its distributors and interfaces, its promotional intermediaries, and the audiences who accept, reject, remix, or reinterpret it. A cultural work becomes transnational when these linked practices make it meaningful across more than one social setting; global availability by itself is insufficient. That distinction keeps the analysis centered on culture while recognizing the institutions that condition cultural meaning [2][6][11].

## Core Concepts

### Circulation Is a Structured Route, Not Frictionless Diffusion

A cultural flow has an origin, but it also has routes, gates, delays, exclusions, and destinations. Appadurai's model of disjunctive flows explains why migration, media distribution, technology, finance, and ideology cannot be assumed to align [1]. The author's illustrative synthesis is that a work may move without all of its political context, language support, distribution rights, or payment infrastructure moving with it. Such mismatches are not exceptions to circulation; they are consequences of infrastructures that connect some actors and places more completely than others [1][6].

The term transnational is narrower and more useful than an undifferentiated claim that culture is global. It directs attention to relationships that cross state borders while retaining national regulation, language, institutions, and identity as active forces. Straubhaar's account of global, regional, national, and translocal scales shows that several spatial levels can operate at once [3]. A television drama can be nationally financed, regionally familiar, globally distributed, and locally reinterpreted. The author's synthesis is that scale should be treated as an empirical question: identify where a work was made, through which circuits it moved, and at which level audiences attached meaning [3][4].

### Localization Rebuilds Meaning Rather Than Merely Translating Words

The author's operational definition treats localization as the set of interventions that make a work usable and interpretable in another setting, including language translation, editing, presentation, and release choices. Linguistic translation is essential for many works, but it does not guarantee cultural intelligibility or attraction. Kyong Yoon's research on K-pop describes cultural translation as the audience's negotiation of racial, emotional, and local meanings, not only a conversion of Korean words into English [9]. A viewer can enjoy sound, choreography, performance, or star persona before understanding lyrics, while later translations can reorganize how the same performance is interpreted [9].

Television formats make the distinction between a portable structure and a localized text especially clear. Heidi Keinonen argues that format appropriation is a cultural negotiation across production, text, and reception. Executives and producers negotiate licenses, brand rules, production practices, and local expectations; the resulting program combines a deterritorialized apparatus with nationally or locally legible performers, language, humor, and institutions; audiences and critics then decide whether the result feels relevant, derivative, or illegitimate [11]. The process is unequal because format owners, broadcasters, producers, and audiences possess different kinds of influence [11].

Localization is therefore selective. It may retain a genre while changing social values, preserve a visual style while replacing jokes, or keep a celebrity image while altering the promotional story around it. The author's synthesis is that every localization creates a ledger of gains and losses: access may increase while ambiguity narrows; a minority language may gain subtitles while English becomes the required bridge language; a local cast may appear inside a standardized format; or a culturally specific work may become discoverable only after its marketing stresses supposedly universal themes [9][11][12].

### Cultural Proximity Is Multiple, Learned, and Situational

Cultural proximity predicts that people often prefer media that connect with familiar language, values, history, humor, religion, or everyday life. La Pastina and Straubhaar complicate this principle by showing that identities and preferences operate at local, subnational, national, regional, and global levels. Their telenovela research finds that genre familiarity and moral relevance can cross a national border, while programs from the same nation can feel socially distant to particular viewers [4]. Proximity is therefore not a fixed distance between two countries.

Proximity can also be learned through repeated exposure. Iwabuchi documents audiences becoming familiar with Japanese popular forms across East Asia, while Yoon shows Canadian K-pop fans building interpretive competence through sustained media and fandom participation [5][9]. Historical conflict or racialization can also make geographically close media feel distant or contested [5][9]. The author's synthesis is that proximity should be disaggregated into linguistic, genre, emotional, historical, racial, religious, and platform familiarity, with each dimension tested against evidence from the relevant audience [4][9].

### Audiences Decode, Revalue, and Sometimes Redistribute

Audience activity does not mean unlimited freedom. The author's synthesis is that interpretation occurs within a bounded menu shaped by catalog acquisition, platform visibility, translation, devices, and subscription access [6][7][9]. Audience research matters because a circulation count cannot reveal whether people admire, mock, reject, imitate, or politically contest what they encounter. Yoon's interviews with 26 self-identified K-pop fans in three Canadian locations showed young Asian Canadian fans integrating K-pop with racial and affective identities while also navigating the homogenizing pressures of social media and entrepreneurial celebrity culture [9].

Audiences can become distributors and translators. Jennifer Duggan and Anne Dahl's study of the Norwegian series SKAM argues that fan translation, communication, and dissemination contributed to its transnational success. English served as a lingua franca that opened a non-Anglophone work to wider publics, yet using English also reproduced the language's dominant position online [12]. This dual result is central: the same practice can challenge Anglo-American content dominance while reinforcing English as the infrastructure through which the challenge becomes visible [12].

Remixing, reaction videos, dance covers, memes, playlists, recaps, and fan archives add further interpretive layers. These practices can supply context that official distributors omit, create new entry points, and sustain attention after formal promotion ends. They can also simplify, stereotype, or detach fragments from their source. The author's synthesis is that participatory circulation should be evaluated by function: who performed the labor, what context was added or removed, which audience gained access, and which platform captured the resulting data and attention [8][9][12].

### Diasporas Are Cultural Brokers, Not Transparent Representatives

Diasporic communities often possess language skills, family ties, media knowledge, and social networks that connect cultural settings. In Yoon's interview study of 15 young people of Korean heritage in Vancouver, participants selectively consumed Korean, Canadian, and United States media; diasporic youth also acted as literal and cultural translators who could subtitle content and explain it in a new context [10]. Their involvement demonstrates how migration can create circulation routes that precede or exceed official export campaigns.

Diasporic reception is not a simple recovery of a homeland identity. Participants can reject older media favored by parents, use a new genre to negotiate racial identity in the country of residence, or criticize representations marketed as authentic. The community itself contains differences of generation, class, language fluency, gender, and migration history [10]. The author's synthesis is that a diaspora should be studied as a heterogeneous field of producers, intermediaries, critics, and audiences. Treating it as one loyal market erases the negotiations that make its transnational role analytically important [9][10].

### Hybridity Describes Mixture but Does Not Settle Power

Hybrid popular culture combines forms whose histories and institutional positions differ. Keinonen documents how television formats preserve licensed structures while local production teams change language, performers, and cultural references [11]. Wang's Genshin Impact case identifies a Chinese-developed game that combines Chinese motifs, Japanese anime aesthetics, global mythologies, and hybrid monetization forms [13]. The author's synthesis is that such mixtures show creative recombination, but their mere existence does not establish reciprocity, justice, or resistance [2][11][13].

Kraidy's central warning is that a celebratory inventory of mixture can hide the inequality through which the mixture was produced and distributed [2]. Credit, ownership, bargaining power, cultural prestige, and market access may remain concentrated even when a work looks cosmopolitan. A dominant producer can appropriate local difference as style, while a marginal producer may need to suppress difference to pass an international gate. The author's synthesis is to pair every claim of hybridity with four questions: who selected the elements, who controls the rights, who receives recognition and revenue, and which audiences can contest the representation [2].

### Platforms Join Distribution, Curation, and Measurement

A digital platform is not a neutral shelf. It commissions or licenses works, configures national catalogs, and localizes interfaces and access conditions. Lotz, Eklund, and Soroka's February 2021 library study shows that Netflix's national catalogs vary and contain broad foreign sourcing, but domestic representation remains limited in many sampled markets and commissioning is geographically concentrated [6]. UNESCO separately identifies discoverability, platform concentration, and imbalanced algorithmic environments as risks to cultural diversity [7]. Availability and exposure are therefore different variables.

Discoverability means that a work can be found or recommended among an abundant catalog, including by a person who was not already searching for it. UNESCO's 2022 report identifies platform concentration, unequal digital access, imbalanced algorithms, and business models that do not favor diverse content as risks to cultural diversity [7]. A platform may host thousands of works from many countries while directing most attention toward a narrow subset. The author's synthesis is that a diversity audit must examine at least catalog presence, interface visibility, recommendation frequency, promotional placement, language support, viewing completion, and the duration of availability [6][7].

Platforms also reorganize time. Jin, Lee, and Hong contrast earlier routes requiring theatrical release, broadcast sale, or physical purchase with a platform environment in which local content can become available across many countries nearly simultaneously [8]. Simultaneity can reduce the delay that once separated core and peripheral markets, but the power to trigger simultaneous attention remains concentrated in organizations that control global release, recommendation, and marketing. Faster circulation is not the same as decentralized control [7][8].

### Cultural Intermediaries Make Foreignness Legible

The author's operational definition of a cultural intermediary includes people and systems positioned between production and reception, such as buyers, distributors, critics, translators, format consultants, fan accounts, and recommendation services. Keinonen identifies format consultants and producers as gatekeepers who mediate between brand protection and local production, while Yoon's work shows audiences and opinion leaders continuing cultural translation after official release [9][11]. These actors do not merely relay a finished meaning; their selections and explanations affect how a work becomes legible in another setting.

Intermediaries do not merely transmit value; they help create it. Calling a drama "universal" can broaden entry while concealing specific history. Labeling a genre "exotic" can attract attention while fixing it outside normal culture. Translating all works through English can widen circulation while making English competence a hidden admission cost. The author's synthesis is that researchers should record each intermediary intervention as a transformation with an accountable actor, stated purpose, affected audience, and plausible alternative [2][9][12].

### States Shape Circulation Without Determining Reception

States affect transnational popular culture through public funding, local-content rules, broadcasting mandates, trade agreements, censorship, visa policy, education, language policy, and support for translation or export. UNESCO reports that 68 percent of surveyed parties used quotas concerning local content, languages, or social groups, while regulatory authorities increasingly monitored video-on-demand services [7]. Such measures can sustain production capacity and linguistic diversity, but they do not ensure that supported works are discoverable, well translated, or embraced by audiences.

Policy can also convert culture into national branding, which belongs partly to the adjacent subject of soft power. The pop-culture question is narrower: how policy changes the creation, circulation, and interpretation of cultural artifacts. Iwabuchi's study shows that regional circulation can complicate national claims because producers borrow foreign forms, audiences assign meanings that exporters did not control, and a successful work may become culturally familiar while its national origin recedes [5]. The author's synthesis is that policy effectiveness should be judged through cultural plurality and access, not export volume alone [5][7].

## Evidence

### Telenovelas Show That Reach and Reception Follow Different Maps

La Pastina and Straubhaar examine telenovelas that moved from domestic Latin American schedules into regional and global markets. Their approach combines the cultural-proximity framework with audience and genre evidence. The finding is not that audiences always choose national content. Viewers often prefer local production, but a Mexican telenovela could attract Brazilian viewers when its melodramatic structure and moral world felt more relevant than an available domestic alternative. Language, genre, religion, values, and subnational identity created multiple proximities rather than one national measure [4].

This case separates distribution from consumption. Telenovelas achieved wide export, but the reasons for selection differed among communities, and transnational success was not predicted fully by prior domestic success or shared language. The method's limitation is that specific reception sites do not represent every audience. Its value is demonstrating what aggregate trade data miss: a program's cultural distance is produced in the encounter among text, genre history, and audience position [4].

### Japanese Regional Flows Demonstrate Decentered but Unequal Globalization

Iwabuchi studied Japanese popular music and television dramas through interviews with producers, promoters, and consumers in Japan and other East Asian settings. The cases document a localizing strategy in which Japanese industries repackaged Western popular forms for Asian consumption, while audiences in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore attached regional meanings to Japanese works [5]. The evidence challenges a model in which all consequential cultural traffic begins in the United States.

The same evidence does not establish a flat cultural world. Japan's relation to neighboring societies was shaped by industrial strength, historical memory, and the country's ambiguous self-positioning as both Asian and superior to Asia. Regional resonance could coexist with resentment or selective disavowal [5]. The author's cross-source synthesis is that this case illustrates Kraidy's warning: hybrid and counter-flowing culture can redistribute visibility without eliminating hierarchy [2][5].

### Netflix Libraries Reveal Diversity Across Countries and Concentration Within Commissioning

Lotz, Eklund, and Soroka used title-level data from Ampere Analysis to compare a February 2021 snapshot of Netflix libraries in 17 countries covering an estimated 80 percent of Netflix subscribers. They found that United States-produced titles ranged from 36 to 44 percent in the libraries, while roughly 60 percent came from about 80 other countries. Domestic titles averaged 7.7 percent across the 17 libraries and 3.8 percent after excluding three outliers. Commissioned titles averaged 28 percent of each library; productions originating in the 17 sampled national markets accounted for 95 percent of Netflix commissions, although those commissioned titles were generally distributed across libraries [6].

The method measures catalog composition, not what recommendation systems displayed to each user or how audiences interpreted the titles. The authors explicitly call for qualitative and multi-method audience work and note that textual analysis is needed to determine whether a nominally domestic title carries cultural specificity [6]. The evidence nevertheless establishes that a single global brand contains differentiated national catalogs and that wide source-country variety can coexist with low domestic shares and concentrated commissioning.

### Korean Popular Culture Shows Platform Acceleration and Continuing Dependence

Jin, Lee, and Hong provide the editorial introduction to an 11-essay special section on Netflix and the Korean Wave. They describe a platform era in which works such as Squid Game became available and popular across more than 90 countries with little delay, while local culture and global infrastructure converged. The introduction reports that contributions include interviews with 32 viewers in Japan, the United States, Brazil, and France and a quantitative analysis of non-United States Netflix Originals from 2015 through 2021 [8]. The evidence summarized in the introduction spans distribution, reception, genre, labor, and industry structure rather than treating popularity rankings as a complete explanation.

The combined evidence supports two propositions. Platforms can expose audiences to non-Western content that older import routes might not have supplied, and globally successful local content can become strategically important to the platform. Yet the authors state that this new flow does not imply the demise of United States hegemony. Their cited genre analysis also found strong viewing alongside limited genre diversity in Korean Netflix Originals [8]. Platform partnership expands reach while shaping which forms of local culture receive repeated investment and visibility.

### Diasporic and Fan Translation Converts Access Into Cultural Intelligibility

Yoon's 2017 study analyzed semistructured interviews with 26 self-identified K-pop fans across Toronto, Vancouver, and Kelowna. Fans translated K-pop through racial and affective affinities, integrated it into Asian Canadian identities, and used social media to sustain imagined closeness to performers. Language difference did not prevent engagement, but meaning depended on contextualization, and the same platform practices could promote localization while normalizing neoliberal celebrity ideals [9].

Yoon's 2020 study interviewed 15 Korean-heritage youth aged 19 to 32 in Vancouver. Participants consumed Korean, Canadian, and United States media selectively rather than acting as a homogeneous ethnic market. Diasporic youth could provide subtitles and cultural explanation, helping content circulate beyond the diaspora while negotiating their own identities [10]. The small, qualitative samples limit statistical generalization, but they reveal mechanisms that catalog and audience-size data cannot measure.

Duggan and Dahl provide a complementary language case. Their study of SKAM fan practices finds that volunteer translation and dissemination helped a Norwegian series become transnational. Translating into English made non-Anglophone culture accessible and valued, while simultaneously consolidating English as the common language of global online fandom [12]. The author's cross-source synthesis is that the three studies show how audience labor can widen cultural exchange while reproducing a hierarchy in the means of exchange [9][10][12].

### Television Formats Expose Negotiation Across Production, Text, and Reception

Keinonen develops a synthesis from format studies, media-industry research, and structuration theory, then illustrates it with Finnish television. The method follows negotiation at three levels: production decisions and institutional constraints, the localized program text, and audience or critical reception. The study identifies executives, producers, and format consultants as actors with greater programming power than audiences, although public controversy and ratings can still shorten a program's life [11].

The evidence corrects two opposite simplifications. A licensed format is not an empty global recipe into which local content is poured, and a local version is not wholly autonomous. Production practices, brand controls, local norms, performers, critics, and audiences jointly shape the result, but with unequal authority [11]. This is the cultural process at issue here; the economics of format licensing are relevant only insofar as they constrain meaning and agency.

### Genshin Impact Tests Hybrid Contra-Flow in Games

Yaohan Wang's 2026 DiGRA study uses participant observation, textual analysis, and thematic analysis of reviews and social-media discourse to examine the Chinese-developed game Genshin Impact before and after English-language localization. The study finds a strategic fusion of Chinese motifs, Japanese anime aesthetics, global mythologies, and hybrid monetization forms that supports a cultural and economic contra-flow beyond longstanding United States and Japanese dominance [13].

Reception did not validate every choice. The study reports controversies involving race, representation, and translation, demonstrating that successful global distribution does not erase disagreement over cultural sensitivity [13]. As one recent conference study of a single game, it should not be generalized to all games or all Chinese popular culture. It nevertheless provides a game-based test of the broader framework: hybridity can expand a new producer's reach while localization decisions remain contested at the point of reception.

### UNESCO Data Show That More Circulation Has Not Produced Balanced Power

UNESCO's 2022 global report monitors implementation of the 2005 Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions. Its chapter on international flows reports that the value of cultural goods and services exports doubled between 2005/2006 and 2019, while developing-country participation in cultural-goods flows stagnated. Developed countries accounted for an average of 95 percent of cultural-services exports, and least developed countries represented less than 0.5 percent of cultural-goods trade [14]. These are trade measures rather than direct measures of meaning, but they establish the material asymmetry within which cultural exchange occurs.

The report's executive summary separately identifies platform concentration, unequal internet access and literacy, imbalanced algorithms, weak discoverability, and unsustainable remuneration as digital risks. It records increased use of local-content and representation measures while recommending investment in local content, discoverability, fair remuneration, and better data [7]. The author's synthesis is that the evidence rejects any assumption that digitization alone will either equalize cultural power or extinguish local agency: balanced expression also depends on production capacity, accessible infrastructure, plural intermediaries, and policies that reach beyond formal availability [7][14].

## Implications

### For Cultural Analysis: Follow the Work Through the Whole Chain

The author's first analytical recommendation is not to infer influence from export volume, catalog presence, chart rank, or social-media mentions alone. These indicators answer different questions: availability, visibility, selection, consumption, and interpretation are not interchangeable. The author's synthesis is a chain method with seven stages: production, selection, translation, distribution, discoverability, reception, and recirculation. At each stage, record the actors, resources, transformations, exclusions, and evidence [2][6][11].

The author's second recommendation is to preserve scale and population in comparisons. A regional hit is not automatically global; one diaspora is not a national audience; a platform's national catalog is not one user's interface; and a fan community is not the public. Audience evidence should identify place, language, age, migration history, and source knowledge where the study permits. The qualitative findings from K-pop and telenovela research are valuable because they explain mechanisms, not because small samples establish universal frequencies [4][9][10].

### For Creators and Translators: Design for Context, Not Placelessness

Creators seeking cross-border audiences face pressure to remove references thought to be difficult, but cultural specificity can be the source of recognition and value. Iwabuchi's regional cases and the Korean Wave show that foreignness, familiarity, and learned affinity can coexist [5][8]. The author's assessment is that the safer design rule is not to make a work culturally neutral; it is to make crucial relations intelligible without presenting the receiving audience with a false copy of the source context.

The author's recommended practice is to make the localization brief identify which meanings depend on language, genre convention, history, race, religion, humor, music, interface, or performance. Translators should receive contextual material and room to flag consequential choices rather than only isolated strings. Multiple language tracks, glossaries, and translated promotional material can widen access, while consultation can test whether a literal equivalent changes a social relation. Where commercial distributors rely on fan-created translation or promotion, the author's ethical recommendation is to make that labor visible and agree on permission, credit, and support [9][10][12].

### For Platforms: Measure Exposure, Not Just Catalog Diversity

A platform can truthfully report content from many countries while most users may still encounter only a narrow portion. The author's audit recommendation is therefore to pair catalog counts with measures of search retrieval, recommendation impressions, homepage placement, artwork, language coverage, promotional exposure, completion, and retention. Lotz, Eklund, and Soroka establish the difference between source-country breadth and low domestic shares; UNESCO identifies discoverability and platform concentration as separate policy concerns [6][7].

The author's synthesis is a four-part diversity test. Availability asks whether the work is licensed in the territory. Accessibility asks whether language, price, device, and disability barriers permit use. Discoverability asks whether someone not already seeking the work can encounter it. Agency asks whether creators, translators, and audiences can understand or contest the systems that govern visibility. A service passes no cultural-diversity test merely by satisfying the first element [6][7].

The author's recommended audit should test for feedback loops rather than assume them. For example, researchers should ask whether early promotion changes viewing data and whether those data affect later placement, while comparing otherwise similar works that received different levels of exposure. Because catalog research does not reveal personalized ranking, independent researchers and regulators would need appropriate access to impression and prominence data to test this hypothesis under privacy safeguards. This proposed research design follows from the documented gap between catalog variety and algorithmic visibility; it is not evidence that any one ranking method will guarantee plural consumption [6][7].

### For Cultural Policy: Build Capacity Without Freezing Culture Into National Boxes

Policies can support creation through public finance, training, archives, public-service media, local-language production, translation, and local-content obligations. They can support circulation through co-production agreements, mobility, subtitling funds, open metadata, and discoverability rules. UNESCO's findings justify attention to structural imbalance because growth in total trade has not produced balanced participation, and digital concentration adds a new gate to older inequalities [7][14].

The author's policy analysis also identifies failure modes to test rather than assume away. A quota may be satisfied by content with little cultural specificity; national branding may reduce creators to diplomatic instruments; censorship may be defended as cultural protection; and a domestic incumbent may invoke cultural policy against smaller or diasporic competitors. The author's synthesis is that measures should be assessed by plurality of creators, languages, genres, and ownership, together with audience access and artistic freedom, rather than by national market share alone [5][6][7].

### For Diasporas, Fans, and Audiences: Participation Is Real but Unequally Resourced

Diasporic and fan communities can translate, explain, disseminate, and introduce works to audiences that official distributors did not anticipate; Yoon's studies and the SKAM case document these mechanisms directly [9][10][12]. The author's synthesis is that such activity can expand identity options, create transnational affiliation, and challenge the assumption that audiences outside the origin culture are passive recipients [9][10][12].

The author's assessment is that participation should not be romanticized. Duggan and Dahl document volunteer fan translation as a condition of SKAM's wider circulation, while UNESCO identifies platform concentration and unsustainable creator remuneration as digital cultural risks [7][12]. The author's synthesis is that community contribution and institutional control must therefore be measured separately rather than treating participation as proof of shared power. The author's ethical recommendations are visible credit, negotiated permission for commercial reuse, proportionate compensation where community labor creates commercial value, and preservation options that do not depend entirely on one platform.

The author's recommendation for audiences is a discipline of contextual reception. Before treating one artifact as representative of a country or culture, ask who made it, for which initial public, through which genre conventions, under which distribution pressures, and how differently situated viewers interpreted it. This does not prohibit enjoyment or criticism. It prevents visibility from being mistaken for representativeness and popularity from being mistaken for consensus [4][9][13].

### For Cross-Border Comparison: Keep Adjacent Questions Separate

The author's scope recommendation is to keep industry structure, foreign policy, and adaptation adjacent to, but analytically distinct from, transnational popular culture. Media-industry valuation asks who earns revenue and whether a business has durable economics; the present topic asks how institutional power changes cultural visibility and meaning. Soft-power analysis asks whether attraction advances state objectives; the present topic asks how audiences receive, reinterpret, or resist the cultural artifact. Adaptation-across-media analysis asks how a story changes when it moves between forms; the present topic asks how an artifact changes when it moves between social and linguistic contexts, even if the medium remains the same [5][11].

The boundaries matter because one event can support different claims. A Korean drama commissioned by a United States platform may be profitable, diplomatically useful, formally innovative, and culturally reinterpreted abroad, but evidence for one result does not prove the others. The author's synthesis is to state the dependent variable before gathering cases: revenue, state preference, formal transformation, audience meaning, or cultural diversity. This topic is strongest when the dependent variables are meaning, visibility, and cultural power [6][8].

### A Practical Transnational Culture Audit

The following audit is the author's synthesis from the cited evidence. First, identify the artifact, origin setting, and original audience. Second, map production resources, rights, and state or institutional support. Third, trace distribution territories, catalogs, release timing, and unavailable regions. Fourth, document translation, dubbing, editing, marketing, and metadata choices. Fifth, test discoverability rather than assuming that availability creates attention. Sixth, separate audience groups and collect reception evidence appropriate to each group. Seventh, record diasporic, fan, critic, influencer, and algorithmic mediation. Eighth, compare credit, ownership, data, and revenue with the visible cultural mixture. Ninth, identify what was localized, what remained foreign, and what became newly hybrid. Tenth, state whether the evidence supports exchange, homogenization, asymmetry, resistance, or a documented combination [2][4][6][7][9][11][12].

The single worst analytical outcome is a borderless success story that erases every gate between creation and reception. The reverse error is a cultural-imperialism verdict that treats audiences as blank surfaces and every local transformation as irrelevant. Evidence supports a harder conclusion: circulation can diversify what is available, localization can create new meanings, and audiences can exercise agency, while production resources, language, platforms, and policy still distribute power unevenly [1][2][6][7].
