# Video Games -- How Interactive Agency Turned Play into the 21st Century's Dominant Cultural Art Form

Video games have grown from arcade curiosities into the largest and most
culturally influential entertainment medium of the 21st century, reaching
billions of players across every demographic and, by some estimates,
surpassing film and music combined in revenue. What distinguishes games
from every earlier medium is agency -- the player's power to act within
and shape the experience -- which produces a fundamentally different kind
of storytelling, one in which the audience is a participant rather than a
spectator. This topic examines games as cultural artifacts: how their
interactive form creates new kinds of narrative, how the art-form debate
evolved from skepticism to institutional recognition, and what gaming
culture, e-sports, and streaming reveal about the society that produced
them.

## Background

The video game as a commercial form begins in the early 1970s with arcade
machines such as Pong (1972) and the home consoles that followed, but its
cultural history is older and stranger than the technology suggests. The
first documented video game competition took place on 19 October 1972 at
Stanford University, when students organized an "Intergalactic spacewar
olympics" around the game Spacewar! -- a tournament whose grand prize was
a year's subscription to Rolling Stone (Wikipedia, "Esports"). From the
start, games were social, competitive, and community-forming, not merely
solitary screen-time.

The intellectual foundation for treating games as a serious cultural
object arrived in the late 1990s, and it arrived split in two. Janet
Murray's "Hamlet on the Holodeck" (1997) argued that the computer offered
a genuinely new medium for storytelling -- an "incunabular" moment like
the birth of the novel or cinema -- in which interactivity and immersion
could produce narratives that no earlier form could. The same year, Espen
Aarseth's "Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature" (1997) insisted
that interactive works demand a new analytic vocabulary, coining the term
"ergodic literature" for texts that require nontrivial effort to traverse:
the reader does not simply interpret but actively constructs the path
through the work. These two founding texts seeded the central academic
dispute in game studies -- the so-called "narratology versus ludology"
debate over whether games are best understood as stories or as rule
systems -- a dispute that shaped how a generation of scholars and critics
would take games seriously.

The public question of whether games could be art ran in parallel. In the
early 2000s, film critic Roger Ebert notoriously argued that "video games
can never be art," while media scholar Henry Jenkins countered in his 2005
essay "Games, the New Lively Art" that games inherit and combine the
expressive grammars of dance, film, music, and architecture into something
new (Jenkins, 2005). Institutional recognition followed. In 2006 the
French Minister of Culture declared video games cultural goods and "a form
of artistic expression," inducting three game designers into the Ordre des
Arts et des Lettres. In 2011 the United States Supreme Court, in Brown v.
Entertainment Merchants Association, held that video games are protected
speech under the First Amendment "like the protected books, plays, and
movies that preceded them." The Smithsonian American Art Museum mounted
"The Art of Video Games" exhibition in 2012, a signal that the medium had
earned the standing its critics had once denied it.

## Core Concepts

### Agency and the Interactive Turn

The concept that most cleanly separates games from every prior medium is
agency. Murray (1997) defined agency as "the satisfying power to take
meaningful action and see the results of our decisions and choices."
In a novel or a film, the audience interprets a fixed sequence of events;
in a game, the player's choices produce consequences the author did not
and cannot fully script. This transforms the relationship between creator
and audience. The player is neither passive receiver nor co-author in the
literary sense, but something in between: an actor who realizes one path
through a space of possibilities the designer has built. Agency is why
players describe a game's events in the first person -- "I won," "I died,"
"I made that choice" -- in a way no viewer says of a film's protagonist.

### Half-Real: Rules and Fiction

Jesper Juul's "Half-Real" (2005) offers the most influential account of
the game's dual nature. A game, Juul argues, is half real rules and half
fictional world. The rules are real: they are enforced, binding, and
objective -- jump and you clear the gap, mistime it and you fall. The
fiction is also real in a different sense: a game of chess can be about
nothing, but a game of The Legend of Zelda or Grand Theft Auto presents a
world with characters, places, and stakes. Games are defined by the
interaction between these two halves. Unlike a film, whose fiction can be
described apart from its mechanics, a game's meaning cannot be separated
from how it plays. This is why critics can analyze the narrative of a
game and the "feel" of its systems as equally constitutive of meaning.

### Ludology versus Narratology

The founding methodological dispute in game studies set "ludologists,"
who insisted games be studied as games -- systems of rules, challenge, and
play -- against "narratologists," who treated games as a storytelling
medium continuous with film and literature. Gonzalo Frasca and others in
the ludology camp warned against "colonizing" games with literary theory,
arguing that a game's essence is its simulation and rules, not its plot.
Murray and the narratology camp replied that narrative is a fundamental
human cognitive frame and that games plainly deploy it. Juul's synthesis
was decisive: games can carry stories, but they carry them through
interactive systems, and the two perspectives are complementary rather
than opposed. The debate matters because it forced the field to define
what is genuinely new about the medium rather than importing categories
wholesale from its predecessors.

### The Evolution of Game Storytelling

Game storytelling evolved from near-absence to cinematic ambition in
roughly four decades. Early arcade games (Space Invaders, Pac-Man, Donkey
Kong) had minimal fiction; the goal was mastery, not meaning. Text
adventures and the point-and-click era (Zork, Myst) put words and puzzles
at the center. The "cinematic" turn of the late 1990s and 2000s -- Final
Fantasy, Metal Gear Solid, later The Last of Us and Red Dead Redemption --
imported film grammar (cutscenes, voice acting, orchestral scores) while
tethered to player action. Alongside this linear strand, "environmental
storytelling" (Half-Life, Gone Home) let players reconstruct narrative
from the world itself, and "emergent" games (Minecraft, The Sims, Dwarf
Fortress) generated stories from the interaction of systems rather than
any authored plot. Each strand represents a different answer to the same
question: how do you tell a story when the audience is in the middle of
it?

### Procedural Rhetoric and Games as Argument

Ian Bogost's "How to Do Things with Videogames" (2011) and his earlier
concept of "procedural rhetoric" reframe games as a medium for making
arguments. A game, Bogost argues, does not merely depict a claim; it
encodes a claim in its rules and lets the player experience it. A game
about a bureaucracy does not tell you the bureaucracy is broken -- it
makes you navigate the broken process. This is a form of persuasion unique
to the medium, and it underlies a growing field of "serious games" that
address policy, health, and education. Bogost's framing completes the
move begun by Jenkins: games are not a lesser imitation of older arts but
a distinct medium with its own expressive and rhetorical powers.

### Gaming Culture and Participatory Identity

Games do not end at the screen. Around the medium has grown a global
subculture -- gaming culture -- defined by shared identity ("gamer"),
language, and practice (Wikipedia, "Video game culture"). It encompasses
local-area-network (LAN) parties, fan conventions such as PAX, and the
rise of streaming platforms like Twitch and YouTube, where playing became
a spectator activity. Competitive play professionalized into e-sports,
which began as amateur tournaments and, after South Korea licensed
professional players in 2000, grew into a global industry of leagues,
sponsorships, and stadium-filling championships. Games have become what
sociologist Ray Oldenburg called a "third place" -- a social space apart
from home and work -- and, for many, the primary site of friendship,
competition, and community in a digital age.

## Evidence

The cultural scale of games is measurable. Ampere Analysis recorded that
the global games market grew 26 percent from 2019 to 2021, reaching a
record $191 billion, and by the early 2020s games "compete with movies,
music, and television in terms of both popularity and revenue" (Wikipedia,
"Video game industry"). Reach is equally broad: a 2011 Entertainment
Software Association survey found 71 percent of Americans aged six to
forty-nine played video games, and a 2008 survey of U.S. households put
the proportion of children aged twelve to seventeen who played at 97
percent. The average player age reached roughly thirty-one by 2016, and
the gender distribution was approaching parity (59 percent male, 41
percent female), though women remain less likely to self-identify as
gamers due to stigma (Wikipedia, "Video game culture").

The competitive dimension has its own quantitative story. The first known
esports tournament was the 1972 Spacewar! olympics; contemporary esports
began with face-to-face arcade competition and Sega's 1974 All Japan TV
Game Championships. By the late 2010s, projected esports audience for 2020
was 454 million viewers with revenue exceeding $1 billion, with China
accounting for roughly 35 percent of global esports revenue. The
International Olympic Committee has moved toward including esports,
scheduling the inaugural Olympic Esports Games for 2027 (Wikipedia,
"Esports").

The legal and institutional record confirms the medium's cultural
standing. In Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association (2011), the U.S.
Supreme Court struck down a California law restricting violent-game sales
to minors, holding that video games "communicate ideas -- and even social
messages -- through many familiar literary devices" and are entitled to
full First Amendment protection. The French Ministry of Culture recognized
games as artistic expression in 2006, the U.S. National Endowment for the
Arts began funding "interactive games" in 2011, and the Smithsonian's
"The Art of Video Games" (2012) marked the medium's entry into the
curatorial canon (Wikipedia, "Video games as an art form").

The scholarly evidence base is no less substantial. Murray (1997) and
Aarseth (1997) established the field's conceptual foundations; Juul (2005)
provided its account of the game's dual nature; Jenkins (2005) made the
public case for games as art; Bogost (2011) articulated procedural
rhetoric; and James Paul Gee's "What Video Games Have to Teach Us About
Learning and Literacy" (2003) documented how games function as effective
learning environments through feedback, challenge calibration, and
identity. Taken together, these sources describe a medium that is
economically dominant, demographically universal, legally protected as
speech, and theoretically established as an object of serious study.

## Implications

For cultural analysis, games are primary documents of unusual richness.
The games a society makes and plays encode its anxieties, fantasies, and
values with a concreteness that passive media cannot match: the
power-fantasy shooter and the pastoral "cozy game" are both statements
about what their players want to escape and what they want to become.
Reading games as artifacts means asking not only what a game's story says
but what its systems reward, what its rules make effortless, and what
freedoms it grants or withholds. The author's assessment is that a
generation of critics and scholars now treats games the way earlier ones
treated film: as a lens into the era that produced them.

For storytelling, agency changes the meaning of authorship. When the
player completes the narrative, the "author" of the experience is a
hybrid of designer and player, and the emotional weight of consequence --
regret over a choice, grief over a companion's death the player failed to
prevent -- is something no fixed narrative can reproduce. This has begun
to leak into adjacent media: interactive film and television such as
"Bandersnatch" borrow the grammar of games, and prestige television's
serialized, participatory fandoms described in the linked topic on
prestige television parallel the engaged spectatorship games pioneered.
Understanding agency is increasingly a prerequisite for understanding
where narrative itself is heading.

For learning and psychology, Gee's work suggests games are not the enemy
of education but one of its most effective models -- environments that
teach through immediate feedback, calibrated challenge, and the chance to
try and fail safely. The motivational architecture of games (clear goals,
visible progress, meaningful choice) has been studied and borrowed far
beyond entertainment. At the same time, the contested question of games'
effects on behavior -- especially violence -- is a bridge into the
psychology-behavior domain, where it belongs as a media-effects question
rather than a cultural-analysis one.

For social connection, games now function as durable third places. The
COVID-19 pandemic accelerated a trend already underway, as multiplayer
games and streaming became primary venues for socializing at a distance;
the demographic shift toward near-parity between male and female players,
and the aging of the player base, mean "gamer" increasingly describes
everyone (Wikipedia, "Video game culture"). The rise of streaming turned
play into a shared, watched, and discussed activity, binding audiences in
the same way live sport binds fans -- with the same tribal identities,
rituals, and dramas that attend any mass cultural institution.

The most durable implication is that the art-form debate is, for
practical purposes, over. The question is no longer whether games are
art or culture but how they function as both -- what kinds of meaning
they make that no other medium can, and what a world in which the
dominant cultural form is interactive, rather than receptive, implies
for how people think, connect, and tell stories. A medium built on
agency does not merely entertain; it trains its audience, at every
moment of play, to expect that their choices matter.
