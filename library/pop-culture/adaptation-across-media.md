# Adaptation Changes Stories Because Every Medium Reassigns Attention, Agency, and Cultural Memory

Adaptation is not the transport of an unchanged story between neutral containers. When a novel, comic, film, television series, stage work, or game is remade in another medium, the new work redistributes what can be shown, told, enacted, repeated, chosen, and remembered; the result is an interpretation shaped by form, collaborators, institutions, audiences, and historical context [1][6][7]. Fidelity can identify selected continuities, but cultural analysis becomes stronger when it also asks what the new medium makes possible, what it suppresses, and whose version of the story gains authority [4][5][9].

## Background

Stories have always crossed forms. Oral accounts have become written narratives; myths have become plays, poems, paintings, operas, films, comics, television series, games, and digital performances; and later works have returned to earlier forms with images, structures, and expectations acquired elsewhere. Julie Sanders places adaptation within a larger network of intertextual relations in which works revise, relocate, quote, and appropriate prior works rather than arise in isolation [9]. Linda Hutcheon and Siobhan O'Flynn likewise treat adaptation as central to the storytelling imagination and examine it across film, opera, games, popular music, theme parks, and digital platforms [1]. These accounts make adaptation a general cultural practice, not a special defect that occurs when literature is converted into film.

Early adaptation criticism often privileged a source, especially a canonical novel, and judged a later film by what it retained or lost. That model made comparison easy, but it also built the verdict into the method: the verbal source possessed an assumed wholeness, while the screen work appeared as an abbreviated derivative. Thomas Leitch's handbook situates contemporary adaptation studies within debates over fidelity, medium specificity, intertextuality, aesthetics, and method, showing that the field developed by challenging inherited hierarchies as well as by revising its own reactions against them [2]. Timothy Corrigan's definition distinguishes adaptation as process, product, and reception, three dimensions that prevent the object on screen or page from being treated as the whole event [3].

Fidelity remains contentious because it names several different relationships at once. It can refer to plot events, themes, tone, characterization, style, historical setting, audience experience, or an adapter's declared intention. David T. Johnson documents the repeated rejection and return of fidelity within adaptation studies [4]. James Harold separates story fidelity from thematic fidelity and argues that preserving a sequence of events is not ordinarily an aesthetic merit by itself, whereas finding a medium-specific way to preserve or rework a theme can require significant artistic achievement [5]. The useful conclusion is not that fidelity is meaningless. It is that any fidelity claim must state what is supposedly faithful, why that feature matters, and how its preservation was achieved.

A media-conscious approach changed the unit of comparison. Marie-Laure Ryan's collection on narrative across media asks how narratives migrate and mutate when expressed through verbal, visual, gestural, musical, and digital forms, with the properties of the supporting medium shaping both narrative form and experience [6]. Ryan and Jan-Noel Thon's later volume tests which concepts -- including storyworld, narrator, subjectivity, and fictionality -- travel across media and which require revision [7]. This approach preserves comparison without assuming that all media possess equivalent resources. A first-person sentence, an actor's glance, a panel transition, an episode break, and a rule that blocks an action may perform related narrative work, but they do not perform it in the same way.

Adaptation also belongs to a longer history of media borrowing. Jay David Bolter and Richard Grusin call the refashioning of one medium by another remediation. Their examples show that media establish cultural meaning partly by paying homage to, rivaling, and reorganizing earlier forms: film remediated photography and stage practice, while television and digital media in turn refashioned film and other predecessors [8]. Adaptation is one deliberate and recognizable form of this broader relation. It announces or invites comparison with a prior work, while remediation explains why even a work without one named source can carry the conventions of older media.

The shift from one text to a network also changed the account of authorship. A book may carry one prominent author's name, but its publication already involves editing, design, rights, marketing, and distribution. A screen, stage, or game adaptation makes collaboration especially visible through direction, performance, cinematography, sound, production design, programming, testing, and institutional approval. Simone Murray argues that adaptation studies must examine industrial and digital conditions rather than treating adaptation only as textual comparison, particularly when platforms and amateur production alter who can revise and circulate material [10]. Nafiseh Mousavi's study of the making-of documentary for Persepolis similarly treats production evidence as part of adaptation and shows how source work, autobiographical experience, performance, and multiple creative agents enter the transformation [11].

Media convergence further complicates the boundary between adaptation and expansion. Henry Jenkins defines transmedia storytelling as the coordinated dispersion of integral elements of a fiction across delivery channels, ideally with each medium making a distinctive contribution [16]. Colin B. Harvey distinguishes adaptation from narrative expansion while examining how play and memory organize science-fiction and fantasy storyworlds across media [17]. An adaptation substantially retells or reworks recognizable material; a transmedia extension adds a new episode, perspective, location, or historical layer to a larger storyworld. Actual franchises combine both operations, so analysts must identify what a particular object does rather than classify an entire property with one label.

The history of adaptation is therefore not a march from books to screens. It is a many-directional circulation among forms, institutions, and audiences. Film can become a novelization, game, musical, or television serial; a game can become television; a comic can become animation and return as a game; a stage work can relocate a myth to another language and political setting. The author's synthesis is that every move solves one communication problem by creating another. Adaptation makes some relations newly perceptible, but it also selects one path through a source's ambiguities and gives that selection material, institutional, and mnemonic force [1][7][8][9].

## Core Concepts

### Adaptation Is Product, Process, and Reception

An adaptation is first a product: a recognizable work that declares or permits a sustained relation to another work. It is also a process of selection, interpretation, transformation, and production. Finally, it is an event of reception in which audiences compare the new work with a source they may know directly, know through cultural reputation, or not know at all [1][3][14]. These dimensions can diverge. A producer may announce fidelity, a work may transform major structures, and different audiences may nevertheless experience it as either a respectful return or a radical break.

This three-part model prevents a false choice between formal and social analysis. The product supplies observable evidence: scenes, words, images, sounds, episodes, interfaces, or rules. The process supplies decisions and constraints: rights, collaborators, budgets, technologies, schedules, censorship, and intended publics. Reception supplies recognition, comparison, affect, argument, and afterlife. No dimension alone establishes the meaning of an adaptation, and evidence from one dimension should not be used as if it proved the others [2][3][10][14].

### What Travels Is a Structured Set of Relations

A story cannot be reduced to a list of proper names and plot points. What may travel includes causal relations, themes, conflicts, character functions, spatial arrangements, motifs, genre expectations, and a larger storyworld. Ryan and Thon's media-conscious narratology asks which narrative concepts apply across media while preserving differences in narration, subjectivity, multimodality, and interactivity [7]. The storyworld concept is useful because it directs attention to environments, agents, events, rules, and histories rather than only to a fixed sequence.

Different adaptations preserve different relations. One may retain the principal events but alter their moral framing. Another may omit much of the plot while preserving a conflict or atmosphere. A third may move a story into another country or period so that analogous roles expose different institutions. The author's synthesis is that adaptation should be mapped as a vector of continuities and transformations, not scored on one line from faithful to unfaithful. Plot, theme, viewpoint, style, world, and audience relation can move in different directions at the same time [1][5][7][9].

### Medium Affordances Redistribute Narrative Work

A medium affords some operations more readily than others without absolutely determining content. Prose can describe thought, compress decades in a sentence, shift grammatical distance, and leave bodies or spaces partly unspecified. Film can coordinate framing, editing, movement, performance, sound, and scale in a shared temporal sequence. Comics can arrange several moments on one page and make sequence depend on panels, gaps, and page layout. Theater joins live bodies, voices, space, and an audience in one event. Serial television can revisit characters and institutions across episodes and seasons. Games add executable rules, player action, failure, repetition, and variable paths [1][6][7][12].

These differences make apparent losses inseparable from gains. A film that cannot reproduce pages of interior discourse may externalize conflict through performance, sound, blocking, or montage. A game that cannot guarantee one dramatic sequence may model a conflict through choices and consequences. A stage adaptation that lacks cinematic location changes may turn spatial restriction into intimacy or symbolic concentration. The author's synthesis is that the central question is functional: what did a feature do in the source, and by what resources does the adaptation preserve, replace, question, or abandon that function [1][6][8]?

Affordance is not destiny. Film can use voice-over, text, or long duration; prose can imitate montage; television can be episodic or serial; games can constrain action tightly; and theater can use projection and recorded sound. Bolter and Grusin's remediation explains these borrowings as constitutive rather than exceptional [8]. Medium analysis should therefore identify a work's actual formal choices, not assign fixed essences to "the novel," "film," or "games."

### Compression and Expansion Are Interpretive

Feature-length adaptation often compresses characters, incidents, settings, and exposition, while serial television can expand secondary figures, institutional detail, and consequences over time. Compression is not only subtraction. Combining characters can change who carries knowledge or blame; moving a revelation can alter suspense; replacing repeated events with one scene can convert a social pattern into an individual conflict. Expansion is equally interpretive because new material selects which gaps deserve explanation and which implied possibilities become canonical [1][6][7].

A source's length does not mechanically dictate the operation. A short work can be expanded into a film; a long novel can be reorganized into a short performance; an existing film can support a game that repeats one narrow action; and a serial can compress several sources while adding an original arc. The author's synthesis is that compression and expansion should be analyzed through redistributed emphasis. Count what changed, but then ask which values, causes, and perspectives the new allocation of time made central [1][7][17].

### Point of View Becomes Access, Alignment, and Knowledge

Point of view is often treated as a problem of converting a verbal narrator into a camera, but the broader issue is how a work allocates access to knowledge and feeling. Prose may name thought directly or control it through diction and free indirect discourse. Film and television can align viewers through performance, framing, sound, editing, and restricted information without equating the camera with a person. Comics can juxtapose external images with captions or thought. Theater can use soliloquy, direct address, staging, and the audience's simultaneous view of space. Games can join viewpoint to controllable action and to information revealed only through exploration [6][7][12].

Changing access changes ethics. Moving from one consciousness to an ensemble can distribute credibility; visualizing an ambiguity can close one interpretation; withholding a character's thoughts can make behavior more difficult to excuse; letting a player act as a formerly marginal figure can reorganize responsibility. These are not technical side effects. They determine whose fear, desire, labor, and memory become available as the story's center. The author's synthesis is that an adaptation's politics often appear most clearly in the gap between who had narrative access before and who has it now [7][9][11].

### Embodiment Makes Abstraction Socially Specific

Written description can leave voice, body, gesture, accent, race, age, disability, clothing, and physical environment partly open. Performance and visual design materialize many of those variables. Casting, vocal delivery, movement, makeup, costume, lighting, architecture, and sound place an adapted character inside a specific social world. Theater adds co-presence and the variability of performance; screen media add framing, editing, and reproducibility [1][9][11].

Embodiment can expose assumptions that a source left unmarked, but it can also narrow ambiguity or reproduce stereotypes. A change of setting or cast may reveal the portability of a conflict, yet it can also appropriate another community's history as decoration. Sanders's distinction between adaptation and appropriation is important here because relocation carries cultural and aesthetic politics, not merely updated scenery [9]. The author's synthesis is that cultural translation requires evidence about both contexts and attention to who has authority to transform the material.

### Interactivity Converts Meaning Into Constrained Action

Games do not simply add buttons to a story. Their rules define available actions, consequences, failure states, repetition, and the relation between player intention and authored possibility. Stang's analysis of Telltale adaptations argues that The Walking Dead creates a powerful illusion of agency partly by using principal game characters who are not bound to repeat the source's established outcomes [12]. The case demonstrates why a familiar storyworld may be more adaptable to play than a fixed sequence: recognition supplies context, while new agents preserve uncertainty.

Moving in the other direction removes performed choice. A television adaptation of a game can no longer require each viewer to navigate, fail, repeat, or enact violence. It must translate the thematic effect of those actions into fixed performance, editing, serial structure, and spectatorship. Spence's analysis of The Last of Us argues that its television adapters used television's distinct affordances to rework themes surrounding ludic and real-world violence [13]. The relevant fidelity is therefore not a count of reproduced encounters but the relation between violence, responsibility, and narrative consequence.

Agency should not be romanticized. A game can offer many actions that change little, while a fixed work can create intense interpretive participation. Choices are authored, programmed, tested, and constrained. The author's synthesis is that adaptation analysis should distinguish physical input, strategic freedom, narrative branching, role identification, and perceived consequence. Calling all of them "interactivity" hides the structure through which a game assigns responsibility [7][12][13].

### Fidelity Is a Question Set, Not a Verdict

A fidelity inquiry can be rigorous when it names its object. Story fidelity asks about events and characters. Thematic fidelity asks whether a concern or argument survives through different formal resources. Stylistic fidelity asks about rhythm, tone, voice, imagery, or genre. Experiential fidelity asks whether audiences are invited into a comparable relation of suspense, intimacy, distance, mastery, or uncertainty. Historical fidelity asks about evidence outside the source-adaptation relation. These dimensions can conflict [4][5].

Harold's distinction is useful because it explains why event preservation may be exact yet inert, while a conspicuous change may carry a theme more effectively in the new medium [5]. Johnson's history cautions that fidelity has repeatedly crowded out other questions even after scholars rejected it [4]. The author's synthesis is that fidelity should begin an explanation, not end one. "Faithful" must be followed by "to what, for whom, by which means, and at what cost?"

### Adaptation, Remediation, and Transmedia Are Related but Distinct

Adaptation reorganizes a recognizable prior work. Remediation describes how media refashion other media at a broader formal and cultural level [8]. Transmedia storytelling distributes different, coordinated elements across platforms rather than primarily retelling the same material [16]. These categories overlap: a game may adapt a film's story, remediate its visual language, and add a transmedia episode to a larger franchise.

The distinction matters because each operation creates a different expectation. An adaptation invites comparison with prior material. A transmedia extension promises additive knowledge or experience. Remediation can occur even without a named source. Harvey's analysis of adaptation versus narrative expansion within transmedia storyworlds shows that memory and canon connect these operations but do not erase their differences [17]. The author's synthesis is that a useful description should identify the operation at the level of the individual work, not rely on a franchise label.

### Authorship Becomes a Chain of Negotiated Agency

Adaptation is often discussed as a dialogue between an original author and a director, but production distributes agency among many participants and institutions. Rights holders define permissions; writers restructure material; performers embody characters; designers and technicians establish sensory worlds; editors determine sequence; publishers, studios, platforms, and marketers shape release; and audiences influence afterlives. Murray's industrial approach makes these mediations visible, while Mousavi's Persepolis case uses a making-of document to reveal source memories, authorial performance, voice acting, and collaborative transformation [10][11].

Credit and responsibility should follow evidence. A director's public explanation establishes intention but not the whole production process or audience effect. A source author's participation does not remove the agency of co-creators, and a corporate license does not create interpretive control over every audience. The author's synthesis is that adaptation is best modeled as a negotiated chain in which different agents control different transformations. The chain can be reconstructed through drafts, interviews, production records, paratexts, contracts, and formal analysis [10][11].

### Audiences Complete the Adaptive Relation

Hutcheon distinguishes knowing audiences, who experience repetition with variation, from audiences who meet an adaptation without direct knowledge of its source [1]. Sullivan pushes reception further by arguing that an audience can construct a meaningful adaptive relation even when creators did not announce one, using Lady Bird and The Tempest as a reception-led case [14]. This claim expands adaptation beyond authorized genealogy and shows that recognition itself is interpretive labor.

Reception is plural because audiences bring different source knowledge, languages, identities, generations, and media competence. A change that appears as loss to one audience may create access for another. Public fidelity disputes can also become struggles over who has authority to define the story's essential identity. The author's synthesis is that reception evidence should be separated by type: reviews reveal critical discourse, interviews and surveys reveal reported interpretation, behavioral data reveal choices, and circulation data reveal reach. None alone represents "the audience" [1][10][14].

### Adaptation Governs Cultural Memory

Repeated adaptation affects which stories, versions, and interpretations remain visible. Loock's history of Hollywood remaking treats serial repetition and variation as a negotiation among industry economics, film history, cultural debate, and generational belonging [15]. Harvey similarly connects transmedia storyworlds with play, canon, and memory [17]. An adaptation may recover an older work, displace it in search and circulation, canonize one interpretation, or expose the historical distance between versions.

Memory is therefore not preserved by repetition alone. A new version selects images, performances, and values that can become the common reference for audiences who never encounter the source. The author's synthesis is that adaptation acts as cultural memory governance: it reallocates attention and authority across an archive. The analytical task is to document what becomes newly available, what becomes harder to see, and which community's memory is treated as universal [9][15][17].

## Evidence

### Field-Level Synthesis Replaces the One-Way Copy Model

Hutcheon and O'Flynn build their theory across forms that include film, opera, games, music, theme parks, and digital media, organizing adaptation through questions of what, who, why, how, where, and when [1]. Corrigan's historical definition separately identifies process, product, and reception [3]. Ryan's edited collection tests narrative across face-to-face, visual, moving-image, musical, and digital forms, while Ryan and Thon's volume examines the cross-media validity of narrator, storyworld, subjectivity, fictionality, multimodality, and intermediality [6][7].

These works use theoretical synthesis and comparative case analysis rather than one controlled experiment. Their convergent finding is that adaptation cannot be explained as the survival rate of plot units. Formal properties alter narration and experience, and the adaptive event includes production and reception. Their limitation is equally important: a general framework does not predict the result of one adaptation. It supplies variables that must be tested against the specific works, production record, and audience evidence [1][3][6][7].

### Fidelity Analysis Shows Why the Unit of Comparison Matters

Johnson reconstructs fidelity as a recurring and contested concept in adaptation studies, examining its rejection and possible future uses [4]. Harold uses philosophical analysis to distinguish story fidelity from thematic fidelity and asks whether each constitutes artistic merit. He concludes that reproducing a story is not typically a merit by itself, while carrying a theme across media can be an accomplishment because it requires transformation through new artistic resources [5].

The method is conceptual rather than statistical: terms are separated, implications are tested, and counterarguments are considered. The finding does not prove that thematic fidelity is always achieved or always desirable. It establishes why an undifferentiated fidelity score is analytically weak. A comparison must identify the feature preserved and the achievement involved. The author's assessment is that Harold's distinction also explains common disagreement: two viewers may use the word "faithful" while one counts events and the other evaluates a theme [4][5].

### Persepolis Exposes Adaptation as Multi-Agent Production

Mousavi studies the making-of documentary The Hidden Face of Persepolis as an intermediary media object rather than relying only on a source-to-film comparison [11]. The case concerns Marjane Satrapi's graphic narrative and the animated film she co-directed, but the production document also records performers, voice work, creative negotiation, promotional construction, autobiographical memory, and the staging of authorship. The method is intermedial production analysis: the making-of is treated as evidence about how adaptation is represented and carried out [11].

The finding is that the adaptive relation is broader than a one-to-one dialogue between a book and a film. Satrapi's lived experience, public persona, prior work, co-direction, and the contributions of other creative agents all enter the new object. The case does not make making-of documentaries transparent records; such documents are themselves edited and promotional. Its value lies in revealing processes and agents that a finished-text comparison can overlook [11].

### The Walking Dead Shows How New Characters Protect Playable Uncertainty

Stang conducts a comparative analysis of Telltale Games' licensed adaptations, with principal attention to The Walking Dead [12]. The article examines source relations, game form, and player agency. It argues that using main characters created for the game rather than requiring players to reproduce the fixed destinies of principal source characters helps generate a powerful illusion of agency [12].

The case identifies a design solution to a cross-media conflict. Recognition of the storyworld supplies stakes and expectations, but new characters permit decisions whose outcomes are not already known from the source. The game remains authored and constrained, so the evidence does not establish unlimited agency. It shows that adaptation can preserve a world's social and ethical pressures while changing the narrative unit that the audience controls [12].

### The Last of Us Shows That Reverse Transfer Requires a New Account of Action

Spence analyzes Naughty Dog's games and HBO's television adaptation of The Last of Us, focusing on storytelling and violence [13]. The method is close comparative analysis of the forms and their narrative techniques. The study argues that the television adaptation used television's distinct affordances to adapt core themes involving ludic and real-world violence rather than merely reproducing game action [13].

The transfer makes a structural problem visible. In a game, the player performs required actions within a rule system; on television, viewers observe a fixed sequence. Reproducing the same number of violent encounters would not reproduce the relation among action, repetition, consequence, and responsibility. The case supports functional rather than surface comparison: analysts should ask how the new form rebuilds the thematic work previously carried by play [13].

### Hollywood Remaking Connects Textual Change to Cultural Memory

Loock's historical study examines remakes, sequels, and franchises across Hollywood production rather than treating repetition as a recent decline in originality [15]. The method joins film history, industrial context, formal comparison, and cultural interpretation. The book argues that serial repetition and variation negotiate stability and change, affect how industries and audiences imagine themselves, foster film-historical knowledge, and support generational belonging [15].

This evidence shows why commercial and cultural explanations should not be separated into rival accounts. A familiar property can be an economic resource and a site of memory at the same time. The finding does not imply that every remake preserves history or creates belonging. It provides a longitudinal framework for asking which past is selected, how it is revised, and how renewed circulation changes the archive [15].

### Reception Can Create Relations That Production Did Not Announce

Sullivan develops a reception-led theory through an extended comparison of Lady Bird and The Tempest, even though Lady Bird is not conventionally announced as an adaptation of Shakespeare's play [14]. The method combines theoretical argument with close reading. The finding is that an audience member's sustained comparison can evolve the meaning of both works and create a meaningful adaptive relation without a formal claim of source descent [14].

This case sets a useful boundary. Reception is not merely a final opinion applied to an already complete relationship; it can constitute the relationship. Yet not every resemblance becomes persuasive adaptation. A reception-led claim must demonstrate sustained structural or thematic productivity rather than list accidental similarities. The author's synthesis is that declared genealogy and experienced relation should be recorded separately: the first is a production fact, while the second is an interpretive argument [3][14].

### Convergence Evidence Distinguishes Retelling From Expansion

Jenkins defines transmedia storytelling through coordinated, distinctive contributions across channels, while Harvey organizes a book-length analysis around adaptation, narrative expansion, play, canon, and memory in science-fiction and fantasy storyworlds [16][17]. Their methods combine conceptual definition with comparative franchise cases. The shared finding is that movement across media is not one operation. Some works retell recognizable material, while others add pieces that depend on a distributed storyworld [16][17].

The distinction explains why a franchise can simultaneously contain close adaptations, original extensions, promotional repetitions, and fan appropriations. It also sets a test: if a work primarily translates an existing narrative into another medium, adaptation is the stronger description; if it adds an independent contribution to a coordinated whole, transmedia expansion is stronger. The categories remain analytical tools, not natural labels, and mixed cases should be described as mixed [16][17].

## Implications

### For Analysis: Compare Functions Before Counting Changes

A rigorous comparison should begin by defining the source relation and the unit of analysis. Is the new work announced as an adaptation, inferred from substantial borrowing, or constructed by reception? Is it retelling a particular work, appropriating an archetype, remediating a style, or expanding a storyworld? Answering these questions prevents an analyst from treating every similarity as descent or every franchise extension as adaptation [3][9][14][16][17].

The next step is a functional map. Record plot and character changes, but also identify viewpoint, access to thought, temporality, spatial organization, embodiment, sound, performance, seriality, rules, agency, and audience knowledge. For each major change, ask what the source feature accomplished and what now performs that work. The author's synthesis is that this method turns "what was cut?" into a stronger sequence: what function disappeared, what replaced it, who gained or lost access, and what new interpretation followed [1][5][6][7].

Evidence types must remain separate. Formal analysis establishes features of the works. Production records establish decisions, constraints, and declared intentions. Rights and release data establish institutional conditions. Interviews, surveys, criticism, and behavior establish different aspects of reception. Revenue or reach establishes circulation, not agreement or artistic success. Murray's industrial account and Mousavi's production case show why textual evidence alone is incomplete, while Sullivan shows why production evidence alone cannot exhaust adaptive meaning [10][11][14].

### For Creators: Translate Effects, Not Surfaces

Creators can use fidelity as a design question rather than a promise of sameness. First identify the source's essential functions: perhaps uncertainty, intimacy, moral complicity, comic timing, a pattern of revelation, a social conflict, or a world rule. Then identify which resources of the target medium can produce an analogous or deliberately different effect. Harold's distinction suggests that thematic continuity may require visible change when media resources differ [5].

This approach legitimizes omission and invention while making them accountable. Compression should preserve or consciously redirect causal and ethical weight. Expansion should state which gaps it fills and avoid presenting invention as recovered fact. A game adaptation should define the actions through which players learn the conflict; a screen adaptation of a game should identify what will replace performed action; a stage adaptation should make co-presence and spatial limitation purposeful; a serial should use duration for more than postponed resolution [1][6][7][12][13].

The worst creative failure is not deviation. It is unexamined equivalence: copying a visible surface while losing the relation that made it meaningful. A scene may reproduce dialogue yet change who possesses knowledge; a game may reproduce a film's locations while converting every conflict into combat; a television series may expand a novel while flattening its ambiguity into explanation. The author's synthesis is that an adaptation brief should include a function register listing what must remain stable, what may change, what the target medium uniquely contributes, and what evidence would show that the translation failed [1][5][7].

### For Cultural Translation: Treat Relocation as a Claim About Power

Moving a story across languages, nations, periods, religions, races, genders, or classes is not neutral modernization. It changes which institutions are recognizable, which stereotypes are activated, which histories can be assumed, and which audiences are addressed. Sanders's account places global and local dimensions, cultural politics, and alternative viewpoints inside adaptation and appropriation [9]. Embodiment and setting therefore require research beyond the source text.

Creators and analysts should ask who has authority to make the move, who participates in production, which community bears representational risk, and whether local history is structurally integrated or used as decorative difference. A declared homage does not settle an appropriation dispute, and a new audience does not erase the source community. The author's synthesis is that responsible cultural translation combines formal comparison with provenance, consultation, historical evidence, and explicit attribution [9][10][11].

### For Audiences and Critics: Make Fidelity Claims Precise

Audience disagreement becomes more informative when critics name the dimension under dispute. A viewer can say that an adaptation preserves major events but changes the theme, reproduces visual iconography but alters point of view, or abandons plot fidelity while preserving an experience of uncertainty. This vocabulary turns "betrayal" from a conclusion into a proposition that can be tested [4][5].

Knowing and unknowing audiences should not be treated as more and less competent versions of one public. Source knowledge creates the pleasure and burden of comparison; lack of source knowledge may reveal whether the adaptation functions independently. Later encounters can also reverse the direction: an audience may read the source through images and performances established by the adaptation [1][14]. Critics should disclose their source knowledge and avoid speaking for a universal audience.

Online controversy is evidence of visible discourse, not a representative measure of reception. Platform ranking, fandom organization, marketing, and prior attachment can amplify particular voices. A stronger account distinguishes professional reviews, audience surveys, interviews, behavioral data, fan production, and long-term cultural references. The author's synthesis is that reception claims need the same population, period, and evidence discipline as other empirical claims [10][14][15].

### For Education: Use Adaptation to Teach Media Literacy

Adaptation provides a controlled comparison because some narrative material remains recognizable while medium and context change. Students can map what each form asks an audience to read, see, hear, infer, wait for, or do. Ryan's media-conscious narratology and Bolter and Grusin's remediation framework support a curriculum in which media are compared through their actual operations rather than ranked by cultural prestige [6][7][8].

A strong assignment separates at least four layers. Students first describe formal changes without evaluating them. They then infer functions and test those inferences against production evidence. Next they study reception from more than one audience position. Finally they state a cultural claim about what the new version makes newly visible or authoritative. This sequence limits unsupported claims about authorial intention and prevents fidelity preference from masquerading as analysis [3][4][10][11].

Games make the method especially visible because rules expose the difference between represented and performed action. Comparing a fixed scene with a playable situation can show how responsibility changes when a user must act. Conversely, adapting a game to television shows how performance, editing, and serial structure can rebuild themes after direct control is removed [12][13]. The exercise teaches that agency is designed and constrained, not simply present or absent.

### For Archives and Platforms: Preserve Version Identity and Provenance

Adaptation can broaden access to an older work, but it can also make one later version so visible that other versions recede. Loock's account of remaking and Harvey's account of transmedia memory show that repetition, canon, and generational belonging are connected [15][17]. Preservation should therefore include relationships among versions, not only the survival of isolated files.

Archives, publishers, and platforms should retain dates, creators, edition and version labels, declared source relations, and links among source, adaptation, restoration, remake, expansion, and derivative commentary. Search and recommendation interfaces should avoid collapsing works with the same title or presenting the most recent authorized version as the origin. These are the author's implications from the memory literature, not tested guarantees of neutral discovery [15][17].

Provenance also protects interpretive plurality. When scripts, drafts, production records, making-of materials, interviews, and audience traces remain available, later researchers can distinguish intention from outcome and official narrative from contested reception. Mousavi's case demonstrates the analytical value of an intermediary production document, while Murray shows why digital paratexts and amateur production belong to the record [10][11].

### For Franchise Governance: Keep Adaptation and Expansion Legible

Franchises should distinguish retellings from new canonical additions, alternate continuities, remakes, and promotional summaries. Jenkins's and Harvey's frameworks show that audiences make different knowledge commitments to a coordinated extension than to an adaptation of material they already know [16][17]. Clear labels do not eliminate disagreement, but they keep institutional claims auditable.

Canon decisions should also be treated as reversible governance rather than erasure. A new adaptation may become the dominant public reference without making earlier versions culturally nonexistent. Version timelines, preserved editions, and transparent continuity statements allow production coordination while acknowledging plural memory. The author's synthesis is that the single worst outcome is a closed loop in which ownership, platform visibility, and official canon combine to make one commercially current interpretation appear to be the only history [15][17].

### A Comparative Adaptation Protocol

The following protocol is the author's synthesis from the cited sources. First, identify the declared and inferred source relations [3][9][14]. Second, classify the operation as adaptation, appropriation, remediation, transmedia expansion, or a documented combination [8][9][16][17]. Third, inventory continuities and changes across plot, theme, viewpoint, style, world, embodiment, temporality, and audience relation [1][5][7]. Fourth, map the target medium's actual affordances without treating them as fixed essence [6][7][8]. Fifth, separate compression, expansion, and relocation, recording whose access and agency change. Sixth, reconstruct authorship and institutional constraints with production evidence [10][11]. Seventh, distinguish intended, textual, and received meanings [3][14]. Eighth, compare reception among knowing and unknowing audiences where evidence permits [1]. Ninth, audit cultural-memory effects: visibility, provenance, canon, and displacement [15][17]. Tenth, state the claim narrowly enough that another analyst could identify contrary evidence.

This protocol replaces the question "Was it faithful?" with a set of answerable questions. What relation was preserved, by which formal means, under whose authority, for which audience, and with what consequence for cultural memory? Adaptation matters because every new version does more than repeat a story: it proposes what that story is, what it can become, and who may inherit it [1][5][9][15].
