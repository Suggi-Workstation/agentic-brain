# Recommendation Systems Co-Produce Cultural Visibility Rather Than Merely Predict Taste

Ranking, recommendation, search, autoplay, and personalized feeds decide which cultural artifacts become easy to encounter inside catalogs too large for anyone to inspect. These systems do not act alone: platform objectives, audience behavior, creator adaptation, human curation, advertising, moderation, rights, and inherited popularity form feedback loops that can widen discovery or repeatedly privilege what is already visible [1][5][11]. The central cultural claim is therefore relational: algorithms participate in gatekeeping by allocating exposure, but they neither create taste from nothing nor determine reception without users and institutions [7][9][13].

## Background

Cultural gatekeeping predates machine learning. Editors, critics, broadcasters, cinema programmers, record labels, retailers, festival selectors, and social networks have long reduced an abundant field of possible works to a manageable set. Recommendation systems continue this selection function while changing its speed, scale, personalization, and observability. Helberger, Karppinen, and D'Acunto define exposure diversity as the content audiences actually select rather than everything formally available, and they emphasize that commercial and strategic choices sit behind decisions about what is prioritized or excluded [1]. Bucher's study of Facebook's former EdgeRank system made the same issue visible in an early social-feed setting: software did not merely transmit posts but organized a scarce field of visibility in which interaction helped produce further visibility [2]. These sources concern different platforms and periods, but both establish that distribution architecture is part of cultural mediation.

Digital catalogs intensified the selection problem. A streaming service can make far more films, programs, songs, games, or creator videos technically available than can fit on one screen or inside one person's attention. Netflix engineers described their 2015 service not as one algorithm but as a collection of rankers, row selectors, search systems, and evidence-selection processes; they reported that recommendations then influenced about 80 percent of hours streamed and that experiments were evaluated partly through retention and medium-term engagement [3]. This is primary evidence about Netflix's design and business purpose at that time, not a universal measure for other services or the present platform. Its cultural importance lies in the location of choice: a catalog title can exist while receiving little practical exposure if it is placed low, represented weakly, or never offered in a relevant row [3][13].

Music streaming joined algorithmic ranking to older forms of playlisting, radio, retail display, and editorial promotion. Maaso and Spilker describe six hybrid gatekeeping mechanisms produced by combinations of automated analysis, interface design, human curation, aggregate use, and platform management [5]. Their evidence combines platform and interface analysis, business and stakeholder materials, and user data. One bounded comparison counted only the items reachable on small mobile front pages without search in May 2020, while the wider study drew on projects spanning several platforms and years [5]. The finding does not show that every listener saw the same items or that one mechanism caused a particular taste. It shows that an interface structures the route from an effectively immense catalog to a finite set of convenient choices.

Social feeds extended recommendation beyond previously followed accounts. TikTok's For You feed made content-level recommendation a default mode of cultural encounter, while Instagram, YouTube, and other services incorporated related feed, short-video, and autoplay systems. Boeker and Urman used paired sock-puppet accounts to test language, location, following, liking, and video-view rate on TikTok in 2021; all tested factors affected the feed [6]. Their abstract lists following, liking, and video-view rate in descending order, but the detailed results and conclusion place following first, video-view rate second, and liking third, with the latter two close [6]. The result documents personalization pathways under one noisy, historically bounded web audit. It does not show that the same ordering remains current, that one action permanently fixes a user's feed, or that recommendation determines what the user believes.

The creator side changed at the same time. Cotter's thematic analysis of online discussions among aspiring and established Instagram influencers from September 2017 through January 2018 found that observed users treated visibility as a game whose partly hidden rules had to be inferred through discussion, trial, analytics, and improvised tests [7]. Some emphasized authentic relationship-building; others discussed reciprocal engagement, follow-unfollow tactics, and other ways of making popularity legible to the system [7]. Cotter did not recruit experimental participants or report a count of groups, posts, or distinct influencers, so the study identifies interpretive patterns rather than prevalence. Its evidence rejects a simple picture of creators passively obeying code: people interpret platforms, exchange folklore, and act on beliefs that need not perfectly describe the underlying system.

Concerns about diversity emerged because recommendation can convert past attention into future exposure. Popular items generate interaction data, high interaction can increase predicted relevance, increased exposure generates more interaction, and the enlarged record can justify still more exposure [2][3][14]. Yet the opposite effect is also possible. A recommender can help a listener find an obscure artist, connect a viewer with a foreign series, or introduce a creator without an inherited audience. Helberger and colleagues therefore reject technological inevitability: recommender systems can be designed to support autonomy, deliberative breadth, or minority visibility, although those goals conflict and require different definitions and measurements of diversity [1].

Regulation and cultural policy increasingly treat discoverability as a public issue. UNESCO's 2022 report, based predominantly on reports from 94 parties to the 2005 Convention, identified platform concentration, digital-access gaps, unsustainable creator remuneration, and business models that do not favor discovery of diverse content [11]. UNESCO's 2026 successor report updates the policy baseline and treats availability as necessary but insufficient for discoverability while emphasizing platform consolidation, algorithmic opacity, data gaps, and accountability [17]. The European Union's Digital Services Act requires qualifying providers of online platforms that use recommender systems to explain their main parameters and any existing user options for influencing them. Its additional risk, audit, non-profiled-option, and data-access duties apply to designated very large online platforms and very large online search engines; Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2050 supplies the procedures and portal for vetted-researcher access under Article 40 [12][21]. These measures do not prescribe one culturally correct feed. They make objectives, alternatives, risks, and evidence more contestable within defined legal scopes.

This topic remains within pop-culture analysis by asking how visibility changes the cultural field: which works become recognizable, which styles creators imitate, which communities encounter one another, and which artifacts acquire the status of a hit, niche, canon, or trend. Recommender engineering belongs primarily to technology; platform market power belongs primarily to industries-sectors; content-moderation doctrine belongs primarily to law-regulation; and individual persuasion effects belong partly to psychology-behavior. Those adjacent questions enter here only where they explain the production, circulation, interpretation, and diversity of popular culture [5][7][11][12].

## Core Concepts

### Gatekeeping Is the Allocation of Exposure

Availability, offered exposure, selection, consumption, and influence are different stages. Availability means that a work can in principle be accessed in a catalog or platform. This topic uses offered exposure for an impression or placement that a person can plausibly encounter, then selection for clicking, playing, or choosing it. Helberger and colleagues use the term exposure diversity more narrowly for content audiences actually select rather than all content available [1]. Consumption adds continuation, completion, or return, while cultural influence is a stronger claim requiring evidence that the encounter affected memory, preference, practice, identity, or institutional action. UNESCO's 2026 report similarly treats availability as necessary but insufficient for discoverability [17]. A large catalog can therefore coexist with narrow offered exposure, and a varied slate can coexist with repetitive selection.

Algorithmic gatekeeping differs from a single editor's decision because it is continuous and individualized. A ranked page is assembled for a person, session, device, region, or inferred context; the person reacts; and the next page may change. Yet individualization should not be confused with unlimited uniqueness. Collaborative systems group people through shared behavior, content-based systems group artifacts through represented features, popularity systems reuse aggregate attention, and platform teams choose shared catalogs, objectives, and presentation rules [3][14]. Netflix's 2015 account, for example, describes a move from fixed row-position business rules toward a template-free personalized row-selection algorithm rather than a timeless fixed layout [3]. The author's synthesis is that recommendation produces patterned personalization: people receive different slates, but those slates are built from common catalogs, models, metrics, and institutional decisions.

### A Recommender Is a Pipeline, Not an Autonomous Agent

The author's generalized pipeline separates several stages: candidate generation retrieves a pool; scoring estimates a target; ranking orders candidates; re-ranking may add diversity or other constraints; and interface design determines rows, labels, artwork, previews, defaults, and search friction. Netflix's 2015 account directly documents multiple rankers, page generation, row selection, search, and evidence-selection systems [3]. Zhao and colleagues separately survey collaborative, content-based, fairness, and diversity objectives, including diversity-aware training and re-ranking [14]. Safety, rights, language, previews, and autoplay are broader institutional examples, not a single pipeline documented by either source.

The target matters because a proxy becomes a cultural incentive when it controls exposure. Netflix documents retention and medium-term engagement as business targets, while Zhao and colleagues survey utility objectives such as recall and click-through rate together with relevance-diversity trade-offs [3][14]. The author's hypotheses are that a click target may reward immediate recognizability, a completion target may reward easily completed works, and a retention target may favor a different sequence; these are mechanisms to test, not findings established by either source. No single proxy equals cultural value. The author's synthesis is that the objective function acts as an editorial constitution: it does not write every artifact, but it defines which observable responses count when the platform allocates future attention.

Interface and automation cannot be separated cleanly. Maaso and Spilker show that interface design, front-page limits, flow prolongation, human curation, and automated processes combine to create pathways through music [5]. A title in the first visible row, a track that begins automatically, and a clip that requires a deliberate search impose different friction even if all are technically available. The cultural unit of analysis is therefore the recommendation pathway: model, policy rule, visual presentation, default action, and the user's available exit.

### Taste Is Elicited and Shaped, Not Simply Read

Recommenders use past behavior as evidence of preference, but behavior occurs inside an already selected environment. A listener cannot click a track never shown, and Netflix's own offline evaluation discussion recognizes the counterfactual problem of learning from behavior generated under prior recommendations [3]. The author's illustrative hypotheses are that autoplay can turn continuation into a weak signal and that inspecting an outrageous clip need not indicate endorsement; the cited sources do not directly test those examples. When behavior under one arrangement becomes training data for the next, the platform does not merely discover taste. It elicits partial expressions of taste under specific conditions and uses those expressions to reorganize future choice [3][4].

This does not imply that users lack agency. Frey's user-centered study of Netflix places algorithmic suggestions among critics, trailers, word of mouth, memory, marketing, and other choice aids; he argues that viewers neither trust nor use recommendations as completely as deterministic accounts assume [13]. Guess and colleagues similarly show why exposure cannot be collapsed into opinion: replacing Facebook and Instagram's default ranked feeds with reverse-chronological feeds for three months changed time spent, activity, and the composition of exposure, but did not significantly change the measured political attitudes, knowledge, or polarization outcomes [9]. A recommendation changes an opportunity to encounter; its downstream meaning depends on selection, attention, interpretation, repetition, and competing influences.

### Feedback Loops Turn Popularity Into an Input and an Outcome

Popularity bias occurs when already popular items receive disproportionate recommendation exposure. Zhao and colleagues' survey explains the general loop: greater exposure generates attention and consumption, which can enlarge popularity and thereby strengthen the item's future recommendation position [14]. Kowald and coauthors tested a deliberately selected subset of the public LFM-1b data containing 1,755,361 user-artist interactions from 3,000 Last.fm users and 352,805 artists [10]. Artist recommendation frequency increased with popularity for the five non-random approaches; the paper's results text says the correlation was absent for Random and weaker for nonnegative matrix factorization [10]. Users in the deliberately sampled low-mainstreaminess group received significantly worse predictions from the four personalized algorithms evaluated for error [10]. The study concerns an offline subset and tested algorithms, not the live ranking of every music service, but it demonstrates how aggregate error can conceal poorer service for users whose histories favor less-mainstream artists.

Feedback loops can support discovery as well as concentration, but the mechanism and its distribution require direct evidence. Netflix's 2015 paper reports that personalization spread viewing over more titles than an unpersonalized system, while two 2026 company-linked working papers report gains concentrated in moderately popular rather than deepest-tail titles [3][19][20]. The author's broader hypothesis is that exposure can supply a previously obscure artifact with enough data to find other receptive users. The relevant question is therefore which starting conditions, exploration rules, and constraints determine who can enter a feedback loop. Kowald's offline evaluation did not test dynamic feedback or exploration, so it cannot establish that an exploration budget will correct inherited visibility [10].

Path dependence follows because early signals affect later opportunity. Two comparable works can diverge after one receives a prominent placement, external publicity, or an initial burst of engagement. Maaso and Spilker report that streaming mechanisms can influence composing, production, and playlist or album selection, while Cotter records an influencer complaint that perceived ranking demands encouraged repetitive images [5][7]. The broader list of possible adaptations in length, hooks, thumbnails, genre labels, sounds, and release schedules is the author's hypothesis, not a measured finding of either study. The proposed recursive mechanism is that selection can affect production, production changes the candidate field, and later selection occurs within a field already reshaped by earlier gates.

### Cultural Gatekeeping Is Hybrid

Calling a system algorithmic can conceal human and institutional gates. Rights determine which works enter a catalog and in which territories. Metadata affects whether a work can be classified. Editors assemble playlists or featured collections. Advertising and marketing can create attention that later appears as an engagement signal. Moderators remove, restrict, or label material. Users follow, search, skip, share, and abandon, while creators adapt to visible metrics. Maaso and Spilker's hybrid-gatekeeping framework is useful because it treats automated coding, interface design, curation, and user choice as a coupled process rather than asking whether a human or machine made the final choice [5]. Some elements in this broader inventory extend beyond the specific mechanisms their music-streaming study measured.

The hybrid view also prevents algorithms from becoming autonomous villains. A model trained on an unequal cultural record can reproduce that record without an explicit instruction to discriminate. A diversity intervention can fail if rights, metadata, language support, or promotion exclude the relevant work before ranking begins. Conversely, a human-curated list can reproduce popularity or institutional bias. The author's assessment is that accountability should follow control: catalog owners answer for acquisition and metadata, platform teams for objectives and interface, moderators for policy and process, creators for deliberate manipulation, and researchers for the limits of their measurements [3][5][10][11].

### Visibility Disciplines Creators

Bucher described a threat of invisibility: participation was governed through the possibility of disappearing from Facebook's default feed [2]. The evidence was intentionally exploratory: it used one personal profile over two months, important parts of EdgeRank remained unknown, and the dual-feed design studied had changed by September 2011 [2]. Cotter later found that observed influencers responded to uncertain visibility by learning perceived rules, comparing tactics, and treating engagement and follower growth as both evidence and generators of visibility [7]. These historically bounded studies do not establish how a current feed ranks content, but they show that creators may devote labor to an inferred evaluator even without complete system knowledge.

This creates an algorithmic imaginary: shared beliefs about what the system rewards. Those beliefs may be accurate, obsolete, platform-promoted, or folklore. They still matter if creators change behavior in response. Cotter's observed influencers performed informal A/B tests, debated authenticity, used engagement groups, and distinguished relational from simulated influence [7]. The author's hypothesis is that opacity can standardize production because imitating visible success may feel safer than testing forms whose value the system may not register. Cotter documents one complaint about repetitive images but does not establish a general standardization effect.

Strategic gaming is endogenous to systems whose measured signals affect visibility. Cotter documents bots, reciprocal engagement groups, and follow-unfollow practices used to produce algorithmically legible popularity [7]. These cases do not establish a general taxonomy of watch-time manipulation, misleading thumbnails, trend imitation, or engagement bait. The author's proposed boundary is whether a tactic improves the cultural encounter or only manufactures a proxy while degrading it. A clearer title or more accessible opening can serve an audience; simulated interaction can raise a measured signal without demonstrating the relationship or value that the signal was intended to represent.

### Filter Bubbles Are Hypotheses, Not a Universal Outcome

A filter bubble claim requires a baseline and a measured dimension. Compared with what would exposure be narrower: a chronological feed, direct search, broadcast schedule, editor's list, friends' choices, or random sampling? Which diversity matters: genre, ideology, language, nationality, gender, race, creator size, style, or source? Over what period, and at what stage: recommendations, clicks, completed consumption, or durable taste? Helberger and colleagues show that diversity serves different purposes under autonomy, deliberative, and adversarial models, so one diversity score cannot resolve every normative question [1].

Empirical findings are correspondingly mixed. Anderson and colleagues analyzed Spotify data from more than 100 million premium users, using a 40-dimensional embedding trained on co-occurrence in more than 850 million predominantly user-created playlists [4]. Their main behavioral window covered 28 days in July 2019, with corresponding 28-day windows over the preceding year for temporal analyses [4]. They found that programmed listening was associated with lower musical diversity than user-directed listening, while greater diversity was strongly associated with conversion and retention. Programmed listening included personalized sources but also editorial playlists and charts, so it cannot be equated with personalized recommendation alone [4]. The observational comparison does not prove that programming caused the diversity difference because listeners select modes differently. A one-week randomized experiment among 540,000 free users tested stream and skip responses to alternative ranking methods within seven decade playlists; it did not test whether recommendation caused diversity to rise or fall [4].

Haroon and colleagues trained 100,000 signed-out YouTube sock puppets across five ideological categories and collected 15,323,930 watched videos in training and testing [8]. Home-page recommendations were predominantly congenial for partisan puppets. For very-right puppets, the share of very-right recommendations rose by 37 percent from the start to depth 20 of an autoplay trail; a comparable progression was not reported for the other groups [8]. Ideological slant among congenial videos rose slightly for the very-left and very-right groups, not as a single average over all recommendations. Recommendations from a compiled list of problematic channels rose from 1.2 percent at depth one to 2.5 percent at depth ten and were encountered by 36.1 percent of puppets [8]. The audit inferred ideology from Twitter-audience landmarks, list coverage declined deeper in trails, and the puppets always followed recommendations. It therefore estimates one historical system pathway, not what ordinary viewers choose, believe, or encounter today.

Guess and colleagues provide the complementary downstream test. Their randomized three-month intervention replaced default ranked feeds with reverse chronology for consenting Facebook and Instagram users. The change reduced platform use and activity and altered exposure, including increasing political and untrustworthy content while changing other content categories, yet it produced no significant changes in the measured political attitudes or knowledge outcomes [9]. Science errata published in 2024 and 2026 corrected Instagram untrustworthy-source, carousel, engagement, and content-classification details; the authors state that treatment-effect estimates, Facebook data, and survey outcomes should not be affected [15][16]. Together, these studies show why deterministic language fails: algorithms can measurably change offered exposure without producing one uniform trajectory or a detectable short-term change in the measured attitudes.

### Cultural Diversity Has Several Dimensions

Zhao and colleagues distinguish individual diversity within one person's list from aggregate diversity across a system and connect aggregate diversity with provider exposure [14]. UNESCO adds language, locality, and discoverability as cultural-policy concerns [11][17]. This topic adds two explicitly author-defined dimensions: commonality, or whether members of a society still encounter shared works, and serendipity, or a recommendation that is both relevant and unexpectedly useful. These added concepts are analytical proposals, not measures established by Zhao's survey. Improving one dimension can reduce another: complete personalization may increase individual relevance while reducing common exposure, and equal exposure may reduce fit when catalog groups differ greatly in size or audience interest.

Cultural diversity is also not equivalent to random variety. A list of unrelated artifacts can be diverse by distance but unusable to a person. Repeating only global hits can create commonality while marginalizing local expression. Promoting minority work without context can produce brief exposure but little understanding. Helberger and colleagues therefore connect diversity design to purpose and user autonomy [1]. The author's synthesis is that a defensible system must state which diversity it seeks, why that dimension matters, how it is measured, which trade-off it accepts, and how users can contest the result.

### Transparency Requires More Than Publishing a Formula

A complete source-code release would not by itself explain a recommendation. Production systems depend on changing models, training data, experiments, business rules, inventories, and user histories. Useful transparency is layered: users need intelligible main parameters and material controls; creators need enforceable eligibility and manipulation rules; researchers and regulators need access proportionate to the question and compatible with privacy, confidentiality, and security. The DSA assigns different duties to different actors. Article 27 covers qualifying online platforms using recommender systems; Articles 34, 37, 38, and 40 add risk assessment, independent audit, non-profiled alternatives, and restricted data access for designated VLOPs and VLOSEs [12]. The 2025 delegated regulation creates procedures, a portal, and service data catalogues for vetted-researcher requests under Article 40, but it does not guarantee every researcher every impression or ranking record [21].

Transparency also creates risks. Exact tactical details can support spam or manipulation; raw data can expose private behavior; and a simple explanation can falsely imply that a dynamic system follows one fixed recipe. The author's assessment is that the objective is contestability rather than total legibility. A person should be able to identify why a class of content is being suggested, choose a materially different mode, report a harmful pattern, and obtain a reasoned response. An independent researcher should be able to test exposure distributions and changes without accepting a platform's public narrative as evidence [7][12][14].

## Evidence

### Spotify Shows a Tension Between Immediate Relevance and Long-Term Breadth

Anderson and colleagues trained a 40-dimensional song embedding on co-occurrence in more than 850 million predominantly user-created Spotify playlists and applied it to a main 28-day interaction window containing more than 100 million premium users [4]. Corresponding 28-day windows from July 2018 through June 2019 supported temporal analyses. They separated user-driven listening, such as search and self-selection, from programmed listening, which included personalized playlists and radio but also editorial playlists and charts [4]. User-driven consumption was typically more diverse; users who became more diverse over time shifted toward user-driven listening; and higher consumption diversity was strongly associated with conversion and retention [4].

The method has unusual scale and uses behavior rather than stated preference, but interpretation must preserve its design. The programmed-versus-user-driven comparison is observational, so unmeasured user motives can explain part of the difference. The embedding operationalizes musical distance through playlist co-occurrence rather than every possible cultural dimension, and the conversion and retention associations are not causal estimates [4]. A separate one-week randomized test involved 540,000 free users and seven decade playlists; it compared stream and skip outcomes under popularity, relevance, and learned ranking, finding a larger stream lift from relevance ranking among specialists than generalists [4]. That experiment measured short-term response, not whether a recommender caused consumption diversity to rise or fall.

### Last.fm Tests Popularity Bias Across Users and Algorithms

Kowald and coauthors selected 1,000 users with the lowest mainstreaminess, 1,000 around the median, and 1,000 with the highest mainstreaminess from the public LFM-1b data, yielding 1,755,361 user-artist interactions and 352,805 artists [10]. They compared Random, MostPopular, UserItemAvg, UserKNN, UserKNNAvg, and nonnegative matrix factorization under an 80/20 split and top-10 recommendation setup. The results text reports a positive correlation between artist popularity and recommendation frequency for the five non-random approaches, with no such correlation for Random and a weaker effect for nonnegative matrix factorization [10]. Except for Random and nonnegative matrix factorization under the group-average-popularity measure, the tested approaches recommended artists that were too popular relative to profiles in all three sampled groups. The deliberately sampled low-mainstreaminess group also received significantly higher error from the four personalized approaches [10].

This reproducibility study makes distributional differences visible. Aggregate error can conceal higher error for a sampled user group, while less-popular artists can remain underexposed even when some users' histories favor them [10]. The equal-sized low-, median-, and high-mainstreaminess groups were deliberately selected and do not establish how common any group is in the full Last.fm population. The offline evaluation also does not reproduce current production systems, catalogs, interfaces, dynamic feedback, or exploration. Its evidentiary role is bounded mechanism testing rather than a claim about every live music service.

### Netflix Demonstrates That Presentation and Business Goals Belong to the System

Gomez-Uribe and Hunt's primary account describes Netflix's 2015 recommender as multiple algorithms assembled across the homepage, search, rows, rankings, and evidence-selection mechanisms [3]. They reported that the system influenced about 80 percent of streamed hours, while search accounted for the remainder through its own algorithms. Netflix evaluated changes with offline historical data and A/B tests, with improved member retention and medium-term engagement as central targets [3]. The authors also explained that intuition about a better algorithm often failed when tested, which made controlled experiments the operational decision rule.

The paper documents a production system from one company at one historical moment, and its authors wrote from inside Netflix. It therefore establishes design, objectives, and reported scale more directly than it establishes cultural consequences. The cultural inference is nevertheless important: ranking and evidence selection jointly shape not only which film or series appears but which artwork, explanation, or row makes it appear relevant [3]. Frey's later user-centered work limits the inference by showing that viewers combine platform suggestions with word of mouth, criticism, memory, marketing, and other filters and do not uniformly trust recommendation aids [13]. The case supports co-production of choice, not algorithmic sovereignty.

Two company-linked 2026 working papers add current causal and structural evidence while remaining unreviewed preprints. Aridor and colleagues report an experiment with 8.5 million Netflix users in which improved recommendation quality increased consumption and reliance on recommendations while shifting recommendations and plays away from the most popular titles toward a larger middle tail [19]. Zielnicki and colleagues estimate that replacing Netflix's current system with matrix factorization or popularity ranking would reduce engagement by 4 percent and 12 percent, respectively, and reduce consumption diversity; their abstract attributes most added consumption to effective targeting and the largest gains to mid-popularity rather than very niche titles [20]. These results qualify a simple concentration story, but company access, unpublished peer review, model assumptions, and platform specificity constrain generalization.

### TikTok Audits Isolate Personalization Signals

Boeker and Urman created paired sock-puppet users that were identical except for one tested factor, then compared metadata from posts appearing in their For You feeds [6]. Thirty-nine scenarios completed successfully between late June and mid-August 2021, covering 30,436 posts, 34,905 hashtags, 21,278 creators, and 20,302 sounds. Language, location, following, liking, and video-view rate all affected recommendations. The abstract orders the last three as following, liking, then video-view rate, but section 4.6 and the conclusion order them as following, video-view rate, then liking, with the latter two close [6]. This topic follows the detailed results while preserving the internal inconsistency.

The paired design improves attribution under the observed setup, but 11 control scenarios found substantial divergence even between nominally identical feeds [6]. The audit used web-based bots, excluded failed scenarios, covered a short 2021 window, and noted possible platform releases during observation. It does not measure a human user's interpretation, long-term cultural development, every undisclosed signal, or whether deliberately selecting contrary material reliably resets an inferred interest. Its bounded contribution is that tested actions and contextual attributes measurably changed subsequent feeds under those conditions [6].

### Creator Research Shows How Ranking Changes Cultural Production

Cotter conducted a thematic analysis of existing influencer discussions in closed Facebook groups and linked online materials between September 2017 and January 2018 [7]. The observed influencers described learning Instagram's rules through shared advice, trial, analytics, and improvised A/B tests. They agreed that engagement and followers affected visibility but split between relational tactics centered on authentic interaction and simulated tactics such as reciprocal engagement groups and follow-unfollow strategies [7]. The study does not verify their theories about Instagram, report a count of groups or distinct influencers, or estimate tactic prevalence. It measures how those theories organized labor and strategy.

That distinction is the evidence. Creators do not need complete knowledge of a ranking system for the system to affect culture; they need only believe that some forms are rewarded and act on that belief. Cotter found that culture shaped the response as much as code: existing ideals of authenticity and entrepreneurship supplied different ways to interpret the same perceived rules [7]. The evidence therefore contradicts both technological determinism and a neutral-tool account. Ranking structured the field of possible success, while creator communities translated uncertain signals into conventions and tactics.

### YouTube and Meta Separate Exposure From Downstream Effects

Haroon and colleagues' YouTube audit used 100,000 signed-out sock puppets across five ideology categories and collected 15,323,930 watched videos in training and testing [8]. Home-page recommendations were predominantly congenial for partisan puppets. Congeniality increased down up-next trails for the very-right group, whose very-right share rose 37 percent by depth 20, but not for the very-left group [8]. Ideological slant among congenial videos rose from 0.75 to 0.79 for very-left puppets and from 0.77 to 0.79 for very-right puppets; no corresponding progression was detected for left, center, or right puppets. Listed problematic-channel recommendations were a small share, peaking at 2.5 percent at depth ten, yet at least one appeared for 36.1 percent of puppets [8]. Ideology was inferred through Twitter-audience landmarks, listed-channel coverage declined deeper in trails, and the bots always accepted up-next recommendations, so the audit cannot establish ordinary human selection or belief effects.

Guess and colleagues randomized consenting United States adults during the 2020 election to reverse-chronological rather than default ranked Facebook or Instagram feeds from September 24 through December 23 [9]. Analytical samples were 23,391 for Facebook and 21,373 for Instagram, with unknown cross-platform overlap. The intervention reduced time and activity, increased political content on both platforms, increased Facebook untrustworthy-source exposure from 2.6 to 4.4 percent, and changed other categories; no significant effect was detected on the measured polarization or election-knowledge outcomes [9]. The null result does not extend to all behavior, duration, populations, or platforms. Science errata corrected Instagram source and carousel metrics in 2024 and 2026, including an untestable estimate that uncivil-content levels would have been about 0.021 rather than 0.016 in both groups. The authors state that Instagram treatment effects, Facebook data, and survey outcomes should not be affected [15][16].

Read together, the audits establish an evidence ladder. Recommendation can be shown to change what is offered. Click or watch data can show what is selected. Experiments can test whether a changed feed alters later attitudes or behavior. Each step requires separate evidence, and a finding at one level cannot substitute for another [8][9]. Cultural analysis is strongest when it preserves that ladder instead of treating visibility, consumption, and conversion as synonyms.

### Policy Evidence Treats Discoverability as a Cultural System

UNESCO's 2022 report used quadrennial reports from 94 parties, other datasets, literature, and expert contributions to monitor the 2005 Convention on the Diversity of Cultural Expressions [11]. It identified digital-access and literacy gaps, platform concentration, unsustainable remuneration, and business models unfavorable to diverse discoverability. It reported that 68 percent of the reporting parties used quotas involving local content, languages, or social groups and warned that quotas cannot compensate for insufficient local production or guarantee discovery [11]. This is comparative policy evidence rather than a causal platform audit.

UNESCO's 2026 successor report updates that baseline. It treats availability as necessary but insufficient for discoverability, identifies platform consolidation and algorithmic opacity as continuing problems, reports that only 48 percent of parties described measures on access to digital media, and recommends stronger algorithmic transparency, data sharing, monitoring, local production, and discoverability requirements [17]. The two editions should therefore be read as historical reporting cycles, not one current percentage series.

The DSA supplies a procedural response with differentiated scope [12]. Article 27 requires qualifying providers of online platforms using recommender systems to explain the main parameters and any existing options to modify or influence them, subject to the regulation's scope and exclusions. Articles 34 and 37 require designated VLOPs and VLOSEs to assess systemic risks and undergo annual independent audits. Article 38 requires those designated services to offer at least one non-profiled option for each recommender system, while Article 40 creates regulatory and vetted-researcher data routes for systemic-risk and mitigation research under statutory conditions [12]. Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2050 operationalizes Article 40 through common procedures, a data-access portal, and service data catalogues [21]. These rules create investigatory conditions; they do not show that cultural diversity has improved.

### A 2026 European Study Measures Discoverability Beyond Availability

A European Commission study released in April 2026 examined discoverability primarily in music and books through desk and sectoral research, data mapping, interviews, focus groups, a workshop, a Europe-wide survey of more than 400 participants, and quantitative analyses of consumption, curation, recommendation, and metadata [18]. Its executive summary treats availability as insufficient: discovery depends on whether audiences are recommended, notice, and engage with diverse cultural expressions. It identifies personalization, algorithmic bias, weak metadata, limited transparency, language silos, and platform concentration as linked constraints [18].

For music, 62 percent of surveyed users said algorithms helped them discover new music, while substantially fewer reported greater exposure to national, other-European-Union, or non-English-language works [18]. Streaming services were reported as a primary discovery route, and the study found that European chart presence was driven more by domestic than cross-border European works. These survey perceptions and platform analyses do not establish one causal algorithmic effect, and audiovisual media were outside the study's main scope. They nevertheless supply current evidence that subjective discovery and culturally diverse exposure are not the same outcome [18].

### The Convergent Finding Is Conditional Gatekeeping

Across technical papers, audits, creator research, audience research, and policy records, the common finding is not that algorithms homogenize culture in every setting. Recommendation systems can change offered exposure, and the direction depends on catalogs, objectives, data, interface, user behavior, and institutional constraints [3][4][6][8][9]. Popularity can be amplified and sampled low-mainstreaminess users can receive higher error, while recent Netflix working papers report improvements that disperse consumption from the very top toward moderately popular titles [10][14][19][20]. Diversity can be included in design, but its definitions and trade-offs must be explicit [1][14]. Creators respond to perceived rules, audiences retain other sources of choice, and exposure changes do not by themselves establish a downstream belief or taste effect [7][9][13].

The author's synthesis is that cultural gatekeeping has become recursive and partially personalized. Earlier gates selected a common schedule, shelf, or edition; contemporary gates repeatedly update a probabilistic slate using reactions to prior slates. This makes cultural power less visible but not wholly centralized. Platforms control objectives and infrastructure, creators influence signals and supply, audiences choose and interpret, institutions regulate, and external publicity changes the data entering the loop. Any adequate explanation must include all five.

## Implications

### For Audiences: Treat the Feed as a Situated Suggestion

The author's interpretation is that a recommended item shows that a system placed or scored it under a current objective; that fact alone does not establish cultural importance, truth, representativeness, or a user's settled preference. Netflix documents predictions and business objectives, Zhao surveys utility metrics, and Frey documents the continuing role of critics, word of mouth, prior knowledge, and other aids, but none of these sources directly proves every negative inference in the preceding sentence [3][13][14]. Audiences can reconstruct the gate by asking which action preceded the suggestion, which catalog and territory constrained it, and what alternative route would produce a different result. Search, direct subscription, editorial lists, libraries, critics, friends, and non-profiled or chronological modes provide different gates rather than gate-free access [1][9][13].

The practical objective is controlled variation, not rejection of personalization. A listener can alternate recommendation with deliberate search, inspect artist or label contexts, and create sessions that begin outside the familiar genre. A viewer can compare the personalized homepage with catalog categories, external criticism, or an available non-profiled ranking option. A creator-video user can deliberately select contrary material as an experiment, but Boeker and Urman did not test whether this reliably resets an inferred interest [6]. These are the author's recommendations derived from evidence that user actions affect feeds and that audience choice remains part of the process [6][9][13], not guaranteed methods for controlling every ranking system.

Media literacy should separate four claims. The system showed the item; the user selected it; the user liked or completed it; and the encounter changed taste or conduct. Platform interfaces often compress these stages into engagement, but research does not support that compression. Haroon and colleagues measured offered pathways, while Guess and colleagues directly tested downstream attitudes and found substantial exposure change without corresponding significant attitude change in the measured period [8][9]. A disciplined audience should resist both fatalism and innocence: feeds matter because they structure opportunity, but seeing is not obeying.

### For Creators: Optimize a Portfolio, Not a Single Proxy

Bucher and Cotter support a bounded premise: creators can experience visibility as conditional on continual participation and respond by inferring partly hidden rules [2][7]. The author's risk analysis extends beyond those studies. Dependence on one opaque metric can make a creator vulnerable to a format change, ranking update, moderation decision, or platform shift, while fear of invisibility may encourage imitation. A reversible response is to maintain a stable creative claim while diversifying distribution, audience contact, formats, and revenue paths; this is a recommendation, not an outcome tested by Bucher or Cotter.

The author's recommendation is to distinguish useful adaptation from proxy manufacture. Clear metadata, accessible captions, intelligible openings, appropriate genre labels, and responsive audience relationships may improve discovery and the encounter, but Cotter did not test those practices [7]. Cotter did document debates about authenticity and entrepreneurship plus simulated tactics such as engagement groups and follow-unfollow [7]. Misleading packaging, trend copying, and duration optimization are additional author examples, not findings from that study. The proposed test is whether a tactic would still improve the audience's encounter if it produced no ranking advantage.

Creators also need an evidence ledger. Record the date, content type, audience source, impression count, completion, saves, direct responses, and off-platform outcomes before attributing success or failure to the algorithm. Compare several releases rather than one anecdote, and preserve changes in platform guidance. Because systems and audiences change together, informal tests cannot identify every cause, but they can prevent folklore from becoming production doctrine. The principle follows from the paired controls in the TikTok audit and the explicit experimental approach described by Netflix: comparison is stronger than intuition, and a metric should be interpreted in relation to the objective that generated it [3][6].

### For Platforms: Make Cultural Objectives Explicit and Measurable

Platforms cannot optimize an undefined concept of diversity. Zhao supports individual-list diversity, aggregate system diversity, and provider exposure; UNESCO and the 2026 European Commission study add language, locality, and cross-border discoverability [11][14][17][18]. The author separately proposes shared cultural commonality as another objective. Each goal requires a baseline, metric, and disclosed trade-off with relevance, satisfaction, safety, and rights. Reporting only catalog size or source-country count does not show what users were offered or selected.

A multi-objective design can reserve controlled offered exposure for novelty, less-popular works, local content, or new creators while preserving relevance. The evidence justifies monitoring both histories and distributions, but with narrower claims: Spotify programmed listening, which included editorial as well as personalized sources, was less diverse than user-driven listening; Kowald's deliberately sampled low-mainstreaminess group received higher error in an offline evaluation [4][10]. The author's proposed tests include exposure share by popularity decile, creator reach conditional on catalog availability, language and territory coverage, repeat concentration, exploration acceptance, and long-term retention. Zhao and colleagues show that fairness and diversity objectives can enter training or re-ranking, but they do not validate this entire audit list [14].

Cold-start policy deserves explicit treatment as the author's design implication rather than a finding of Kowald or Zhao. New artifacts lack interaction records, so a system that equates existing engagement with value may deny them the evidence needed to compete. A bounded trial audience selected by content, context, or randomization could generate evidence without assuming that zero history means zero value. Neither cited study determines a correct exploration rate or establishes that this intervention improves cultural diversity [10][14]. Any platform using it should state the goal, method, and attention cost imposed on users.

User controls must be materially different. Helberger and colleagues connect autonomy to awareness and the ability to adjust recommendations [1]. Under the DSA, Article 27 requires qualifying online platforms to disclose main parameters and any existing influence options, while Article 38 separately requires designated VLOPs and VLOSEs to provide at least one non-profiled option for each recommender system [12]. Chronological, local, followed-only, editorial, diverse-discovery, and reduced-personalization modes are the author's examples, not modes mandated by the cited law. Where offered, a control should explain what changes and preserve an easy return path.

The author's governance proposal extends beyond current legal duties: stable creator rules could identify prohibited manipulation, major ranking objectives, reasons for loss of eligibility, and consequential policy changes; aggregate dashboards could show concentration by creator size, language, geography, or category. The DSA does not generally require those creator notices or dashboards. Its Article 40 route is limited to necessary and proportionate access for qualifying systemic-risk and mitigation research by vetted researchers, with privacy, confidentiality, and security safeguards [12]. The 2025 delegated regulation requires service data catalogues and a portal but still does not guarantee impression-level or ranking-condition data for every project [21].

### For Cultural Institutions: Curate the Conditions of Discovery

Libraries, broadcasters, museums, archives, festivals, schools, public-service media, and independent critics remain important because recommendation need not be monopolized by commercial engagement objectives. Helberger and colleagues argue that public-service institutions may be positioned to realize forms of exposure diversity the market will not supply [1]. UNESCO's 2022 and 2026 reports call for local production, discoverability, monitoring, data access, and fair creator conditions, while the 2026 European Commission study recommends better metadata, hybrid curation, recurring audience measurement, transparency, and support for smaller cultural actors [11][17][18].

These institutions can build contextual gates. A playlist can explain historical lineage, a film collection can connect versions and regions, a game archive can preserve hardware and community context, and a local-culture feed can disclose why an artifact was selected. Context changes exposure from a bare impression into an invitation to understand. The author's synthesis is that public-interest curation should compete on legibility and trust rather than imitate an opaque commercial feed. Its value lies not in being non-algorithmic but in making objectives, selection criteria, and cultural responsibilities visible [1][11].

Funding policy should distinguish supply from discovery. A grant may produce a local work that remains invisible in a dominant interface, and a quota may secure catalog presence without placement, language support, or sustained availability. UNESCO and the European Commission connect production, metadata, platform structure, discoverability, monitoring, and creator conditions, but the author's full chain is an audit proposal rather than a source-defined model [11][17][18]. Evaluation should trace creation, rights, metadata, catalog entry, offered exposure, audience selection, and creator compensation while labeling which links were actually measured.

### For Regulators: Govern Processes Without Choosing Taste

The regulatory objective should not be a state-approved recommendation list. A narrower approach can require explanation, meaningful alternatives where legally applicable, risk assessment, records, independent audit, and qualified research access without selecting approved culture. Under the DSA these duties differ by service category: Article 27 applies to qualifying online platforms, while Articles 34, 37, 38, and 40 apply to designated VLOPs and VLOSEs [12]. Article 40 access is conditioned rather than general, and the 2025 delegated regulation structures applications, catalogues, access modes, and oversight [21]. These duties make defined processes more contestable while leaving creators and audiences free to disagree about cultural value.

Risk assessment should include cultural and linguistic specificity. A satisfactory global aggregate can conceal the disappearance of a minority language, local genre, or small creator class. DSA Article 34 requires designated VLOPs and VLOSEs to consider regional and linguistic aspects when assessing systemic risks, while UNESCO treats local-content discoverability as a cultural-policy concern [12][17]. Exposure distributions and change analyses are the author's proposed evidence for that inquiry, not measurements expressly mandated by Article 34.

Policy also needs a counterfactual. Before requiring chronology, randomization, or another ranking mode, regulators should ask what that mode changes. Guess and colleagues found that reverse chronology decreased use, increased some political and untrustworthy exposure, changed other categories, and produced no significant effect on specified polarization or knowledge outcomes during the measured period [9]. The later Instagram metric errata should accompany that result even though the authors state that treatment-effect and survey conclusions are unaffected [15][16]. The experiment does not settle every platform question; it shows that an intuitive alternative can carry its own trade-offs.

### For Researchers: Measure the Whole Cultural Pathway

Research should begin with a declared dependent variable. Exposure studies need impression and rank data. Consumption studies need selection, duration, completion, and repeat behavior. Creator studies need production routines and resource constraints. Taste studies need longitudinal measures and external choice sources. Cultural-diversity studies need explicit dimensions such as genre, language, nationality, creator size, or identity. Attitude studies need experiments or credible causal designs. The Spotify, TikTok, YouTube, Meta, and creator studies each answer different questions because their methods observe different stages [4][6][7][8][9].

Audits should use more than one baseline. Chronology, popularity, random ordering, followed-only feeds, and personalized ranking each embody selection rules. Testing several can reveal whether a result comes from personalization, popularity, inventory, user history, or the interface. Researchers should report platform version, territory, date, account condition, language, logged-in status, seed behavior, and stopping rule because recommendation systems change continuously [6][8]. Negative and mixed findings deserve preservation; without them, the research literature can reproduce the salience bias it studies.

Cross-platform comparison must preserve media differences. Completion means something different for a song, two-hour film, serial episode, short video, game session, or text post. Autoplay carries different costs, and creator supply responds on different time scales. Maaso and Spilker analyze hybrid gatekeeping in music, while Frey places film recommendation within older media institutions and taste intermediaries [5][13]. The author's synthesis is that a valid comparison should hold the cultural question stable while allowing mechanisms and institutions to differ; neither source alone establishes equivalence across all media.

### A Practical Cultural Gatekeeping Audit

The following protocol is the author's synthesis, not a validated universal standard. First, define the cultural field and population. Second, map the catalog gate: rights, territory, language, metadata, moderation, and eligibility. Third, identify candidate generation, ranking objectives, re-ranking constraints, and interface defaults at the level the evidence permits. Fourth, separate availability, offered exposure, selection, continuation, repeat use, and downstream effect; remember that Helberger uses exposure diversity for selected rather than merely displayed content [1]. Fifth, measure supported dimensions such as individual-list diversity, aggregate coverage, popularity concentration, and provider exposure, and label commonality or other added dimensions as author-defined rather than attributing them to Zhao [10][14].

Sixth, document human curation, advertising, editorial promotion, external publicity, and user-network supply so that the algorithm is not treated as an autonomous cause [5]. Seventh, study creator response: format changes, release timing, metadata, engagement tactics, and withdrawal. Eighth, compare at least two counterfactual orderings and preserve null results. Ninth, test for differences by language, region, creator size, and niche orientation. Tenth, disclose uncertainty, platform changes, inaccessible data, and the strongest claim each method can support [4][6][8][9].

The author's synthesis is that the audit's worst-case failure is to label an opaque popularity loop as revealed audience taste. Preventing it requires inversion: ask what cannot become popular because it is not exposed, what cannot be recommended because it never entered the catalog, and what creators stop making because the platform cannot measure its value. Recommendation is culturally legitimate when it helps people navigate abundance while preserving meaningful choice, plural discovery, and contestable rules. It becomes extractive when the platform's preferred proxy quietly defines which culture is visible and then cites the resulting behavior as proof that the public chose it [1][10][11][14].
