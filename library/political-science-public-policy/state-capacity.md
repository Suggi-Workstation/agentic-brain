# State Capacity -- Why Some Governments Can Deliver and Others Cannot

State capacity is the ability of a government to implement policy, deliver
public services, and enforce rules across the entirety of its territory.
It is not the same as state power in the sense of raw coercion: a state may
wield enormous despotic authority over its citizens yet remain unable to
collect taxes, build roads, or administer a census. The distinction
between despotic power (power over society) and infrastructural power
(power through society), introduced by Michael Mann in 1984, is the
analytical foundation of the field. States that can penetrate society
logistically, coordinate its activities, and enforce their decisions
uniformly possess high infrastructural power. States that can only rule
through personal commands from the center, relying on local intermediaries
whose loyalty is conditional, possess high despotic power but low
infrastructural power. The capacity to convert political authority into
outcomes -- to actually implement policy -- is what separates states that
govern from states that merely rule.

## Background

The concept of state capacity emerged from a convergence of political
sociology, comparative politics, and development economics. Before the
1980s, the dominant frameworks treated the state either as a neutral
arbiter of interests (pluralist theory), as an instrument of the ruling
class (Marxist theory), or as a rational-legal bureaucracy in the Weberian
ideal type. None of these frameworks adequately explained why states with
formally similar institutions produced dramatically different outcomes.
Some states could collect taxes efficiently, regulate markets, build
infrastructure, and deliver education and healthcare. Others, possessed of
identical formal authority and often greater coercive resources, could
not. The puzzle was not one of power in the raw sense but of capacity:
the organizational, logistical, and institutional ability to translate
authority into action.

The intellectual breakthrough came from Michael Mann's 1984 essay "The
Autonomous Power of the State: Its Origins, Mechanisms and Results,"
published in the European Journal of Sociology. Mann argued that state
power has two analytically distinct dimensions. Despotic power is the
range of actions a state elite can take without routine negotiation with
civil society groups -- the power to command, punish, and override. It
is power over society. Infrastructural power is the capacity of the
state to actually penetrate civil society and implement its decisions
logistically throughout its territory. It is power through society. A
state can be despotically powerful but infrastructurally weak, as Mann
argued was the case for many historic empires whose commands could only
be executed by local lords and magnates whose own power was substantially
autonomous of the center. Conversely, a state can be despotically weak
but infrastructurally strong, as Mann argued was the case for modern
capitalist democracies, where leaders rule only with the consent of
representative institutions but possess enormous capacity to assess
income, tax it, store information, and enforce decisions across the
entire national territory.

Mann identified the logistical techniques that produce infrastructural
power: literacy (enabling written rules and records), coinage and
standardized weights and measures (enabling market exchange under state
guarantee), and rapid communication and transport (roads, ships,
telegraphy). These techniques are not inherently statist -- they diffuse
between state and civil society -- but the state's territorial and
centralized character gives it a structural advantage in mobilizing them.
The key insight is that infrastructural power is cooperative as well as
coercive: it depends on a degree of citizen compliance and on the
state's ability to provide services that citizens value. A state whose
institutions do not reach through society, that cannot collect taxes,
enforce contracts, or deliver services, is unlikely to develop the
symbolic power of being seen as a natural force in daily life.

The second major intellectual stream came from Charles Tilly's work on
state formation in Europe. In Coercion, Capital and European States,
AD 990-1990 (1990), Tilly argued that "war made the state and the state
made war." The exigencies of warfare -- the need to field standing armies,
finance prolonged campaigns, and mobilize populations -- created powerful
incentives for European rulers to invest in revenue-raising institutions,
administrative capacity, and territorial control. States that could tax
effectively could wage war; states that could wage war survived; the
survivors built the bureaucratic infrastructure that became the modern
state. Tilly's thesis connected state capacity to its historical origins:
capacity was not a given but the product of centuries of competitive
pressure, institutional investment, and path-dependent development. The
connection between war, taxation, and state-building became a central
theme in the historical political economy literature.

The third stream came from development economics and comparative political
economy, particularly the work of Peter Evans on the developmental state.
In Embedded Autonomy: States and Industrial Transformation (1995), Evans
argued that the effectiveness of states as agents of economic
transformation depends on their internal structure and external ties.
The most effective states -- exemplified by Japan, South Korea, and
Taiwan -- possessed "embedded autonomy": a meritocratic bureaucracy with
a strong corporate identity (autonomy) combined with dense, institutionalized
links to private entrepreneurial elites (embeddedness). The least effective
states -- exemplified by Zaire under Mobutu -- exhibited "incoherent
absolutist domination": undisciplined internal structures and external
ties ruled by the invisible hand of patronage. Intermediate states, like
Brazil, occasionally approximated embedded autonomy but lacked the global
organizational coherence to sustain it. Evans's framework connected
state capacity not just to bureaucratic form but to the quality of
state-society relations.

The fourth stream, from economics, came from Timothy Besley and Torsten
Persson. In "The Origins of State Capacity: Property Rights, Taxation,
and Politics" (2009), they formalized the idea that state capacity
consists of two accumulated stocks: legal capacity (the ability to
enforce contracts and property rights) and fiscal capacity (the ability
to raise revenue through taxation). These stocks are complements -- a
state that invests in legal capacity to protect property rights also
invests in fiscal capacity to tax the resulting economic activity.
Investment in both forms of capacity depends on political stability,
the expected demand for public goods, income levels, and the
representativeness of political institutions. Parliamentary democracies
tend to have better property rights protection and higher taxes than
presidential democracies, a pattern their model explains through the
incentive structures facing ruling groups. The Besley-Persson framework
brought state capacity into formal economic modeling, connecting it to
the broader literature on institutions and growth.

Francis Fukuyama, in "What Is Governance?" (2013), synthesized these
strands into a two-dimensional framework: governance quality is the
product of state capacity (the ability to make and enforce rules and
deliver services) and bureaucratic autonomy (the degree to which
bureaucrats can shape goals and define tasks independently of political
principals). Fukuyama explicitly adopted Mann's focus on infrastructural
rather than despotic power and excluded democratic accountability from
the definition of governance in order to theorize the relationship
between governance and democracy separately. His framework explained
the conundrum that low-income countries are advised to reduce bureaucratic
autonomy (to prevent predation) while high-income countries seek to
increase it (to enable expert policymaking).

## Core Concepts

### Despotic Power -- Power Over Society

Despotic power, in Mann's framework, is the range of actions a state
elite can undertake without routine negotiation with civil society
groups. It is the power to command, to punish, to override -- the
Red Queen's ability to order one's head cut off, in Mann's Alice in
Wonderland analogy. Despotic power is about the scope of what the state
can decide, not about whether the state can actually enforce its
decisions. A dictator who can order anything but whose orders are
ignored outside the capital has high despotic power but low
infrastructural power. Historic monarchies and empires were often
strong in despotic power but weak in infrastructural power: their
commands could only be executed by local lords and intermediaries
whose own authority was substantially autonomous of the center. The
feudal state, in Mann's typology, is the weakest type, with both low
despotic and low infrastructural power. The imperial or patrimonial
state possesses its own governing agents but has only limited capacity
to penetrate and coordinate civil society without the assistance of
other power groups. The authoritarian state is high on both dimensions:
it can command without consultation (despotic) and enforce through its
bureaucratic infrastructure (infrastructural). Nazi Germany and the
Soviet Union approximated this type, though Mann noted they likely
traded some loss of infrastructural penetration for their high despotic
powers, attaining lower levels of social mobilization during World War
II than the "despotically weak but participatory" Great Britain.

### Infrastructural Power -- Power Through Society

Infrastructural power is the capacity of the state to penetrate civil
society and implement its decisions logistically throughout its
territory. It is the Red Queen's ability to actually hunt down Alice and
enforce the decapitation. Infrastructural power is cooperative as well
as coercive: it depends on a degree of citizen compliance and on the
state's provision of services that citizens value. The modern
bureaucratic state exemplifies high infrastructural power: it can assess
and tax income and wealth at source, store and recall massive amounts
of information, enforce its will within the day almost anywhere in its
domain, and directly provide the subsistence of most of its citizens
through employment, pensions, and family allowances. Mann argued that
infrastructural power has grown enormously in modern states due to
industrialization and the modern bureaucratic division of labor. The
complaint that citizens of capitalist democracies voice about "the
growing power of the state" is, Mann noted, more justly leveled against
infrastructural encroachments than against despotic power, which has if
anything declined. Infrastructural power and despotic power exist in
tension in weak states: infrastructural power requires a level of
cooperation and institutional compromise that generally undermines the
arbitrary exercise of despotic power.

### Fiscal Capacity and Legal Capacity

Besley and Persson decomposed state capacity into two accumulated
stocks. Fiscal capacity is the state's ability to raise revenue through
taxation -- the bureaucratic infrastructure to assess, collect, and
enforce tax obligations. Legal capacity is the state's ability to
enforce contracts and property rights -- the judicial and regulatory
infrastructure that underpins market exchange. These two forms of
capacity are complements: a state that protects property rights creates
the economic activity it can then tax, and a state that taxes has the
revenue to invest in courts and enforcement. The historical evidence
supports this complementarity. Rich countries are simultaneously
high-tax countries with strong contract enforcement; poor countries
tend to be low-tax with weak property rights. Investment in both forms
of capacity depends on political stability (ruling groups will not
invest in institutions they expect to lose control of), the expected
demand for public goods (war is an archetypical public good that
creates incentives for revenue-raising institutions), income levels
(higher wealth makes capacity investment more valuable), and the
representativeness of political institutions (more representative
systems align the interests of rulers and ruled, raising investment in
both legal and fiscal capacity). Parliamentary democracies, where
ruling groups face stronger constraints, tend to invest more in both
forms of capacity than presidential or authoritarian systems where a
single group holds concentrated power.

### Tax Collection as a Capacity Signal

Tax collection is the most widely used empirical indicator of state
capacity because it is measurable, comparable across countries, and
directly reveals the state's ability to penetrate society. A state that
can assess income, track transactions, and enforce collection possesses
the logistical infrastructure to carry out other administrative tasks:
running a census, prosecuting crimes, building infrastructure,
regulating markets. The Besley-Persson framework and the broader
development economics literature treat fiscal capacity as a proxy for
state capacity more broadly. Tax collection also reveals the political
economy of capacity: rulers who lack legitimacy or who depend on narrow
coalitions of support have weaker incentives to invest in broad-based
tax systems, because broad taxation requires broad accountability.
Resource-rich states often exhibit the paradox of high revenue but low
capacity: they extract rents from natural resources without needing to
tax their citizens, which weakens the fiscal contract between state and
society and reduces the incentive to build administrative infrastructure.
The World Bank's analysis of Sub-Saharan Africa found that resource-rich
countries in the region tend to have systematically lower capacity for
budget and financial management than non-resource countries, despite
higher revenues. Tax farming -- the practice of auctioning the right to
collect taxes to private intermediaries -- was historically common in
states with weak infrastructural power, from ancien regime France to
the Ottoman Empire, and produced lower efficiency than direct state
collection because intermediaries had no incentive to invest in
long-term capacity.

### Bureaucratic Competence and the Weberian Model

Max Weber's ideal type of bureaucratic organization -- recruitment by
merit, promotion by seniority and achievement, a clear hierarchy of
authority, written rules, and a division of labor by functional
specialization -- remains the procedural benchmark for state capacity.
Weber emphasized the benefits of an even distribution of duties and
power, the hiring of qualified officials, and a written set of rules
that can be universally learned and followed. The methods by which a
state builds infrastructural power mesh with the establishment of a
bureaucracy: literacy enables comprehension of written rules;
standardized measurements and efficient transport and communication
enable distribution of authority; division of labor ensures qualified
officials in each field exploit their expertise. Peter Evans's research
on the developmental states of East Asia confirmed that meritocratic
recruitment, predictable career paths, and competitive compensation
are necessary -- but not sufficient -- conditions for effective state
action. Evans and James Rauch found in a cross-national analysis that
"Weberian" state structures -- meritocratic recruitment, competitive
salaries, long-term career incentives -- were associated with higher
economic growth. However, Evans argued that Weberian bureaucracy by
itself is insufficient. The developmental state's effectiveness depends
on "embedded autonomy": a meritocratic bureaucracy with corporate
coherence (autonomy) paired with dense, institutionalized ties to
private elites (embeddedness). Bureaucratic competence without
external ties produces a competent but isolated apparatus; external ties
without bureaucratic competence produce patronage networks, not
developmental capacity.

### Embedded Autonomy and the Developmental State

Peter Evans's concept of embedded autonomy identifies the structural
conditions under which states become effective agents of economic
transformation. The concept joins two apparently contradictory
properties: bureaucratic insulation (autonomy) and dense ties to society
(embeddedness). Autonomy ensures that the state apparatus is not
captured by particularistic interests -- that bureaucrats pursue
corporate goals rather than private enrichment. Embeddedness ensures
that the state has the information, intelligence, and cooperation needed
to implement policy effectively and to discipline private actors toward
national projects of accumulation. The East Asian developmental states
-- Japan's MITI, South Korea's Economic Planning Board, Taiwan's
industrial policy apparatus -- exemplified embedded autonomy during
their periods of rapid industrialization. Their effectiveness depended
on meritocratic bureaucracies with strong corporate identity paired with
institutionalized relationships with industrial elites. Evans contrasted
these with the "incoherent absolutist domination" of predatory states
like Zaire under Mobutu, where internal structures were undisciplined
and external ties were governed by patronage rather than shared
developmental projects. The concept's power lies in its rejection of
the false dichotomy between state intervention and market freedom: the
question is not whether the state intervenes but whether its
intervention is structured by embedded autonomy or by predation. Evans
and Heller noted that the revolutionary violence and geopolitical chaos
of mid-twentieth-century Northeast Asia had the developmentally
propitious consequence of eliminating landed elites as politically
effective actors, creating space for state-society relations oriented
toward transformation rather than rent extraction.

### Institutional Quality and the Extractive-Inclusive Distinction

Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, in Why Nations Fail (2012), proposed
a framework that connects state capacity to the inclusiveness of
political and economic institutions. Inclusive economic institutions
enforce property rights, create a level playing field, and encourage
investment in new technologies and skills. These are supported by
inclusive political institutions that distribute power widely in a
pluralistic manner and achieve sufficient political centralization to
establish law and order. Extractive economic institutions are structured
to extract resources from the many by the few, and are supported by
extractive political institutions that concentrate power. The framework
implies that state capacity is not neutral: capacity in service of
extractive institutions can produce temporary growth (as under the
Soviet Union) but cannot sustain it, because elites limit access to
economic institutions for the broader population and suppress the
creative destruction that drives innovation. Capacity in service of
inclusive institutions produces sustained growth because it protects
property rights, enforces contracts, and enables broad participation.
Acemoglu and Robinson argued that the Chinese growth model under
extractive political institutions would run out of steam unless political
inclusion followed economic liberalization. The framework's key
implication for state capacity is that capacity and inclusion are
jointly determined: a state that can enforce rules but uses that
capacity to extract rather than enable produces different outcomes than
a state that uses equivalent capacity to protect rights and enable
participation.

### The Measurement Problem

State capacity is notoriously difficult to measure. The concept is
multidimensional -- encompassing fiscal, legal, coercive, and
administrative capacity -- and different dimensions may vary
independently within a single country. Jonathan Hanson and Melina
Sigman identified three core dimensions -- extractive, coercive, and
administrative -- and estimated them as a latent variable through
Bayesian factor analysis, producing a measure covering up to 163
countries from 1960 to 2009. The World Bank's Government Effectiveness
indicator, one of six Worldwide Governance Indicators, captures
perceptions of public service quality, civil service quality, and
policy implementation credibility, aggregating dozens of sub-indicators.
The State Fragility Index measures the capacity to manage conflict,
make and implement policy, and deliver services, combining effectiveness
and legitimacy sub-dimensions. The Fragile States Index, produced by the
Fund for Peace, synthesizes over 100 indicators across 12 dimensions.
Research by Antonio Savoia found that these measures have high
convergent validity (they are highly correlated) but low
interchangeability: even highly correlated measures can produce
completely different statistical inferences, because they disagree
substantially on individual country scores. The disagreement depends on
differences in underlying components and on the level of state capacity.
The implication is that researchers must match their measure to their
conceptual definition of state capacity rather than assuming any
highly correlated indicator is appropriate.

## Evidence

### Tilly's Bellicist Theory and European State Formation

Charles Tilly's argument that "war made the state and the state made
war" is supported by the European historical record. In Coercion,
Capital and European States, AD 990-1990 (1990), Tilly documented how
the escalating scale of warfare from the medieval period through the
nineteenth century created competitive pressure for revenue-raising
institutions. States that could field standing armies -- those with
access to large rural populations, capitalists, and commercialized
economies -- survived; those that could not were absorbed or destroyed.
The organization of extraction and the struggle over the means of war
created the central organizational structure of states. Tilly's thesis
has been tested and refined by subsequent scholars. The general
finding is that warfare and preparation for war provided pivotal moments
for state transformation, though the relationship is more nuanced than
the simplified "more war, more state" formulation. Warfare can also
undermine state capacity when it destroys infrastructure and
depopulates territory, and the bellicist mechanism operates primarily
in the European context where competitive pressure between roughly
equal states drove institutional investment. Outside Europe, where the
international system conferred sovereignty on territorial units
regardless of their internal capacity (as in post-colonial Africa), the
bellicist mechanism did not operate in the same way. Jeffrey Herbst
argued that the fundamental problem facing state-builders in Africa was
projecting authority over vast territories with low population
densities, and that the international system's recognition of borders
removed the competitive pressure that had driven European state
formation. The result was states with sovereignty over territories
they could not administer.

### Besley and Persson: Fiscal and Legal Capacity as Complements

Besley and Persson (2009) provided both a theoretical model and
empirical evidence for the complementarity of fiscal and legal
capacity. Their framework modeled state capacity as accumulated stocks
that constrain current policy choices: a state cannot enforce property
rights or collect taxes beyond the institutional capacity it has
invested in. Investment in capacity depends on political institutions,
income, and the expected value of public goods. Their empirical
analysis showed that rich countries are simultaneously high-tax
countries with strong contract enforcement, confirming the predicted
complementarity. Parliamentary democracies have better property rights
protection and higher taxes than presidential democracies, consistent
with the model's prediction that more representative institutions align
ruling group incentives with broad capacity investment. The model also
explains why countries with similar income levels can have very
different levels of state capacity: political institutions and the
expected demand for public goods (including war) mediate the
relationship between income and capacity investment. Besley and Persson
extended the framework in "State Capacity, Conflict, and Development"
(2010, Econometrica), showing that state capacity, conflict, and
development form mutually reinforcing clusters: countries with high
capacity experience less conflict and more development, which in turn
reinforces capacity, while countries with low capacity fall into
conflict traps that further erode capacity.

### Evans: Embedded Autonomy and East Asian Development

Peter Evans's comparative research on the IT industries of Brazil,
India, and South Korea provided empirical support for the embedded
autonomy framework. In Embedded Autonomy (1995), Evans showed that the
Korean state's effectiveness in promoting industrial transformation
depended on a meritocratic bureaucracy (the Economic Planning Board and
related agencies) with strong corporate identity, paired with dense,
institutionalized ties to the chaebol (industrial conglomerates). The
state could discipline private elites toward national projects of
accumulation because it had both the autonomy to resist capture and the
embeddedness to know what to do. Brazil, by contrast, exhibited partial
embedded autonomy: pockets of efficiency within the bureaucracy
(bolsoes de eficiencia -- "pockets of efficiency") achieved developmental
results in specific
sectors, but the broader state apparatus was compromised by extensive
political appointments (Brazilian presidents appointed thousands of
officials, compared to dozens in Japan) and patronage-based recruitment.
India presented yet another pattern: the commanding heights of the state
enjoyed significant capacity and autonomy in macroeconomic policy, but
as one moved downward through subnational and local government, state
capacity deteriorated so severely that local government had almost no
developmental capacity. Evans and Rauch (2000) confirmed the
cross-national pattern: countries with more "Weberian" bureaucratic
structures (meritocratic recruitment, competitive salaries, long-term
career incentives) had higher economic growth, controlling for other
factors. The evidence supported the conclusion that bureaucratic
structure is a causal determinant of developmental outcomes, not merely
a correlate.

### State Capacity Variation in Sub-Saharan Africa

The World Bank's analysis of governance in Sub-Saharan Africa
documented systematic variation in state capacity across the region.
Effective state institutions -- capable of maintaining peace, fostering
growth, and delivering services -- have developed unevenly. Some
countries made progress on fundamentals like revenue mobilization and
public financial management; overall progress on building strong
institutions stagnated, with repeated reform failures and reversals,
particularly in fragile and resource-rich countries. Resource-rich
countries in Sub-Saharan Africa tend to have systematically lower
capacity for budget and financial management than non-resource
countries, despite higher revenues, because resource rents weaken the
fiscal contract between state and citizens. Fragile and conflict-ridden
countries appear stuck in low-capability equilibria, where low capacity
coexists with little progress on capacity development. Jonathan di
John's research at the Crisis States Research Centre documented
substantial variation in capacity within countries: South Africa's tax
collection capacity was much greater than its ability to undertake
industrial policy or address public health crises; Botswana maintained
robust democratic institutions but performed poorly on HIV/AIDS
response; Tanzania and Zambia maintained political stability despite
poor economic performance, while neighbors with similar conditions
collapsed into civil war. This within-country variation challenges
aggregate measures of state capacity and suggests that capacity is
sector-specific rather than monolithic.

### Acemoglu, Garcia-Jimano, and Robinson: Network Effects of State Capacity

Daron Acemoglu, Camilo Garcia-Jimano, and James Robinson, in "State
Capacity and Economic Development: A Network Approach" (2015),
estimated the effect of state capacity on public goods provision and
prosperity using cross-country data. They found that a country's own
state capacity and its neighbors' state capacity both affect outcomes:
state capacity has network effects, spreading across borders through
diffusion of institutional practices, trade, and security spillovers.
This finding implies that state capacity is not purely a domestic
phenomenon but is shaped by regional institutional environments. The
network approach also helped explain the clustering of high-capacity
and low-capacity states geographically. The study estimated the effect
using multiple empirical approaches and found robust evidence that
state capacity -- measured by fiscal capacity and legal capacity
indicators -- causally affects prosperity, not merely correlates with it.

### Fukuyama: Capacity Versus Autonomy

Francis Fukuyama's "What Is Governance?" (2013) proposed a
two-dimensional framework for measuring governance quality: capacity
(resources and professionalization) and autonomy (the degree to which
bureaucrats can shape goals independently of political principals).
Fukuyama rejected output measures of governance (measuring outcomes
like GDP growth or literacy) because outputs confound state capacity
with other factors. He argued that procedural measures (Weberian
criteria) are necessary but insufficient because procedures may not
correlate with outcomes. The capacity-autonomy framework explained
why low-income countries are advised to reduce bureaucratic autonomy
(to prevent predation and ensure that bureaucracies serve elected
principals) while high-income countries seek to increase it (to enable
expert, long-term policymaking insulated from short-term political
pressure). Fukuyama noted that a given level of taxation does not
necessarily translate into efficient use of revenues: revenues can be
wasted on poor administration, unproductive transfers, or corruption.
Bureaucratic outputs depend not just on resource inputs but on
organizational culture, which procedural measures cannot capture.
The framework's empirical application to China showed that measuring
governance quality in authoritarian states is particularly challenging
because standard procedural measures assume democratic accountability
mechanisms that do not apply.

## Implications

### For Investors and Economic Analysis

State capacity is a fundamental but underappreciated variable in
investment analysis. A government's ability to enforce property rights,
regulate markets, collect taxes, and deliver infrastructure directly
affects the risk profile of operating within its jurisdiction.
Investors who assess governance quality as a factor in investment
decisions are implicitly assessing state capacity. The capacity of a
state to enforce contracts and protect property rights -- Besley and
Persson's legal capacity -- is a precondition for any long-term
investment commitment. States with high infrastructural power provide
a stable institutional environment: rules are enforced uniformly,
tax obligations are predictable, and infrastructure is maintained.
States with low infrastructural power create uncertainty: rules are
enforced selectively, tax obligations are arbitrary, and infrastructure
deteriorates. The Acemoglu-Robinson framework adds that the inclusiveness
of institutions matters as much as capacity: a state with high capacity
but extractive institutions may produce temporary growth (as under the
Soviet Union) but cannot sustain it. Investors evaluating emerging
markets should assess not just the current policy environment but the
underlying state capacity that determines whether policy commitments
will be honored over the investment horizon. The variation in capacity
within countries -- Evans's finding that capacity deteriorates as one
moves from national to local government in India -- implies that
investors must assess capacity at the level where it affects their
operations, not just at the national level. A country may have a
competent central bank and finance ministry but a local government
incapable of enforcing contracts or maintaining infrastructure. The
resource curse literature suggests that resource-rich states with
weak capacity are particularly risky, because their revenues do not
depend on taxing their citizens and therefore do not generate the
accountability pressures that build capacity over time.

### For Development Policy and Institutional Reform

The state capacity literature has direct implications for development
policy. The traditional "good governance" agenda -- promoted by the
World Bank and IMF since the 1990s -- often assumed that institutional
reform could be imported wholesale: adopt Weberian bureaucratic
procedures, pass anti-corruption laws, hold elections, and capacity
would follow. The evidence suggests this approach is inadequate.
Evans's research showed that Weberian bureaucratic structure is
necessary but insufficient: embedded autonomy requires not just
meritocratic recruitment but dense institutional ties between state
and society that take decades to build. di John's research showed that
capacity is path-dependent and sector-specific: states that perform
well in one domain (tax collection) may fail in another (public health),
and the transfer of capacity across domains is not automatic. Besley
and Persson's framework showed that capacity investment depends on
political institutions: reforming bureaucratic procedures without
changing the incentive structure facing ruling groups is unlikely to
produce sustained capacity investment. The Fukuyama framework suggests
that the appropriate institutional reform depends on the level of
development: low-capacity states need to reduce autonomy to prevent
predation, while high-capacity states need to increase autonomy to
enable expert policymaking. Importing the institutional forms of
high-capacity states into low-capacity environments without the
underlying political and social conditions produces form without
function. The World Bank's Sub-Saharan Africa analysis found that
institutional development is an extremely slow-moving process and that
reform failures and reversals are the norm rather than the exception,
particularly in fragile and resource-rich states. The implication is
that capacity-building requires sustained political commitment over
decades, alignment of political incentives with capacity investment,
and sequencing that matches reforms to existing institutional
constraints.

### For Political Science and Comparative Analysis

The state capacity concept has reshaped comparative political analysis
by providing a dimension of state variation that is distinct from
regime type. Before the capacity framework, scholars often conflated
state strength with democracy or authoritarianism. Mann's distinction
between despotic and infrastructural power showed that these are
analytically independent: a democracy can be infrastructurally strong
(high capacity, low despotic power) and an autocracy can be
infrastructurally weak (low capacity, high despotic power). This
distinction has major implications for the study of democracy. As
the special issue edited by Soifer and vom Hau demonstrated, the
capacity to implement political decisions is an attribute of political
authority, not a regime quality. Drawing a clear distinction between
state infrastructural power and government accountability allows
researchers to disentangle the causal relationship between regime
type and state capacity. Dan Slater's research on Southeast Asia
suggested the causal arrow can be reversed: competitive elections in
a context of robust mass mobilization can lead to increases in state
infrastructural power. The measurement problem -- Savoia's finding
that common measures have high convergent validity but low
interchangeability -- implies that comparative research must be
explicit about which dimension of capacity is being measured and
must not assume that any highly correlated indicator is appropriate.
The Hanson-Sigman three-dimensional framework (extractive, coercive,
administrative) provides a theoretically grounded measurement
structure, but the low interchangeability of measures means that
different studies may be measuring different things under the same
label, undermining the accumulation of knowledge.

### For Understanding State Failure and Fragility

The state capacity framework provides the analytical tools for
understanding state failure and fragility. A failed state is one that
has lost the capacity to perform core functions: maintain security,
collect taxes, enforce rules, deliver services, and maintain legitimacy.
The failed states literature, reviewed by di John, identified two
strands. The first treats state failure as a deviation from the
Weberian bureaucratic ideal: neo-patrimonial politics, clientelism, and
corruption prevent the state from performing its functions. The second
treats failure as a political process: leaders adapt to the constraints
of their environment by constructing informal mechanisms of social
control, and the question is not why states deviate from an ideal type
but why some patterns of contestation generate crisis and collapse while
others do not. The capacity framework bridges these strands: capacity
is shaped by path-dependent factors (colonial history, geography, ethnic
fragmentation) but is also created through political decision and
action, as the developmental states of East Asia demonstrated. The
World Bank's finding that fragile states in Sub-Saharan Africa are
trapped in low-capability equilibria -- where low capacity coexists
with little progress on capacity development -- suggests that
fragility is a self-reinforcing condition, not a temporary
shortfall. Breaking out of such equilibria requires not just
institutional reform but changes in the political economy that
determine whether ruling groups have incentives to invest in capacity.
The Herbst thesis that African states face the problem of projecting
authority over vast, sparsely populated territories -- compounded by
an international system that grants sovereignty regardless of internal
capacity -- implies that the standard policy toolkit (elections,
anti-corruption laws, bureaucratic reform) addresses symptoms rather
than the structural conditions that produce low capacity. The
implication for international policy is that state-building is a
long-term, politically embedded process, not a technical fix that
can be imposed from outside.
