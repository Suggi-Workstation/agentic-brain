# Public Budgeting Makes Political Priorities Binding Only When Fiscal Institutions Constrain Discretion

Public budgeting converts forecasts, laws, political priorities, and financing constraints into authority to collect revenue and spend public money [1][2]. An announced priority becomes operational only when the budget assigns resources, the legislature authorizes them, administrators can execute them, and reporting and audit make departures visible [1][3]. The central institutional problem is therefore not merely how much government spends, but who can propose, amend, authorize, move, withhold, supplement, review, and contest public resources across the budget cycle [1][8][10].

## Background

A public budget has two connected meanings. It is a forecast of receipts and expenditures, and it is an authorization that permits the state to raise and use resources for stated purposes [10]. The forecast looks forward under assumptions about revenue, prices, demand for services, existing legal commitments, and financing. The authorization creates a legal boundary: executive agencies may normally spend only for purposes and amounts approved through the applicable budget law [1][2]. These functions make the budget simultaneously a planning document, a political settlement, an administrative instruction, and an accountability baseline [1][3].

In nearly all contemporary systems, the executive prepares a draft and submits it to a legislature for approval, but the distribution of effective power varies greatly [10]. Finance ministries or other central budget authorities coordinate forecasts and expenditure limits, while line ministries translate programs and obligations into requests [1]. Legislatures may possess broad amendment powers, specialized staff, and enough time to scrutinize the proposal, or they may confront restrictive amendment rules, short calendars, and strong party discipline [10]. Approval can therefore range from genuine revision to near-ratification even when constitutional language assigns the legislature the power of the purse [10].

The annual budget originally emphasized legal control over inputs: which office could spend, on what category, and up to what amount. Modern budget systems retain those controls but add a wider institutional cycle covering fiscal strategy, medium-term projections, policy priorities, performance information, in-year execution, financial reporting, external audit, and legislative follow-up [1][3]. The PEFA framework expresses this wider cycle through seven pillars: budget reliability; transparency; management of assets and liabilities; policy-based fiscal strategy and budgeting; predictability and control in execution; accounting and reporting; and external scrutiny and audit [3]. The sequence matters because a well-presented proposal can still fail if cash is released unpredictably, commitments are not controlled, accounts are incomplete, or audit findings produce no response [3].

A second development has been the extension of the planning horizon beyond one fiscal year. Annual appropriations remain the usual legal authorization, but many policies create obligations that extend for years through wages, pensions, transfers, procurement contracts, maintenance, and capital projects [2][4]. Medium-term budgeting is intended to show the future consequences of present choices and to make expected resource availability more predictable [4]. Top-down budgeting begins with economic forecasts, current-policy baselines, fiscal objectives, and aggregate or ministerial expenditure ceilings before detailed requests are negotiated [4]. This architecture attempts to prevent the annual process from becoming a collection of separate claims that exceed the resources available to the government as a whole [1][4].

A third development has been the effort to connect money to results. Performance budgeting adds information about objectives, outputs, and outcomes to financial allocations, while spending reviews examine existing expenditure for savings, reallocation, or improved effectiveness [1][5][6]. These tools do not eliminate political judgment because objectives can conflict, outcomes may be jointly produced, and measurement can be incomplete [1][5]. Their institutional contribution is narrower: they make the reasons for allocations and the evidence about existing programs more explicit during negotiation, oversight, and revision [5][6].

Fiscal rules and independent fiscal institutions add further constraints and information. A fiscal rule places a lasting numerical limit on an aggregate such as expenditure, the budget balance, revenue, or debt, while an independent fiscal institution can assess forecasts, baselines, budget proposals, long-term sustainability, and compliance with rules or official targets [7][9]. The IMF's review of second-generation rules emphasizes that simplicity, flexibility, and enforceability are difficult to obtain at the same time [9]. Rules without credible budget management can encourage reclassification, off-budget activity, or temporary compliance rather than durable control, while oversight bodies without independence, resources, access to information, and a usable mandate may produce analysis that does not constrain decisions [7][9].

The modern budget is also broader than appropriated cash. Tax expenditures deliver support through reduced tax liability; guarantees and public-private arrangements can create contingent claims; extrabudgetary entities can conduct public activity outside the central budget; and intergovernmental transfers move resources across territorial levels [2][3][11]. If these channels are excluded from decision documents, formally compliant appropriations can understate the cost and distribution of policy [2][11]. Budget unity and comprehensive reporting therefore matter because political actors can otherwise shift activity to a less visible instrument while leaving its economic purpose largely unchanged [2][11].

Transparency and participation complete the historical expansion from an internal control document to a public accountability process. The IMF Fiscal Transparency Code calls for accessible budget information, clear allocation of executive and legislative responsibilities, comparable forecasts and reports, and public opportunities to participate in deliberation [2]. The Open Budget Survey assesses whether governments publish key documents, create opportunities for participation, and enable legislative and audit oversight across formulation, approval, execution, and audit [8]. Publication alone does not settle distributional conflict, but it makes the assumptions, authorizations, changes, and outturns available for contest and review [2][8].

The author's synthesis is that public budgeting evolved by adding institutions around the appropriation rather than replacing it. Forecasts define the feasible envelope; political bargaining selects priorities; law authorizes action; administration turns authority into commitments and payments; reporting compares outturn with authorization; audit tests legality, reliability, and performance; and later budgets incorporate or ignore what was learned [1][2][3]. A failure at any link can break the chain between stated priorities and public outcomes, which is why a budget should be studied as a governing system rather than as a table of spending totals [1][3].

## Core Concepts

### The budget cycle is a chain of constrained decisions

The budget cycle can be organized into formulation, legislative approval, execution, reporting, audit, and follow-up [1][3]. Formulation combines macro-fiscal forecasts, current-law or current-policy baselines, new proposals, and expenditure ceilings. Approval converts a proposal into legal authority after whatever amendment, debate, and voting procedures the political system permits. Execution converts authority into commitments, contracts, payroll, transfers, and payments. Reporting compares actual revenue, expenditure, and financing with the approved plan, while audit and legislative review test whether money was used lawfully and whether identified weaknesses were corrected [1][2][3].

These stages are analytically distinct but politically connected. An executive can dominate formulation by controlling forecasts and technical information, a legislature can reshape allocations through amendment, and administrators can alter the practical distribution of resources through cash releases, procurement timing, virement, or implementation capacity [3][10]. Audit can identify departures after the fact, but its deterrent and learning value depends on publication, legislative attention, and executive follow-up [3][8]. The author's synthesis is that budget power belongs not only to the actor who approves the total, but also to actors who define the baseline, set the ceiling, control the calendar, classify transactions, authorize in-year changes, and decide whether findings require correction [1][3][10].

### Forecasts and baselines structure the bargaining range

Revenue and economic forecasts determine the resources that the proposed budget treats as available, while baselines estimate the future cost of continuing existing policies under stated assumptions [2][4]. A top-down process uses those estimates and the government's fiscal objectives to set an aggregate envelope before ministries submit or finalize detailed bids [4]. This order matters because bottom-up requests are claims by separate organizations, whereas the central envelope represents the constraint facing the government as a whole [1][4].

A baseline is not a neutral prediction of what must happen. It depends on conventions about current law, current policy, inflation, demographic changes, expiring provisions, expected take-up, and the treatment of discretionary programs. The author's assessment is that baseline conventions distribute bargaining power because the actor who defines the starting point also affects whether a proposal appears to be a cut, an increase, or no change [4][7]. Transparency therefore requires publication of assumptions, reconciliation of revisions, and comparability between forecasts, budgets, and outturn reports [2]. Independent review can reduce the informational advantage of the executive without transferring elected officials' authority to choose policy [7][12].

Forecast error is unavoidable, but concealment and asymmetric adjustment are institutional choices. A credible system distinguishes an error caused by new information from a revision designed to create room for a preferred measure [2][7]. It also explains whether windfall revenue finances temporary expenditure, debt reduction, reserves, or permanent commitments [1][9]. The author's synthesis is that a forecast becomes a governing constraint only when later reports reconcile projections with outcomes and when deviations change future assumptions or decisions [1][2].

### Medium-term frameworks connect annual choices to future obligations

Medium-term budgeting normally covers three to five years and places the annual proposal inside a multi-year fiscal and expenditure path [4]. Multi-annual ceilings can be set for the whole government, ministries, programs, or other aggregates, with nearer years more binding and outer years more indicative [4]. The purpose is not to appropriate every future payment in advance; it is to show how today's decision changes the space available for later priorities [2][4].

A credible framework separates the cost of maintaining existing policy from the cost of new decisions, records the future operating costs of capital investment, and updates projections when assumptions or policies change [2][3][4]. Rolling frameworks add a new outer year as each annual budget is prepared, which preserves the horizon but can also permit repeated postponement if ceilings are routinely reset [4]. The author's assessment is that the key test is reconciliation: each new framework should explain why the path differs from the prior one and whether changes arose from forecasts, legal commitments, policy decisions, or reclassification [2][4].

Medium-term frameworks also expose trade-offs that an annual document can hide. A temporary grant can create permanent staffing or maintenance costs; a wage agreement can compound across years; a tax preference can reduce revenue indefinitely; and a capital project can create operating obligations after construction [2][3][11]. The framework is valuable when it makes those consequences contestable before authorization, not when it merely appends nonbinding projections to an unchanged annual negotiation [2][4].

### Appropriation divides initiative from authorization

The executive normally has the informational and organizational capacity to assemble a coherent proposal, while the legislature supplies democratic authorization and oversight [1][10]. The resulting balance is shaped by amendment powers, the time available for review, committee organization, access to technical support, and the consequences if a budget is not adopted on time [10]. Legislative power also depends on whether the executive can later withhold appropriated funds, transfer money between purposes, use reserves, or initiate additional spending without renewed approval [10].

Appropriation can be broad or detailed. Detailed line items restrict administrative movement and make departures visible, but they can impede adaptation and encourage compliance with categories rather than purposes. Broad program appropriations can give managers flexibility, but they require stronger reporting, performance information, internal control, and ex post scrutiny [1][3][5]. The author's synthesis is that the appropriate level of detail depends on trust, administrative capacity, task uncertainty, and the strength of review; neither maximum rigidity nor undefined flexibility is a general solution [1][3].

Supplementary authorization is the legitimate mechanism for changing the plan when material needs or forecasts change. Its accountability value depends on timing, explanation, coverage, and whether the legislature can examine the cumulative effect of changes rather than approve isolated additions [2][8]. Repeated supplements can indicate genuine shocks, weak forecasting, deliberate underbudgeting, or an attempt to shift decisions into a less scrutinized part of the year. The author's assessment is that the diagnosis requires comparison of original appropriations, in-year changes, cash releases, commitments, and final outturns [2][3].

### Execution determines whether legal authority becomes service delivery

An appropriation permits spending but does not ensure that the responsible agency receives cash, completes procurement, manages commitments, or delivers the intended output [1][3]. Execution systems control commitments before liabilities arise, schedule cash, authorize payments, reconcile accounts, and report deviations during the year [3]. PEFA treats predictable in-year resource allocation, internal control, internal audit, accounting, and reporting as separate dimensions because a weakness in one can disrupt the others [3].

Execution discretion is unavoidable. Revenue can differ from forecast, procurement can take longer than planned, emergencies can change priorities, and agencies can encounter operational constraints. Institutional design therefore specifies who may transfer resources, under what thresholds, with what notice or approval, and how changes will appear in reports [1][2][10]. Flexibility that is not recorded can nullify appropriation; rigidity that prevents lawful adaptation can produce arrears, rushed year-end spending, or service interruption [3].

Budget reliability is observable by comparing approved amounts with actual revenue and expenditure, including changes in composition [3]. A government can meet the aggregate total while shifting resources away from selected sectors or programs, so composition matters alongside the headline outturn [3]. The author's synthesis is that faithful execution means neither literal immobility nor executive freedom; it means controlled adaptation that remains traceable to authority, reason, and result [1][3].

### Comprehensiveness prevents policy from escaping the budget

Budget unity requires revenues, expenditures, and financing of central government entities to be presented on a gross basis and authorized through the legislature [2]. Comprehensive reporting extends further to public assets, liabilities, guarantees, tax expenditures, extrabudgetary units, and fiscal risks [2]. These boundaries matter because a government can deliver support through direct spending, a tax concession, a loan, a guarantee, a public corporation, or a special fund, even though each instrument appears differently in formal accounts [2][11].

Tax expenditures are reductions in liability relative to a specified benchmark tax system and can function as alternatives to direct outlays [11]. Their estimated cost depends on the benchmark, data, behavioral assumptions, and estimation method, which means the estimate should be documented rather than treated as exact [11]. Reporting them with or early in the budget cycle gives the legislature an opportunity to compare their cost with explicit programs, impose review or sunset provisions, and assess distributional effects [2][11].

Off-budget vehicles and contingent commitments create a similar problem. Excluding a public entity or guarantee from the main appropriation does not eliminate the public purpose or the possibility that taxpayers will bear costs [2][3]. The author's assessment is that the relevant test is economic control and fiscal exposure, not the legal label attached to the vehicle [2]. Comprehensive documents reduce incentives to move politically difficult choices beyond the ordinary ceiling or scrutiny process [2][9].

### Fiscal rules constrain aggregates but require institutions to bind behavior

A fiscal rule is a lasting numerical constraint on a budget aggregate, commonly expenditure, revenue, the balance, or debt [9]. Rules seek to counter short political horizons, common-pool pressures, and incentives to spend windfalls or postpone adjustment [9]. They can also signal the government's intended fiscal path to citizens and markets [9].

Rule design involves a three-way tension among simplicity, flexibility, and enforceability [9]. A simple rule is easier to understand and monitor but may respond poorly to recessions, disasters, or commodity shocks. A flexible rule can accommodate the cycle or exceptional events but may require estimates that are difficult to observe and can be manipulated. A strongly enforceable rule can discipline policy but may encourage cuts to investment, creative accounting, or off-budget activity if the underlying budget and reporting systems are weak [9].

Second-generation designs use expenditure ceilings, escape clauses, correction mechanisms, independent monitoring, and medium-term anchors to address these tensions [9]. These additions do not remove politics; they specify how discretion may be used and how the government returns to the rule after a permitted departure [9]. The author's synthesis is that an emergency clause is accountable only when the trigger, temporary measures, fiscal effects, reporting, and path back are explicit. Otherwise emergency flexibility can become a parallel budget process that bypasses ordinary authorization [8][9].

### Performance information and spending reviews support reallocation

Performance budgeting is the systematic use of performance information as a direct input or as context for budget decisions [5]. It can connect appropriations to objectives, outputs, and outcomes, but it does not imply an automatic formula in which a missed target causes a proportional funding cut [5]. Results can depend on external conditions, data quality, program design, or implementation, so performance information should inform judgment rather than replace it [1][5].

Spending reviews examine existing expenditure, not only new proposals, and connect evidence about programs to options for saving, reallocating, or improving effectiveness [6]. Their influence depends on a direct link to the budget calendar, clear governance, political ownership, and participation by the finance ministry and line ministries [6]. A review conducted after allocation decisions or without authority to propose changes may add analysis without changing resource choices [6].

The author's assessment is that these tools correct an asymmetry in annual budgeting: new initiatives receive concentrated advocacy, while existing programs can continue through inertia. Performance information and spending reviews create an institutional occasion to ask whether inherited allocations remain relevant, effective, and affordable [5][6]. They still require political choice because evidence cannot by itself rank competing public values [1][5].

### Audit and independent analysis close different accountability gaps

Internal control and internal audit operate during administration, while supreme audit institutions examine financial, compliance, and performance questions with organizational independence from the executive [3]. Audit value depends on access to records, timely reports, public availability, legislative hearings, recommendations, and documented executive follow-up [3][8]. A technically sound report that receives no response does not complete the accountability cycle [3].

Independent fiscal institutions address a different information problem. They provide nonpartisan analysis of forecasts, baselines, executive proposals, rule compliance, fiscal risks, and long-term sustainability, but they do not ordinarily make the budget choices reserved for elected institutions [7][12]. Their credibility depends on local ownership, independence, a clear mandate, adequate resources, access to information, relations with the legislature, transparency, communications, and external evaluation [7].

The author's synthesis is that auditors ask whether authority and standards were followed and what the records show, while fiscal councils or parliamentary budget offices test the assumptions and consequences of choices before and during decision-making [3][7]. Both reduce information asymmetry, but neither substitutes for legislative authorization, executive responsibility, or public contest [7][10].

### Intergovernmental transfers and participation distribute budget power beyond the center

Transfers between levels of government can finance shared services, equalize fiscal capacity, or support national priorities, but their allocation and timing affect subnational autonomy and service reliability [3]. PEFA therefore treats transparent transfer systems and predictable resource allocation as parts of public financial management rather than as peripheral federalism questions [3]. The author's assessment is that a formally decentralized responsibility is not operationally decentralized when the receiving government cannot predict the amount, timing, or conditions of its funding [3].

Citizen participation can occur during formulation, legislative approval, implementation, and audit [2][8]. Its value depends on timely information, accessible formats, representative participation, an opportunity to influence decisions before they are fixed, and feedback explaining how input was used [1][8]. Participation cannot eliminate the need for elected authorization or technical analysis, but it can reveal service conditions, distributional effects, and implementation failures that aggregate financial data miss [1][8].

The full institutional map therefore includes executives, legislatures, finance ministries, line agencies, subnational governments, auditors, fiscal councils, courts, civil society, and citizens. Their roles overlap but are not interchangeable [1][2][3]. Public budgeting makes priorities binding when these actors can identify who decided, what was authorized, how resources changed, what was delivered, and which institution must answer for a departure [1][3][8].

## Evidence

### Comparative evidence on medium-term and top-down budgeting

The OECD's 2023 Senior Budget Officials Survey on Budget Frameworks collected self-reported central or federal government practices from 36 OECD countries [4]. The 2025 report found that 26 of the 36 respondents, or 72 percent, used multi-annual top-down expenditure ceilings [4]. Among those 26 countries, 20 used rolling medium-term frameworks, and 14 used indicative rather than fully binding ceilings beyond the upcoming budget year [4]. The method establishes the prevalence and design of formal practices among respondents; it does not by itself estimate the causal effect of ceilings on fiscal outcomes [4].

This evidence supports two bounded conclusions. Medium-term and top-down methods are common but not universal among OECD governments, and formal ceilings differ in how long and how tightly they bind [4]. The variation matters because a one-year ceiling backed by outer-year forecasts creates a different constraint from binding ministry ceilings across several years [4]. The author's interpretation is that the survey measures institutional architecture, not faithful implementation, so actual budget documents and revisions must be examined before treating a reported framework as an effective commitment [2][4].

### Comparative evidence on performance budgeting and spending reviews

The OECD's 2023 Performance Budgeting Survey covered 33 countries and identified 28 that implemented some form of performance budgeting [5]. Among those 28, 20 used performance information to inform annual resource allocations, but only 9 used it to inform multi-year planning [5]. Twenty-four published performance reports, 19 regularly reported performance-budgeting elements to parliament, and only 3 reported structured engagement with civil society [5]. These are descriptive responses from senior budget officials, so they show use and accountability arrangements rather than the independent effect of performance budgeting on service outcomes [5].

The OECD's 2023 Spending Review Survey covered 35 countries and reported that 34 had used a spending review at least once, including 20 that used reviews annually and 9 that used them periodically [6]. Twenty-four of 30 countries with available information identified improving program effectiveness as an objective, while 16 identified expenditure control and 12 alignment with government priorities [6]. In 22 of 33 responding countries, review recommendations were presented before budget formulation began; 13 integrated recommendations into the upcoming budget, and 7 included them in multi-year planning [6]. The survey therefore shows broad adoption but also a gap between conducting a review and integrating its recommendations into binding resource decisions [6].

Taken together, the two OECD surveys show that governments increasingly produce performance evidence and review existing spending, yet integration into multi-year allocation and structured civic engagement remains limited [5][6]. The author's synthesis is that information is more common than institutional consequence: reports can be published without altering ceilings, appropriations, or program design [5][6]. The relevant test is whether the evidence enters negotiation before decisions and whether later documents identify the accepted changes [1][6].

### Global evidence on transparency, participation, and oversight

The International Budget Partnership's Open Budget Survey 2023 assessed 125 countries containing about 95 percent of the world's population and more than USD 33.5 trillion in fiscal-year 2022 spending [8]. Its locally led process used in-country researchers, peer review, and government review to score publicly available information, participation mechanisms, and oversight [8]. The global averages were 45 out of 100 for transparency, 15 for public participation, 45 for legislative oversight, and 62 for audit oversight [8]. These scores are comparative indicators derived from the survey methodology, not direct estimates of the causal effect of openness on growth, equity, or service quality [8].

Document availability varied across the cycle. The survey reported publication rates of 81 percent for executive budget proposals and 90 percent for enacted budgets, compared with 68 percent for in-year reports, 43 percent for mid-year reviews, and 65 percent for audit reports [8]. This pattern indicates that formal approval documents are more commonly available than some documents needed to monitor execution and close the accountability loop [8]. The survey also found that 83 percent of countries had at least one participation mechanism, while participation remained much less common during execution and audit than during formulation or approval [8].

The survey recorded another institutional risk: in almost half of assessed countries, governments used executive decrees to introduce fiscal policies while bypassing the normal legislative approval process [8]. The finding does not establish that every decree was unlawful or unjustified because emergencies and constitutional systems differ [8]. It does show why crisis spending, supplementary authority, and in-year changes require explicit reporting and later scrutiny if exceptional speed is not to become durable executive displacement of the legislature [2][8].

### Evidence on fiscal-rule effectiveness and institutional complements

The IMF's 2018 staff discussion note reviewed theory, case evidence, and a global panel of 142 countries from 1985 to 2015 [9]. Once the empirical analysis addressed the potential endogeneity of rule adoption, the estimated effect of adopting a fiscal rule on the deficit was statistically insignificant [9]. This caveat matters because governments that adopt rules may already differ in political commitment, administrative capacity, or fiscal conditions from governments that do not [9]. A simple comparison can therefore attribute to the rule what belongs partly to the institutions and preferences that produced it [9].

The note nevertheless found that design and supporting institutions matter. Its cases emphasized sound public financial management, political buy-in, budget planning, execution, accounting, reporting, transparency, and external monitoring as enabling conditions [9]. It reported compliance with budget-balance rules at about one-half over the preceding three decades, before correcting for escape clauses and contingencies [9]. It also identified trade-offs: complex flexibility can weaken observability, strict rules can encourage off-budget activity, and adjustment can fall disproportionately on capital spending when current outlays are difficult to compress [9].

The bounded conclusion is not that rules never work. It is that adoption of a numerical limit is an incomplete treatment whose effect cannot be separated from the annual budget, data, enforcement, and political institutions through which the limit operates [9]. The author's synthesis is that a rule constrains priorities only when the budget itself controls commitments, the reported aggregate covers the relevant activity, departures are visible, and a correction mechanism has political and administrative force [2][9].

### Evidence on legislative budget capacity

Wehner's peer-reviewed comparative study constructed an index of legislative budget institutions from variables covering approval of medium-term parameters, amendment authority, time for scrutiny, technical support, and restrictions on executive flexibility during execution [10]. The study used OECD and World Bank budget-practice data to compare institutional arrangements rather than assuming that the formal act of legislative approval measures actual power [10]. It treated executive withholding, transfers between appropriations, reserve use, committee structure, and access to audit information as part of the legislature's effective power of the purse [10].

The method's contribution is conceptual and comparative. It shows that budget authority is multidimensional and that two legislatures with the same constitutional power to approve a budget can differ materially in information, calendar, amendment, and execution control [10]. The index does not by itself establish that a stronger legislature always produces lower deficits or better services, because legislative preferences, party systems, and executive-legislative relations also matter [10]. It does provide an evidence-based framework for diagnosing where nominal authorization is weakened by procedure or by later executive flexibility [10].

### Evidence from public-financial-management diagnostics

PEFA's 2016 framework operationalizes the budget cycle through 31 performance indicators and 94 dimensions grouped under seven pillars [3]. It measures observable practices such as expenditure and revenue outturns, public access to documents, transfer transparency, budget calendars, medium-term estimates, predictability of cash, commitment control, internal audit, in-year reporting, annual accounts, external audit, and legislative scrutiny [3]. Its scoring approach supports repeated diagnosis and cross-country structure, but a PEFA score is an ordinal assessment against defined criteria rather than a causal estimate of development outcomes [3].

The framework is useful evidence because it prevents a single reform label from standing in for the system. A government can have a medium-term document but weak execution control, a published budget but incomplete extrabudgetary coverage, or a capable audit institution but weak legislative follow-up [3]. The author's assessment is that the disaggregated dimensions identify where the chain between authorization and outcome breaks, which is more actionable than classifying a budget system as simply strong or weak [3].

Across these studies, the common empirical result is institutional complementarity. Medium-term ceilings, performance information, fiscal rules, transparency, legislative powers, and audit each address a different failure, and none consistently substitutes for the others [1][3][4][5][8][9][10]. Evidence is strongest about the prevalence and design of practices and more conditional about causal effects on deficits, efficiency, or services [4][5][6][9]. That distinction should govern how reforms are interpreted: adoption is observable, operation must be verified, and outcomes require a separate causal argument [3][9].

## Implications

### For executives and central budget authorities

The executive should begin with a complete and reconcilable fiscal envelope rather than with uncoordinated bids. Forecasts, baseline conventions, aggregate ceilings, ministry ceilings, fiscal risks, tax expenditures, and prior-year outturns should use definitions that allow the proposal to be compared with later execution reports [1][2][4][11]. The central budget authority should explain major changes from the previous medium-term path and distinguish forecast revisions from new policy decisions [2][4]. This makes trade-offs visible before political negotiation and reduces the ability to create apparent room through changed classifications or assumptions [2][9].

Budget preparation should also include implementation feasibility. Capital projects require full-life costs, programs require staffing and administrative systems, and transfers require rules that receiving governments can plan around [3]. The author's assessment is that an allocation unsupported by procurement capacity, cash plans, data, or delivery institutions is not a funded priority in the practical sense; it is an authorized aspiration [1][3]. Finance ministries should therefore test both affordability and executability before the proposal reaches the legislature [1][3].

In-year flexibility should be designed rather than improvised. Rules should state thresholds for transfers, use of contingency reserves, emergency procedures, supplementary appropriations, and the treatment of revenue surprises [1][2]. Each material change should preserve a trail from original authorization to revised authority and final outturn [2][3]. During an emergency, faster procedures may be warranted, but temporary powers, reporting deadlines, coverage of off-budget vehicles, and a return path should be explicit [8][9].

The worst avoidable failure is to make the annual budget nonbinding. If commitments arise outside control, arrears substitute for appropriation, or public entities shift liabilities beyond reports, fiscal rules and performance systems rest on an unstable base [2][3][9]. The prevention is basic but demanding: comprehensive coverage, commitment control, reliable cash management, comparable classification, timely accounts, and enforceable correction [2][3].

### For legislatures

Legislative scrutiny should begin before the final proposal is tabled. Pre-budget debate can examine the fiscal envelope, baseline, major priorities, and medium-term constraints while choices are still open [1][8]. Once the proposal arrives, committees need sufficient time, relevant staff, access to assumptions, and authority to question both financial and performance information [8][10]. Amendment powers should be paired with rules that preserve the aggregate constraint, so democratic revision does not become an unfinanced collection of additions [1][10].

Approval should specify how much execution discretion the executive retains. The legislature should know which transfers require approval, which require notice, how reserves may be used, when a supplement is mandatory, and what happens if authorization lapses or is delayed [2][10]. The author's synthesis is that legislative power is incomplete when it applies only on budget day and not to material changes during the year [8][10]. Regular in-year review can detect whether composition, timing, or off-budget instruments are replacing the priorities that were publicly approved [2][8].

Legislatures should integrate tax expenditures into the same comparative frame as direct spending. Detailed estimates, legal authority, beneficiaries, objectives, duration, and evaluation plans permit comparison with appropriated programs [11]. The estimates will contain uncertainty, but omission is not neutrality; it gives a durable policy instrument less scrutiny because its cost appears as forgone revenue rather than an outlay [2][11]. Sunset or review provisions can create an occasion to revisit tax support that would otherwise continue automatically [11].

Technical support changes the balance of information. Parliamentary budget offices and other independent fiscal institutions can produce baselines, cost proposals, test forecasts, and monitor rules, while supreme audit institutions provide ex post assurance [3][7][10]. Elected officials should retain the choice among priorities, but they need independent analysis to contest an executive that controls most administrative data and forecasting capacity [7][10].

### For auditors and independent fiscal institutions

Audit institutions should connect legality to correction. Financial and compliance work should verify authority, classification, recording, and control, while performance audit can examine economy, efficiency, and effectiveness under the institution's mandate [3][8]. Reports should be timely and public, committees should hold hearings where appropriate, audited entities should provide formal responses, and later work should verify implementation [3]. Without follow-up, repeated findings become descriptions of tolerated weakness rather than controls [3].

Independent fiscal institutions should protect credibility through transparent methods, published assumptions, nonpartisanship, and clear distinctions between analysis and policy preference [7]. Their mandate should fit the local constitutional system and may include forecasts, baselines, budget analysis, rule monitoring, risk reports, or long-term projections [7][12]. Adequate resources and access to information are not administrative conveniences; they determine whether formal independence produces usable analysis [7].

These institutions should not be treated as substitutes. A fiscal council may identify an optimistic forecast before approval, while an auditor may later identify unauthorized transfers or unreliable accounts [3][7]. The author's synthesis is that ex ante challenge and ex post assurance are complementary because the first improves the decision and the second tests whether authority and reporting survived execution [3][7].

### For line ministries and program managers

Managers need enough flexibility to respond to operating conditions, but flexibility should be bounded by objectives, ceilings, controls, and reporting [1][3]. Detailed line-item control can prevent misuse while also slowing reallocation among inputs, whereas broad program authority can support management while increasing the need for reliable performance and cost information [1][5]. The appropriate design should reflect the task and capacity of the organization rather than copy a uniform level of detail [1][3].

Performance information should be used diagnostically. A missed target can reflect bad design, weak execution, an external shock, gaming, or an unrealistic target, so automatic reward or punishment may destroy information rather than improve it [5]. Managers should explain the causal chain from inputs to activities, outputs, and outcomes, identify what they control, and reconcile performance with financial outturns [5]. Spending reviews can then compare continuation, redesign, scale reduction, transfer, or termination rather than defaulting to uniform cuts [6].

A program should also be evaluated against alternative instruments. Direct expenditure, tax relief, guarantees, regulation, and intergovernmental transfers can pursue similar objectives while distributing visibility, risk, and administrative responsibility differently [2][11]. The author's assessment is that instrument choice should not determine whether a policy enters the budget debate; comparable fiscal exposure and public purpose should [2][11].

### For subnational governments

Subnational budgeting depends on the design of intergovernmental finance. Formula clarity, timely information, predictable disbursement, conditions, and reporting determine whether local governments can align services with authorized resources [3]. A central government that changes transfers late in the year can shift its adjustment burden downward even when the formal allocation of responsibilities does not change [3].

Medium-term information is especially important for services with staffing, maintenance, and capital obligations. The receiving government needs to know whether a transfer is recurrent, temporary, matching, conditional, or exposed to annual discretion [3][4]. The author's synthesis is that decentralization without predictable finance creates responsibility without control, while unconditional finance without reporting can weaken accountability for shared objectives [3]. Transfer systems should therefore combine transparent allocation rules with reporting proportionate to the discretion and risk involved [3].

### For citizens, civil society, and journalists

Users should follow the entire cycle rather than the headline proposal. The relevant document set includes the pre-budget statement, executive proposal, enacted budget, citizens budget, in-year reports, mid-year review, year-end report, and audit report [8]. Comparing them reveals whether an announced priority received legal authority, whether funds were released and spent, whether outputs appeared, and whether audit identified unresolved weaknesses [3][8].

Participation should occur before choices close and after execution begins. Communities can supply information about service access, local costs, distributional effects, and implementation barriers that central financial data do not capture [1][8]. Governments should publish what they heard and how it affected decisions, because attendance without a response trail can become consultation theater [1][8]. Special effort is needed during execution and audit, where the Open Budget Survey finds fewer participation mechanisms than during formulation and approval [8].

Journalists and civil society should distinguish transparency from accountability. A published spreadsheet can still omit tax expenditures, guarantees, extrabudgetary entities, or revisions, and a disclosed audit finding can remain uncorrected [2][3][11]. The useful questions are whether coverage is complete, definitions are comparable, authority is identified, changes are reconciled, and an institution can require a response [2][3].

### For analysts and reformers

Institutional diagnosis should begin with the failure mode rather than a favored reform label. If the binding problem is unrealistic revenue, stronger audit alone arrives too late. If the problem is weak commitment control, a medium-term framework may project arrears more elegantly without preventing them. If the problem is executive dominance, performance indicators do not replace legislative time, staff, and amendment authority. If the problem is opaque tax relief or guarantees, a stricter ceiling on appropriated spending can intensify incentives to move policy outside it [2][3][9][10][11].

Reform sequencing should therefore protect budget credibility first: coverage, classification, calendars, forecasting, authorization, commitment control, accounts, and audit [2][3]. Medium-term ceilings, performance budgeting, spending reviews, and fiscal rules can then operate on a budget that actually controls public decisions [4][5][6][9]. This is not an argument for waiting until every basic system is perfect; it is an argument for testing whether the proposed advanced instrument relies on data or controls that do not yet function [3][9].

Evaluation should distinguish adoption, operation, and outcome. A law can establish a fiscal council, a document can display a medium-term framework, and a ministry can publish performance indicators without any of them changing decisions [4][5][7][12]. Researchers should examine whether forecasts changed, ceilings bound, appropriations were respected, review options entered negotiations, audit findings were corrected, and participation occurred while choices remained open [3][6][8]. Only then should they test causal effects on fiscal discipline, allocation, service delivery, equity, or trust [3][9].

The final implication is institutional rather than macroeconomic. Public budgeting is the procedure by which political claims become ranked, financed, authorized, administered, and reviewed [1][10]. It protects democratic choice when the executive can assemble a feasible plan, the legislature can contest and authorize it, administrators can execute it predictably, independent institutions can test assumptions and records, and citizens can observe both the choice and the departure [1][2][3][7][8]. Without that chain, spending can occur, but priorities are not reliably binding and accountability cannot identify where the public decision changed [1][3].
