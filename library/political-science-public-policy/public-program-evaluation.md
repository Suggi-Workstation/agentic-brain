# Public Program Evaluation -- Causal Evidence Improves Policy Only When Design, Delivery, and Decisions Are Evaluated Together

Public program evaluation is the structured use of evidence to assess a public intervention's design, implementation, results, efficiency, equity, or sustainability [1][5][6]. It can determine whether a program plausibly caused better outcomes only when the evaluation defines the intervention, measures its delivery, constructs a credible comparison, and connects findings to an institution capable of acting on them [1][3][7].

## Background

Governments continually produce information about public programs, but not every information process is an evaluation. Performance measurement is the ongoing monitoring of progress toward pre-established goals, while program evaluation is a discrete inquiry that uses systematic data collection and analysis to answer questions about design, implementation, effectiveness, efficiency, or impact [5][6]. An audit may determine whether money was spent legally, records are reliable, or controls operated as required. An evaluation instead asks whether the program logic was plausible, whether the intervention reached and treated people as intended, what changed, what caused the change, for whom it changed, and whether the result justified the resources used [1][3][5]. These functions can share administrative records and performance indicators, but they answer different questions and should not be treated as substitutes [1][5].

The central difficulty is attribution. An outcome observed after a program may reflect the program, prior trends, economic conditions, demographic change, participant selection, another policy, maturation, measurement changes, or chance. A before-and-after improvement therefore does not by itself identify a program effect. Impact evaluation addresses this problem by estimating a counterfactual: the outcome that the same eligible population would have experienced, over the same period, without the intervention or under a defined alternative [6][7]. Because that state cannot be observed for the same unit at the same time, evaluators use randomized assignment, natural or administrative rules, comparison groups, longitudinal changes, thresholds, or other designs whose assumptions make the missing comparison more credible [3][4][7].

Program evaluation also asks broader questions than causal impact alone. Process evaluation examines whether essential elements were present, how services were delivered, what participants experienced, and why delivery varied [5][6]. Economic evaluation compares costs with consequences through cost-effectiveness, cost-benefit, or related methods [3][5]. Theory-based evaluation tests the proposed mechanisms connecting activities to outcomes, especially when a complex intervention cannot be represented by one treatment indicator [3][4]. Evidence synthesis combines multiple studies to estimate the consistency, limits, and contextual conditions of findings [4]. A complete evaluation portfolio can therefore explain both whether an outcome changed because of a program and why an observed effect was large, small, absent, uneven, or difficult to transfer [3][4][6].

A theory of change supplies the causal map that holds these questions together. It states how inputs support activities, how activities produce outputs, how outputs alter intermediate conditions, and how those conditions are expected to generate final outcomes [5][6]. It also identifies assumptions and external conditions that must hold. A cash benefit, for example, may be expected to reduce material constraints, change service use, and improve a later outcome; an administrative reform may work through shorter processing times, fewer errors, or more consistent frontline decisions. The logic model is not proof that these mechanisms operate. It is an explicit set of propositions against which process data, outcome measures, comparison designs, and qualitative evidence can be tested [5][6].

Modern governments have increasingly formalized evaluation as an institutional function rather than an occasional research project. The OECD's 2022 Recommendation calls for whole-of-government responsibilities, evaluation champions, professional and ethical standards, functional autonomy, accessible data, and mechanisms that embed findings in policy and budget decisions [1]. In the United States, statutes and executive guidance summarized by the Government Accountability Office link evaluation to agency learning agendas, evidence-building plans, capacity assessments, data reviews, and resource allocation [5]. These arrangements recognize that technical quality is necessary but insufficient: a rigorous report issued after the relevant budget, procurement, or legislative decision may have little practical effect [1][2].

Evaluation use is itself a political and organizational process. Officials decide which programs are evaluated, which outcomes count, what time horizon is acceptable, which comparison is legitimate, and whether unfavorable results trigger redesign, retrenchment, or further study. Weiss argued that use is broader than a single decision: evaluation can alter concepts, indicators, routines, questions, and organizational learning even when no recommendation is adopted immediately [13]. The OECD nevertheless reports persistent gaps between production and follow-through, showing why publication, review, and responsibility for responding to findings must be designed rather than assumed [2].

Ethics and legitimacy are part of evaluation quality. The CDC framework requires protection of dignity, rights, safety, privacy, and confidentiality, attention to power and context, and insulation from political or other undue influence [6]. The OECD likewise calls for integrity, independence, culturally appropriate practice, and safeguards for participants [1]. These obligations apply to random assignment, administrative-data linkage, interviews, subgroup analysis, and dissemination. A design that identifies an effect but exposes participants, hides material uncertainty, or excludes affected groups from relevant questions is not a complete public evaluation [1][6].

## Core Concepts

### Begin with the decision, question, and evaluand

An evaluation should start with the decision it is intended to inform and the evaluand, meaning the exact program, policy, delivery model, or component being assessed. The relevant question may concern need, design coherence, implementation, outcome achievement, causal impact, distribution, sustainability, or value for money. These questions require different evidence. A minister deciding whether to expand a pilot needs evidence about effect, delivery capacity, cost, and transfer to new settings. A program manager diagnosing low take-up needs process and administrative evidence. A legislature deciding whether to reauthorize a mature program may need a portfolio covering impact, distribution, cost, and alternatives [1][3][5].

An evaluability assessment tests whether the program is defined well enough, has a plausible theory of change, collects usable data, has sufficient variation or a comparison strategy, and can produce evidence before the decision deadline [5]. This step can reveal that the worst failure would be a precise answer to the wrong question. If the intervention varies radically across sites, a single average treatment label may conceal the object being evaluated. If the intended outcome cannot change within the observation period, a short follow-up cannot test the program's ultimate purpose. If assignment records are unreliable, a sophisticated estimator cannot reconstruct exposure. The author's synthesis is that evaluation scope should be reduced until the intervention, population, comparison, outcome, horizon, and intended use can each be stated plainly [3][5][7].

### Use a theory of change as a testable map

A theory of change should distinguish inputs, activities, outputs, intermediate outcomes, final outcomes, assumptions, and contextual conditions [5][6]. Each arrow is a claim. Funding may enable staffing, but staffing does not guarantee service quality. Service receipt may alter knowledge, but knowledge may not alter behavior. Behavior may change without producing the final social outcome if complementary institutions are missing. Making these links explicit determines which data are needed to diagnose a break in the chain [3][6].

The theory also separates program failure from theory failure. If activities were not delivered, a null final outcome does not establish that the underlying service would be ineffective under adequate delivery. If delivery was faithful and intermediate mechanisms changed but the final outcome did not, the causal chain may be incomplete or the horizon too short. If outputs and outcomes changed in places where the program was absent, the comparison may be contaminated by a common external force. Process tracing, contribution analysis, realist evaluation, and qualitative comparative analysis test mechanisms and contexts when a simple average-effect design is insufficient, although theory-based methods generally do not provide the same precise effect-size estimate as a well-identified experimental or quasi-experimental design [3][4].

### Separate monitoring, measures, and outcomes

Administrative data can provide enrollment dates, payments, case actions, service contacts, waiting times, sanctions, exits, and costs at a scale that primary surveys often cannot match. Monitoring systems can show whether a program reached its intended population, whether sites met delivery standards, and whether outputs changed after operational reforms [3]. These records are especially valuable when identifiers and time stamps permit linkage across agencies or long follow-up. The MTO evaluation, for example, used tax records to observe adult outcomes that were unavailable during earlier follow-up [9].

Administrative data also inherit the purpose and incentives of the system that created them. A field used for payment can be accurate for billing but poor as a measure of service quality. A closed case may mean successful completion, ineligibility, disengagement, or an administrative deadline. Changes in coding, eligibility, enforcement, or data systems can create apparent outcome changes without changing lived conditions. Data review should therefore test definitions, missingness, timing, linkage error, coverage, and whether measurement differs by treatment status [5][6]. The author's synthesis is that an operational field becomes an evaluation measure only after its meaning, provenance, and vulnerability to gaming are documented.

Performance measures need a hierarchy. Inputs describe resources; activities describe what implementers do; outputs describe immediate products or services; intermediate outcomes describe changes expected to lead toward the public objective; final outcomes describe the welfare condition the intervention ultimately seeks to alter [5][6]. Output targets are often available sooner and under greater managerial control, but they cannot be relabeled as impact. Processing more applications may be valuable while leaving access unequal or final outcomes unchanged. Conversely, a final outcome may improve while service quality deteriorates because an external trend moved in the same direction. Evaluation needs both levels and a design connecting them.

### Measure implementation fidelity and adaptation

Implementation fidelity is the degree to which delivery matches the program model. Important dimensions include adherence to core content, participant exposure or dosage, quality of delivery, participant responsiveness, and differentiation from other services [12]. Fidelity measures can use direct observation, records, interviews, staff reports, participant reports, and validated rating instruments. Multiple sources are preferable because implementer self-report can differ from observed practice, and delivery can vary across staff, participants, sites, and time [12].

Fidelity is not identical to rigid replication. Programs often need adaptation for law, language, staffing, geography, technology, culture, or participant need. The evaluation task is to distinguish core functions that carry the causal mechanism from forms that can change without destroying it. It should document what changed, why it changed, who authorized it, and how the modification relates to outcomes [12]. Without these records, a successful adaptation may be misclassified as noncompliance, while program drift that removes the active mechanism may be treated as faithful delivery.

Implementation data prevent a Type III error: concluding that a program model failed when the tested intervention was not actually delivered as specified [12]. They also prevent the opposite mistake of excusing every weak result as an implementation problem. If fidelity measures are defined after outcomes are known, analysts can select a favorable delivery threshold or exclude difficult sites. Core components, expected variation, and fidelity analyses should therefore be specified before outcome comparisons where possible. The strongest design relates measured delivery variation to the theory of change without conditioning naively on post-treatment behavior that the intervention itself affected [7][12].

### Identify causal impact through a credible counterfactual

A causal impact is a contrast between outcomes under defined alternatives for a defined population and time horizon. Randomized assignment creates comparable groups in expectation because eligibility or program offers are allocated by a known chance mechanism [7]. The intention-to-treat effect compares groups as assigned and preserves the protection of randomization even when some people do not take up an offer. An effect of actual participation may be policy-relevant, but estimating it requires additional assumptions because participants and nonparticipants can differ after assignment. The Oregon study used lottery selection as randomized encouragement and reported that selection increased Medicaid coverage by 24.1 percentage points, making the distinction between an offer and coverage explicit [8].

Randomization does not solve every problem. Attrition, spillovers, outcome measurement, nonadherence, selective reporting, treatment variation, and limited follow-up can still bias or narrow an inference [7][8]. Cluster assignment may be needed when services are delivered through schools, clinics, jurisdictions, or communities, but the effective sample size follows the assignment unit rather than the number of individual records. A stepped or randomized rollout can be appropriate when demand exceeds immediate capacity, but ethical justification still requires genuine uncertainty, fair allocation, legal authority, protection of participants, and a comparison consistent with ordinary public obligations [1][6][7].

Quasi-experimental methods seek a counterfactual from institutional variation rather than investigator-controlled assignment. Regression discontinuity compares units near a threshold when treatment changes at a cutoff and other outcome determinants remain continuous there. Difference-in-differences compares changes across treated and comparison groups under an assumption about untreated trends. Interrupted time series tests whether an intervention coincides with a change in level or trend not explained by another event. Instrumental variables use a factor that changes treatment exposure while affecting the outcome only through that exposure under stated conditions. Matching and weighting balance observed pre-treatment characteristics but cannot by themselves remove unmeasured confounding [3][4][7].

No method name certifies causality. Each design is a bundle of institutional facts and assumptions. A threshold can be manipulated, a comparison trend can diverge, an instrument can affect the outcome through another path, and a matched data set can omit the decisive cause. Evaluators should state what variation identifies the effect, show diagnostics tied to the assumption, test plausible rival explanations, and bound the conclusion to the supported estimand. Statistical precision quantifies uncertainty within the design; it does not include bias from an invalid identification assumption [3][4][7].

### Add costs, opportunity costs, and distribution

An effective program is not necessarily the best use of public resources. Cost-effectiveness analysis compares costs per unit of a common outcome when alternatives pursue the same objective. Cost-benefit analysis attempts to express a broader set of social costs and benefits in commensurable monetary terms. Other approaches can compare fiscal effects, cost consequences, or multi-criteria outcomes when monetization would hide important values [3][5]. All require an explicit perspective, time horizon, discounting rule, treatment of transfers, and distinction between startup, operating, participant, and external costs.

Economic evaluation should be built on a credible impact estimate. Dividing cost by an outcome change that would have occurred without the program creates a false cost-effectiveness ratio. Scale can also alter both numerator and denominator: fixed costs are spread more widely, but marginal participants may be harder to reach, delivery quality may fall, and system capacity may constrain outcomes. Sensitivity analysis should therefore vary uncertain effects, costs, duration, and scale assumptions rather than presenting one ratio as a physical constant [3][5].

Distributional evaluation asks who receives benefits, bears costs, gains access, experiences burden, or remains exposed to harm. An average positive effect can coexist with a negative effect for a vulnerable group, and equal formal eligibility can produce unequal take-up when information, documentation, travel, or waiting costs differ [5][6]. Subgroup analyses should follow a theory and address multiplicity rather than search every partition for a favorable result. Quantitative heterogeneity should be combined with process evidence about access, treatment, and context. Equity is not established by reporting demographic columns; it requires testing whether program operations and outcomes are fair, systematic, and responsive to populations that face different constraints [5][6].

### Test external validity and synthesize evidence

Internal validity asks whether the study identifies an effect in the evaluated setting. External validity asks whether that result applies to another population, institution, treatment version, scale, or time. Transport requires evidence about effect modifiers, delivery capacity, baseline risk, alternatives available to the comparison group, and contextual institutions [3][11]. Vivalt found substantial heterogeneity across impact evaluations and systematically smaller effects for government-implemented programs than for programs implemented by academic or nongovernmental organizations, even after controlling for sample size [11]. That finding does not prove that government delivery always performs worse; it shows that implementer and context are predictive information rather than noise to ignore.

Evidence synthesis reduces dependence on one study. A systematic review frames a question, searches for relevant evidence, assesses study quality, extracts results, and interprets the set using explicit procedures [4]. Meta-analysis can combine sufficiently comparable effect estimates, but its validity depends on included-study quality, compatible outcomes, and defensible inclusion rules. Rapid evidence assessments trade completeness for decision speed. Realist synthesis asks what works, for whom, in what contexts, and through which mechanisms, making it useful for complex public interventions whose implementation differs across sites [4]. Publication bias, selective outcomes, inconsistent definitions, and dependence among studies can still distort a synthesis.

The author's synthesis is that policy should rely on an evidence portfolio rather than a hierarchy treated as automatic. A credible randomized study may best identify a local causal effect. Process evidence may best explain delivery. Administrative data may best provide coverage and long-run follow-up. Qualitative work may best reveal mechanisms, burden, or unintended effects. Economic evaluation may best compare feasible alternatives. Synthesis should preserve these distinct questions while testing whether conclusions converge across methods that fail in different ways [3][4][6][7].

### Design evaluation for institutional use without compromising independence

Evaluation use begins before data collection. The decision calendar, responsible authority, primary users, publication rule, response process, and possible actions should be identified during scoping [1][3]. A budget office may need estimates before a spending review; an agency may need rapid process evidence during rollout; a legislature may require a longer independent impact study before reauthorization. Timing an evaluation after irreversible commitments invites symbolic use rather than learning.

Independence and engagement are complementary rather than opposing requirements. Affected communities, frontline staff, managers, finance officials, and decision makers can improve the relevance of questions and interpretation of context [1][6]. They should not control whether unfavorable findings are reported or alter methods after seeing results. Functional autonomy, conflict disclosure, pre-specified analysis, external review, publication by default, and transparent documentation of changes protect credibility [1][2][6].

Findings should be connected to an action protocol. Possible responses include continuation, modification, targeted expansion, additional measurement, a new comparison, pause, replacement, or termination. The protocol should state which result is decisive, which is diagnostic, and which remains uncertain. Weiss's broader account of evaluation use adds that concepts, indicators, and participation in the evaluation process can change later decisions even when the immediate policy is not reversed [13]. The author's synthesis is that evaluation becomes institutional learning when evidence changes a documented decision, operating rule, measurement system, or future question, not merely when a report is delivered.

## Evidence

### Cross-government institutions exist, but follow-through is incomplete

The OECD's 2023 survey provides comparative evidence on the institutionalization of public policy evaluation. Among 31 responding OECD countries, 26, or 84 percent, reported at least one institution with a formal mandate to coordinate evaluations across government [2]. Nineteen countries, or 61 percent, published evaluation results by default. Nine countries, or 29 percent, lacked follow-up mechanisms at line-ministry level to ensure that results were implemented and monitored [2]. The method is a cross-national institutional survey, so it documents reported rules and arrangements rather than the causal effect of those arrangements on policy quality. Its finding is nevertheless diagnostic: creating evaluation units and guidelines does not guarantee peer review, publication, or action.

The same survey found that only New Zealand subjected all evaluations to peer review, while 12 of 31 countries used peer review for some evaluations [2]. This supports the OECD Recommendation's distinction among institutionalization, quality, and impact on decisions [1][2]. A system can produce many evaluations yet lack professional standards, independent scrutiny, or a response mechanism. The author's assessment is that evaluation capacity should therefore be measured through the complete chain from agenda and data to quality assurance, publication, response, and verified follow-up.

### Implementation fidelity changes how null and positive results are interpreted

An Administration for Children and Families technical brief synthesized implementation research and reported that a review of more than 500 studies found mean effect sizes at least two to three times higher when programs had high implementation fidelity, especially adherence and exposure [12]. The underlying evidence spans programs and measures, and labels such as high and low fidelity can be site-specific; the brief therefore recommends reporting the full range of fidelity values and using multiple data sources [12]. The result does not prove that fidelity alone caused every effect difference, because better organizations may both implement well and produce better outcomes. It does establish that delivery variation is too consequential to leave unmeasured.

The brief also shows why implementation evidence must be tied to adaptation. Provider characteristics, organizations, participants, communities, training, and technical assistance can affect delivery, while local modifications can either preserve or remove core mechanisms [12]. A multi-site impact estimate without site-level implementation data can average together different interventions. Conversely, an evaluator who excludes low-fidelity sites after seeing outcomes changes the estimand from program assignment under ordinary conditions to receipt under selected conditions. The evidence supports collecting fidelity prospectively and reporting both the policy effect under real delivery and mechanism-specific analyses with their additional assumptions.

### The Oregon Medicaid lottery separated access, coverage, and multiple outcomes

Oregon's 2008 Medicaid expansion used lottery drawings from a waiting list, creating randomized access to the opportunity to apply. About two years later, Baicker and colleagues collected data from 6,387 adults selected by the lottery and 5,842 adults not selected [8]. Lottery selection increased Medicaid coverage by 24.1 percentage points, so the researchers used assignment to estimate the effect of coverage rather than treating voluntary enrollment as random [8]. The study combined in-person clinical measurement, utilization and medication information, and reports of financial exposure, illustrating an outcome portfolio rather than one success metric.

The evaluation found no significant improvements in measured blood pressure, cholesterol, or glycated hemoglobin over the first two years. It found increased diabetes diagnosis and medication, a 9.15-percentage-point reduction in positive depression screening, increased use of preventive services, and near elimination of catastrophic out-of-pocket medical expenditure [8]. These findings are not contradictory. Insurance can improve access, detection, mental health, and financial protection without producing a detectable short-run mean change in selected physical measures. The study's discussion notes limits from power, follow-up duration, population, geographic data collection, and the relatively small share of the system newly insured [8]. The case demonstrates why a program should be judged against a pre-specified set of mechanisms and outcomes, not one headline.

### Head Start showed early effects, later attenuation, and subgroup uncertainty

The Head Start Impact Study used random assignment in a nationally representative sample of 84 grantee or delegate agencies and nearly 5,000 newly entering eligible three- and four-year-old children [10]. Children assigned access to Head Start were compared with controls who could use other non-Head Start services selected by their families. This design estimated the effect of access in an existing service environment, not the effect of preschool versus no preschool [10]. It also separated age cohorts because the duration and timing of possible participation differed.

The third-grade follow-up found positive preschool effects across developmental domains but few impacts in kindergarten through third grade [10]. Early language and literacy effects largely dissipated, early health effects did not remain by third grade, and the pattern of social-emotional and parenting findings differed by cohort and reporter. Some subgroup differences appeared, but they did not create one simple universal effect [10]. The study's representative program sample strengthened relevance to Head Start as operated, while crossover to other services made the comparison realistic and narrowed the interpretation. The case shows that outcome horizon, counterfactual services, cohort definition, and multiple outcomes can materially change the policy conclusion.

### Moving to Opportunity revealed effects only after long follow-up and age stratification

The Moving to Opportunity experiment randomly offered housing vouchers to selected families living in high-poverty public housing, enabling an intention-to-treat comparison of voucher offers [9]. Chetty, Hendren, and Katz linked participants to federal tax data to observe earnings, college attendance, neighborhood, marriage, and fertility outcomes in adulthood [9]. Earlier evaluations lacked mature adult outcomes for children who were young at random assignment. The later administrative linkage therefore changed the available outcome horizon without changing the original experiment.

For children younger than 13 when their families moved, taking up the experimental voucher was associated with annual income in their mid-twenties that was USD 3,477, or about 31 percent, higher than the control mean of USD 11,270 [9]. College attendance increased, later neighborhood quality improved, and single parenthood declined. Children older than 13 at the move had, if anything, negative long-run effects, possibly reflecting disruption [9]. Adults did not show comparable economic gains. Pooling ages would have obscured positive estimates for younger children and negative estimates for older children [9].

The experiment randomized voucher offers, not the age at which children moved, so the exact age profile and duration mechanism require more than the randomization alone [9]. Take-up also means the treatment-on-treated estimate requires assumptions beyond the intention-to-treat effect. Even with those limits, the case establishes a broader evaluation lesson: follow-up length, administrative linkage, treatment take-up, and theoretically motivated heterogeneity can reveal policy effects hidden by an early pooled average.

### Cross-study heterogeneity limits mechanical scaling

Vivalt assembled impact-evaluation results across interventions and found substantial effect heterogeneity, with study characteristics explaining part of the variation [11]. Government-implemented programs had smaller effect sizes on average than academic or nongovernmental-organization implementations after controlling for sample size [11]. The design is cross-study analysis rather than random assignment of implementers, so the difference may reflect program selection, setting, implementation, populations, or other correlated features. It should not be used as a universal discount factor.

The finding aligns with the fidelity evidence and the cases above. MTO effects depended on age and follow-up; Head Start effects depended on horizon, cohort, outcome, and comparison services; Oregon's findings differed across physical, mental-health, utilization, and financial outcomes [8][9][10][12]. The author's synthesis is that scaling requires an explicit transport argument: which mechanism is expected to persist, which capacities and services differ, what population will be reached, what treatment version will be delivered, and what outcome horizon matters. Replication across contexts and systematic synthesis provide stronger evidence than assuming that a locally valid average is a policy constant [4][11].

### Evaluation can diffuse programs and reshape decision concepts

The Mexican Progresa/Oportunidades conditional cash-transfer program began in 1997 and became a model for programs in more than 60 countries, in part because its initial evaluation used an experimental design and later studies followed its effects [14]. Parker and Todd's review also applies multiple-hypothesis corrections when summarizing the evidence, illustrating how a mature evaluation record requires reanalysis and synthesis rather than repetition of early favorable findings [14]. The program's diffusion does not prove that evaluation caused adoption in every country or that effects transfer unchanged. It shows how credible evidence, a defined theory, and repeated follow-up can make a policy model legible to decision makers beyond its origin.

Weiss's research on evaluation use explains a second pathway. Findings may be used instrumentally in a named decision, conceptually by changing how officials understand a problem, procedurally through new indicators and routines, or politically as signals in a contested process [13]. This broader account prevents a false binary between a report that immediately terminates a program and one that had no effect. It also creates a stricter documentation need: evaluators should distinguish verified changes in decisions or practice from claimed influence that cannot be traced.

## Implications

### For policy designers: make evaluation part of the intervention

Evaluation should be designed while rules, rollout, data systems, contracts, and delivery responsibilities remain changeable [1][3]. The policy team should state the theory of change, primary outcomes, distributional questions, time horizon, intended comparison, data requirements, implementation measures, and decision date before launch. When capacity is limited or demand exceeds supply, a randomized or phased rollout may create both fair allocation and credible evidence if ethical and legal conditions are satisfied [1][6][7]. When randomization is inappropriate, eligibility thresholds, staged implementation, or jurisdictional variation may still support a quasi-experimental design if their identifying assumptions are protected.

The evaluation plan should include process, impact, and value-for-money questions rather than ask one method to do all work [3]. A process study can detect that sites lack staff before an impact estimate arrives. An impact study can show whether improved outputs caused final outcomes. Economic analysis can compare the resulting effect with feasible alternatives. Distributional analysis can reveal who was missed or burdened. The single worst outcome is a program locked into national scale with unusable baseline data, no comparison, undefined delivery, and only output targets; early design prevents that failure.

### For program managers: treat delivery data as causal diagnostics

Managers should monitor reach, dosage, timeliness, quality, participant response, staff capacity, and authorized adaptations against the theory of change [6][12]. These data should be collected at a level that supports site, population, and time comparisons without turning every performance field into a punitive target. If staff expect that a diagnostic field will be used mechanically for sanctions, coding behavior may replace operational learning. Data governance should therefore distinguish records used for payment or compliance from measures used to test mechanisms, while preserving auditable definitions.

A null impact should trigger a structured diagnosis rather than automatic continuation or termination. Was the target population reached? Were core functions delivered? Did the intermediate mechanism change? Was the follow-up long enough? Was the outcome measured consistently? Was the comparison contaminated? A positive impact requires the same discipline: did benefits arise from the intended mechanism, were harms or burdens shifted elsewhere, and can delivery quality survive expansion? Fidelity evidence should inform these questions without redefining the treatment after outcomes are known [8][10][12].

### For analysts: match claims to designs and expose assumptions

Every result should name the estimand, assignment or identification strategy, population, treatment version, outcome, horizon, and uncertainty. Intention-to-treat, treatment-on-treated, threshold-local, complier, and average effects answer different policy questions [7][8][9]. A confidence interval does not measure uncertainty from an invalid trend, omitted confounder, changed coding rule, or failed exclusion restriction. These vulnerabilities should be stated as assumptions and investigated with design-specific diagnostics, falsification tests, sensitivity analysis, and alternative measurements [3][4][7].

Outcome selection should be governed by the theory of change and public purpose. Evaluators should pre-specify primary outcomes, preserve secondary and unintended outcomes, and account for multiple comparisons. The MTO and Head Start cases show why theoretically motivated age cohorts and long follow-up can matter, but they also show the danger of interpreting many subgroup estimates without an explicit rationale [9][10]. Administrative linkage can extend follow-up and reduce respondent burden, but it requires verified identifiers, data rights, privacy protection, and analysis of linkage error [3][6].

### For budget offices and legislatures: distinguish impact from compliance and value

Routine audit and oversight remain necessary for legality, financial control, and accountability, but they cannot establish causal impact merely by confirming that funds were spent as authorized [1][5]. Performance targets can show progress yet cannot reveal what would have happened without the program. Legislatures and budget offices should request the evidence appropriate to the decision: implementation evidence for operational correction, impact evidence for causal claims, economic evidence for resource comparison, and audit evidence for compliance.

Value for money should not be reduced to low unit cost. A cheap service with no causal effect is not efficient, and a program with positive average net benefits can still distribute burdens unjustly [3][5][6]. Decision documents should present effects, costs, uncertainty, opportunity costs, and distribution separately before any summary judgment. They should also specify whether costs fall on the agency, another level of government, providers, participants, or the public. This prevents a fiscal saving from being reported as a social gain when it merely transfers time, risk, or expense to less visible actors.

### For scaling decisions: transport mechanisms, not labels

A pilot's brand name is not the treatment. Scaling changes implementers, caseloads, supervision, participant mix, complementary services, political attention, and cost structure [11][12]. The expansion decision should identify the core function supported by evidence, the delivery conditions present in the study, and the capacities required in the target setting. It should also state which contextual differences are expected to modify effects and how those differences will be monitored.

A reversible scale pathway uses staged expansion, replication, implementation support, and predefined review points. Early sites can test data systems and core delivery; later sites can test transport and capacity; synthesis can compare variation rather than erase it. When effects differ, the response should be to investigate population, treatment, implementation, and context before averaging. Vivalt's cross-study evidence and the MTO age pattern show why a single pooled estimate can hide policy-relevant variation [9][11].

### For evidence synthesis: preserve differences that matter

A review should define intervention, population, comparison, outcome, design, and context before combining studies [4]. It should assess risk of bias, outcome compatibility, publication selection, implementation variation, and dependence among estimates. Meta-analysis is appropriate only when the resulting average answers a coherent question. Realist synthesis or structured narrative comparison may be more useful when policy versions, mechanisms, and settings differ substantially [4].

The evidence table should separate internal validity from transportability. A small, well-executed experiment can provide strong local causality and weak evidence about government scale. A national administrative study can provide broad coverage and weak control of confounding. A process study can explain a mechanism without estimating an average effect. The author's synthesis is that the strongest portfolio combines designs whose limitations are not identical and reports disagreement as information rather than forcing one rank order.

### For ethical and equitable evaluation: protect people and examine power

Evaluators should document consent or other lawful authority, privacy and confidentiality safeguards, data ownership, foreseeable risks, conflicts of interest, and procedures for reporting harm [1][6]. Randomization requires a defensible allocation context and should never be used merely because it is methodologically convenient. Administrative data should not be linked simply because linkage is technically possible. Persons and communities providing data should know, where applicable, how records will be used, protected, retained, and shared [6].

Equity should shape questions and methods. Engagement can identify outcomes, burdens, and mechanisms invisible to central administrators [6]. Analysis should examine reach, take-up, treatment quality, effect, and cost across relevant groups while guarding against re-identification and undisciplined subgroup searches. Qualitative evidence can explain why identical rules produce different experiences. Distributional results should be presented alongside average effects and value-for-money estimates so that decision makers see trade-offs rather than inherit hidden value judgments [5][6].

### For institutional learning: attach findings to responsibility and follow-up

An evaluation system needs an agenda, capable staff, reliable data, independent quality assurance, publication rules, and a response mechanism [1][2][5]. Each completed evaluation should identify who must respond, by when, which recommendation or finding is accepted, what action follows, and how implementation will be verified. Publication by default and legislative or external review can reduce the opportunity to suppress inconvenient results, while functional autonomy protects analysts from pressure to alter conclusions [1][2][6].

Not every result should produce immediate policy reversal. A well-identified local effect may justify a bounded replication; an implementation failure may justify redesign; a precise null on the primary outcome may justify stopping; a wide interval may justify better data rather than a confident decision. The response should match the evidence rather than the political preference. Weiss's account of conceptual and process use also implies that learning agendas, indicators, and future designs should record how prior evaluation changed the institution's questions [13].

Public program evaluation therefore sits between policy design, administration, statistics, economics, ethics, and democratic accountability without collapsing into any one of them. It does not design legislation, replace routine auditing, or make statistical technique sufficient for public judgment. Its specific contribution is to create a traceable chain from a public purpose through a delivered intervention and credible evidence to a revisable decision. When that chain is complete, governments can learn not only whether outcomes improved, but what caused improvement, who benefited, what it cost, where it can travel, and what should change next [1][3][5][6].
