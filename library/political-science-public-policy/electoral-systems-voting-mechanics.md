# Electoral Systems and Voting Mechanics -- How the Rules of the Game Shape Political Outcomes

Electoral systems are the rules that convert votes into offices and seats. Their formulas, district structures, thresholds, and ballot designs affect vote-seat proportionality, strategic coordination, party competition, and who obtains representation [2][3][4]. Those effects are systematic but conditional: social cleavages, party geography, information, and the rest of the constitutional system can strengthen, weaken, or redirect them [2][3][22].

## Background

The systematic study of electoral systems as causal forces in
politics begins with Maurice Duverger, whose 1951 work Political
Parties advanced what became the most influential proposition in
comparative politics: the simple-majority single-ballot system
favours the two-party system, while proportional representation
favours multiparty systems. Duverger did not claim that electoral
rules mechanically produce party systems; he argued that rules exert
a "force, acting in the midst of other forces, some of which move in
an opposite direction" (Duverger, 1959, 217). His contribution was
to identify two distinct causal channels -- a mechanical effect and
a psychological effect -- through which electoral rules shape
political outcomes. The mechanical effect describes how the
translation rule converts votes into seats, systematically
over-representing larger parties and under-representing smaller ones
under plurality. The psychological effect describes how voters and
party elites anticipate the mechanical effect and adjust their
behaviour accordingly: voters desert hopeless candidates to avoid
wasting their votes, and party elites merge, withdraw, or decline to
enter races they cannot win [1][2].

Duverger's framework generated decades of research. Douglas Rae's
1967 study The Political Consequences of Electoral Laws provided
the first large-N cross-national empirical test of the relationship
between electoral rules and party systems. Rein Taagepera and
Matthew Shugart's 1989 work Seats and Votes refined the measurement
of electoral system effects, introducing the concept of district
magnitude -- the number of seats elected per district -- as the
single most important variable determining proportionality. Arend
Lijphart's 1994 book Electoral Systems and Party Systems
systematized the comparison of electoral formulas across dozens of
democracies. Gary Cox's 1997 Making Votes Count recast Duverger's
insights in game-theoretic terms, treating electoral systems as
posing coordination problems that voters and parties must solve
through strategic voting and strategic entry. Cox's framework
generalized Duverger's law beyond plurality systems and identified
the conditions under which coordination succeeds or fails across all
electoral families [2][3][5].

The field evolved through several intellectual phases. In the 1950s
and 1960s, the focus was descriptive: cataloguing electoral systems
and documenting their apparent effects on party systems. In the
1970s and 1980s, the focus shifted to formalization: deriving
Duverger's propositions from first principles and subjecting them to
rigorous empirical testing. The formal-theoretic work of Cox (1994,
1997) showed that Duverger's law describes one of two possible
equilibria under plurality rule. A Duvergerian equilibrium has
exactly two viable candidates; a non-Duvergerian equilibrium has
three. Which equilibrium obtains depends on voter expectations and
information -- a finding that explained why plurality systems
sometimes sustain multiparty competition despite the law's
prediction [2][3].

In the 1990s and 2000s, attention turned to mixed electoral systems, which combine plurality or majority districts with proportional seats. Shugart and Wattenberg's 2001 comparative volume asked whether these arrangements could combine local district representation with proportional outcomes; Bochsler's later analysis of 58 national MMP elections formalized how the size of the proportional tier constrains that promise [14][15]. Germany and New Zealand remain prominent MMP cases, but they use different rules for reconciling district winners with party-vote entitlements [16][18]. Germany's former overhang-and-compensation design produced 736 seats after the 2021 election; legislation adopted in 2023 instead fixed the Bundestag at 630 seats and made each district mandate conditional on sufficient second-vote support, a core mechanism the Federal Constitutional Court upheld in 2024 [16][17].

The 2010s and 2020s also brought renewed US attention to ranked ballots. Ranked-choice voting (RCV) is an umbrella description; in single-winner contests the commonly adopted rule is instant-runoff voting (IRV), while the single transferable vote is a proportional multi-winner rule [4][13]. Advocates expect IRV to limit vote splitting and encourage broader appeals, but the evidence does not establish automatic moderation or majority support among all original ballots. Studies instead identify conditional effects, campaign-civility signals, and ballot exhaustion when voters do not rank a continuing candidate [11][12][27].

The intellectual trajectory of electoral studies reveals a field
that has accumulated knowledge in the scientific sense: Duverger's
original propositions have been formalized, tested, revised, and
extended. What began as a comparative observation about the
association between plurality and two-partyism has become a
rigorous, theory-driven subfield with game-theoretic foundations,
cross-national datasets, and natural-experiment designs. The
fundamental insight has endured: electoral rules are not neutral.
They are among the most consequential institutional choices a
democracy makes, shaping everything from the number of parties to
the quality of representation to the stability of governments [2][3][4].

## Core Concepts

### Electoral Formulas: The Four Families

Electoral systems are commonly classified by the formula that converts votes into seats. International IDEA groups them into three broad families -- plurality/majority, proportional representation (PR), and mixed systems -- plus a residual category for systems such as the single non-transferable vote, limited vote, and Borda count [4]. The families are analytical categories rather than guarantees about outcomes: district magnitude, thresholds, ballot structure, and political context can make variants within one family perform very differently [4][5].

Plurality and majority systems may use either single-member or multi-member districts. First-past-the-post (FPTP) normally elects one member per district and awards the seat to the candidate with the most votes, even without an absolute majority. Two-round rules seek a majority through a later ballot, but eligibility for that ballot varies by jurisdiction and is not always limited to exactly two candidates. The alternative vote, or single-winner IRV, uses ranked ballots and sequential elimination until a candidate has a majority of continuing ballots. Block vote and party block vote show why it is inaccurate to define the whole plurality/majority family as single-member only [4].

Proportional representation systems aim to equate a party's seat
share with its vote share. List PR is the most common variant:
parties present lists of candidates, and seats are allocated
proportional to vote share, using formulas such as D'Hondt or
Sainte-Lague. Open-list PR lets voters influence which candidates from a party
receive its seats, while closed-list PR fixes candidate order through
the party; specific open-list ballots may use preference votes rather
than a full ranking. The single transferable vote (STV) is a PR variant used in
multi-member districts where voters rank candidates and surplus
votes transfer to lower preferences. PR systems typically use
multi-member districts with district magnitude greater than one,
which is the key variable determining proportionality [4].

Mixed systems combine a plurality/majority tier with a proportional tier. In mixed-member proportional (MMP) systems, the list tier compensates for district-tier disproportionality; in parallel systems, the tiers are calculated separately, so district bonuses remain [4][14]. New Zealand's party vote determines each qualifying party's overall entitlement, after which electorate winners and list members fill that allocation [18]. Germany also allocates seats by the second vote, but since the 2023 reform a constituency plurality does not itself guarantee a seat: the candidate must be covered by the party's second-vote entitlement in the relevant Land [16][17].

Other systems include the single non-transferable vote (SNTV),
where each voter casts one vote in a multi-member district and the
top M vote-getters win; the limited vote, where voters cast fewer
votes than there are seats; and the Borda count, where voters rank
candidates and points are assigned by position. These systems are
comparatively rare but illustrate the range of institutional
possibilities [4].

### District Magnitude

District magnitude (M) is the number of seats elected per electoral
district. Taagepera and Shugart identify it as a central determinant
of proportionality and party-system effects. When M equals one, a
party that wins 49 percent of a district vote may receive no seat
while the 51 percent plurality receives the district's entire
representation. As M increases under PR, smaller vote shares can
translate into seats; absent an exclusion threshold and depending on
the allocation formula, 10 percent in a ten-member district may be
enough for approximately one seat [4][5].

The relationship between M and proportionality is generally
curvilinear: the first increases above a single-member district can
produce larger gains than equal increases at already high magnitudes
[4][5]. The same change may also expand the number of viable parties,
but neither effect has a universal cutoff. Allocation formulas, legal
thresholds, district-level vote distributions, and the number of seats
in other tiers can make two systems with the same nominal magnitude
perform differently. Magnitude should therefore be analyzed with the
rest of the electoral rule rather than converted into fixed labels such
as "nearly plurality" or "highly proportional" [4][5].

District magnitude also affects the psychological effect. In small
districts, the threshold for winning a seat is high, so voters and
parties face strong incentives to coordinate around viable
candidates. In large districts, the threshold is low, so small
parties can win seats and voters have less reason to desert them.
This is why PR systems with large district magnitudes sustain more
parties: the mechanical effect (seats are proportional) and the
psychological effect (voters do not need to desert) reinforce each
other [2][3][4].

### Duverger's Mechanical and Psychological Effects

Duverger identified two causal channels through which electoral
rules shape party systems. The mechanical effect is the direct
mathematical consequence of the seat-allocation formula. Under
plurality in single-member districts, the candidate with the most
votes wins the seat and all other votes are wasted. This
systematically over-represents large parties and under-represents
small ones. A party winning 15 percent of the national vote spread
evenly across districts may win zero seats, because it is the
runner-up in every district. The mechanical effect is deterministic
and can be calculated from vote totals [1][2].

The psychological effect is the anticipatory response of voters and
party elites to the mechanical effect. Voters who support a party
they believe cannot win may vote for their second choice to avoid
wasting their vote -- this is strategic or tactical voting. Party
elites may merge with allied parties, withdraw candidates in
districts where they cannot win, or decline to enter races at all.
The psychological effect amplifies the mechanical effect: because
voters desert small parties, those parties lose even more votes,
which further reduces their seat share. The two effects work
together to produce the concentration of party systems that
Duverger observed [2][3].

The distinction matters for research design. Mechanical effects
can be studied by comparing vote-to-seat translation across systems
using aggregate data. Psychological effects require individual-level
or constituency-level data on voter behaviour and party entry
decisions. Cox (1997) demonstrated that strategic coordination --
the process by which voters and parties solve the coordination
problems posed by electoral rules -- operates in all electoral
systems, not just plurality. In PR systems, coordination takes the
form of alliance formation and list-position negotiation; in
plurality systems, it takes the form of strategic desertion and
candidate withdrawal [3][9].

### The Effective Number of Parties

The effective number of parties (ENP), introduced by Laakso and Taagepera, weights parties by their vote or seat shares rather than counting every party equally. Its formula is 1 divided by the sum of squared shares: a system with several similarly sized parties receives a higher value than one dominated by a single large party and several tiny ones [6]. The effective number of electoral parties (ENEP) uses vote shares, while the effective number of parliamentary parties (ENPP) uses seat shares. Comparing ENEP with ENPP can show how electoral rules concentrate representation, but the difference is not itself the standard measure of vote-seat disproportionality [6][7].

Electoral rules create tendencies in ENP rather than fixed numerical ranges. Single-member plurality usually constrains viable district-level competition, while larger district magnitudes and lower effective thresholds permit more parties to win seats; national party systems can nevertheless remain multiparty when support is regionally concentrated or coordination differs across districts [2][3][5]. ENP should therefore be reported for a specified election and level of aggregation, not inferred from the electoral-system label alone [3][6].

### Proportionality and Disproportionality

Proportionality refers to the degree to which seat shares match
vote shares. The most widely used measure is the Gallagher index
(Least Squares Index), which computes the square root of half the
sum of squared differences between vote shares and seat shares. A
Gallagher index of zero indicates perfect proportionality; higher
values indicate greater disproportionality. The Gallagher index is
preferred over the older Loosemore-Hanby index because it is more
sensitive to large discrepancies -- a few large vote-seat
differentials are weighted more heavily than many small ones [7].

No universal Gallagher score separates a proportional from a disproportional system. Scores depend on the election, the set of parties included, district structure, and the distribution of support [7]. Canada's 2016 Special Committee on Electoral Reform recommended that a proposed replacement system seek a Gallagher score of 5 or less, but that was a policy benchmark for one reform process, not a general empirical law that all PR systems satisfy [25].

Disproportionality has substantive consequences. When seat shares
diverge sharply from vote shares, the legislature does not reflect
the distribution of voter preferences, which can erode legitimacy.
Governments can be formed by parties that lost the popular vote.
Small parties are systematically excluded, narrowing the range of
political debate. These effects are most pronounced in plurality
systems with geographically dispersed minor parties, as exemplified
by the UK 2015 election, where UKIP won 12.6 percent of the national
vote but only one seat out of 650 [7][8].

### Strategic Voting

Strategic voting occurs when a voter supports a candidate other
than their most-preferred option to achieve a better outcome given
the expected behaviour of other voters. It is driven by the
psychological effect of electoral rules. Three main patterns have
been identified in the literature. First, desertion of hopeless
candidates: voters abandon a preferred but non-viable candidate for
a viable second choice. Second, compromising: voters support a
centrist candidate to prevent an extreme candidate from winning.
Third, balancing: in mixed or multi-tier systems, voters split
their votes across parties to achieve a preferred coalition outcome [3].

Strategic incentives are strongest when voters can identify viable
contenders and believe the race is close. Measurement is difficult
because an observed vote for a second-choice candidate can reflect
strategy, preference change, candidate withdrawal, or survey error.
Researchers therefore compare stated preferences with final votes,
model constituency viability, or exploit rule changes such as
Ponattu's natural experiment; each approach requires explicit
assumptions about information and counterfactual behavior [3][9].

Ponattu (2018) used the externally mandated shift from plurality to PR in UK European Parliament elections as a natural experiment. With a difference-in-differences design, he found that the reform increased the effective number of parties, produced a more proportional outcome, and increased new-party entry. The study therefore provides causal evidence for behavioral and entry responses in this setting, rather than only a cross-sectional association between rules and party systems [9].

### Gerrymandering and District Design

Partisan gerrymandering is the manipulation of electoral district
boundaries to advantage a political party. Two
core techniques are employed. Packing concentrates opponents'
supporters into a few districts to minimize their overall
influence, creating supermajority districts where the opponent wins
by unnecessarily large margins. Cracking disperses opponents'
supporters across many districts to prevent them from forming
majorities in any single district. Both techniques exploit the
winner-take-all property of single-member districts: in a
plurality system, a party that wins 51 percent of the vote in a
district wins 100 percent of the representation [21][26].

Gerrymandering is primarily a feature of plurality systems with
single-member districts, where district boundaries directly
determine electoral outcomes. PR systems with large district
magnitudes are less susceptible because seats are allocated
proportionally within districts, making boundary manipulation less
consequential. The term originated in 1812 when Massachusetts
Governor Elbridge Gerry signed legislation creating irregularly
shaped districts to preserve his party's majority; one district's
outline resembled a salamander, inspiring the coinage
"Gerry-mander" [4][21][26].

Empirical research has found that partisan gerrymandering
amplifies seat bonuses for the controlling party at the state level
in the United States, though national effects often partially cancel
out due to offsetting manipulations by opposing parties. A study
using simulated nonpartisan district plans found that gerrymandering
reduces electoral competition and increases the gap between popular
vote shares and seat shares, but that political geography (the
geographic sorting of voters) also contributes significantly to
disproportionality independent of intentional gerrymandering [21].

The interaction between partisan line drawing and political geography requires explicit counterfactual maps. Best and colleagues compared enacted 2022 US congressional plans with large ensembles of simulated plans and estimated an eight-seat structural and geographic disadvantage for Democrats and an additional two-seat disadvantage from partisan gerrymandering at the national level. Their study also found that enacted plans reduced electoral responsiveness by about 16 percent relative to the simulated nonpartisan baseline, even though opposing state-level gerrymanders partly canceled in the national seat total [21].

### Thresholds

Electoral thresholds are minimum levels of support required for seat eligibility. They may be explicit in law or implicit in district magnitude and allocation formulas; a five-member PR district, for example, usually creates a substantially higher effective barrier than a nationwide district [4][5]. Germany generally applies a 5 percent second-vote threshold, while the Federal Constitutional Court's 2024 remedy preserved an exception for parties winning at least three constituency mandates after finding the reformed threshold unconstitutional without that protection [16][17]. Turkey is a clear staleness warning: its national threshold was 10 percent for decades, but Law No. 7393 lowered it to 7 percent in 2022, and the new threshold applied in the 2023 parliamentary election [24]. Thresholds can reduce fragmentation, but they also exclude votes below the cutoff and create strategic incentives around expected eligibility [3][4].

## Evidence

### Duverger's Law: The Foundational Evidence

Duverger's original evidence was comparative and observational. He
surveyed party systems across democracies and found that countries
using the simple-majority single-ballot system (plurality in
single-member districts) invariably had two-party systems, while
countries using PR had multiparty systems. The United Kingdom,
United States, and pre-reform New Zealand exemplified the
plurality-two-party pattern. Belgium, the Netherlands, and the
Scandinavian countries exemplified the PR-multiparty pattern.
Duverger acknowledged that the relationship was not purely
mechanical: "a given electoral regime does not necessarily produce a
given party system; it simply exerts an influence in the direction
of a particular type of system" (Duverger, 1959, 217) [1][2].

Rae (1967) provided the first systematic cross-national test,
examining 78 elections in 20 countries. He found that plurality
systems produced higher disproportionality and fewer parties than
PR systems, supporting Duverger's law. However, Rae also documented
exceptions: plurality systems with regionally concentrated third
parties (such as Canada) sustained multiparty competition, and PR
systems with small district magnitudes could produce concentration
approaching two-partyism. These exceptions motivated the
refinement of Duverger's law from a deterministic claim to a
probabilistic tendency mediated by district magnitude, social
cleavages, and the geographic concentration of party support [2][5].

Riker (1982) traced the history of Duverger's law in political
science, documenting its formulation, testing, and reformulation.
He concluded that the proposition had survived extensive empirical
scrutiny, though with important qualifications: the law describes
an equilibrium tendency, not an iron rule, and it operates through
both the mechanical and psychological channels Duverger identified.
Riker's analysis demonstrated that political science can accumulate
knowledge: Duverger's original proposition, while not perfectly
accurate, was sufficiently precise and falsifiable to generate a
productive research program that refined and extended it over
three decades [2].

### The UK 2015 Election: Mechanical Effects and Causal Caution

The 2015 UK general election provides a vivid illustration of the
mechanical vote-seat effect of plurality rule. The
Conservative Party won 36.9 percent of the national vote and 50.9
percent of the seats. The Labour Party won 30.4 percent of the vote
and 35.7 percent of the seats. UKIP won 12.6 percent of the vote
but only one seat out of 650 (0.2 percent of the seats). The
Liberal Democrats won 7.9 percent of the vote and 1.2 percent of
the seats. The mechanical effect was stark: UKIP's 3.9 million
votes produced a single seat because its support was geographically
dispersed. The Conservative Party, by contrast, received a seat
bonus of 14 percentage points -- winning half the seats with little
more than a third of the votes [8].

The 2015 result directly demonstrates the mechanical effect, but later UKIP-to-Conservative switching cannot be treated as a clean measure of the psychological effect. Hobolt's analysis of the 2017 election reports that 57 percent of 2015 UKIP voters moved to the Conservatives and attributes the collapse of UKIP largely to Brexit: leaving the European Union had been accepted by both major parties, and the Conservatives offered the clearer hard-Brexit position [28]. Electoral viability may have contributed, but the observed switch combined policy realignment, candidate supply, campaign strategy, and electoral incentives. Ponattu's design below provides a more credible attempt to isolate an electoral-rule effect [9].

### Ponattu (2018): Causal Evidence for the Psychological Effect

Ponattu (2018) exploited a natural experiment to estimate Duverger's psychological effect. UK European Parliament elections shifted from plurality to closed-list PR in 1999 under a supranational requirement, making the reform plausibly external to the immediate domestic party contest. Using panel data and a difference-in-differences design, he found that the shift produced more effective parties, a more proportional result, and additional party entry [9]. The design identifies the combined behavioral and entry response to this reform; it does not imply that every plurality-to-PR change will have the same magnitude.

### Lijphart (2012): Consensus vs. Majoritarian Democracy

Lijphart's Patterns of Democracy compares 36 democracies across an executives-parties dimension and a federal-unitary dimension. Electoral-system disproportionality is one component of the first dimension, alongside party systems, cabinets, executive-legislative relations, and interest-group structures [10]. The design therefore evaluates packages of majoritarian and consensus institutions; it does not identify every observed difference as the causal effect of PR alone.

In the updated 2012 analysis, consensus democracies averaged about 9 percentage points more women in their lower or only legislative chambers than majoritarian democracies in both 1990 and 2010. Consensus scores were also associated with higher turnout, lower income inequality, and stronger measures of democratic quality, while the macroeconomic evidence did not show the broad performance penalty often attributed to consensus government [10]. These are comparative associations across bundled institutions, not a license to claim that changing only the electoral formula will reproduce every outcome.

### Ranked-Choice Voting: Evidence from US Adoptions

Ranked-choice voting is used for statewide elections in Alaska and Maine, the only two US states doing so in 2026, and in a number of local jurisdictions [13]. In the common single-winner IRV form, voters rank candidates; if no candidate has a majority of active first preferences, the last-place candidate is eliminated and ballots transfer to the next ranked continuing candidate. The process ends when a candidate has a majority of continuing ballots. That denominator matters because exhausted ballots no longer participate in the final round [12][13].

The empirical case for broad RCV benefits is mixed. The American Bar Association's 2025 review reports studies associating local adoption with an initial increase in candidate entry and some evidence of more civil campaigning, while also identifying limits and areas where more research is needed [11]. Formal and simulation work reaches conditional conclusions: IRV can suppress extreme winners under some voter distributions, but center squeeze can eliminate a broadly acceptable candidate before the final round, and one national survey-based analysis found IRV winners farther from the state median than alternative ranked methods in polarized settings [27]. Claims that IRV necessarily reduces polarization, elects the Condorcet winner, or eliminates every spoiler effect are therefore unsupported.

Ballot exhaustion is a documented limitation, not a universal failure rate. Burnett and Kogan analyzed images of more than 600,000 ballots in four US local IRV elections and found exhaustion rates ranging from 9.6 to 27.1 percent; in each case the winner had a majority of ballots remaining in the final round but less than a majority of all ballots originally cast [12]. The study's four cases do not establish the rate for every RCV election, but they show why reports must state the denominator and why ballot instructions, ranking limits, and voter behavior matter.

### Mixed-Member Proportional: The German and New Zealand Cases

Germany's electoral system combines constituency contests with proportional party allocation. Voters cast a first vote for a constituency candidate and a second vote for a Land party list; the second vote determines each qualifying party's national seat entitlement [16]. Under the system used through 2021, overhang mandates and compensatory seats preserved constituency winners and proportionality at the cost of a variable chamber size. The 2021 election produced 736 members [16][17].

The 2023 reform changed that balance. The Bundestag is now fixed at 630 seats, overhang and compensation seats are no longer created, and constituency winners are ranked for mandates only to the extent that their party's second-vote entitlement covers them in the Land [16][17]. The Federal Constitutional Court upheld this second-vote-coverage mechanism in July 2024 but rejected the 5 percent threshold as then designed; pending new legislation, it ordered continued protection for parties that win at least three constituency seats [17]. The 2025 election seated 630 members under the new rules. Germany therefore illustrates a concrete design trade-off: fixed size and national proportionality now take precedence over guaranteeing a seat to every constituency plurality winner.

New Zealand adopted MMP after referendums in 1992 and 1993 and first used it in the 1996 general election [18]. The party vote determines proportional entitlement for parties that meet the statutory eligibility rule, while electorate winners and party-list candidates fill the seats [18]. Official Electoral Commission material reports that Parliament became more diverse under MMP, with party lists serving as an important route for women, Maori, Pasifika, and Asian members [20]. Public acceptance was tested again in 2011: 57.77 percent of valid referendum votes favored keeping MMP, compared with 42.23 percent for change [19]. That was a clear 15.54-point margin, so describing it merely as a modest majority understates the official result.

## Implications

### For Democratic Design and Constitutional Engineering

The most direct implication of electoral-systems research is that institutional choices create recurring trade-offs rather than guaranteed outcomes. Plurality rules usually give larger parties seat bonuses and can make governing responsibility easier to assign, while PR usually represents vote shares and smaller parties more faithfully [4][7][22]. Lijphart found that consensus institutional packages combined broad representation with democratic-quality advantages and no general macroeconomic penalty, but his comparison bundles electoral rules with cabinet, party-system, and executive-legislative features [10]. Designers should therefore treat accountability and representation as dimensions to measure, not as fixed properties delivered by a family label.

Mixed systems attempt to combine local district representation with proportional party allocation, but the two tiers must be reconciled explicitly. Bochsler's model shows that an insufficient proportional tier can fail to compensate district results [15]. Germany formerly preserved district winners and added overhang and balance seats, allowing chamber size to grow; its 2023 reform instead fixed 630 seats and made district mandates conditional on party-vote coverage [16][17]. New Zealand normally starts from a 120-seat proportional entitlement but can add overhang seats when a party wins more electorates than its party vote warrants [18][19]. These choices show that proportionality, guaranteed district winners, and fixed chamber size can conflict; each MMP design must specify which constraint yields.

Threshold design is a second-order rule with first-order consequences. A lower threshold permits more parties to compete for representation; a higher threshold concentrates seats but excludes more votes and can induce strategic desertion near the cutoff [3][4]. Germany generally uses 5 percent, with the court-preserved three-constituency exception, while Turkey reduced its unusually high national threshold from 10 to 7 percent in 2022 [17][24]. These cases show why threshold claims require a date and legal context: a once-correct percentage can become a factual error after reform.

### For Policy Outcomes and Governance Quality

Electoral systems affect not only who wins but what policies result.
Research has identified several pathways. First, candidate-centred
systems (plurality, open-list PR) incentivize personal vote-seeking
behaviour, including pork-barrel spending targeted at home
districts. Party-centred systems (closed-list PR) incentivize
party-reputation-building, including national public goods provision.
The GSDRC review (Rocha Menocal, 2011) found that pork-barrel
politics is more prevalent in candidate-centred systems because
individual legislators must cultivate personal constituencies to
win re-election [22].

Second, the relationship between ballot structure and corruption is conditional rather than a simple closed-list-versus-open-list ranking. Chang and Golden found that district magnitude interacts with list type: corruption increased with magnitude under open-list PR but decreased with magnitude under closed-list PR, reversing the conventional comparison above a threshold in their estimates [23]. Other cross-national results remain sensitive to institutional endogeneity and corruption measurement. The defensible implication is that voter control, intra-party competition, district magnitude, party selection, and monitoring must be analyzed together; it is not accurate to state categorically that closed-list PR is more corrupt.

Third, electoral rules influence coalition formation, but they do not determine government stability by themselves. Single-member plurality often concentrates seats enough to permit one-party cabinets, while PR more often yields coalition or minority government because more parties win representation [4][22]. Lijphart's comparison found that consensus democracies could match majoritarian systems on broad macroeconomic performance while scoring better on several democratic-quality measures [10]. The defensible inference is conditional: cabinet type, party-system polarization, constitutional veto points, and coalition rules mediate the connection between an electoral formula and policy stability.

Fourth, electoral systems are endogenous institutions: parties help choose rules, and social cleavages and geographic concentration condition how those rules operate [2][22]. Before predicting a reform's policy effects, analysts should separate the mechanical vote-seat calculation from behavioral responses and from correlated institutions such as cabinet type, federalism, legislative procedure, and party organization. Natural experiments such as Ponattu's can identify a narrower causal effect; broad cross-national comparisons are better used to map recurring associations and boundary conditions [9][10].

### For Representation and Inclusion

Electoral rules affect who can obtain representation by changing party eligibility, district opportunity, and candidate-selection incentives. In Lijphart's 2012 comparison, consensus democracies averaged about 9 percentage points more women in their lower or only legislative chambers than majoritarian democracies in both 1990 and 2010 [10]. New Zealand's Electoral Commission reports that representation of women, Maori, Pasifika, and Asian members rose under MMP and identifies party lists as an important route into Parliament [20]. Neither comparison proves that PR alone caused every change: party recruitment, quotas, social change, and electorate design also matter. The supported conclusion is that proportional and list tiers can lower some entry barriers when parties use electable list positions to broaden recruitment.

District design affects representation through partisan bias,
electoral competition, and responsiveness, but intention and
geography must be separated. Best and colleagues compared the enacted
2022 US House plans with simulated alternatives and found that
intentional partisan gerrymanders were widespread but partly offset
at the national level. Structural political geography independently
disadvantaged Democrats, while the enacted plans reduced competition
and responsiveness relative to the nonpartisan baseline [21]. The
study demonstrates why map evaluation requires explicit
counterfactual plans rather than inference from irregular boundaries
or the national seat total alone.

### For Electoral Reform

US RCV adoption is a significant live reform program, but the evidence supports bounded claims. Alaska and Maine use RCV statewide, and local jurisdictions provide additional cases [13]. Existing studies support attention to candidate entry, campaign behavior, voter understanding, and ballot exhaustion; they do not show that IRV necessarily produces moderate, majority-preferred, or spoiler-proof winners [11][12][13][27]. Reform evaluation should therefore publish round-by-round counts, exhausted-ballot denominators, ballot-error data, and comparisons with plausible alternative rules rather than relying on the final-round majority label alone.

New Zealand's experience shows the political value of staged adoption and later review. Voters selected MMP over FPTP in the binding 1993 referendum, used it first in 1996, and voted by 57.77 to 42.23 percent to retain it in 2011 [18][19]. MMP changed party competition and broadened the routes through which women and minority candidates entered Parliament, while coalition bargaining became normal [18][20]. The case is evidence that reform changes incentives and representation over multiple elections, not that one institutional design automatically resolves every governance problem.

The broader lesson is that electoral-system choices are political because they allocate risks and opportunities among voters, parties, candidates, and regions. Rocha Menocal's review describes electoral design as a balancing exercise whose effects are mediated by party organization, social divisions, federal structure, and administrative capacity [22]. A sound reform proposal should therefore state its objective, model vote-seat consequences, identify behavioral assumptions, test distributional effects, and specify how outcomes will be audited after implementation. Electoral rules have predictable mechanisms, but their practical effects remain context-dependent.
