# Electoral Systems and Voting Mechanics -- How the Rules of the Game Shape Political Outcomes

Electoral systems are the institutional rules that translate votes
cast by citizens into seats held by representatives in legislatures.
These rules are not neutral transmission belts; they actively shape
party systems, the incentives facing voters and politicians, the
accountability of governments, and the stability of democratic
regimes. The central finding of electoral studies is that small
changes in electoral rules -- from plurality to proportional
representation, from single-member to multi-member districts, from
one round to two -- produce systematic, predictable differences in
how political power is distributed and exercised. Understanding
electoral systems is essential for understanding why democracies
govern differently and why the same democratic ideal produces
sharply different institutional realities across countries.

## Background

The systematic study of electoral systems as causal forces in
politics begins with Maurice Duverger, whose 1951 work Political
Parties advanced what became the most influential proposition in
comparative politics: the simple-majority single-ballot system
favours the two-party system, while proportional representation
favours multiparty systems. Duverger did not claim that electoral
rules mechanically produce party systems; he argued that rules exert
a "force, acting in the midst of other forces, some of which move in
an opposite direction" (Duverger, 1959, 217). His contribution was
to identify two distinct causal channels -- a mechanical effect and
a psychological effect -- through which electoral rules shape
political outcomes. The mechanical effect describes how the
translation rule converts votes into seats, systematically
over-representing larger parties and under-representing smaller ones
under plurality. The psychological effect describes how voters and
party elites anticipate the mechanical effect and adjust their
behaviour accordingly: voters desert hopeless candidates to avoid
wasting their votes, and party elites merge, withdraw, or decline to
enter races they cannot win.

Duverger's framework generated decades of research. Douglas Rae's
1967 study The Political Consequences of Electoral Laws provided
the first large-N cross-national empirical test of the relationship
between electoral rules and party systems. Rein Taagepera and
Matthew Shugart's 1989 work Seats and Votes refined the measurement
of electoral system effects, introducing the concept of district
magnitude -- the number of seats elected per district -- as the
single most important variable determining proportionality. Arend
Lijphart's 1994 book Electoral Systems and Party Systems
systematized the comparison of electoral formulas across dozens of
democracies. Gary Cox's 1997 Making Votes Count recast Duverger's
insights in game-theoretic terms, treating electoral systems as
posing coordination problems that voters and parties must solve
through strategic voting and strategic entry. Cox's framework
generalized Duverger's law beyond plurality systems and identified
the conditions under which coordination succeeds or fails across all
electoral families.

The field evolved through several intellectual phases. In the 1950s
and 1960s, the focus was descriptive: cataloguing electoral systems
and documenting their apparent effects on party systems. In the
1970s and 1980s, the focus shifted to formalization: deriving
Duverger's propositions from first principles and subjecting them to
rigorous empirical testing. The formal-theoretic work of Cox (1994,
1997) showed that Duverger's law describes one of two possible
equilibria under plurality rule. A Duvergerian equilibrium has
exactly two viable candidates; a non-Duvergerian equilibrium has
three. Which equilibrium obtains depends on voter expectations and
information -- a finding that explained why plurality systems
sometimes sustain multiparty competition despite the law's
prediction.

In the 1990s and 2000s, attention turned to mixed electoral systems,
which combine plurality and proportional elements. Shugart and
Wattenberg's 2001 volume Mixed-Member Electoral Systems asked
whether these hybrids offer "the best of both worlds": the
proportionality of PR and the local representation of single-member
districts. Germany and New Zealand became the paradigmatic cases,
and both revealed unexpected complications. Germany's Bundestag
grew from 598 to 736 members as overhang and compensation seats
accumulated with rising party fragmentation, exposing a fundamental
trilemma: proportionality, guaranteed district representation, and
fixed parliament size cannot all be maintained simultaneously. New
Zealand's transition from first-past-the-post to MMP in 1996
produced more proportional outcomes but also generated micro-parties
and coalition politics that the public had not fully anticipated.

The 2010s and 2020s saw renewed interest in electoral reform in the
Anglophone world, particularly ranked-choice voting (RCV), also
known as instant-runoff voting. Advocates argue that RCV elects
candidates with broader support, reduces polarization, and
eliminates the spoiler effect. Critics note ballot exhaustion --
votes discarded when voters do not rank all candidates -- can result
in winners who receive less than a majority of total votes cast. The
empirical evidence, drawn primarily from municipal and state-level
adoptions in the United States, remains developing but growing.

The intellectual trajectory of electoral studies reveals a field
that has accumulated knowledge in the scientific sense: Duverger's
original propositions have been formalized, tested, revised, and
extended. What began as a comparative observation about the
association between plurality and two-partyism has become a
rigorous, theory-driven subfield with game-theoretic foundations,
cross-national datasets, and natural-experiment designs. The
fundamental insight has endured: electoral rules are not neutral.
They are among the most consequential institutional choices a
democracy makes, shaping everything from the number of parties to
the quality of representation to the stability of governments.

## Core Concepts

### Electoral Formulas: The Four Families

Electoral systems are classified by their electoral formula -- the
rule that converts votes into seats. The four main families are
plurality/majority systems, proportional representation (PR)
systems, mixed systems, and other systems. Each family contains
multiple variants, and the variants within a family can differ as
much from each other as they do from members of other families.

Plurality and majority systems elect a single candidate per
district. Under first-past-the-post (FPTP), the candidate with the
most votes wins, regardless of whether that constitutes a majority.
Under the two-round system, if no candidate wins a majority in the
first round, a runoff is held between the top candidates. Under the
alternative vote (AV), also called ranked-choice voting, voters rank
candidates and sequential elimination transfers ballots from
eliminated candidates until one reaches a majority. These systems
share a common feature: they produce one winner per district, which
maximizes the potential for disproportionality between national vote
shares and seat shares.

Proportional representation systems aim to equate a party's seat
share with its vote share. List PR is the most common variant:
parties present lists of candidates, and seats are allocated
proportional to vote share, using formulas such as D'Hondt or
Sainte-Lague. Open-list PR lets voters rank candidates within a
party's list; closed-list PR lets the party determine candidate
order. The single transferable vote (STV) is a PR variant used in
multi-member districts where voters rank candidates and surplus
votes transfer to lower preferences. PR systems typically use
multi-member districts with district magnitude greater than one,
which is the key variable determining proportionality.

Mixed systems combine elements of plurality and PR. Mixed-member
proportional (MMP) systems, used in Germany and New Zealand, give
voters two votes: one for a district candidate and one for a party
list. District seats are won by plurality; list seats are allocated
to ensure overall proportionality. Parallel systems, used in Japan
and South Korea, also combine district and list seats but do not
compensate: list seats are allocated independently of district
results, so the overall result can be disproportional. The
distinction between compensatory (MMP) and non-compensatory
(parallel) systems is critical because it determines whether the
system achieves overall proportionality.

Other systems include the single non-transferable vote (SNTV),
where each voter casts one vote in a multi-member district and the
top M vote-getters win; the limited vote, where voters cast fewer
votes than there are seats; and the Borda count, where voters rank
candidates and points are assigned by position. These systems are
comparatively rare but illustrate the range of institutional
possibilities.

### District Magnitude

District magnitude (M) is the number of seats elected per electoral
district. It is, in the assessment of Taagepera and Shugart (1989),
the single most important variable determining electoral system
effects. When M equals one (single-member district), the system
maximizes disproportionality: a party winning 49 percent of the vote
in a district wins zero seats, and the 51 percent winner takes all.
As M increases, proportionality rises because smaller vote shares
can translate into seats. A party winning 10 percent of the vote in
a ten-member district can win approximately one seat.

The relationship between M and proportionality is not linear but
asymptotic: each additional seat yields diminishing returns to
proportionality. Moving from M=1 to M=5 produces a large gain in
proportionality; moving from M=15 to M=20 produces a much smaller
gain. This is why the effective number of parties (a measure of
party system fragmentation) tends to increase with district
magnitude but at a decreasing rate. PR systems with small district
magnitudes (M=2 to M=5) can be nearly as disproportional as
plurality systems, while PR systems with large magnitudes (M=10 or
more) are highly proportional.

District magnitude also affects the psychological effect. In small
districts, the threshold for winning a seat is high, so voters and
parties face strong incentives to coordinate around viable
candidates. In large districts, the threshold is low, so small
parties can win seats and voters have less reason to desert them.
This is why PR systems with large district magnitudes sustain more
parties: the mechanical effect (seats are proportional) and the
psychological effect (voters do not need to desert) reinforce each
other.

### Duverger's Mechanical and Psychological Effects

Duverger identified two causal channels through which electoral
rules shape party systems. The mechanical effect is the direct
mathematical consequence of the seat-allocation formula. Under
plurality in single-member districts, the candidate with the most
votes wins the seat and all other votes are wasted. This
systematically over-represents large parties and under-represents
small ones. A party winning 15 percent of the national vote spread
evenly across districts may win zero seats, because it is the
runner-up in every district. The mechanical effect is deterministic
and can be calculated from vote totals.

The psychological effect is the anticipatory response of voters and
party elites to the mechanical effect. Voters who support a party
they believe cannot win may vote for their second choice to avoid
wasting their vote -- this is strategic or tactical voting. Party
elites may merge with allied parties, withdraw candidates in
districts where they cannot win, or decline to enter races at all.
The psychological effect amplifies the mechanical effect: because
voters desert small parties, those parties lose even more votes,
which further reduces their seat share. The two effects work
together to produce the concentration of party systems that
Duverger observed.

The distinction matters for research design. Mechanical effects
can be studied by comparing vote-to-seat translation across systems
using aggregate data. Psychological effects require individual-level
or constituency-level data on voter behaviour and party entry
decisions. Cox (1997) demonstrated that strategic coordination --
the process by which voters and parties solve the coordination
problems posed by electoral rules -- operates in all electoral
systems, not just plurality. In PR systems, coordination takes the
form of alliance formation and list-position negotiation; in
plurality systems, it takes the form of strategic desertion and
candidate withdrawal.

### The Effective Number of Parties

The effective number of parties (ENP) is the standard measure of
party system fragmentation. Developed by Laakso and Taagepera
(1979), it weights parties by their size, so that a system with one
large party and several tiny parties has a lower ENP than a system
with several equally sized parties. There are two variants: the
effective number of electoral parties (ENEP), based on vote shares,
and the effective number of parliamentary parties (ENPP), based on
seat shares. The gap between ENEP and ENPP measures the
disproportionality of the electoral system: large gaps indicate
that the electoral system is translating votes into seats
inefficiently.

Typical ENPP values vary systematically by electoral system type.
Plurality (FPTP) systems typically produce ENPP values between 2.0
and 2.5, consistent with Duverger's law. Majority-runoff systems
produce values between 2.5 and 3.5. Mixed systems produce values
between 3.0 and 4.0. PR systems with moderate thresholds produce
values between 3.5 and 5.0. PR systems with low or no thresholds
produce values of 5.0 or higher. These ranges are empirical
regularities, not deterministic laws, and they are mediated by
factors such as social cleavage structure, the geographic
concentration of party support, and the age of the democracy.

### Proportionality and Disproportionality

Proportionality refers to the degree to which seat shares match
vote shares. The most widely used measure is the Gallagher index
(Least Squares Index), which computes the square root of half the
sum of squared differences between vote shares and seat shares. A
Gallagher index of zero indicates perfect proportionality; higher
values indicate greater disproportionality. The Gallagher index is
preferred over the older Loosemore-Hanby index because it is more
sensitive to large discrepancies -- a few large vote-seat
differentials are weighted more heavily than many small ones.

Electoral system families produce characteristically different
Gallagher index values. PR systems with large district magnitudes
typically produce values below 5. Plurality systems typically
produce values above 10 and often above 15. Mixed systems fall in
between. These differences are not merely statistical artefacts;
they reflect the fundamental design choice between
winner-take-all and proportional seat allocation. The Canadian
Special Committee on Electoral Reform recommended that any new
electoral system achieve a Gallagher score of 5 or less, a
benchmark drawn from the performance of established PR systems.

Disproportionality has substantive consequences. When seat shares
diverge sharply from vote shares, the legislature does not reflect
the distribution of voter preferences, which can erode legitimacy.
Governments can be formed by parties that lost the popular vote.
Small parties are systematically excluded, narrowing the range of
political debate. These effects are most pronounced in plurality
systems with geographically dispersed minor parties, as exemplified
by the UK 2015 election, where UKIP won 12.6 percent of the national
vote but only one seat out of 650.

### Strategic Voting

Strategic voting occurs when a voter supports a candidate other
than their most-preferred option to achieve a better outcome given
the expected behaviour of other voters. It is driven by the
psychological effect of electoral rules. Three main patterns have
been identified in the literature. First, desertion of hopeless
candidates: voters abandon a preferred but non-viable candidate for
a viable second choice. Second, compromising: voters support a
centrist candidate to prevent an extreme candidate from winning.
Third, balancing: in mixed or multi-tier systems, voters split
their votes across parties to achieve a preferred coalition outcome.

Strategic voting is most prevalent in electoral systems with high
disproportionality, where the incentive to avoid wasted votes is
strongest. It requires clear information: voters need reliable
polls or past results to identify which candidates are viable.
Strategic incentives are strongest in close races where the outcome
is uncertain. Measurement is challenging because it is difficult
to distinguish strategic voting from sincere preference change
using observational data. Survey-based methods suffer from social
desirability bias; comparing pre-election preference polls to
actual votes provides indirect evidence; experimental methods and
list experiments offer more reliable estimates but are
resource-intensive.

Ponattu (2018) used a natural experiment -- the exogenous shift
from plurality to PR in UK European Parliament elections mandated
by the EU -- to estimate the psychological effect. Using a
difference-in-differences design, he found that the shift to PR
significantly increased electoral support for small parties and
produced a one-unit increase in the effective number of electoral
parties. This is causal evidence that electoral rules change voter
behaviour, not just party strategies.

### Gerrymandering and District Design

Gerrymandering is the manipulation of electoral district boundaries
to advantage the party controlling the redistricting process. Two
core techniques are employed. Packing concentrates opponents'
supporters into a few districts to minimize their overall
influence, creating supermajority districts where the opponent wins
by unnecessarily large margins. Cracking disperses opponents'
supporters across many districts to prevent them from forming
majorities in any single district. Both techniques exploit the
winner-take-all property of single-member districts: in a
plurality system, a party that wins 51 percent of the vote in a
district wins 100 percent of the representation.

Gerrymandering is primarily a feature of plurality systems with
single-member districts, where district boundaries directly
determine electoral outcomes. PR systems with large district
magnitudes are less susceptible because seats are allocated
proportionally within districts, making boundary manipulation less
consequential. The term originated in 1812 when Massachusetts
Governor Elbridge Gerry signed legislation creating irregularly
shaped districts to preserve his party's majority; one district's
outline resembled a salamander, inspiring the coinage
"Gerry-mander."

Empirical research has found that partisan gerrymandering
amplifies seat bonuses for the controlling party at the state level
in the United States, though national effects often partially cancel
out due to offsetting manipulations by opposing parties. A study
using simulated nonpartisan district plans found that gerrymandering
reduces electoral competition and increases the gap between popular
vote shares and seat shares, but that political geography (the
geographic sorting of voters) also contributes significantly to
disproportionality independent of intentional gerrymandering.

Groll and O'Halloran (2025) linked partisan and racial
gerrymandering to electoral and legislative outcomes, showing that
redistricting creates systematic trade-offs between descriptive
representation (electing minority legislators) and substantive
representation (advancing minority policy interests). Concentrating
minority voters in majority-minority districts increases descriptive
representation but reduces their distributive leverage as pivotal
swing groups. This trade-off is central to the Voting Rights Act's
implementation and illustrates how district design choices encode
normative priorities about what representation means.

### Thresholds

Electoral thresholds are minimum vote shares that a party must
exceed to win any seats. Explicit thresholds are codified in law:
Germany requires 5 percent of the national vote (or three district
wins) for parliamentary representation. Implicit thresholds arise
from the interaction of district magnitude and electoral formula:
in a five-member district under PR, a party needs roughly 10-15
percent of the district vote to win one seat. Thresholds reduce
fragmentation by excluding the smallest parties, but they also
increase disproportionality by wasting votes for parties that fall
below the cutoff. The 5 percent threshold in Germany was designed
to prevent the fragmentation that characterized the Weimar Republic;
it has succeeded in limiting party fragmentation while allowing
five or six parties to compete. Thresholds create strategic
incentives similar to those in plurality systems: voters may desert
parties polling near the threshold to avoid wasting their votes.

## Evidence

### Duverger's Law: The Foundational Evidence

Duverger's original evidence was comparative and observational. He
surveyed party systems across democracies and found that countries
using the simple-majority single-ballot system (plurality in
single-member districts) invariably had two-party systems, while
countries using PR had multiparty systems. The United Kingdom,
United States, and pre-reform New Zealand exemplified the
plurality-two-party pattern. Belgium, the Netherlands, and the
Scandinavian countries exemplified the PR-multiparty pattern.
Duverger acknowledged that the relationship was not purely
mechanical: "a given electoral regime does not necessarily produce a
given party system; it simply exerts an influence in the direction
of a particular type of system" (Duverger, 1959, 217).

Rae (1967) provided the first systematic cross-national test,
examining 78 elections in 20 countries. He found that plurality
systems produced higher disproportionality and fewer parties than
PR systems, supporting Duverger's law. However, Rae also documented
exceptions: plurality systems with regionally concentrated third
parties (such as Canada) sustained multiparty competition, and PR
systems with small district magnitudes could produce concentration
approaching two-partyism. These exceptions motivated the
refinement of Duverger's law from a deterministic claim to a
probabilistic tendency mediated by district magnitude, social
cleavages, and the geographic concentration of party support.

Riker (1982) traced the history of Duverger's law in political
science, documenting its formulation, testing, and reformulation.
He concluded that the proposition had survived extensive empirical
scrutiny, though with important qualifications: the law describes
an equilibrium tendency, not an iron rule, and it operates through
both the mechanical and psychological channels Duverger identified.
Riker's analysis demonstrated that political science can accumulate
knowledge: Duverger's original proposition, while not perfectly
accurate, was sufficiently precise and falsifiable to generate a
productive research program that refined and extended it over
three decades.

### The UK 2015 Election: Mechanical and Psychological Effects in Action

The 2015 UK general election provides a vivid illustration of both
the mechanical and psychological effects of plurality rule. The
Conservative Party won 36.9 percent of the national vote and 50.9
percent of the seats. The Labour Party won 30.4 percent of the vote
and 35.7 percent of the seats. UKIP won 12.6 percent of the vote
but only one seat out of 650 (0.2 percent of the seats). The
Liberal Democrats won 7.9 percent of the vote and 1.2 percent of
the seats. The mechanical effect was stark: UKIP's 3.9 million
votes produced a single seat because its support was geographically
dispersed. The Conservative Party, by contrast, received a seat
bonus of 14 percentage points -- winning half the seats with little
more than a third of the votes.

The psychological effect manifested in subsequent elections. Having
seen UKIP win nearly four million votes but only one seat, many UKIP
supporters concluded that voting for the party was a wasted act. In
subsequent elections, a substantial portion of UKIP voters shifted
to the Conservative Party, which had adopted positions on Brexit and
immigration that overlapped with UKIP's platform. This strategic
migration illustrates Duverger's psychological effect: voters
deserted a small party not because their preferences changed but
because the electoral system made supporting it futile.

### Ponattu (2018): Causal Evidence for the Psychological Effect

Ponattu (2018) exploited a natural experiment to provide causal
evidence for Duverger's psychological effect. UK European Parliament
elections shifted from plurality to closed-list PR in 1999, mandated
by the EU against UK opposition, making the reform exogenous to
domestic political dynamics. Using panel data from 1979 to 2004 and
a difference-in-differences design (with EP elections as treatment
and local elections as control), Ponattu found that the shift to PR
significantly increased electoral support for small parties and
produced a one-unit increase in the effective number of electoral
parties. The reform also caused more new parties to enter electoral
competition. These findings demonstrate that the psychological effect
is causal: when the electoral system changes, voter behaviour changes
in the direction Duverger predicted, even holding constant other
factors.

### Lijphart (1999): Consensus vs. Majoritarian Democracy

Lijphart's Patterns of Democracy compared 36 democracies along ten
institutional dimensions, organized into two clusters: the
executives-parties dimension (covering party systems, cabinet
composition, electoral systems, and interest groups) and the
federal-unitary dimension (covering territorial distribution of
power, legislative structure, and constitutional rigidity). He
found that consensus democracies -- which use PR, multiparty
coalitions, and power-sharing -- consistently outperformed
majoritarian democracies on measures of democratic quality,
including minority representation, women's representation, voter
turnout, and income equality. Critically, Lijphart found no
trade-off between democratic quality and government effectiveness:
consensus democracies managed economic growth, inflation, and
unemployment as well as or better than majoritarian democracies.

Lijphart's comparative study of 13 parliamentary democracies (four
plurality, nine PR) found that PR parliaments had approximately four
times more women, 10 percentage points higher voter turnout, and
greater income equality. On economic management -- growth, inflation,
unemployment -- he found no significant differences, except on
unemployment, where PR countries performed better. This finding
directly challenges the conventional wisdom that plurality systems
produce more effective governance; it suggests that the
accountability advantage of plurality is offset by the
representativeness advantage of PR without a cost in economic
performance.

### Ranked-Choice Voting: Evidence from US Adoptions

Ranked-choice voting (RCV), also called instant-runoff voting, has
been adopted by two US states (Alaska and Maine) and over 50 local
jurisdictions. In instant-runoff voting, if no candidate receives a
majority of first-preference votes, the candidate with the fewest
ballots is eliminated and their ballots are redistributed to voters'
next preferences. The process repeats until one candidate exceeds 50
percent. Proponents argue that RCV elects candidates with broader
support, reduces polarization, and eliminates the spoiler effect
that can occur when multiple candidates split the vote.

The American Bar Association's 2025 review of RCV evidence found
that RCV increases the number and diversity of candidates, gives
voters more choices (especially independents), and may reduce
polarization by electing more consensus-oriented candidates. A study
of Alaska's top-four primary and RCV general election found more
moderate winners and more moderate policy outcomes compared to the
previous plurality system. Research from Italy and Brazil on
two-round systems (which share RCV's majority requirement) shows
that runoff systems induce more candidates to run, reduce strategic
voting, and produce more moderate platforms.

However, RCV faces a documented limitation: ballot exhaustion.
Analysis of over 600,000 ballots from four US local RCV elections
found that substantial numbers of votes were discarded when voters
did not rank all candidates. As a result, the winner in all four
cases received less than a majority of total votes cast. This
finding challenges the claim that RCV always produces majority
winners and raises concerns about representativeness when voters
fail to complete full rankings.

### Mixed-Member Proportional: The German and New Zealand Cases

Germany's MMP system, adopted in 1949 and modelled on the Weimar
Republic's PR system with added district representation, gives each
voter two votes: a constituency vote (first vote) and a party list
vote (second vote). The party vote determines the overall seat
distribution, with district wins subtracted from the party's total
entitlement. This creates a compensatory system: list seats
compensate for disproportionalities in the district tier. The
system was long praised as offering "the best of both worlds"
(Shugart and Wattenberg, 2001): high proportionality with direct
district representation.

However, rising party fragmentation exposed a fundamental trilemma.
As Germany's party system fragmented from 3-4 parties to 6-7,
mainstream parties (CDU/CSU and SPD) continued winning most district
seats but their declining party vote share generated insufficient
list seats to cover them. This produced growing numbers of overhang
and compensation seats, expanding the Bundestag from its statutory
598 members to 736 after the 2021 election. The constitutional
court ruled in 2012 that overhang seats above a limit must be
compensated to preserve proportionality, accelerating the growth.
In 2023, the governing coalition passed reforms that strengthen
proportionality but weaken district representation. This case
demonstrates that MMP systems cannot simultaneously guarantee
proportionality, district representation, and fixed parliament size
when party fragmentation increases.

New Zealand's transition from FPTP to MMP in 1996, following
referendums in 1992 and 1993, provides a quasi-experimental case.
Under FPTP, New Zealand was a quintessential Westminster
majoritarian democracy with two dominant parties and single-party
majority governments. After MMP, the effective number of parties
increased, coalition or minority governments became standard, and
representation of women and Maori improved substantially. The
average vote won by the majority party decreased from 41.7 percent
under FPTP to 33.5 percent under MMP. However, the effective number
of parties in New Zealand has generally declined over time --
contrary to the German pattern -- due to the two major parties'
continued dominance of district seats and the persistence of
micro-parties enabled by New Zealand's alternative threshold (one
district win suffices for list representation). A 2011 referendum
on whether to retain MMP passed with only a modest majority,
indicating public ambivalence about the system's effects.

## Implications

### For Democratic Design and Constitutional Engineering

The most direct implication of electoral systems research is that
institutional design choices are consequential and predictable.
Countries adopting or reforming electoral systems face fundamental
trade-offs that the research literature has mapped with
considerable precision. The choice between plurality and PR
involves a trade-off between accountability and representativeness.
Plurality systems produce clearer accountability: voters know which
party governs and can reward or punish it directly. PR systems
produce better representativeness: legislatures mirror the
distribution of voter preferences more faithfully, and minority
groups gain voice. Lijphart's evidence suggests that this trade-off
is not as severe as commonly believed: PR systems do not sacrifice
economic performance for representativeness.

Mixed systems attempt to capture both benefits, but the German
experience reveals that the combination generates its own
constraints. The MMP trilemma -- proportionality, district
representation, and fixed parliament size -- is not a design flaw
but a structural feature of mixing winner-take-all district seats
with proportional list seats. Countries considering MMP adoption
must decide which of the three goals to sacrifice when party
fragmentation increases. New Zealand's experience suggests that the
choice depends on political culture: a country accustomed to
single-party majority government may find coalition politics
disorienting even when the system produces better representation.

The design of thresholds is a second-order choice with
first-order consequences. A threshold that is too low allows
fragmentation that can produce unstable coalition governments; a
threshold that is too high excludes legitimate parties and wastes
votes. Germany's 5 percent threshold was designed to prevent Weimar-
style fragmentation and has succeeded, but it has also been
criticized for excluding parties with significant but sub-threshold
support. Turkey's 10 percent threshold -- the highest among
democracies -- has been criticized for severely under-representing
large segments of the electorate, including the Kurdish minority.
The choice of threshold encodes a judgment about the acceptable
level of fragmentation and the value of inclusion.

### For Policy Outcomes and Governance Quality

Electoral systems affect not only who wins but what policies result.
Research has identified several pathways. First, candidate-centred
systems (plurality, open-list PR) incentivize personal vote-seeking
behaviour, including pork-barrel spending targeted at home
districts. Party-centred systems (closed-list PR) incentivize
party-reputation-building, including national public goods provision.
The GSDRC review (Rocha Menocal, 2011) found that pork-barrel
politics is more prevalent in candidate-centred systems because
individual legislators must cultivate personal constituencies to
win re-election.

Second, closed-list PR systems appear more susceptible to corruption
because they weaken the link between individual performance and
re-election, giving party leaders excessive control over candidate
selection and making monitoring difficult. Open-list PR and
plurality systems, where voters can punish individual
underperformers, provide stronger accountability mechanisms. This
finding illustrates a general principle: electoral systems that
centralize candidate selection in party leadership reduce voter
control and increase the potential for rent-seeking.

Third, electoral systems shape coalition formation and government
stability. Plurality systems typically produce single-party majority
governments, which can govern decisively but may alternate sharply
when power changes hands. PR systems typically produce coalition
governments, which may be more moderate and consensus-oriented but
also more fragile. Lijphart's evidence suggests that coalition
governments in consensus democracies tend to produce more stable,
incremental policy than the sharp oscillations of majoritarian
systems. The trade-off between decisiveness and stability is a
direct consequence of electoral system choice.

Fourth, research on electoral systems and immigration policy found
that countries using sufficient plurality systems tend to adopt more
restrictive immigration policies than those using necessary majority
systems (PR or two-round). This finding connects electoral rules to
substantive policy outcomes through the mechanism of how much voter
support is needed to win power: when a plurality suffices, parties
target the median voter, who may favour restriction; when a
majority is required, parties must build broader coalitions that
include immigrant communities and their allies.

### For Representation and Inclusion

Electoral systems determine who gets represented. PR systems
consistently produce higher representation of women, ethnic
minorities, and minor parties than plurality systems. Lijphart found
that PR parliaments had approximately four times more women than
plurality parliaments. New Zealand's MMP increased women's
representation from 21.6 percent under FPTP to over 30 percent
under MMP, and increased Maori representation substantially. These
effects arise because PR systems lower the barrier to entry for
underrepresented groups: list positions can be allocated to diverse
candidates, and multi-member districts allow multiple candidates
from different groups to win.

Gerrymandering intersects with representation in complex ways. The
creation of majority-minority districts under the US Voting Rights
Act increased descriptive representation by electing minority
legislators. However, Groll and O'Halloran (2025) showed that this
comes at a cost: concentrating minority voters in packed districts
reduces their influence as pivotal swing voters in other districts,
potentially reducing substantive policy representation. The
trade-off between descriptive and substantive representation is
mediated by district design choices that electoral systems make
possible. This finding has implications beyond the US context: any
democracy using single-member districts must confront how boundary
drawing affects the representation of geographically concentrated
minorities.

### For Electoral Reform

The evidence on electoral systems has direct implications for reform
movements. RCV adoption in the United States represents the most
significant electoral reform experiment in a major democracy in
recent decades. The evidence suggests that RCV can reduce the
spoiler effect, increase candidate diversity, and potentially
reduce polarization. However, ballot exhaustion remains a
documented limitation that reformers must address through voter
education and ballot design. The Alaska experiment -- combining a
top-four primary with RCV general election -- produced more moderate
winners, suggesting that the combination of primary reform and
electoral reform can shift outcomes in ways that neither reform
alone achieves.

New Zealand's reform experience offers lessons about the political
feasibility of electoral change. The shift from FPTP to MMP was
driven by public dissatisfaction with the distortions of the old
system, institutionalized through a two-stage referendum process
that gave voters a direct choice. The reform produced better
representation but also generated unanticipated consequences
(micro-parties, coalition instability) that generated public
ambivalence. The 2011 referendum to retain MMP passed with only a
modest majority, illustrating that electoral reform is not a
one-time event but an ongoing process of institutional adjustment.

The broader lesson is that electoral system choices are not
technical but political. As Rocha Menocal (2011) concluded, the
design of constitutional structures and electoral rules is a
balancing act that produces a wide range of both problems and
solutions. The effects of changes are likely to be incremental but
can be quite important over time. Context matters: societal
cleavages, federal versus unitary structures, and the quality of
political parties mediate the effects of electoral rules.
Understanding these interactions is essential for constitutional
engineers, reform advocates, and citizens evaluating proposals for
change.
