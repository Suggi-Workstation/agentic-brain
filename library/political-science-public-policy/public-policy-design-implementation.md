# Public Policy Design and Implementation -- Why Legislative Intent Rarely Survives Contact With the Implementation Pipeline

Public policy design and implementation is the study of how legislative intent travels through a chain of administrative, organizational, and discretionary stages to produce observable outcomes. The field emerged from the discovery that policy adoption -- the passage of a law or issuance of a mandate -- is not the endpoint of the policy process but merely its midpoint. Implementation theory, street-level bureaucracy research, policy instrument analysis, and evidence-based evaluation together form a body of knowledge explaining why well-intentioned policies routinely produce outcomes that diverge from their stated goals. The central finding of four decades of research is that the gap between policy intent and policy outcome is not an anomaly but a structural feature of governance under complexity.

## Background

The systematic study of policy implementation began with Jeffrey Pressman and Aaron Wildavsky's 1973 book Implementation, which examined the Economic Development Administration's (EDA) public works program in Oakland, California. The Oakland Project was a federally funded, USD 23 million employment program announced in 1966 to address concentrated unemployment among inner-city Black residents. The program enjoyed broad political support, was well funded, and had clear statutory authority. By every measure of policy adoption, it should have succeeded. It did not. Pressman and Wildavsky documented how the project stalled across multiple decision points, each requiring agreement from different actors with different perspectives and different intensities of commitment. Their analysis revealed that implementation success depends on the multiplication of probabilities across a chain of decision points: if each of 70 clearance points has an 80 percent probability of agreement, the overall probability of completion is less than one in a million. Even at an unrealistically high 99 percent per point, the odds drop to roughly one in two. The book's subtitle captured the finding: How Great Expectations in Washington Are Dashed in Oakland (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973).

The Oakland study did more than document a single failure. It established implementation as a distinct field of inquiry within policy studies. Before Implementation, the field of policy research focused primarily on policy design and policy outcomes -- what policies were intended to achieve and what they actually achieved. The processes by which programs were put into practice, how resources were allocated across levels of government, how administrative discretion was exercised, and how implementing agencies interacted with target populations were not studied systematically. Pressman and Wildavsky filled this gap by arguing that the "divorce of implementation from policy" was the real devil, not the false messiahs of coordination and communication that policymakers typically invoked. Their central prescription was that implementation should be part of a policy's essential conception, not an afterthought delegated to administrators (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973).

The field expanded rapidly in the 1970s and 1980s. Two traditions emerged: top-down approaches, which began from the perspective of policy makers seeking to structure mandates so that implementers would have limited discretion, and bottom-up approaches, which began from the perspective of street-level actors navigating the actual conditions of service delivery. Michael Lipsky's 1980 book Street-Level Bureaucracy formalized the bottom-up perspective. Lipsky argued that frontline public servants -- teachers, police officers, social workers, public defenders -- are the de facto policymakers because their individual decisions, made under conditions of resource scarcity, ambiguous mandates, and high caseloads, determine what policies actually mean in practice. Policy as written and policy as performed are two different things, and the latter is what citizens experience (Lipsky, 1980).

Parallel to implementation theory, scholars developed frameworks for understanding the stages before implementation. Harold Lasswell, regarded as the founder of the policy sciences, proposed a seven-stage model in 1956: intelligence, promotion, prescription, invocation, application, termination, and appraisal. This was refined over decades into the modern five-stage policy cycle: agenda setting, policy formulation, decision-making, implementation, and evaluation. John Kingdon's 1984 book Agendas, Alternatives, and Public Policies introduced the multiple streams framework (MSF), which modeled agenda setting as the convergence of three independent streams -- problems, policies, and politics -- at critical moments called policy windows. Paul Sabatier's advocacy coalition framework (ACF), developed from 1988 onward, explained policy change as the interaction of competing coalitions held together by shared belief systems operating within policy subsystems over periods of a decade or more.

On the instrument side, Christopher Hood's 1983 book The Tools of Government introduced the NATO taxonomy (nodality, authority, treasure, organization), classifying the resources governments deploy to effect change. This framework provided a parsimonious language for comparing policy instruments across sectors and political systems. The rise of behavioral economics in the 2000s added nudge-based instruments to the policy toolkit, expanding the range of tools available while raising questions about their interaction with traditional regulatory and fiscal instruments.

The evaluation strand developed in parallel. The movement toward evidence-based policy gained momentum in the 1990s and 2000s, driven by the idea that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) could determine whether policies actually work. The UK Behavioural Insights Team, established in 2010, pioneered the "test, learn, adapt" methodology, applying RCTs to public policy interventions. The OECD published guidance on rigorous impact evaluation, emphasizing the construction of credible counterfactuals to separate correlation from causation in policy assessment. By the 2020s, the field had matured into a multi-framework enterprise spanning agenda setting, instrument selection, implementation, and evaluation, with growing attention to the interactions among these stages.

## Core Concepts

### The Policy Cycle and Its Limitations

The policy cycle model disaggregates the policy process into discrete stages: agenda setting, policy formulation, decision-making, implementation, and evaluation. Harold Lasswell's original 1956 seven-stage model (intelligence, promotion, prescription, invocation, application, termination, appraisal) was the first systematic attempt to break policymaking into analyzable components. The model was refined by scholars including Brewer, deLeon, Jones, and Anderson into the modern five-stage cycle. Its strength is dual: it is descriptive, helping analysts understand how policies come into being, and normative, suggesting how policies ought to be made. By separating problem identification from implementation, or evaluation from agenda setting, analysts can pinpoint where a policy went off course (Lasswell, 1956; Howlett et al., 2020).

The policy cycle has attracted serious criticism. The most common objection is that it is too neat: real policymaking rarely moves from one stage to the next in orderly fashion. Stages overlap, skip, or run in parallel. Policies are sometimes implemented before they are fully formulated; evaluation sometimes happens before implementation is complete. The model lacks any notion of causation -- it does not explain what or who drives a policy from one stage to the next. It remains silent about the content of a policy and how that content may affect policy-making styles. Most public policy textbooks acknowledge these limitations while still using the cycle as a heuristic for organizing analysis (Cairney, 2019; Howlett et al., 2020; John, 2013).

### Implementation Theory -- The Pressman-Wildavsky Model

Pressman and Wildavsky's implementation model formalizes the structural sources of implementation failure. The model identifies three factors that frustrate implementation: the difficulty of translating broad agreement into specific decisions given a wide range of participants and perspectives; the opportunities for blockage and delay that result from a multiplicity of decision points; and the economic theories on which programs are based. Their model has three main components. Any complex project requires joint action by numerous participants. These participants hold diverse perspectives reflecting their own preferences, a set of available resources they may or may not commit, and an intensity of commitment to the project. Implementation success is a function of the number of decision points, the number of participants at each point, and the intensity of their preferences. Because every complex project requires multiple decision points and numerous participants, the essential policy problem is how to provide incentives to maintain high positive intensity through lengthy delays (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973).

The model has been refined. Alexander (1989) identified five mitigating factors that increase the probability of successful implementation beyond what the original model implies. First, actors may make multiple attempts at clearance rather than a single try. Second, clearance points are not always independent; they may be packaged or bundled so that one agreement covers multiple points. Third, the intensity of commitment can change over time. Fourth, some participants have veto power while others do not, making the effective number of decision points smaller than the nominal count. Fifth, institutional structures can reduce the number of required clearances through hierarchical authority. These refinements make the model less deterministic while preserving its core insight: implementation complexity scales with the number of interdependent actors and decision points (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973; Pal, 2014).

### Street-Level Bureaucracy -- Lipsky's Framework

Michael Lipsky's 1980 book Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Services introduced the concept that frontline public servants function as de facto policymakers. Street-level bureaucrats are public service workers who interact directly with citizens in the course of their jobs and who have substantial discretion in the execution of their work. They include teachers, police officers, social workers, public defenders, and other frontline staff. Lipsky argued that these workers, rather than higher-level officials or formal policy statements, effectively determine the substance of public services due to the inherent challenges of implementation. Policy is an abstraction until it is realized when delivered to citizens, and street-level policy realizations define policy (Lipsky, 1980).

The conditions that produce street-level discretion are structural. Street-level bureaucrats face demands that exceed their capacity: high caseloads, inadequate resources, ambiguous or conflicting goals, and clients whose needs are non-routine and unpredictable. These conditions compel workers to exercise broad discretion because policies cannot prescribe responses to every unique case involving human variability. Discretion serves as both a necessity for effective service and a source of potential deviation from intended policy goals. To manage unmanageable workloads, street-level bureaucrats develop coping mechanisms: prioritizing cases, simplifying procedures, altering eligibility criteria, and routinizing client interactions. These coping mechanisms are not aberrations but systematic responses to structural conditions. They become institutionalized and shape the actual policy that citizens experience (Lipsky, 1980; Hupe and Buffat, 2014).

Lipsky's framework led to a paradigm shift in the study of bureaucracy and policy implementation. It turned the study of organizations on its head by placing frontline workers' discretion, judgments, and power at the center of research on the administrative state. The framework has been applied across diverse contexts: social work, policing, education, healthcare, immigration, and tax administration. Subsequent research has examined how managerialism, deprofessionalization, and user empowerment have changed the dynamics of street-level discretion. Critics have argued that Lipsky's analysis belongs to a gentler age before the rise of management power within welfare organizations, and that contemporary managerial strategies have significantly constrained the discretion of frontline professionals. Lipsky himself was not an apologist for bottom-up implementation: he recognized that discretion is necessary to make policy work but worried about how it is used (Lipsky, 1980; Evans, 2016; Howe, 2009).

### Policy Instruments -- The NATO Framework

Christopher Hood's The Tools of Government (1983) introduced the NATO taxonomy, which classifies the resources governments deploy to effect change. NATO stands for nodality, authority, treasure, and organization. Nodality denotes the government's information position -- its ability to influence behavior by providing or withholding information. Authority denotes legal or official power -- the power to demand, forbid, guarantee, and adjudicate. Treasure denotes the possession of fungible financial resources -- the power to spend, tax, subsidize, and procure. Organization denotes the possession of people, skills, land, buildings, and equipment arranged for administrative action (Hood, 1983; Hood and Margetts, 2007).

The NATO framework's appeal is its parsimony. It reframes the question of what governments do as a resource problem, enabling comparison across sectors and states while remaining agnostic about normative desirability. The framework accommodates both traditional instruments (regulation, spending, public provision) and newer instruments (information campaigns, digital platforms, behavioral interventions) by treating novelty as recombination or technological transformation of underlying resource types. In practice, most policies combine multiple instruments. A regulation (authority) may require reporting (nodality) and create an enforcement unit (organization). A subsidy (treasure) may be conditional on compliance with standards (authority). This multi-instrument character makes policy design inherently complex (Hood, 1983; Hood and Margetts, 2007).

The rise of behavioral economics expanded the instrument toolkit. Nudges -- interventions that alter the choice architecture to guide behavior without forbidding options -- occupy a middle position between top-down regulation and pure libertarianism. Thaler and Sunstein characterized nudge as "libertarian paternalism": it leads people toward socially desired outcomes while preserving an element of free choice. The nudge approach has been adopted by governments worldwide through behavioral insights units. Empirical research has examined how nudges interact with traditional instruments: information nudges and subsidies can substitute or complement each other, and the direction of interaction is design-dependent. The addition of nudge to the toolkit raises questions about public legitimacy: survey evidence suggests that citizens generally prefer nudges over regulation, but support varies by policy domain and depends on perceived fairness and efficacy (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008; List et al., 2022; Davidai et al., 2023).

### Agenda Setting -- The Multiple Streams Framework

John Kingdon's 1984 book Agendas, Alternatives, and Public Policies introduced the multiple streams framework (MSF), which models agenda setting as the convergence of three independent streams. The problem stream encompasses the factors that bring conditions to the attention of policymakers as problems requiring action: indicators and data, focusing events such as disasters, and feedback from existing programs. The policy stream is a "primeval soup" in which ideas float around, confront one another, and combine. Some ideas rise to the top of the agenda while others sink. The environment of this soup is composed of policy communities -- networks of actors with expertise in a particular domain. The politics stream encompasses political processes: changes in administration, shifts in public mood, election results, and interest group activity (Kingdon, 1984; Zahariadis et al., 2023).

The three streams flow independently until they converge at a policy window -- a brief opportunity during which a problem is recognized, a solution is available in the policy community, and the political climate allows for action. Policy windows are opened by events in any of the three streams: a crisis in the problem stream, a maturing idea in the policy stream, or a change in the politics stream. Policy entrepreneurs -- actors willing to invest resources in pushing their proposals -- play a critical role by coupling solutions to problems and both to politics at the moment a window opens. The MSF emphasizes the unpredictable and contingent nature of agenda setting: change is partly random, dependent on the timing of events across independent streams (Kingdon, 1984; Howlett, 1998).

The MSF has been widely applied beyond its original US context. Scholars have extended it to comparative policy analysis across political systems with different institutional structures. The framework has been criticized as a loose metaphor that generates a high ratio of citations to rigorous empirical scholarship. Confusion persists in the literature about the relationship between stream coupling and policy windows: some scholars argue that coupling occurs after windows open, while others argue that coupling opens windows. Despite these ambiguities, the MSF remains one of the most influential frameworks for understanding the pre-decision stages of the policy process (Zahariadis, 2007; Zohlnhofer et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2016).

### Policy Change Over Time -- The Advocacy Coalition Framework

Paul Sabatier's advocacy coalition framework (ACF), developed from 1988 onward with Hank Jenkins-Smith, explains policy change as the interaction of competing coalitions within policy subsystems. A policy subsystem consists of actors from public and private organizations who are actively concerned with a policy problem and regularly seek to influence related public policy. Actors within a subsystem are grouped into advocacy coalitions: individuals who share a particular belief system -- a set of basic values, causal assumptions, and problem perceptions -- and who show a non-trivial degree of coordinated activity over time. Belief systems have a hierarchical structure. Deep core beliefs are fundamental values and ontological assumptions about human nature and the role of government that are very difficult to alter. Policy core beliefs are normative preferences pertaining to the appropriate means and ends of public policy within a subsystem. Secondary aspects are specific decisions and empirical information that are more readily modified (Sabatier, 1988; Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith, 1993).

The ACF identifies two primary pathways of policy change. First, policy-oriented learning -- the relatively enduring alternations of thought or behavioral intentions that result from experience or new information -- can lead coalitions to refine and adapt their belief systems. However, learning is most likely to concern only secondary aspects, leaving the policy core intact, and thus produces only minor policy change. Second, external events originating outside the subsystem -- shifts in socioeconomic conditions, changes in governing coalitions, court decisions -- can shift the power distribution among subsystem actors and produce major policy change by altering policy cores. Major change is unlikely in the absence of external shocks. The ACF was developed with 15 testable hypotheses and has been applied across diverse policy domains including environmental policy, energy policy, drug policy, and forestry, in countries ranging from the United States to Switzerland, Japan, and the Netherlands (Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith, 1993; Weible et al., 2011; Kubler, 1999).

### Evidence-Based Policy Evaluation

The evidence-based policy movement advocates for the use of rigorous empirical methods -- particularly randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental designs -- to determine whether policies achieve their intended outcomes. The core insight is that correlation does not imply causation: to know whether a policy works, one must construct a credible counterfactual estimating what would have happened in the absence of the policy. RCTs accomplish this by randomly assigning participants to treatment and control groups, ensuring that the two groups are statistically equivalent except for the intervention. The UK Behavioural Insights Team's "test, learn, adapt" methodology formalized a nine-step process for conducting RCTs in public policy: identify interventions to compare, determine the outcome, determine the sample size, identify the target population, determine how the intervention will be allocated, decide how the intervention will be delivered, determine the length of the trial, analyze the results, and adapt policy to reflect findings (Haynes et al., 2012; OECD, 2025).

RCTs have well-known limitations in the policy context. External validity -- the extent to which findings from a trial population transfer to a different policy population -- is a persistent challenge. A systematic review of RCTs published in top economics journals between 2009 and 2014 found that the majority of papers do not discuss potential hazards to external validity, including Hawthorne effects (altered behavior among participants who know they are being studied), general equilibrium effects, specific sample problems, and special care in treatment provision. Ethical concerns arise when randomization means some individuals are denied a potentially beneficial intervention. These concerns can be addressed through waitlist designs, staggered rollouts, and consent procedures. Despite these limitations, RCTs and quasi-experimental methods have become increasingly common in public policy evaluation, with the OECD and national evaluation offices promoting their use as part of a broader "test and learn culture" (OECD, 2025; Australian Centre for Evaluation, 2025; Mueller and Rau, 2025).

## Evidence

### The Oakland Project -- Pressman and Wildavsky (1973)

The foundational case study in implementation research is Pressman and Wildavsky's analysis of the EDA's Oakland Project. Announced in 1966, the project aimed to spend USD 23 million on public works to create jobs for unemployed minority residents of Oakland, California, where unemployment stood at 8.4 percent. The program enjoyed wide political support and was well funded. Yet it resulted in almost no useful outcomes after significant expenditures, years of effort, and apparent agreement among all parties.

Pressman and Wildavsky identified three factors behind the failure. First, the difficulty of translating broad agreement into specific decisions given a wide range of participants and perspectives. Federal officials, local officials, community organizations, and contractors each had different preferences and different levels of commitment. Second, the multiplicity of decision points created opportunities for blockage and delay. The project required approximately 70 separate clearance points across multiple agencies and levels of government. Third, the economic theories on which the program was based -- that public works spending would automatically generate employment -- did not account for the administrative complexity of implementation. The authors' probabilistic model showed that even at an 80 percent probability of agreement per clearance point, the chances of completion were one in a million. At 99 percent per point, the odds were only one in two. The Oakland Project demonstrated that implementation failure is not an accident but a predictable consequence of structural complexity in multi-actor, multi-level programs (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973; Pal, 2014).

### Street-Level Discretion in Welfare Services -- Lipsky and Successors

Lipsky's framework has been tested across multiple policy domains. In adult social care in England, research has traced the relationship between different forms of discretion and developments since the publication of Street-Level Bureaucracy, including managerialization, deprofessionalization, and user empowerment. The study found that while managerial reforms have changed the institutional context, an irreducible core of discretion remains at street level. Practitioners continue to exercise administrative discretion beyond its authorized scope, sometimes in ways at odds with professional codes of ethics, as a response to the gap between demands and resources. The finding supports Lipsky's central claim: discretion is a structural feature of street-level work, not a failure of management control (Lipsky, 1980; Ellis, 2011).

Research on street-level discretion in the West has documented how the concept has evolved. The gap between "policy as written" and "policy as performed" remains the central concern. Street-level work is highly scripted yet requires improvisation and responsiveness to individual cases. The key tension -- how to treat all citizens alike while being responsive to individual circumstances -- persists across contexts. Studies have examined how different forms of discretion (administrative, professional, and personal) interact with institutional reforms. The managerialization of welfare services has altered the balance of power between practitioners and managers but has not eliminated discretion. Instead, it has reshaped the conditions under which discretion is exercised, making some forms more visible and others more constrained (Hupe and Buffat, 2014; Maynard-Moody and Portillo, 2010; Tummers et al., 2015).

### Nudge Versus Regulation -- Empirical Evidence on Instrument Choice

A 2023 study published in Regulation and Governance tested public support for behavioral nudges relative to alternative policy instruments using a survey experiment. Respondents were randomly assigned to four conditions along a choice continuum: top-down regulation without choice, a non-deliberative nudge, a nudge with a deliberative component, and free choice. The study found that citizens preferred nudge options over regulation, consistent with Thaler and Sunstein's characterization of nudge as a normatively preferred middle ground. However, nudge was the preferred option over free choice only for retirement savings, not for carbon offsetting, suggesting that support varies by policy domain. Support for nudges was mediated by perceived fairness and efficacy. The indirect effect through fairness was larger than the effect through efficacy for non-deliberative nudges compared to regulation (Davidai et al., 2023).

A large-scale field experiment published in the Journal of the European Economic Association in 2025 examined the interaction between information nudges and subsidies. The study, conducted with a major European appliance retailer, cross-randomized information nudges about energy efficiency with financial subsidies for LED bulbs. The findings revealed that information nudges and subsidies can substitute for each other: more informative nudges reduced the additional effect of subsidies. Coarse information disclosure boosted demand for LEDs by 27 percent, while more precise information increased demand for less energy-efficient CFL bulbs by 221 percent. The direction of the nudge effect was design-dependent and not predictable ex ante. These results have direct implications for optimal policy design: the size of optimal taxes and subsidies may be substantially different in markets where information interventions are also deployed (List et al., 2022).

### Randomized Trials in Policy Evaluation -- The OECD and Australian Evidence

The OECD's 2025 report on rigorous impact evaluation documented the growing but still underutilized role of RCTs in public policy. The report identified key barriers to evaluation uptake: assumptions that programs already work effectively, lack of resources and skills, and insufficient political demand. It argued that increasing evaluation requires cultural change toward a "test and learn" environment where identifying unsuccessful programs is seen as progress rather than failure. The report highlighted a randomized trial in Bangladesh where workers who received free reading glasses earned 33 percent more than those who did not -- a finding that would not have been established without rigorous evaluation. The OECD emphasized that counterfactual-based impact evaluations, including RCTs and quasi-experimental approaches such as regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences, help policymakers separate correlation from causation (OECD, 2025).

The Australian Centre for Evaluation's 2025 review identified 369 published randomized policy trials in Australia going back to 1976. Sixty percent of these were published since 2017, indicating rapid growth. Randomized trials were most common in public health and education policy, with government behavioral insights units producing 30 percent of all published trials. The review presented four case studies spanning early years child development, employment services, behavioral insights, and crime and justice, each showing a clear link between trial results and subsequent policy decisions. The review acknowledged persistent challenges: few government policies in Australia are evaluated using RCTs, and growth requires institutional humility to recognize uncertainty about program effectiveness. It emphasized that RCTs must be ethical, culturally appropriate, well-designed, and used thoughtfully to inform policy, not merely counted (Australian Centre for Evaluation, 2025).

### Implementation in Network Settings -- O'Toole and Colleagues

Laurence O'Toole's research extended implementation theory to network settings, where policy implementation involves multiple interdependent organizations rather than a single hierarchical chain. In a 1979 paper with Montjoy, O'Toole argued that many mandates require participation by more than one agency, creating situations where traditional institutional tools for eliciting and coordinating efforts are not controlled by a single actor. The probability of implementation varies with the structure of interdependence among implementing units. Using Thompson's typology, they showed that the probability of implementation can increase with the number of units when the type of interdependence requires little coordination at the formative stage, but decreases when tight coordination is required throughout (Montjoy and O'Toole, 1979).

O'Toole's 2004 assessment of the theory-practice gap in implementation research was sobering. More than a quarter century after Pressman and Wildavsky's opening, theories about implementation had become plentiful but theoretical consensus was not on the horizon. The number of variables offered by researchers as plausible explanations for implementation results was large and growing. Applying implementation theory to practice remained rare, due to the difficulty of the theoretical challenge, the varied needs of practitioners, and complicating normative issues. O'Toole identified two strategies for bridging the gap: building on points of theoretical consensus, and systematically probing points of theoretical dispute to sketch practical implications. The assessment acknowledged that while the field had generated substantial knowledge, its practical application remained limited (O'Toole, 2004).

## Implications

### For Legislative Design and Policy Formulation

The implementation research literature has direct implications for how legislation is designed and policies are formulated. Pressman and Wildavsky's central prescription -- that implementation should be part of a policy's essential conception, not an afterthought -- means that legislators and policy designers must consider implementation feasibility at the design stage. Policies that require complex joint action across multiple agencies, levels of government, and stakeholder groups carry structurally higher risks of implementation failure. The probability of successful implementation can be improved by reducing the number of decision points, aligning the incentives of implementing actors, and building in feedback mechanisms that detect and correct implementation problems early. This means that policy design is not merely about specifying goals and instruments but about architecting the implementation chain: who must do what, in what sequence, with what resources, and under what incentives. Legislative designs that delegate implementation to agencies without specifying the inter-organizational coordination mechanisms, resource flows, and accountability structures are setting up the conditions for the implementation gap that Pressman and Wildavsky documented (Pressman and Wildavsky, 1973; O'Toole, 2004; Howlett and Mukherjee, 2018).

The multiple streams framework adds a different implication for formulation: because policy windows are brief and unpredictable, policy entrepreneurs must have well-developed proposals ready in the policy stream before a window opens. This means that the investment in policy development -- the work of refining ideas, building coalitions, and testing feasibility -- must occur continuously, not reactively after a crisis. Organizations that maintain a portfolio of developed policy proposals are better positioned to seize windows when they open. This has practical implications for think tanks, advocacy organizations, and policy units within government: sustained investment in policy development, rather than reactive policy formation, is the strategy that positions actors to capitalize on political opportunities (Kingdon, 1984; Zahariadis et al., 2023).

### For Public Administration and Bureaucratic Practice

Lipsky's street-level bureaucracy framework has profound implications for how public agencies are managed and how frontline discretion is structured. If street-level bureaucrats are de facto policymakers, then managing implementation is fundamentally about managing discretion -- not eliminating it, which is neither possible nor desirable, but shaping the conditions under which it is exercised. Agencies can influence street-level behavior through several mechanisms: training and professional socialization that internalize norms consistent with policy goals; procedural standardization that reduces unnecessary discretion while preserving necessary judgment; workload management that reduces the pressure to develop dysfunctional coping mechanisms; and feedback systems that surface street-level patterns for organizational learning. The recognition that coping mechanisms -- simplifying procedures, rationing services, prioritizing cases -- are systematic responses to structural conditions, not individual failures, shifts the management focus from blaming frontline workers to redesigning the conditions under which they work (Lipsky, 1980; Evans, 2016; Tummers et al., 2015).

The finding that managerialism has reshaped but not eliminated street-level discretion has practical consequences. Managerial reforms -- performance targets, standardized procedures, audit systems -- can constrain some forms of discretion while creating new ones. The shift from professional to managerial control can reduce some forms of arbitrary decision-making while introducing new pressures: performance targets can create perverse incentives, audit systems can generate compliance theater rather than genuine accountability, and standardization can reduce responsiveness to individual cases. Effective management of street-level bureaucracy requires balancing standardization with discretion, accountability with autonomy, and consistency with responsiveness. This is an ongoing design problem, not a problem with a one-time solution (Ellis, 2011; Howe, 2009).

### For Evidence-Based Governance

The evidence-based policy movement has implications for how governments learn whether their policies work. The core insight -- that correlation does not imply causation and that credible counterfactuals are needed to estimate causal effects -- challenges the common practice of evaluating policies by examining outcomes without a comparison group. The OECD's finding that rigorous impact evaluations remain underused despite their potential points to a structural problem: political incentives often favor spending on new programs over evaluating existing ones, and there is often an assumption that programs work until proven otherwise. Building a "test and learn culture" requires institutional changes: evaluation budgets built into program design, administrative data infrastructure that supports counterfactual analysis, training for policy advisors in evaluation methods, and political leadership willing to treat the identification of ineffective programs as progress rather than failure (OECD, 2025; Haynes et al., 2012).

The external validity challenge has direct implications for how evaluation evidence is used. A single RCT showing that a program worked in one context does not guarantee it will work in another. The systematic review finding that the majority of published RCTs do not discuss external validity hazards means that policymakers using evaluation evidence must exercise judgment about transferability. This requires attention to contextual factors: the characteristics of the study population, the implementation conditions, the institutional environment, and the scale at which the intervention is tested. Replication across contexts, complemented by process evaluation that documents how the intervention was implemented, strengthens the evidence base for scaling. The implication is that evidence-based policy is not a single-trial enterprise but an iterative process of testing, learning, adapting, and retesting (Mueller and Rau, 2025; Australian Centre for Evaluation, 2025).

### For Understanding Policy Change Over Time

The advocacy coalition framework has implications for how policy observers understand long-term policy change. If major policy change typically requires external shocks rather than policy-oriented learning alone, then periods of policy stability are not failures of democratic responsiveness but structural features of belief-driven subsystem politics. Advocacy coalitions resist changes to their policy core beliefs through selective processing of information, and learning that challenges core beliefs is typically absorbed into secondary aspects without altering the core. This means that policy debates that appear to be about evidence are often fundamentally about values -- the deep core and policy core beliefs that define coalition identity. Changing minds with evidence alone is unlikely to produce major policy change when the change challenges core beliefs. Major change occurs when external events -- crises, economic shifts, changes in government -- alter the political opportunity structure and weaken the dominant coalition's position (Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith, 1993; Weible et al., 2011).

For practitioners seeking policy change, the ACF implies a dual strategy. First, invest in policy-oriented learning that refines the evidence base and builds credibility for alternative approaches, even if immediate change is unlikely. This learning positions a coalition to seize opportunities when they arise. Second, monitor and prepare for external events that could open windows for major change. The combination of well-developed alternative proposals (the policy stream) and readiness to act when external shocks shift the political landscape (the politics stream) is the strategy that the ACF, combined with the MSF, suggests for long-term policy entrepreneurship. This implies that policy change is neither purely rational -- driven by evidence -- nor purely political -- driven by power. It is both, and the interaction between learning and external events is the mechanism through which beliefs eventually translate into policy (Sabatier, 1988; Kingdon, 1984).
