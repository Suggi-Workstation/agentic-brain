# Civil-Military Relations and Democratic Control -- Civilian Supremacy Rests on Institutions That Bound Military Autonomy, Not on the Mere Absence of Coups

Civil-military relations is the study of how elected or ruling civilians, the armed forces, and the wider society divide authority over the organized means of violence: who sets defense policy, appoints and promotes commanders, approves budgets, directs operations, and decides when soldiers may be used at home. Its founding problem is that any armed force strong enough to defend a country is also strong enough to take it over [5], and comparative research now treats democratic control not as the absence of coups but as a distribution of decision-making power in which civilians decide across elite recruitment, public policy, internal security, national defense, and military organization, supported by legislative oversight and internalized professional norms [5][6]. Autocrats face a sharper form of the same dilemma, because the soldiers who can suppress mass opposition can also remove the ruler, and the coup-proofing devices rulers adopt protect them only partially and at a cost [8][9].

## Background

Kees Homan, a retired Dutch major general writing for the Clingendael institute, opens his survey by calling the subordination of the military to political authority "one of the oldest problems of human governance" [4]. Samuel Finer put the puzzle in its sharpest form in 1962: instead of asking why soldiers engage in politics, "we ought surely ask why they ever do otherwise", since the military possesses "vastly superior organization" and arms (Finer, as quoted by Bruneau and Matei) [5]. Bruneau and Matei, citing Peter Feaver's 1996 article on the "civil-military problematique", summarize the central concern of the classic literature as reconciling a military strong enough to do what civilian leaders want with a military subordinate enough to do only what civilians authorize [5].

The field's founding text is Samuel Huntington's The Soldier and the State (1957). Huntington defined civilian control as "the proper subordination of a competent, professional military to the ends of policy as determined by civilian authority" (as quoted by Homan) [4]. He distinguished two routes to it. Subjective civilian control maximizes civilian power by both civilianizing and politicizing the military, making it politically dependent and denying it a distinct professional identity; objective civilian control maximizes military professionalism, so that a corps of experts in the "management of violence" stays out of politics in exchange for autonomy over operations [1][4]. Huntington wrote that "the antithesis of objective control is military participation in politics" and that relations were best when the military remained "politically sterile and neutral" (as quoted by Brooks) [1]. Feaver and Kohn report that this model is still taught in American professional military education as the "normal" theory of civil-military relations [2], and Brooks quotes a former commandant of West Point and the Army War College saying that soldiers "have been raised on Huntingtonian logic" since they were junior lieutenants [1].

Morris Janowitz's The Professional Soldier (1960) answered Huntington. Janowitz argued that traditional professionalism was giving way to a "pragmatic professionalism" in which the military, while not participating directly in politics, is linked to the political system and integrated into civilian society as a "constabulary force" [4]; Brooks quotes his view that professionalism does not mean being "above politics" to the point of being unpolitical [1]. Feaver and Kohn observe that both founders worried about the same thing, a gap between military and civilian society, but prescribed opposite cures: Huntington wanted society to move toward military values, Janowitz wanted the military to move toward society [2].

Later theory diversified. Michael Desch argued that the mix of threats determines the quality of control, which should be best when external threat is high and internal threat low and worst in the reverse case, as in the military dictatorships of the Southern Cone [4][6]. Another family of explanations, associated with Wendy Hunter, Barbara Geddes, and Harold Trinkunas, explains military withdrawal and the success or failure of civilian control as the outcome of strategic interaction between civilian and military actors (as classified by Croissant, Kuehn, and Lorenz) [6]. Feaver's principal-agent approach, as Brooks summarizes it, found that during the Cold War professionalism did not forestall officers' efforts to "shirk" or act contrary to civilian preferences, and that civilian monitoring of the military belied any neat division of labor [1]; Feaver and Kohn describe officers as "the agents, not the principals" [2].

Democratization turned the theory into an applied agenda. The third wave of democracy began with the military coup in Lisbon on 25 April 1974 that became a revolution, and many later transitions required civilians to retake authority from armed forces that had ruled or co-ruled [5]. In post-communist Europe, objective control was introduced through the depoliticization and de-partyization of armies that had been governed by subjective, party-based control [4]. The participating states of the CSCE codified the emerging norm in the 1994 Code of Conduct on Politico-Military Aspects of Security, which calls democratic political control of military, paramilitary, and internal security forces, intelligence services, and police "an indispensable element of stability and security" [10]. From the late 1990s, development agencies folded these concerns into security sector reform; Bruneau and Matei report that the concept was first put to a wider public in a 1998 speech by Clare Short, the first head of Britain's Department for International Development, and count at least 15 definitions in use [5].

Two corrections followed in the 2000s. Croissant and colleagues argued that defining civilian control negatively, as the absence of coups and military rule, ignores more nuanced forms of military influence, a "fallacy of coup-ism", and they redefined civilian control as a distribution of decision-making power [6]. Bruneau and Matei argued that control alone was insufficient and proposed a "trinity" of democratic control, effectiveness, and efficiency [5]. In parallel, Powell and Thyne built the most comprehensive dataset of coup attempts then available [7], and scholars of authoritarianism such as Svolik and De Bruin analyzed why rulers who depend on soldiers for repression must also guard against them [8][9].

## Core Concepts

### The Civil-Military Problematique

The core dilemma is structural. A state needs armed forces capable of defeating external enemies and, in some regimes, internal ones; the same capacity makes the armed forces the actor best placed to seize power. Bruneau and Matei call the question "Who guards the guardians?" the formulation behind most analyses of civil-military relations, both into and out of military government [5]. The dilemma does not disappear in stable democracies; it changes form. Feaver and Kohn note that the United States has never had a successful or even a serious coup attempt, yet nearly every secretary of defense since James Forrestal has taken office worried that the military had gained too much influence under his predecessor, a paradox they summarize as "the unbroken record of civilian control and the nearly unbroken record of worry about civilian control" [2]. They attribute part of the worry to the fiscal, political, and prestige power of a superpower military, which they judge to be far beyond what the Framers would have thought safe [2].

### Objective and Subjective Control

Huntington's two models remain the field's reference points. Under objective control, civilians decide whom to fight and when, how large budgets will be, which weapons will be purchased, and which policies govern the services, and then grant the military autonomy over how to fight; as one journalist explained the military view to Feaver and Kohn, "Civilians tell us where they want to go but leave the driving to us" [2]. Brooks identifies four assumptions behind the model: that civilian and military spheres are easily distinguished, that advice is a transactional exchange of goals for options, that a professional military must stand apart from liberal society, and that professionalism means abstaining from everything "political", from partisanship to public advocacy to reasoning about political effects on strategy [1].

Subjective control works in the opposite direction, by making the military politically dependent on a civilian group. Homan lists its main mechanisms in Cold War communist states as control by recruitment and selection, by indoctrination, and by organization [4]. Taiwan before democratization illustrates the type (the author's classification): the National Army was founded as the military wing of the Kuomintang, and party control operated through a political commissar system and a Political Warfare System, even as the army kept broad autonomy in defense and internal security [6].

The two models are best read as ideal types (the author's interpretation, consistent with Brooks's observation that the objective control approach is "an analytical and normative construct") [1]. Feaver and Kohn report that civilian officials rarely accept a fixed boundary: "The dividing line is where civilian leaders say it is at any given time" [2]. Their account of the US system shows why: the line moves with the president's personal interests, the threat and political stakes, changes in technology, and even what is going viral on social media that day [2].

### Civilian Control as a Distribution of Decision-Making Power

The comparative literature replaced the coup-centered view with a multidimensional definition. Croissant and colleagues define civilian control as a distribution of decision-making power in which civilians "have exclusive authority to decide on national politics and their implementation", may delegate implementation to the military, and alone "define the boundaries between policy-making and policy-implementation" [6]. They divide the field into five areas [6]:

| Area | What civilians must decide | Typical military encroachment |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Elite recruitment | Who holds office and how | Reserved seats in cabinet or parliament, vetoes over appointments, active-duty officers in political posts |
| Public policy | Agenda setting, policy formulation, implementation | Military influence over social, economic, and political policy |
| Internal security | Whether and how the military is deployed at home | Military domination of police, law enforcement, and intelligence |
| National defense | Defense policy, using military expertise | Officers deciding defense policy rather than advising on it |
| Military organization | Missions, structure, procurement, education, promotions | Autonomy that civilians cannot define or limit |

Two clarifications follow. First, civilian control is not the same as democratic control: communist one-party states achieved civilian control without democracy [6]. Second, civilian control does not require an apolitical military; Croissant and colleagues cite Israel as a liberal democracy with close interconnection between military and civilian elites and conclude that the question is "not whether the military yields political influence, but how and how much" [6]. Richard Kohn's formula, quoted in both sources, is that the military's role is "to defend society, not to define it" [2][6]. The five-area scheme also shows why a country can rank differently on each dimension: in 2010 the Philippines had formal civilian control over all five areas while officers informally retained influence over internal security and military affairs [6].

### Instruments of Democratic Control

Bruneau and Matei extend control to all three instruments of state coercion, the military, the police, and intelligence services, and break control into three components [5]. Institutional control mechanisms are the formal architecture: a clear legal basis, ministries of defense, parliamentary committees with authority over policy and budgets, national security councils, and officer-promotion processes. Oversight is whether civilians actually track what security forces do, through executive, legislative, and judicial bodies and through media, NGOs, and think tanks. Professional norms are whether recruitment, education, training, and promotion lead officers to internalize the first two [5].

The OSCE Code of Conduct expresses the same architecture as politically binding commitments of the participating states: control "by constitutionally established authorities vested with democratic legitimacy", clearly defined roles and missions, legislative approval of defense expenditures, transparency and public access to information, politically neutral armed forces, no tolerance of forces not accountable to constitutional authorities, and decisions to assign the armed forces to internal security missions taken by constitutional procedure [10].

Legislatures carry much of this weight. In the United States, the open letter signed in 2022 by eight former secretaries of defense and five former chairmen of the Joint Chiefs of Staff lists Congress's Article I powers to raise and support forces, appropriate funds, compel testimony, and, through the Senate, consent to promotions of officers at pay grade O-4 and above [3]. Feaver and Kohn add that before confirmation, senior officers promise under oath to give Congress their personal professional opinion when asked, which makes Congress also the "civilian" in civilian control [2]. In the Netherlands, the minister of defense must wait at least three weeks before signing any procurement contract above 100 million euros so that parliament can debate it, and the defense committee approves the operational requirement for projects between 25 and 100 million euros [4].

### Military Advice and the Unequal Dialogue

Most civil-military interaction in democracies concerns advice, not insubordination. Feaver and Kohn argue that the phrase "best military advice" can sound to civilians like a threat, because "best" implies a single option that boxes in the decision maker; they cite Lincoln's lesson that the civilian's problem is not whether to listen to generals but which generals to listen to [2]. Their prescription is that officers should "speak up, not speak out": press contrary views in private before a decision, present politically unpalatable options, and then execute any legal decision, since "only one outcome is acceptable: obedience" [2]. The former secretaries and chairmen state the corollary principle that elected and appointed civilians "have the right to be wrong", while the military reinforces control when it raises second- and third-order effects and proposes alternatives [3].

Feaver and Kohn reject a norm of resignation in protest at the top ranks, arguing that a threatened resignation is an attempt to impose military preferences and that a resignation norm would push civilians to select pliable officers [2]. Brooks criticizes Huntington's prescribed sequence, in which "the military man ideally should formulate his military solution first" and alter it afterward on political advice, for inhibiting advisory processes in which civilians can see how military tools might or might not serve their political objectives [1].

### Political Neutrality and Politicization

Political neutrality means that the armed forces as an institution take no partisan side, while individual members retain civil rights; the OSCE Code commits states to ensure that "armed forces as such are politically neutral" [10]. The 2022 open letter notes that service members accept limits on public expression of their private political views "that would be unconstitutional if imposed on other citizens" [3]. Feaver and Kohn distinguish three forms of politicization in the United States: the officer corps acquiring a partisan identity, campaigns recruiting retired generals and admirals for endorsements, and retired officers acting as policy pundits [2]. Brooks proposes rethinking the apolitical norm so that officers become politically aware, able to distinguish partisan behavior contrary to civilian control from the political engagement that strategic success requires [1].

Politicization runs in both directions. Croissant, Kuehn, and Lorenz conclude from Thailand that keeping the military out of politics "is only half of the challenge. The other half is to protect the military from becoming a vehicle for the partisan interests of government leaders" [6]. Prime Minister Thaksin Shinawatra placed relatives and academy classmates in key commands and expanded military budgets to build a personal power base; officers who saw this as a threat to the army's unity and to the monarchy staged the September 2006 coup [6].

### Coup-Proofing and the Moral Hazard of Repression

In autocracies the problem takes the form Svolik calls a moral hazard. Police and internal security agencies handle everyday repression but lack the personnel to suppress uprisings of tens of thousands, so "soldiers, therefore, are any dictator's repressive agent of last resort"; yet "the very resources and privileges that enable soldiers to suppress the regime's opposition also empower them to act against the regime itself" [9]. Svolik describes three resulting patterns: perfect political control, where rulers keep the army small and out of repression, as Tunisia's Bourguiba and Ben Ali did; military tutelage, where indispensable soldiers dictate terms, as in 1930s Cuba; and in between, brinkmanship bargaining, in which military dictatorships emerge when one side "rocks the boat" too much [9].

Rulers respond with coup-proofing. De Bruin defines counterbalancing as dividing coercive power among overlapping forces such as presidential guards, militarized police, and militias, which, following Horowitz as cited by De Bruin, must sit outside the normal military chain of command to work as counterweights [8]. Svolik adds that rulers often staff such forces through sectarian or ethnic loyalties, citing Tikritis in Baathist Iraq and Alawis in Syria [9].

### Control, Effectiveness, and Efficiency

Control can conflict with performance. Bruneau and Matei argue that well-designed control can improve effectiveness, citing the US Goldwater-Nichols Act of 1986, which both reinforced civilian control and mandated "jointness", and Romania, where the executive's National Supreme Defence Council and parliamentary committees reduced the intelligence community from nine organizations to six [5]. Efficiency is different: multiple agencies aid control because no single body monopolizes knowledge or force, but they duplicate effort, and oversight itself consumes resources [5]. Homan's Dutch cases show the costs of excessive involvement: in 1992 parliament insisted that troops sent to Bosnia carry non-provocative armament, which left the unit handicapped when violence escalated, and in 2001 it insisted on deploying Apache helicopters to Djibouti, at a cost of about 14 million euros, although the chief of defence staff saw "no military reasons" for them [4].

## Evidence

### Coups Worldwide: The Powell and Thyne Dataset (2011)

Powell and Thyne set out to measure the most extreme failure of civilian control consistently. They defined a coup attempt as an illegal and overt attempt "by the military or other elites within the state apparatus to unseat the sitting executive", and a success as one in which the perpetrators "seize and hold power for at least seven days" [7]. Their method was to merge 14 existing datasets into 2,141 potential events for 1950 to 2006, verify each alleged event against historical sources such as the New York Times, Keesing's, and Lexis-Nexis, and exclude plots, rumors, rebel fighting, popular protests, and self-coups [7]. The final dataset contains 457 coup attempts from 1950 to 2010, of which 227 (49.7 percent) succeeded [7]. Coups occurred in 94 states; Africa accounted for 36.5 percent and the Americas for 31.9 percent, the Middle East 15.8 percent, Asia 13.1 percent, and Europe only 2.6 percent [7]. Attempts peaked in the mid-1960s, with smaller bubbles in the mid-1970s and early 1990s, and declined over time, but the success rate rose again after 2003: 12 of the 18 attempts since then had succeeded [7].

De Bruin, using later releases of the same data, reports that between 2000 and 2019 soldiers in more than 30 countries staged more than 52 coup attempts, 23 of which succeeded [8]. Croissant, Kuehn, and Lorenz use the Powell and Thyne series to show the declining frequency of coups and military regimes in Asia over the preceding 25 years, while noting that Thailand's 2006 coup and repeated failed coup attempts and mutinies in the Philippines since 1986 show that the coup was "a continuing danger" for electoral democracies there (quoting Barracca) [6]. Powell and Thyne flag possible reporting bias in states with little Western media presence, although their tests found no statistically significant regional or temporal bias [7].

Later counts show a renewed wave concentrated in one region. Writing in December 2025, John Chin of Carnegie Mellon University counted the seizure of power by army generals in Guinea-Bissau on 26 November 2025 as the 11th successful coup in Africa since 2020 and the second of 2025, after the military takeover in Madagascar in October [17]. He reported that nearly three-quarters of all coup attempts worldwide since 2020 had taken place in West Africa or the Sahel, and that seven of the nine countries with successful coups since 2020 had already suffered a successful coup within the previous 20 years [17].

### Counterbalancing and Coup Outcomes: The State Security Forces Data

De Bruin's review summarizes the quantitative evidence on whether coup-proofing works [8]. Her State Security Forces dataset codes security forces in 110 countries from 1960 to 2010 and identifies those with the potential to counterbalance the army, meaning forces independent of military command and deployed with access to centers of political power [8]. The mean number of counterweights per country rose from 0.88 in 1960 to 1.32 in 2010; counterbalancing is most common in the Middle East, North Africa, and Eastern Europe and least common in Latin America, Western Europe, and North America; personalist dictatorships counterbalance most and democracies and military regimes least, although 51 percent of regimes scoring 7 or higher on the Polity2 scale used at least one potential counterweight [8].

The findings divide by outcome. Across data sources and measures, counterweights are strongly associated with coup failure: where rulers do not counterbalance, attempts succeed about half the time, and where they use three or more counterweights the success rate falls to about one quarter [8]. An examination of 16 coup attempts found that counterweights frequently resisted coups but little evidence that they created coordination problems while the coups were under way [8]. Deterrence is a different matter. Statistical evidence that counterbalancing deters attempts is "weak and inconsistent"; De Bruin finds that creating a new counterweight is associated with an elevated risk of a coup attempt in the following year, and Albrecht's study of the Middle East and North Africa from 1950 to 2013 found that the risk of a ruler being ousted in a coup stayed constant even in coup-proofed regimes, leading him to call coup-proofing deterrence a "myth" (as reported by De Bruin) [8]. On violence, De Bruin finds no association between counterbalancing and coup fatalities but concludes that the evidence linking counterbalancing to increased repression and civil war is the strongest, citing Greitens's finding that more fragmented security sectors in Taiwan, the Philippines, and South Korea created incentives for more indiscriminate repression [8].

### Five East Asian Democracies (Croissant, Kuehn, and Lorenz 2012)

This East-West Center study compares Indonesia, the Philippines, South Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand across the five decision-making areas, combining case narratives with data on military representation in cabinets, legislatures, and state enterprises [6]. By 2010, civilians controlled all five areas in South Korea and Taiwan; Indonesian civilian control was rather stable but under-institutionalized and dependent on presidential patronage; the Philippines had formal but not informal control; and Thailand was "a clear case of failed civilian control", where, observers agreed, the military could in principle veto almost any political decision [6].

The South Korean case shows control built by strategy. Retired officers held 25.6 percent of cabinet posts under Park Chung-hee, 19.4 percent under Chun Doo-hwan, and 17.2 percent under Roh Tae-woo, but 5.0 percent under Kim Young-sam and 6.7 percent under Kim Dae-jung [6]. Kim Young-sam purged the senior members of the Hanahoe faction that had run the 1979-1980 seizure of power, transferred its mid-ranking members to units on the North Korean border, and in 1996 saw Chun and Roh tried together with 13 other generals; the authors credit his success to unified presidential authority over promotions, existing monitoring mechanisms within the military, and the lack of any incentive among officers excluded under the old faction to defend it [6]. Successive governments from 1988 also downsized the military's domestic intelligence command and abolished its civilian surveillance bureau, and Kim Dae-jung introduced merit-based promotion [6].

The Thai and Philippine cases show control lost or stalled. Thailand had 18 coups between 1932 and September 2006, and the share of soldier-senators rose from 2 percent in 2000 to 15.3 percent in 2008 after the 2006 coup; a post-coup Internal Security Act let the military arrest civilians without a warrant [6]. In the Philippines, Corazon Aquino's reforms triggered a series of seven abortive coups in the authors' count, and later presidents bought loyalty through appointments, cycling through 12 chiefs of staff in nine years under Gloria Macapagal Arroyo, which the authors judge protected governments in the short run but politicized the officer corps [6]. Across cases, the authors find that authoritarian legacies, internal threat environments, and the legitimacy of democracy shaped what civilian strategies could achieve [6].

### Norms in the US Officer Corps: Survey Evidence

Brooks assembles survey evidence that Huntingtonian norms are weakly internalized [1]. In surveys by the Triangle Institute for Security Studies in the late 1990s, 28 percent of elite veterans said resignation was an appropriate response to an "unwise" order; in a 2013 YouGov survey the figure was 63 percent, and in the same survey of retired officers who had served in senior positions, 23 percent said an officer should resist carrying out such an order even at the risk of a court-martial [1]. In Heidi Urben's survey of more than 4,000 active-duty Army officers, a quarter considered it appropriate for active-duty personnel to criticize elected officials publicly and a third saw no limits on their public political expression; in her 2015-2016 survey, 44 percent reported that military friends often discussed politics on social media and 35 percent had seen active-duty friends share insulting comments about specific elected officials (as reported by Brooks) [1]. Feaver and Kohn document the civilian side of the strain in the 2017-2021 period: an administration in which, at one point, a recently retired four-star Marine was secretary of defense, an active-duty three-star general was national security adviser, and another retired four-star Marine was White House chief of staff; and a 2020 photo event in Lafayette Park after which the chairman of the Joint Chiefs and the defense secretary apologized publicly for appearing at a political event [2].

### Stress Tests: South Korea 2024, Brazil 2022-2023, and the United States 2025

Three recent cases tested consolidated institutions directly. On 3 December 2024, South Korean President Yoon Suk Yeol declared the country's first martial law in 44 years, banning political activity and allowing troops to arrest without warrant; lawmakers forced their way past soldiers and police into the National Assembly, where 190 members voted unanimously to lift the decree, which Yoon withdrew about three hours later [14]. A district court sentenced Yoon to life imprisonment in February 2026 for leading an insurrection, and former defense minister Kim Yong-hyun to 30 years [14][15]; a Hongik University law professor quoted by CNN credited citizens and "civil disobedience by some members of the military" with the outcome [14]. The appeal verdict is scheduled for 26 January 2027, with the special counsel again seeking the death penalty [15].

In Brazil, six weeks after losing the October 2022 election, President Jair Bolsonaro's defense minister handed the service chiefs a draft decree to invalidate the result; the air force and army commanders refused, and the investigation and trial concluded that the plot failed mainly for that reason [12]. On 11 September 2025 a Supreme Court panel convicted Bolsonaro by four votes to one and sentenced him to 27 years and three months; former defense minister Walter Braga Netto received 26 years, former navy commander Almir Garnier 24 years, General Augusto Heleno 21 years, and General Paulo Sergio Nogueira 19 years [11][12]. In February 2026 the Military Prosecutor's Office asked the Superior Military Court, whose 15 members include 10 officers, to strip the convicted officers of rank [13]; as of November 2026 that court was expected to decide only in 2027, while the officers continued to draw reserve pay [16].

In the United States the test concerned internal security. On 4 October 2025 the President called 300 members of the Illinois National Guard into federal service to protect federal personnel and property in and around Chicago, relying on 10 U.S.C. 12406(3), which permits this when the President is "unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States"; the district court barred the deployment [18]. On 23 December 2025 the Supreme Court, with three justices dissenting, denied the government's application for a stay, concluding that the provision likely applies only where the regular military could legally execute the laws, which the Posse Comitatus Act forbids except where the Constitution or an Act of Congress expressly authorizes it [18]. On 31 December 2025 the President announced that he would abandon efforts to deploy National Guard troops over the objections of state officials in California, Illinois, and Oregon, and the district court dismissed the Illinois case as moot in April 2026 [19].

## Implications

### For Legislators and Constitution Drafters in New Democracies

The evidence implies that the decisive institutions are those that give civilians authority in the five decision areas, not merely provisions against coups [6]. Croissant, Kuehn, and Lorenz conclude from the Korean and Indonesian cases that personnel management and promotion policies, divide-and-conquer strategies, civilian acquiescence, and the legitimization of civilian control are the most crucial elements of civilian strategy [6]. Their cases also indicate that timing matters: civilians in South Korea did not tackle transitional justice until almost a decade after the transition, and in Taiwan it never reached the political agenda, while Thailand shows that robust strategies attempted without the necessary resources can end in the breakdown of civilian control and democracy [6]. Bruneau and Matei's components translate into a design checklist: a legal basis for each security force, a civilian-led defense ministry with expert staff, parliamentary committees with budget authority, a functioning national security council, and promotion processes that civilians can review [5]. The authors' interpretation is that formal powers without staff are hollow; Croissant and colleagues found that Philippine defense agencies lacked civilian experts and were staffed mostly by former officers, so the military designed all major reform programs and then pushed them through Congress [6].

Legislatures face a calibration problem. Feaver and Kohn argue that legislators who lack expertise need strong staffs, because "a capable member of Congress can do much good, but a misinformed member can do extraordinary harm" [2]. Homan's Dutch evidence warns in the other direction: detailed parliamentary intervention in force composition imposed operational and financial costs and, he argues, risks a "self-inflicted co-optation" that weakens later review [4]. The author's reading is that legislatures do best when they set budgets and mandates in advance, stay involved across the whole decision chain, and concentrate on ex-post accountability rather than operational detail, as Homan recommends [4].

### For Elected Executives and Civilian Defense Officials

For executives, the moral hazard literature carries a warning. In Svolik's account, dictators who rely heavily on the army for domestic repression hand soldiers political leverage they can exploit, while rulers who kept the army out of repression, as in Tunisia, retained political control at the price of exposure to mass uprisings [9]; the author's interpretation is that elected executives who turn the armed forces against domestic opponents run a comparable risk. The OSCE commitments that internal security deployments follow constitutional procedure and that armed forces not be used to limit the peaceful exercise of rights express this lesson in normative form [10], and the 2022 open letter states that the military should play "a supporting rather than a leading role" in law enforcement [3]. The Thai case shows a second risk: civilian leaders who co-opt the officer corps through promotions and budgets can turn the military into a partisan instrument and provoke the intervention they meant to prevent [6]. Feaver and Kohn's account of the 2017-2021 period adds that staffing civilian posts with recently retired officers weakens the civilian side of the relationship, because the secretary of defense is meant to be the senior civilian below the president [2].

Civilians also carry obligations within the advisory process. The open letter asks civilians to give the military ample opportunity to express doubts in appropriate venues, to characterize military advice accurately in public, and to take responsibility for the consequences of the actions they direct [3]. Brooks's argument that civilian leaders who embrace Huntingtonian norms delegate wartime authority and loosen oversight implies that civilian control in the full sense requires active engagement with how operations serve political goals, not deference [1].

### For Military Professionals

For officers, the sources converge on a few operating rules. Advise candidly and privately before decisions, present options including unwelcome ones, and then execute legal orders [2][3]. Treat illegality, not personal judgments of morality or wisdom, as the ground for refusing an order; Feaver and Kohn, citing the retired deputy judge advocate general Charles Dunlap, note that the Uniform Code of Military Justice makes no allowance for disobeying "immoral" or "unethical" orders [2], while the OSCE Code states that orders contrary to national and international law must not be given and that a superior's responsibility does not exempt subordinates [10]. Recognize that the legislature is also a legitimate civilian principal [2][3]. Keep partisan expression private while serving, and, after retirement, avoid trading on the institution's nonpartisan reputation in campaigns [2][3]. The author's reading of the Brazilian and Korean cases is that these norms matter most in a crisis: in Brazil the refusal of two service chiefs stopped the plot, and in Korea observers credited civil disobedience by some members of the military [12][14].

### For Security Sector Reform Practitioners and New Democratic Governments

Bruneau and Matei find that security sector reform has lacked a consistent conceptualization, that many programs labeled as such reformed only the police or only the armed forces, and that the concept is often reduced to a long checklist [5]. Their alternative is to ask, for each of the three instruments of coercion and each of the six roles that security forces now perform (external war, internal war, terrorism, crime, humanitarian assistance, and peace operations), whether control mechanisms, oversight, and professional norms are in place, and whether the forces are effective at an affordable cost [5]. The OSCE Code points the same way by placing paramilitary forces, intelligence services, and the police under the same democratic control commitments as the army [10]. Svolik adds a warning for governments emerging from dictatorship: elected leaders can use popular support to face down a politically pivotal military, but the bargaining is risky, as Pakistan's Nawaz Sharif found when he forced one army chief to resign in 1998 and was deposed in 1999 after trying to dismiss the next [9].

### For Analysts of Regime Stability and Democratic Backsliding

For forecasters, the evidence cautions against reading the absence of coups as evidence of civilian control. Coup risk persists: Powell and Thyne read the post-2003 rise in coup success rates as suggesting that "coups remain a key element of governmental instability" [7], and Chin counted 11 successful coups in Africa between 2020 and November 2025 [17]. A coup history is itself a warning sign: Chin's finding that seven of the nine countries with successful coups since 2020 had suffered one within the previous 20 years underlies his conclusion that "coups beget more coups" [17]. Across the regimes in De Bruin's data, counterbalancing is a useful predictor that attempted coups will fail but a poor predictor that they will not be attempted [8]. The East Asian evidence points to observable indicators of control short of coups: officers in cabinet and legislative seats, military command of police and intelligence, and civilian authority over promotions [6]. The author's interpretation is that the Korean and Brazilian episodes add a newer indicator for democracies: whether elected executives try to use the armed forces against other branches of government or an election result, and whether commanders, courts, and legislatures refuse [11][12][14]. A further interpretation by the author is that the accountability phase also deserves attention: in Brazil, rank-loss cases against officers whose convictions are final were still pending before a court with a military majority more than a year after sentencing, while the officers kept drawing reserve pay [13][16].

## Criticisms and Limits of the Evidence

Huntington's model has been criticized from within the field. Brooks argues that its norms can enable military political activity while proscribing it, promote civilian authority while undermining civilian control, and support operational effectiveness while corroding strategic effectiveness [1]. Feaver and Kohn observe that Huntington's "real genius" lay in describing an approach that "already aligned with a traditional military point of view" [2]. The comparative scholars criticize structural theories for neglecting agency and agency-based theories for neglecting context, and acknowledge that their own framework does not yield specific hypotheses about which variables determine outcomes [6]. On measurement, Powell and Thyne caution that their coup data may miss events in states with little Western media coverage and that other conflict datasets code some coups as civil wars [7]; De Bruin notes that the apparent effect of counterbalancing on coup failure may partly reflect a shift toward lower-ranking plotters, whose coups fail more often [8]; and Bruneau and Matei acknowledge that success in fulfilling security roles is "very difficult to measure" in most instances [5]. Finally, the Brazilian case shows a normative dispute about credit: a University of Brasilia security researcher quoted by The Guardian argued that the commanders who refused the coup should have reported it to the police and that the armed forces cannot present themselves as the saviors of democracy [12].
