# Political Economy of Development -- Why Growth Depends on How Power Is Organized

Political economy of development studies how the distribution of political power, the rules that constrain its use, and the capacity to implement those rules shape economic development. Its central claim is that development cannot be explained by policy technique alone: investments, public goods, and market exchange depend on political arrangements that determine who can make credible commitments, who can capture rents, and who can enforce collective decisions. This topic concerns domestic institutions and policy performance rather than international strategy (North 1990; World Bank 2017).

## Background

The political economy of development begins with a simple departure from models that treat policy as an external input. A tax code, property-rights rule, procurement system, or public-investment plan does not implement itself. It is selected, interpreted, financed, and enforced by actors with unequal resources and potentially conflicting interests. Institutional economics therefore treats institutions as formal rules, informal constraints, and enforcement arrangements that structure incentives and reduce or redistribute uncertainty. In this view, economic performance depends not only on the stock of labor, capital, and technology, but also on whether participants expect contracts to be enforced, public authority to be bounded, and returns to investment to be sufficiently secure (North 1990).

The field developed by joining political science questions about power to development economics questions about production and income. Political scientists asked why states with similar legal forms could produce very different public outcomes. Development economists asked why technically plausible reforms could fail to be adopted, reversed, or implemented selectively. The joint answer is not that economics is irrelevant to development; rather, economic instruments operate through political institutions. The World Bank's 2017 synthesis defines governance as the process through which actors with unequal power interact within rules while pursuing conflicting interests. It identifies credible commitment, coordination, and cooperation as functions needed for effective policy, and it emphasizes that exclusion, capture, and clientelism can prevent policies from producing security, growth, or equity (World Bank 2017).

A major research program made this institutional argument empirically testable across countries. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson examined former colonies and used differences in expected European settler mortality as an instrument for institutional development. Their argument was that mortality influenced colonial settlement strategies, which influenced the institutions established, and that institutional differences persisted. Their 2001 American Economic Review article reported large estimated effects of institutions on income per capita after controlling for institutional effects; it did not demonstrate that all historical differences are reducible to a single colonial mechanism (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001). The study became influential because it redirected attention from geography alone toward historically formed political and economic institutions.

The colonial-origins result also illustrates why political economy requires scrutiny of causal identification rather than simple correlations. Albouy argued that the mortality data and cross-country assignments used in the original instrument were insufficiently robust, and that the resulting instrumental-variable estimates became unreliable under his corrections. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson replied that some of Albouy's exclusions and coding choices were themselves inconsistent and that their results strengthened under several robustness checks. The documented disagreement does not establish that institutions do not matter; it establishes that the magnitude and causal pathway in a particular historical design must be assessed with attention to source data, measurement, and identification (Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

This literature supplied the background for the inclusive-versus-extractive institutional framework associated with Acemoglu and Robinson. In that framework, economic institutions affect investment, innovation, and participation, while political institutions allocate the power that determines which economic rules endure. Inclusive arrangements are characterized analytically by broader participation and constraints on arbitrary power; extractive arrangements concentrate power and enable a narrower group to obtain rents. The framework is a theoretical guide to political incentives, not a universal country scorecard or a substitute for measuring concrete institutions such as tax administration, courts, procurement, or civil-service systems (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012; World Bank 2017).

Political economy also expanded the idea of the state from a regulator that merely corrects market failures to an organization that accumulates capacities. Besley and Persson model fiscal capacity and legal capacity as forward-looking public investments. Fiscal capacity permits a state to raise revenue; legal capacity supports markets through contract and property-rights enforcement. Their analysis links incentives to invest in those capacities with political instability, conflict, natural-resource dependence, and the expected value of public goods. Development thus includes construction of administrative and legal infrastructure, not only expansion of private output (Besley and Persson 2010).

The resource-curse literature made this point especially clear. Natural-resource abundance is not mechanically equivalent to poor development. Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik reported cross-country evidence that resource abundance was associated with different outcomes depending on institutional quality: countries with weak, "grabber friendly" institutions faced worse outcomes, while countries with producer-friendly institutions could benefit. This reframes the relevant political-economy question from whether resources are inherently harmful to how resource rents alter incentives for public authority, property rights, accountability, and rent seeking (Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

The contemporary field therefore studies development as a problem of organized power under constraints. It asks how political competition, representation, bureaucracy, credible rules, and accountability enable societies to turn resources into durable public and private investment. It also recognizes that institutions are measured imperfectly. The Worldwide Governance Indicators aggregate perception-based information on six dimensions, including government effectiveness, rule of law, and control of corruption, across more than 200 economies. These indicators support comparison but do not replace case-specific evidence about particular agencies, laws, or causal mechanisms (World Bank 2025).

## Core Concepts

### Institutions as rules, enforcement, and expectations

Institutions are not synonymous with organizations. A ministry, court, legislature, central bank, or tax authority is an organization. The rules governing appointment, discretion, transparency, appeal, and enforcement within and around that organization are institutions. This distinction matters because a country can copy an organizational form without reproducing the incentives and enforcement that make it function. A procurement agency, for example, may exist formally while contracts are allocated through discretionary patronage; a commercial court may exist formally while litigants cannot obtain timely or impartial enforcement. Political economy asks whether rules are credible in practice, not merely whether legislation names them (North 1990; World Bank 2017).

Institutions reduce uncertainty when they make behavior more predictable. A firm considering a long-lived investment needs more than a nominal statement of property rights. It needs an expectation that contracts will be enforceable, tax obligations will not change arbitrarily, permits will follow intelligible procedures, and political rivals will not be able to seize assets without recourse. These expectations are generated by repeated enforcement, administrative competence, and constraints on officials. The author's synthesis is that a rule has developmental value when it changes expected behavior for a broad class of actors, rather than when it merely announces an aspiration (North 1990; Besley and Persson 2010).

### Credible commitment and property rights

Credible commitment is the capacity of a government or other political actor to make a promise that remains believable after circumstances change. It is central to development because investment often requires costs today for returns that arrive later. A government may promise not to expropriate a factory, alter a concession arbitrarily, or redirect infrastructure funds, but the promise is weak if the same government can profitably reverse it without institutional constraint. Courts, legislative oversight, transparent fiscal rules, professional civil services, and dispersed political power can make commitments more credible when they impose costs on arbitrary reversal. These arrangements are not guarantees of investment; they are mechanisms that alter the expected security of returns (North 1990; World Bank 2017).

Property rights should therefore be interpreted broadly. They include rights to possess or transfer assets, but their development relevance also depends on contract enforcement, impartial administration, access to information, and the ability to challenge official decisions. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson used risk of expropriation as one institutional measure in their colonial-origins analysis. Their result supports institutional relevance within their sample and design; the later comment-reply exchange cautions against treating a single historical proxy as a complete measure of contemporary property-rights quality (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

### Political power, rents, and distributional conflict

Political economy treats policy as distributive because most rules create concentrated gains and diffuse costs. A subsidy, licensing regime, tariff, tax exemption, land allocation, public contract, or natural-resource concession can create rents: returns above those available in a competitive alternative. Groups that receive such returns have incentives to preserve the underlying rules, while those who bear the dispersed cost may find collective action difficult. Policy persistence can therefore reflect the political value of a rule to its beneficiaries rather than its aggregate contribution to development (World Bank 2017).

This does not imply that every public program is rent seeking or that all redistribution is detrimental. Public revenue can finance infrastructure, health, education, courts, and administrative systems that expand productive opportunity. The relevant analytical distinction is between arrangements that permit broad, contestable access under general rules and arrangements that allocate benefits through unreviewable political connections. The World Bank's governance framework identifies power asymmetries, exclusion, capture, and clientelism as pathways through which technically well-designed policies can fail. Its point is institutional: a policy's effectiveness depends on how actors can bargain, coordinate, cooperate, and be held accountable (World Bank 2017).

### Inclusion, constraints, and political competition

The inclusive-extractive framework links economic opportunity to the political allocation of power. Inclusive political institutions, as used in the framework, distribute political influence more broadly and impose constraints on executive or elite discretion; inclusive economic institutions create more open opportunities for investment and exchange. Extractive institutions concentrate power and may permit a narrow group to transfer resources from others. The framework predicts self-reinforcement because political power can preserve the economic privileges that help sustain it (Acemoglu and Robinson 2012).

This concept should not be collapsed into a binary label for every regime. Empirical analysis needs to specify the institution at issue: electoral competition, judicial independence, bureaucratic recruitment, local representation, audit capacity, procurement rules, or legal access. Democracy is one institutional dimension, but it is neither a synonym for administrative capability nor an automatic guarantee of growth. Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo, and Robinson estimated that transitions to democracy increased GDP per capita by about 20 percent in the long run in their panel design, with similar estimates from propensity-score and instrumental-variable approaches. That result concerns average effects under the authors' specified methods and does not establish that each democratization produces the same path or that every component of democratic governance has the same mechanism (Acemoglu et al. 2019).

### State capacity as fiscal and legal infrastructure

State capacity is the ability to implement collectively chosen rules across territory. It has fiscal, legal, administrative, and informational dimensions. Fiscal capacity includes identifying tax bases, collecting revenue, and enforcing compliance. Legal capacity includes enforcing contracts and property rights. Administrative capacity includes recruiting, coordinating, and supervising personnel who deliver services and implement policy. Political economy treats capacity as an investment and incentive problem because rulers choose whether to build institutions whose benefits may be broad and long term while whose constraints on arbitrary discretion may be immediate (Besley and Persson 2010).

Fiscal and legal capacity can complement each other. A state that supports market activity can enlarge the tax base available to finance public goods, while revenue can fund courts, records, and enforcement. Conversely, dependence on a narrow revenue source can reduce incentives to develop broad-based taxation and its associated accountability. This logic does not mean that taxation automatically produces representation or good government. It identifies a channel through which the structure of revenue affects the state's relationship to citizens and its incentives to build durable administrative systems (Besley and Persson 2010; World Bank 2017).

Capacity is distinct from unconstrained coercion. A government may issue commands but be unable to maintain accurate records, collect taxes predictably, enforce commercial judgments, or deliver comparable services outside major cities. For development analysis, the relevant question is whether public authority can implement general rules rather than merely exercise episodic power. The related topic on state capacity develops this distinction and its measurement problems in greater detail (`library/political-science-public-policy/state-capacity.md`).

### Corruption, accountability, and principal-agent problems

Corruption is a political-economy mechanism because it reallocates authority and public resources through private exchange. When public officials have discretion, information advantages, and weak monitoring, they may exchange access to permits, contracts, tax treatment, or enforcement for private benefit. The immediate effect is not only a transfer. It can alter which firms invest, which projects are selected, which rules are enforced, and whether citizens regard public obligations as reciprocal. The World Bank identifies corruption and clientelism as expressions of power asymmetry that can weaken policy effectiveness (World Bank 2017).

Accountability addresses this problem through mechanisms that make office holders answerable for decisions. Electoral competition can provide vertical accountability; courts, audit bodies, legislatures, and oversight agencies can provide horizontal accountability; civil society and media can provide social monitoring. Each mechanism depends on information, institutional independence, and the ability to impose consequences. The author's synthesis is that accountability is most useful when it connects observable performance to a credible response rather than merely adding reporting requirements. The related topic on corruption examines principal-agent and collective-action accounts in depth (`library/political-science-public-policy/corruption-accountability-institutional-quality.md`).

### Path dependence, critical junctures, and institutional change

Path dependence means that earlier institutional choices and political settlements shape later options. It does not mean that history fixes outcomes permanently. It means that organizations, expectations, legal arrangements, and beneficiaries of existing rules create switching costs and political resistance. The colonial-origins literature is one argument about a historical pathway; the continuing debate over its identification shows why path dependence should be examined through evidence rather than asserted as a catch-all explanation (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001; Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

Institutional change requires attention to coalitions and incentives. A reform that threatens a beneficiary's rents may be blocked even when it improves aggregate welfare. A crisis, leadership transition, election, fiscal shock, or social mobilization can alter bargaining power and open a period in which rules become contestable. The World Bank describes change as arising through shifts in incentives and preferences, elite bargains, citizen engagement, and sometimes international influence. For domestic political-economy analysis, the operational question is which actors gain or lose from a rule change and whether a coalition can sustain implementation after formal adoption (World Bank 2017).

### Natural resources and the fiscal contract

Natural resources create large, geographically concentrated, and often state-controlled rents. These features can increase the political value of controlling public office and can weaken incentives to build broad tax systems. The political-economy problem is not the physical presence of oil, minerals, or other resources; it is how revenue rights, concession decisions, information, and spending authority are governed. Where institutions permit opaque allocation and weak constraints, rents can intensify competition for control and reduce the expected return to productive activity (Besley and Persson 2010; Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik's analysis provides a conditional formulation. They reported that resource-rich countries included both growth winners and losers, and that institutional quality distinguished the outcomes in their cross-country analysis. Their result supports treating resource wealth as a governance challenge rather than a deterministic curse. It also cautions against importing a resource-management model without examining property rights, bureaucratic quality, transparency, accountability, and the domestic distribution of political power (Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

### Measurement and the level-of-analysis problem

Measures of institutions are useful only when their meaning matches the research question. The Worldwide Governance Indicators report estimates for voice and accountability, political stability, government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption. They are composite perception-based measures and are suited to broad comparative description. They do not directly establish a causal effect of one particular reform, nor do they identify whether a weakness originates in national law, local implementation, organizational competence, or political interference (World Bank 2025).

Political-economy analysis should therefore combine broad indicators with institution-specific evidence. A researcher examining tax capacity may use revenue administration data, compliance records, and legal procedures; a researcher examining public procurement may use contract-level data, audit outcomes, and competition measures. The author's assessment is that the most useful question is not "Does governance matter?" in the abstract, but "Which rule, organization, incentive, and enforcement failure is binding in this setting?" This approach keeps the analysis within the anchor's focus on how political rules convert authority into outcomes (World Bank 2017; World Bank 2025).

## Evidence

### Colonial institutions and long-run income -- Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's 2001 study is a central empirical test of the institutional-development claim. The authors analyzed former colonies and used estimated European settler mortality as an instrument for contemporary institutional quality, measured partly through protection against expropriation. Their causal argument was sequential: disease environments affected European settlement feasibility; settlement feasibility affected whether colonizers created institutions that protected settlers or extracted resources; those institutions persisted; and the institutional differences affected income per capita. They reported large estimated institutional effects and found that the Africa and latitude variables were no longer negative once institutional effects were controlled for in their specification (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001).

The method is important because income and institutions can influence each other. A simple comparison of richer and poorer countries cannot determine whether institutions caused income, whether income improved institutions, or whether a third factor produced both. An instrument is intended to isolate variation in institutions that is not generated by current income. The credibility of the conclusion consequently depends on the quality of the instrument and the causal exclusion assumptions, not merely on statistical significance (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2001).

The subsequent scholarly exchange is part of the evidence, not an incidental dispute. Albouy's 2012 comment argued that many mortality observations were assigned from other countries or drawn from non-comparable populations, and that corrections made the first-stage relationship and instrumental-variable estimates unreliable. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's reply argued that Albouy's data choices and exclusions were inconsistent, and that their findings remained robust under their corrected approaches. The unresolved lesson for users of this literature is methodological: colonial history is relevant to institutional development, but individual estimates should be read alongside data provenance, alternative coding decisions, and replication evidence (Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

### Democratization and long-run income -- Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo, and Robinson

Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo, and Robinson studied the effect of democracy on GDP per capita using a country panel covering 1960 to 2010. Their design used country fixed effects and GDP dynamics, a consolidated democracy measure intended to reduce measurement error, propensity-score reweighting, and an instrumental-variable strategy based on regional waves of democratization and reversal. Their baseline estimates indicated that democratizations raised GDP per capita by about 20 percent in the long run, and they reported similar effects across different levels of development (Acemoglu et al. 2019).

The reported mechanisms were increased investment, schooling, health, economic reform, public-good provision, and reduced social unrest. These mechanisms are compatible with a political-economy account in which political inclusion and constraints can change both the selection of policies and the credibility of their implementation. They are not a claim that elections alone create bureaucracy, courts, or development. The study estimates an average treatment effect under particular data and modeling choices; the mechanisms and outcomes remain conditional on complementary institutions, conflict, and administrative capacity (Acemoglu et al. 2019; World Bank 2017).

This study also helps distinguish a level from a mechanism. It does not compare every democracy with every nondemocracy at one moment, which would confound regime type with prior income, conflict, and historical trajectories. Instead, it focuses on changes in political regime and models income dynamics. That design addresses some selection problems but cannot eliminate all uncertainty about institutional measurement and the heterogeneous experiences of individual countries. Its evidentiary contribution is stronger than an unconditional cross-sectional correlation, but it should not be used as a universal forecast for any single transition (Acemoglu et al. 2019).

### State capacity, conflict, and development -- Besley and Persson

Besley and Persson developed a political-economy model in which fiscal and legal capacity are accumulated investments by governments. The model asks how political stability, external and internal conflict, natural-resource dependence, and the expected value of public goods affect incentives to invest in revenue collection and market-supporting institutions. It links weak capacity to the persistence of weak states, because a state that cannot raise revenue or support markets has fewer means to finance public goods and fewer incentives for broad-based economic development (Besley and Persson 2010).

The contribution is both conceptual and empirical in orientation. Rather than treating state capacity as an endowment, the authors model it as a choice shaped by expected future political conditions. This produces an explanation for feedback loops: weak capacity can be associated with conflict and low development, while political stability and broad benefits from public goods can support investments in capacity. The result does not imply that conflict always reduces capacity or that resource income always prevents development. It identifies political incentives and revenue structure as variables that can alter whether capacity investments are made (Besley and Persson 2010).

For the present topic, the study supplies a bridge between institutions and implementation. Property rights and tax systems are not merely legal texts; they require records, personnel, enforcement, and political willingness to invest. A country can announce market-supporting rules without possessing the fiscal or legal capacity to deliver them consistently. This distinction is crucial for explaining why formal reform can produce weak observable outcomes (Besley and Persson 2010; World Bank 2017).

### Resources and institutional quality -- Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik

Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik examined cross-country data on resource abundance and economic performance and reported that the apparent effect of resources differed by institutional quality. Their reported sample included 87 countries. In countries characterized by weak bureaucracy and insecure property rights, greater resource abundance was associated with lower growth and greater instability; in countries with stronger public bureaucracy and more secure property rights, resource abundance was associated with better outcomes. The study's central finding is conditional rather than deterministic: institutional arrangements mediate the relationship between resource wealth and development (Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

The proposed mechanism is political. When institutions make appropriation attractive, a resource windfall can draw effort into rent seeking and intensify competition to control public authority. When institutions protect production and constrain discretion, resource revenue can finance investment and public goods instead. This is consistent with Besley and Persson's emphasis on how resource dependence affects incentives to invest in fiscal and legal capacity. It also directs analysts to test specific channels, such as concession allocation, revenue transparency, public financial management, and the independence of oversight, rather than treating resource exports as an explanation by themselves (Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006; Besley and Persson 2010).

### Governance evidence and measurement -- World Bank reports and indicators

The World Bank's 2017 World Development Report synthesizes comparative evidence on governance and development rather than presenting one single causal experiment. It argues that policy effectiveness depends on commitment, coordination, and cooperation, and that policy failure often results when power asymmetries enable capture, exclusion, or clientelism. Its cases and framework show why a technically sound intervention may fail if the political actors required to adopt, finance, or implement it have incentives to block or redirect it. This supports an institutional interpretation of development policy while leaving the specific binding constraint to be established in each context (World Bank 2017).

The Worldwide Governance Indicators provide descriptive evidence for comparison across countries and time. The 2025 revision reports six composite dimensions for more than 200 economies and draws on perception data from 35 cross-country sources. Government effectiveness, for example, captures perceptions of public-service quality, civil-service quality, policy formulation and implementation, and the credibility of government decisions. The indicators are valuable for screening patterns and generating hypotheses, but their source composition and perception basis mean they should not be interpreted as direct measurements of all institutional behavior or as causal evaluations of a policy (World Bank 2025).

Taken together, the evidence supports a bounded conclusion. Political institutions, capacity, and accountability are plausible and repeatedly studied contributors to development outcomes. The evidence does not support a mechanical recipe in which one formal institution always produces growth. Historical instruments are contested, composite indicators are indirect, and causal effects can vary with revenue structure, administrative capacity, conflict, and the distribution of political power. The author's synthesis is that political economy is strongest when it identifies a concrete institutional mechanism, a credible empirical strategy, and the limits of generalization (Albouy 2012; World Bank 2025).

## Implications

### For institutional reform and public administration

Political-economy analysis changes the starting point for reform. The initial question is not only which policy instrument is technically efficient, but also which actors must authorize, finance, implement, monitor, and comply with it. A reform that threatens concentrated rents can fail despite favorable cost-benefit analysis; a reform that does not create credible implementation incentives can fail despite formal adoption. The World Bank's framework suggests examining commitment, coordination, and cooperation alongside the distribution of power in the policy arena (World Bank 2017).

For public administration, this means separating formal design from operational capacity. A new law or agency is not evidence that its functions can be performed. Analysts should identify the records, skills, budget authority, information systems, appeal routes, supervisory mechanisms, and enforcement powers required to implement the rule. Besley and Persson's capacity framework provides a reason to assess whether fiscal and legal infrastructure are being accumulated, not merely whether a policy has been announced. The related topic on policy implementation provides a complementary account of how multiple decision points and administrative discretion can separate legislative intent from observed outcomes (`library/political-science-public-policy/public-policy-design-implementation.md`).

The author's assessment is that reforms should be diagnosed as institutional systems rather than as isolated legal changes. For example, a procurement reform may require transparent rules, competition, professional staffing, audit capacity, accessible complaint processes, and credible sanctions. Removing only one defect may shift the channel of discretion rather than change the incentive structure. This is an analytical implication, not a claim that one uniform sequence is suitable for all countries (World Bank 2017; Besley and Persson 2010).

### For development organizations and policy evaluation

Development organizations frequently operate through projects with defined technical objectives: build infrastructure, expand a service, alter a tax rule, strengthen an agency, or distribute resources. Political economy requires an additional assessment of who controls the relevant decisions and how implementation will be sustained when external attention or funding changes. The World Bank argues that reforms are more likely to be effective when they shift incentives, take account of excluded participants, and strengthen coalitions that support implementation. This framing rejects the assumption that "best practice" can be transferred independently of domestic political arrangements (World Bank 2017).

Evaluation design should distinguish outputs from institutional effects. A training program can count officials trained; an anti-corruption program can count laws passed; a public-finance project can count systems installed. Those counts do not by themselves show whether officials use the systems, whether rules are enforced impartially, or whether performance persists after project closure. Institution-specific indicators, administrative data, audit results, user experience, and credible counterfactuals are needed to assess whether the proposed mechanism operated. Composite governance measures can contextualize these evaluations, but they cannot substitute for them (World Bank 2025).

The evidence on historical institutions also advises caution in causal claims. The colonial-origins debate demonstrates that influential estimates can depend on disputed data construction and exclusion assumptions. Organizations should therefore state the empirical basis for a diagnosis, test alternative explanations where feasible, and distinguish association from causal evidence. This practice is particularly important when a diagnostic may influence funding, political legitimacy, or institutional restructuring (Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

### For states dependent on natural-resource revenue

Resource dependence should be analyzed as a fiscal and political structure. Large rents controlled through public authority can reduce the need for broad taxation while increasing the prize associated with control over offices, concessions, and public spending. Political-economy analysis directs attention to how resource rights are allocated, how revenue is disclosed and audited, how spending decisions are constrained, and whether citizens and oversight institutions can contest misuse. These are domestic governance questions within this domain, not claims about international commodity strategy (Besley and Persson 2010; Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik's conditional finding is especially useful for avoiding fatalism. It does not say that resource abundance must cause stagnation, nor that institutional quality can be inferred from a country's export mix. It says that the association between resources and outcomes differs across institutional settings. The practical implication is to investigate producer incentives and appropriability: whether political and economic rules reward productive investment or extraction through privileged access. This framing is more informative than treating "the resource curse" as a fixed national destiny (Mehlum, Moene, and Torvik 2006).

The author's assessment is that accountability arrangements should be evaluated as part of the revenue system. Revenue transparency without institutional capacity to audit, investigate, publish findings, or impose consequences is unlikely to establish a durable fiscal contract. Conversely, legal and fiscal capacity can make resource revenue more governable when they constrain discretion and support public goods. The required institutional combinations will vary by constitutional structure, administrative reach, and political settlement (Besley and Persson 2010; World Bank 2017).

### For firms, investors, and economic participants

Firms and investors face political economy through the reliability of contracts, permits, tax administration, infrastructure, and dispute resolution. A formal investment incentive may have little value if its implementation is discretionary or if the relevant authority can reverse it without constraint. Political risk analysis should therefore examine the specific public institutions that affect an investment horizon: commercial courts, revenue authorities, regulators, local administration, procurement systems, and rules governing political transitions. Broad governance indicators can provide a comparative starting point, but they should be supplemented by sector- and location-specific evidence (World Bank 2025; North 1990).

The distinction between broad capacity and arbitrary power is material. A government may appear decisive while lacking the administrative reach to honor contracts or provide predictable services. Conversely, a state may have substantial administrative capacity but deploy it selectively for politically connected interests. The appropriate question is not whether the state is simply "strong" or "weak," but whether its capacity is governed by general, contestable rules and whether commitments are credible for actors outside the ruling coalition (Besley and Persson 2010; World Bank 2017).

For value-investing analysis, institutional quality is part of the operating environment rather than a substitute for firm-level work. The author's assessment is that country-level rule-of-law, corruption-control, and government-effectiveness measures can identify areas for deeper due diligence, but they cannot determine a business's intrinsic value, governance quality, or cash-generation capacity. Company analysis still requires direct examination of competitive position, management incentives, balance-sheet obligations, and the relevant regulatory and tax exposure. This is an application of the measurement principle: a broad proxy should not be used as a complete causal explanation (World Bank 2025; North 1990).

### For citizens, researchers, and democratic accountability

Citizens encounter political economy when public rules are applied predictably or selectively. Accessible information, independent oversight, avenues for appeal, and meaningful political competition can reduce the information asymmetries that permit capture and corruption. However, transparency is not equivalent to accountability. Information must be usable by institutions or coalitions that can investigate, contest, or sanction misconduct. The World Bank's emphasis on citizen engagement and power asymmetries indicates why disclosure reforms can have limited effects when the surrounding enforcement system is weak (World Bank 2017).

Researchers should make the unit of analysis explicit. A national governance score may conceal substantial variation between sectors, regions, or agencies. A study of property rights should specify whether it measures land registration, contract enforcement, expropriation risk, concession security, or judicial independence. A study of capacity should specify whether it concerns taxation, public health, procurement, policing, or courts. The WGI methodology supports comparative aggregation but also advises users to consult underlying indicators and recognize uncertainty; it is not a warrant to replace detailed institutional inquiry with a single rank (World Bank 2025).

The evidence base also requires disciplined skepticism. Causal claims about institutions confront reverse causation, omitted variables, historical measurement problems, and heterogeneous effects. The most productive response is neither to reject institutional analysis nor to treat any single paper as decisive. It is to compare research designs, examine counterarguments, specify mechanisms, and seek evidence that would distinguish among competing explanations. The colonial-origins comment and reply are a concrete example of how a field can advance through scrutiny of assumptions rather than through unqualified repetition (Albouy 2012; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson 2012).

Political economy of development therefore supplies a framework for asking better questions about growth and public performance. It directs attention to who holds power, which commitments are credible, how rents are allocated, whether the state can implement general rules, and which actors can demand correction when rules fail. Its central implication is analytical: sustainable development is more likely when institutional arrangements align political incentives with broad-based investment, accountable public authority, and effective implementation. The empirical strength of that implication in any setting depends on evidence about the specific institutions and mechanisms involved (North 1990; World Bank 2017).
