# Superforecasting -- Why Some Ordinary People Are Dramatically Better at Predicting the Future Than Experts

Superforecasting is the empirically demonstrated capacity of a small
subset of people -- roughly 2% of forecasters -- to predict
geopolitical and economic events with accuracy that consistently
surpasses professional intelligence analysts with access to classified
information. Identified by Philip Tetlock and Barbara Mellers through
the Good Judgment Project (2011-2015), a forecasting tournament
sponsored by the U.S. intelligence community's IARPA, superforecasters
are not distinguished by IQ, domain expertise, or access to special
information. They are distinguished by how they think: they are
probabilistic, actively open-minded, calibration-obsessed, and
relentlessly self-correcting. The superforecasting research program
ranks among the most important findings in decision science because it
demonstrates that forecasting skill is real, measurable, and trainable
-- and because it offers a concrete playbook for improving judgment
under uncertainty.

## Background

The superforecasting story begins with a humbling finding about
expertise. In 1984, Philip Tetlock at the University of California,
Berkeley, launched a twenty-year study of expert political judgment.
He recruited 284 professional forecasters -- political scientists,
economists, intelligence analysts, and journalists -- and tracked
roughly 28,000 predictions about the future. The results, published in
"Expert Political Judgment: How Good Is It? How Can We Know?" (2005),
were devastating to the conventional wisdom about expertise. The
average expert was barely better than random guessing. On long-range
forecasts, experts performed worse than simple extrapolation
algorithms. More strikingly, the experts who appeared most confident on
television were the least accurate, while those who qualified their
judgments with probabilities and caveats performed better.

Tetlock's most influential finding from this first phase was the
distinction between two cognitive styles, which he labeled using Isaiah
Berlin's metaphor of the fox and the hedgehog. Hedgehogs know one big
thing -- they organize their thinking around a single grand theory and
apply it to every problem. Foxes know many small things -- they draw
eclectically from multiple frameworks, hold their views tentatively,
and update readily when evidence shifts. Foxes systematically
outperformed hedgehogs on forecasting accuracy. The critical variable
was not what forecasters believed but how they thought.

Encouraged by these findings, Tetlock launched the Good Judgment
Project (GJP) in 2011 as part of an IARPA-sponsored forecasting
tournament that pitted five research teams against one another to
predict real geopolitical events. The GJP recruited thousands of
volunteer forecasters, trained some of them in probabilistic reasoning
techniques, and scored everyone's predictions using Brier scores --
proper scoring rules borrowed from meteorology that reward calibration
and penalize overconfidence. The GJP won the tournament decisively,
beating the official intelligence community control group by margins
that caused competing teams to withdraw from the tournament.

Within the GJP's volunteer pool, Tetlock and his colleagues identified
a small elite: roughly 2% of participants whose forecasting accuracy
was so consistently superior that it could not be attributed to luck.
Dubbed "superforecasters," these individuals outperformed regular
forecasters by approximately 60%, prediction markets by roughly 20%,
and -- most remarkably -- intelligence analysts with access to
classified information by over 30%. Tetlock and journalist Dan Gardner
published the full account in "Superforecasting: The Art and Science
of Prediction" (2015), which synthesized the findings into both a
scientific argument and a practical guide.

After the IARPA tournaments ended in 2015, many superforecasters
continued forecasting professionally through Good Judgment Inc, the
commercial successor to the GJP. A 2021 technical white paper by
Christopher Karvetski analyzed six additional years of data (2015-2021)
and confirmed that the superforecaster advantage persisted: across 108
diverse geopolitical forecasting questions, superforecasters were 35.9%
more accurate than a large peer group of online forecasters, and their
aggregate forecasts were 25.1% more accurate than the crowd aggregate.

## Core Concepts

### Foxes vs. Hedgehogs: The Cognitive Style That Predicts Accuracy

The fox-hedgehog distinction is the foundational insight of Tetlock's
research program. In his original 20-year study, hedgehog experts --
those who organized their thinking around a single Big Idea, whether
Marxism, free-market fundamentalism, or environmental doomsterism --
were systematically the worst forecasters. They squeezed complex
problems into preferred cause-effect templates, dismissed contradictory
evidence as irrelevant distractions, and piled up reasons why they were
right using connecting phrases like "furthermore" and "moreover." They
were more likely to declare events "impossible" or "certain" and
stubbornly refused to change their minds when their predictions failed.

Fox experts drew on many analytical tools, gathered information from
diverse sources, and shifted mental gears frequently. Their speech was
sprinkled with transition markers like "however," "but," and "although."
They talked in probabilities rather than certainties and readily
admitted error. Tetlock's critical finding was that foxes beat
hedgehogs on both calibration (the match between confidence and
accuracy) and discrimination (the ability to separate events that will
happen from those that will not). Foxes had real foresight; hedgehogs
did not.

One of Tetlock's most counterintuitive observations is that fox-like
thinking is an aggregation process conducted inside a single skull.
Just as Francis Galton discovered that averaging crowd estimates
produces surprising accuracy, foxes aggregate diverse perspectives
internally. The superforecaster does not rely on one model but
synthesizes many. This is why intellectual diversity within one mind is
as important as intellectual diversity across a group.

### Calibration and the Brier Score: Keeping Honest Score

The superforecasting research program could not exist without a
rigorous scoring system, and the Brier score -- developed by
meteorologist Glenn Brier in 1950 -- is the standard. For a binary
forecast (an event either happens or does not), the Brier score is the
squared difference between the forecasted probability and the outcome
(1 if true, 0 if false), averaged across all forecasts. A perfect score
is 0; a coin-flip forecast on a 50-50 event scores 0.25; forecasting
100% on the wrong outcome scores the maximum of 1. Lower is better.

The Brier score has two critical properties. First, it rewards
calibration directly: a forecaster who says 70% must be right roughly
70% of the time, or the squared errors accumulate. Second, it
decomposes into components that separate calibration error from
resolution, allowing analysts to diagnose whether a forecaster is
overconfident, underconfident, or simply unable to discriminate signal
from noise.

Superforecasters are distinguished by their relationship to the Brier
score: they track their own scores obsessively. Most people are
confidently wrong -- they claim 90% certainty for predictions that come
true only 50% of the time. Superforecasters achieve Brier scores around
0.15 on difficult geopolitical questions, compared to roughly 0.25 for
ordinary smart participants. Their calibration curves are nearly
diagonal: when they say 20%, events happen roughly 20% of the time;
when they say 80%, events happen roughly 80% of the time. This
calibration is not a gift; it is the product of deliberate practice and
a cultural commitment to keeping score.

### The Traits and Habits of Superforecasters

Tetlock's research identified a consistent set of cognitive traits and
behavioral habits that distinguish superforecasters. None are rare
innate gifts; they are cultivated dispositions.

**Actively open-minded thinking (AOM).** Coined by psychologist
Jonathan Baron, AOM is the disposition to seek out evidence that might
disprove one's beliefs rather than confirm them. Superforecasters score
highly on AOM tests, agreeing with statements like "people should take
into consideration evidence that goes against their beliefs" and
disagreeing with "changing your mind is a sign of weakness." One
superforecaster, Doug Lorch, built a database of hundreds of news
sources tagged by ideological orientation and wrote a program that
selects reading material emphasizing viewpoints contrary to his own.
This is open-mindedness as a deliberate practice, not a passive trait.

**Probabilistic thinking.** Superforecasters think in degrees of
belief, not binary certainties. They resist the temptation to ask "why"
something happened -- a question that leads to fatalistic narratives --
and instead ask "how likely" various outcomes were, which directs
attention to base rates and evidence. In Tetlock's survey, they
overwhelmingly rejected fatalistic statements like "everything happens
for a reason" and embraced probabilistic statements like "nothing is
inevitable."

**Fermi estimation and problem decomposition.** Faced with a vague
forecasting question, superforecasters break it into tractable
sub-questions. "Will Country X default on its debt by year-end?"
becomes a chain of narrower inquiries: What is the current debt-to-GDP
ratio? What is the historical base rate of sovereign defaults at this
ratio? What are bond yields signaling? Are there political
stabilization mechanisms? Each sub-question yields a probability
estimate, and these are combined into a holistic forecast. This is
Fermi estimation applied to geopolitics.

**Incremental updating.** Superforecasters update frequently but by
small amounts. The 2021 Karvetski analysis found that superforecasters
made roughly 11 forecasts per question with an average update of 0.036
probability units, compared to 2.8 forecasts per question and 0.059
units for ordinary forecasters. They do not swing from 20% to 80% on a
single headline; they nudge by a few percentage points as evidence
accumulates, approximating Bayesian updating in practice.

**Intellectual humility and the commitment to being wrong.**
Superforecasters hold strong opinions weakly. They commit to a number
but treat it as provisional. Being proven wrong is not humiliation; it
is data. They are comfortable saying "I do not know" and treat their
beliefs as hypotheses to be tested rather than positions to be defended.

**The outside view.** Borrowing from Kahneman's terminology,
superforecasters anchor their forecasts in base rates -- the outside
view -- before adjusting for case-specific details -- the inside view.
If asked whether a particular startup will succeed, they begin not with
the startup's compelling story but with the base rate that roughly 90%
of startups fail. Specific details can raise or lower the probability,
but the base rate provides the gravitational center.

### Teams, Aggregation, and the Wisdom of the Elite Crowd

One of the GJP's most practical findings concerns the power of teams
and aggregation. Superforecasters placed in collaborative teams --
"superteams" -- outperformed even the best individual forecasters.
Teams benefited from having a coordinator who managed discussion
dynamics, and from extremizing algorithms that pushed aggregated
forecasts away from 50% when individual forecasts clustered toward one
outcome. The combination of elite individual forecasters and thoughtful
team process produced the highest accuracy the GJP observed.

Aggregation also benefited ordinary forecasters dramatically, but with
a revealing asymmetry: aggregating mediocre forecasts improved them
more than aggregating elite forecasts improved the elite. This means
prediction markets and crowd-wisdom approaches work -- but an elite
crowd works better. The superforecaster approach is not to replace
individual judgment with crowd aggregation but to cultivate elite
individual judgment and then aggregate it.

## Evidence

The evidence base for superforecasting spans three decades and two
major research phases, making it one of the most thoroughly documented
findings in social science.

**Phase 1: Expert Political Judgment (1984-2005).** Tetlock's original
study tracked 284 experts making 28,000 predictions over twenty years.
Key findings: the average expert barely outperformed random guessing;
experts with the highest media profiles were the least accurate;
fox-like cognitive style systematically outperformed hedgehog-like
cognitive style; and simple statistical models outperformed the
majority of human experts. This phase established that expertise alone
is an unreliable predictor of judgment quality and that cognitive style
matters more than credentials.

**Phase 2: The Good Judgment Project (2011-2015).** The GJP was a
multi-year forecasting tournament with thousands of participants
making predictions on real geopolitical events under controlled
conditions. Key findings: a small elite (roughly 2% of participants)
consistently achieved accuracy far beyond the mean; these
superforecasters outperformed intelligence analysts with classified
access by over 30%; training in probabilistic reasoning improved
accuracy measurably; and superteams combining elite forecasters with
structured aggregation were the highest-performing units in the
tournament. The GJP won the IARPA tournament so decisively that
competing teams withdrew before the final year.

**Phase 3: Commercial Validation (2015-2021).** Karvetski's 2021 white
paper, "Superforecasters: A Decade of Stochastic Dominance," analyzed
six years of post-tournament forecasting data. Across 108 diverse
questions, superforecasters' daily average Brier scores were 35.9%
lower (better) than those of a 14,000-person peer group (0.166 vs.
0.259). Superforecaster aggregate forecasts were 25.1% more accurate
than crowd aggregates. On calibration specifically, superforecasters
showed a miscalibration score of 0.014 (near-perfect diagonal),
compared to 0.068 for peers -- a 79% improvement. Their discrimination
(AUC = 0.950 vs. 0.867) was dramatically better, and their between-
forecaster noise was 55% lower (standard deviation of 0.046 vs. 0.102
probability units).

**Temporal Advantage.** UC-Irvine researcher Mark Steyvers found that
superforecasters anticipated events 400 days in advance as accurately
as regular forecasters could see those events 150 days ahead. And when
GJ Open (regular) forecasters were categorically wrong -- assigning
near-100% to the wrong outcome -- superforecasters on the same question
and same day averaged a forecast of 0.78/0.22, meaning they were wrong
but meaningfully less wrong. Even at their worst, superforecasters were
rarely blindsided.

**Replication and Generality.** The superforecaster advantage has held
across diverse question categories (elections, economics, COVID-19,
geopolitics), across time horizons from weeks to years, and across
multiple cohorts of forecasters identified years apart. The effect is
not attributable to a single tournament or cohort. The 2021 analysis
showed that superforecasters identified in the original 2011-2015
tournaments remained dominant years later, while newly identified
superforecasters showed the same performance characteristics.

## Implications

The superforecasting research has far-reaching implications for how
organizations make decisions under uncertainty and how individuals can
improve their own judgment.

**For intelligence and national security.** The most direct implication
is that intelligence agencies can improve forecasting accuracy by
adopting tournament-based forecasting, proper scoring rules, and
structured aggregation -- and by identifying and cultivating
individuals with superforecaster cognitive traits. The fact that
superforecasters without security clearances outperformed analysts with
classified access suggests that cognitive style can outweigh
information advantage. The U.S. intelligence community has incorporated
elements of the superforecasting methodology into some analytical
tradecraft training, though full adoption remains inconsistent.

**For investing and business strategy.** Superforecasting methods
translate directly to investment analysis. Value investors already
practice several superforecaster habits: anchoring in base rates
(P/E means and historical returns), seeking disconfirming evidence
(focusing on what could go wrong with a thesis), and updating
incrementally as quarterly results arrive. The superforecasting
framework adds rigor: explicit probability estimates, tracked track
records, and the discipline of scoring predictions rather than
remembering only the winners. A fund manager who kept a Brier score
track record of their investment theses would have a genuine measure of
whether their judgment adds value -- something remarkably few investors
possess.

**For organizational decision-making.** The team and aggregation
findings suggest a template for high-stakes decisions: assemble
individual forecasters with demonstrated calibration, have them make
independent initial forecasts, then allow structured deliberation with
a facilitator, and finally apply an extremizing algorithm to the
aggregated estimate. This process -- independent judgment first,
deliberation second, algorithmic aggregation third -- avoids the
groupthink and anchoring that plague conventional meetings.

**For personal judgment.** The superforecaster traits are trainable.
The GJP found measurable improvement in Brier scores after roughly 100
logged forecasts, and substantial improvement after a year of
consistent practice. The core practices are accessible to anyone: keep
a written record of your predictions with probabilities and deadlines;
score them honestly after resolution; seek out disconfirming evidence
deliberately; break complex questions into Fermi-able components; and
update incrementally rather than swinging on each new headline. The
cultural shift is from "being right" as an identity to "becoming less
wrong" as a process.

**Limitations and boundaries.** Superforecasting applies to questions
with definable time horizons and verifiable outcomes -- typically,
short-to-medium-term geopolitical and economic events. It does not
apply to questions that are fundamentally unpredictable (complex
adaptive systems with sensitive dependence on initial conditions),
unique singularities with no reference class, or events so far in the
future that the causal chains cannot be traced. Superforecasters are
not oracles; they are disciplined probabilistic thinkers operating
within the limits of knowability. Recognizing those limits -- knowing
when a question is forecastable and when it is not -- is itself a
superforecaster trait.
