# Scenario Planning -- Why Imagining Multiple Futures Beats Predicting One

Scenario planning is a structured method for reasoning about multiple
plausible futures under conditions of irreducible uncertainty. Pioneered
by Pierre Wack at Royal Dutch/Shell in the early 1970s and codified by
Peter Schwartz in the 1990s, it rejects the single-forecast habit in
favor of constructing a small set of internally consistent narrative
worlds, then stress-testing strategy against each. Its purpose is not
to predict which future will arrive but to make decisions that are robust
across several of them. The discipline rests on a hard distinction:
scenarios describe what is possible; forecasts assert what is likely.

## Background

The intellectual lineage of scenario planning runs from military
wargaming through Cold War nuclear strategy to corporate strategic
planning. The term "scenario" itself was borrowed from the performing
arts -- a sketch of a scene -- and repurposed to denote a structured
narrative about how the future might unfold. The method's deepest roots
lie in the work of Herman Kahn at the RAND Corporation in the late
1940s and 1950s. Kahn used scenarios to investigate alternative
trajectories of nuclear conflict with the Soviet Union, a domain where
the outcome was genuinely unknown and where the cost of being
unprepared for any single branch of possibility was catastrophic. His
phrase "thinking the unthinkable" captured the method's core ambition:
to force serious consideration of futures that the mind resists
because they are uncomfortable or seem implausible against the
present trend. Kahn later founded the Hudson Institute in 1962 to
extend this work beyond defense into broader strategic and social
questions.

The transition from military and policy tool to corporate strategic
instrument is the work of Pierre Wack. Wack was a Shell economist who,
beginning in 1971, led the group that developed scenario planning as
a systematic practice inside Royal Dutch/Shell. The defining case is
Shell's preparation for the 1973 oil crisis. Shell's planners had
constructed scenarios that included a disruption of Middle Eastern oil
supply and a sharp price increase -- not because they predicted the
specific trigger, but because the scenario was internally consistent
and plausible given the political fragility of the region. When the
Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries enacted the embargo
in October 1973, Shell was better prepared than its major competitors.
Wack documented this work in two Harvard Business Review articles in
1985 -- "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead" and "Scenarios: Shooting
the Rapids" -- which remain foundational texts. Wack's central argument
was that scenarios succeed not by predicting the future but by changing
the mental models of the decision-makers who use them. The goal was
"the liberation of people's insights" -- reperception, not
prognostication.

Peter Schwartz, who had worked with Wack at Shell, extended and
popularized the practice. His 1991 book "The Art of the Long View"
became the standard introduction for a generation of corporate
strategists. Schwartz co-founded the Global Business Network (GBN) in
1987, which exported scenario planning to a wide range of corporate and
governmental clients. The two-by-two matrix of critical uncertainties
that most practitioners now treat as canonical is in large part
Schwartz's pedagogical simplification of what Wack and his colleagues
had done more fluidly inside Shell. Schwartz framed scenarios as
"stories about the way the world might turn out tomorrow" and
described their use as "rehearsing the future, running through
simulated events as if you're already living them."

The academic consolidation of the field came through the work of Paul
J. H. Schoemaker at the University of Chicago and the Wharton School.
Schoemaker's 1995 Sloan Management Review article "Scenario Planning:
A Tool for Strategic Thinking" systematized the method into
identifiable steps and connected it to the cognitive psychology of
judgment under uncertainty. His 1993 Strategic Management Journal paper
"Multiple Scenario Development: Its Conceptual and Behavioral Basis"
provided the empirical and theoretical foundation, identifying three
characteristics that set scenarios apart from traditional planning:
the narrative or script approach, uncertainty across rather than
within models, and the decomposition of a complex future into discrete
states. Schoemaker explicitly linked scenario planning to the
cognitive biases catalogued by Kahneman and Tversky, arguing that
scenarios exploit one set of biases (such as the conjunction fallacy,
which makes detailed narratives feel vivid) to counteract another
(such as overconfidence, which makes people assign too-narrow
confidence ranges to future change).

The field has since diversified into several schools. The intuitive
logics school, descended from Shell and GBN, relies on narrative
construction and managerial judgment. The probabilistic modified
trends school, with French roots in La Prospective, integrates
quantitative trend modification. The cross-impact analysis school uses
formal models of how uncertainties interact. Despite this
fragmentation, the core proposition is stable: under deep uncertainty,
constructing multiple plausible futures and testing strategy against
them produces better decisions than betting on a single forecast.

## Core Concepts

### The Distinction Between Scenarios and Forecasts

The foundational concept is the hard separation between a scenario and
a forecast. A forecast asserts a probability distribution over future
outcomes and commits to a central estimate. A scenario describes a
plausible future state without assigning it a probability, and the
method deliberately constructs several that are mutually exclusive. The
purpose is not to pick the "most likely" scenario -- doing so collapses
the exercise back into forecasting -- but to hold all of them
simultaneously and ask which decisions survive across them. Wack
insisted that scenarios are not predictions but tools for
reperception: they change how decision-makers see the present by
exposing the assumptions embedded in their default forecast. A
forecast asks "what will happen?"; a scenario asks "what could happen,
and are we ready for each branch?" The shift matters most where
genuine, unresolvable uncertainty exists. For predictable decisions,
sensitivity analysis around a single forecast is more efficient.
Scenario planning adds value precisely where the critical variables
remain unresolved and where a shift in one of them could invalidate
the entire trend.

### Predetermineds and Critical Uncertainties

Schwartz's framework divides the forces shaping the future into two
categories. Predetermineds are forces whose trajectory is largely
locked in regardless of which scenario unfolds -- demographics with
long lead times, technological maturation already underway,
infrastructural commitments already made. Critical uncertainties are
the forces whose direction is both highly impactful and genuinely
unknown: regulatory posture, political realignment, the pace of
adoption of an emerging technology, the timing of a geopolitical
shock. The method's analytical core is the identification and ranking
of these forces. A PESTLE scan (Political, Economic, Social,
Technological, Legal, Environmental) is the standard structured input,
supplemented by expert interviews and weak-signal monitoring. The
output is a ranked list of driving forces scored by two axes: impact
and uncertainty. The forces that score high on both become the axes of
the scenario matrix. This ranking step is where most of the analytical
judgment resides. If the two uncertainties chosen for the matrix turn
out to be correlated -- if "commercial market maturity" and
"regulatory activism" move together -- then the four quadrants
collapse into a diagonal and the exercise has learned nothing. The
discipline is in the orthogonality of the axes.

### The Two-by-Two Matrix

The canonical output of the intuitive logics school is a two-by-two
matrix formed by crossing the two most critical uncertainties. Each
quadrant is a distinct future world. The four worlds are not
probability-weighted; they are constructed to be plausible, internally
consistent, and mutually challenging. The strength of the matrix is
that it forces the consideration of futures that the organization
would otherwise dismiss. The weakness is that it can reduce genuine
complexity to a cartoon if the axes are poorly chosen. Schoemaker
emphasized that the scenarios must "bound the range of plausible
uncertainties" -- they should stretch imagination without crossing
into science fiction. Each scenario narrative must be traceable to
the identified driving forces, or it degenerates into creative
storytelling with no analytical grounding. The matrix is a tool, not
the deliverable; the deliverable is the strategic conversation it
generates and the robustness test it enables.

### Narrative Construction and Internal Consistency

A scenario is not a bullet list of variables; it is a narrative -- a
story about how the world gets from here to there. The narrative form
matters because of how the human mind processes information. Kahneman
and Tversky's work on the simulation heuristic showed that people
reason about future events by constructing mental simulations, and
that vivid, internally coherent narratives are more persuasive and
more memorable than abstract probability statements. Schoemaker
argued that scenario planning deliberately exploits this: by
constructing detailed narratives, it makes each future world feel
real enough that decision-makers can reason within it. The narratives
must be internally consistent -- the political, economic, and
technological elements of each scenario must fit together logically.
A scenario in which oil prices collapse while oil-producing nations
simultaneously escalate military spending, with no explanation of
the mechanism, fails the consistency test. The consistency check is
what separates disciplined scenario work from speculation. Each
narrative describes a pathway: the starting conditions, the driving
forces, the branching points, and the end state. The end state is
less important than the pathway, because the pathway reveals the
early indicators that would signal which scenario is unfolding.

### Wind-Tunneling and Robustness Testing

The scenarios are not the end product. The end product is the
strategic decision they inform. The link is a process called
wind-tunneling -- taking the organization's current strategy and
testing it inside each scenario to see where it breaks. The metaphor
is from aerospace: a model is placed in a wind tunnel and subjected
to varying conditions to find its failure points. In scenario
planning, the strategy is the model and the scenarios are the
conditions. The output of wind-tunneling is a vulnerability
assessment: which parts of the strategy are robust (they work well
in every scenario) and which are fragile (they work well in only
one or two). The strategic implication follows directly. A robust
strategy is one that creates value across multiple scenarios. A
fragile strategy that depends on a single scenario is a bet, not a
plan. The refinement is to separate the robust components -- keep
them -- from the fragile ones, and to approach the fragile ones from
an options perspective: retain flexibility to adjust if the scenario
that validates them unfolds, and to pull the plug if it does not.
Scenarios without wind-tunneling and early-warning indicators are,
in Schoemaker's phrase, an academic exercise with no strategic value.

### Early Warning Indicators

Because scenarios are not predictions, the organization needs a way
to detect which scenario is beginning to unfold. This is the function
of early warning indicators -- observable, leading signals that are
specified in advance and tied to each scenario's pathway. If a
scenario assumes a regulatory tightening, the indicator might be the
introduction of a draft bill in a relevant jurisdiction. If a scenario
assumes a technological disruption, the indicator might be the
adoption curve of the disruptive technology in an adjacent market.
The indicators are defined during scenario construction, not after
the fact, so that the organization can monitor them continuously and
act before the full scenario materializes. This connects scenario
planning to the broader discipline of environmental scanning and
weak-signal monitoring. The monitoring dashboard is what converts a
one-time scenario exercise into a continuous strategic learning
process. Without it, the scenarios become a static document that ages
and is forgotten.

### Time Horizon and the Plausibility Boundary

Two design choices govern every scenario exercise: how far into the
future the scenarios extend, and where the line between plausible and
impossible is drawn. The time horizon must be long enough to allow
for structural change -- a three-year horizon in a capital-intensive
industry captures little more than the momentum already built into
the system. Van der Elst, drawing on World Economic Forum practice,
argues that the horizon must be "sufficiently long to avoid only
conveying a creative description of the present, and sufficiently
short for the scenarios not to lose focus and relevance." A ten- to
twenty-year horizon is common in energy and infrastructure; a five-
year horizon may suffice in faster-moving technology markets. The
plausibility boundary is the harder judgment. Scenarios must stretch
imagination without crossing into fiction. Schoemaker specified that
scenarios should "bound the range of plausible uncertainties" --
every narrative must be traceable to identified driving forces, or
it becomes creative storytelling with no analytical grounding. The
test is not whether the scenario is likely but whether it is
plausible: could a reasonable person, given the driving forces,
construct a coherent argument for how the world reaches that state?
A scenario in which a single technology solves climate change in
five years fails the plausibility test not because it is optimistic
but because it ignores the institutional and physical constraints
that the driving forces analysis identified. The discipline of
holding the plausibility line is what keeps scenario planning from
degenerating into speculation, and it is the criterion by which a
well-built scenario set is distinguished from a list of guesses.

## Evidence

### The Shell Oil Crisis Case (1971-1974)

The founding empirical case for scenario planning is Royal Dutch/Shell's
preparation for the 1973 oil crisis. Beginning in 1971, Wack's group
constructed scenarios that included a future in which Middle Eastern
oil-producing nations acted collectively to restrict supply and raise
prices. The scenario was not a prediction -- the specific trigger (the
Yom Kippur War and the subsequent Arab oil embargo) was not foreseen.
But the scenario was plausible and internally consistent: the
political conditions for collective action existed, the producing
nations had the capacity to restrict supply, and the consuming nations
had no short-term substitutes. When the embargo hit in October 1973,
Shell had already thought through the implications and had begun
adjusting its refining capacity, investment, and inventory strategy.
The documented result, reported by Wack in his 1985 Harvard Business
Review articles, is that Shell improved its competitive position
during and after the crisis relative to its major oil company peers
who had not done comparable scenario work. Wack's account
emphasizes that the value was not in predicting the embargo but in
having already reperceived the world such that the embargo, when it
came, was recognized and acted upon rather than treated as a shock to
be weathered. This case is the most cited single piece of evidence for
the method's practical value, though it is a single firm's experience
and is subject to the survivorship and hindsight biases that Wack
himself warned against.

### Schoemaker's Experiments on Confidence Calibration (1993)

Schoemaker's 1993 Strategic Management Journal paper provides the
strongest experimental evidence for the cognitive mechanism behind
scenario planning. He conducted experiments testing the impact of
scenario construction on people's subjective confidence ranges for
future outcomes. The design exploited a known tension in the
psychology of judgment: generating detailed narratives for an outcome
can make it feel more probable (the conjunction fallacy and simulation
heuristic), while generating detailed narratives against an outcome
can make it feel less probable. Schoemaker's finding was that asking
subjects to construct scenarios for both favorable and unfavorable
outcomes stretches their confidence ranges -- they become less
overconfident about a single future. However, the effect is not
uniform. If subjects are asked to generate reasons only for extreme
outcomes, their confidence ranges can actually shrink rather than
stretch, because the generated reasons seem incredible. The
implication is that scenario planning works as a debiasing tool
specifically when it constructs scenarios that span the plausible
range without forcing the extremes. This is an empirical
justification for the method's emphasis on plausibility and the
rejection of science-fiction scenarios. The experiments also
tested the internal coherence of subjects' beliefs across scenarios,
finding that scenario construction exposes inconsistencies in
people's implicit mental models that they had not previously
recognized.

### Forecasting Accuracy and the Limits of Extrapolation

A body of evidence from the forecasting literature supports the
premise that motivates scenario planning: that single-point forecasts
under conditions of structural change are frequently wrong in
predictable ways. Hogarth and Makridakis (1981), in an evaluation of
forecasting and planning in Management Science, documented that
extrapolative forecasting methods perform poorly when the environment
undergoes structural shifts, precisely the conditions under which
scenario planning is recommended. The finding is not that forecasting
is useless -- in stable environments, extrapolative forecasts are
often the best available tool -- but that the confidence placed in
them under uncertainty is systematically excessive. Schoemaker
connected this to three cognitive mechanisms: anchoring on the past
or present leads to underadjustment away from the status quo;
availability bias makes it hard to properly weigh novel scenarios;
and overconfidence produces unduly narrow confidence ranges. The
evidence from calibration research (Koriat, Lichtenstein, and
Fischhoff, 1980) showed that generating reasons improves probability
calibration. Scenario planning can be understood as a structured
form of reason generation applied to the future. The evidence base
is consistent with, though it does not uniquely prove, the claim that
scenario construction improves the quality of judgment under
uncertainty. What the evidence does not support is the stronger claim
that scenario planning improves the accuracy of point predictions
-- that is not its purpose, and the literature is careful to
distinguish the two.

### Integration with Real Options and Risk Management

A more recent strand of evidence addresses the integration of
scenario planning with real options analysis, a finance-derived
framework for valuing investments under uncertainty. The argument,
developed in work published in the Journal of Financial and
Quantitative Analysis and related outlets, is that the two methods
have complementary strengths: scenario planning is strong at
qualitative identification of uncertainties and their strategic
implications, while real options analysis is strong at quantitative
valuation of flexibility. The integrated framework involves four
steps: scenario development, exposure identification, risk
management response formulation, and implementation. The evidence
here is primarily conceptual and case-based rather than
experimental, but it illustrates the practical convergence between
the qualitative foresight tradition and the quantitative finance
tradition. The convergence matters because it provides a bridge from
scenario narratives to investment decisions -- a bridge that the
pure qualitative tradition has historically struggled to build
convincingly.

### Methodological Critique and the Limits of the Evidence

The evidence base for scenario planning has recognized limits that
the honest practitioner must acknowledge. The Shell case is a single
firm's experience, documented by the practitioner who led the work;
it is subject to survivorship bias (Shell succeeded and told the
story; firms that tried scenarios and failed are not in the
record) and to hindsight bias (the scenario that proved relevant
looks, in retrospect, more prescient than it was at the time of
construction). Wack himself was explicit about this, warning that
scenarios are not validated by whether any single one came true but
by whether the organization that used them made better decisions.
The experimental evidence from Schoemaker establishes a cognitive
mechanism -- confidence calibration -- but it is laboratory evidence,
not field evidence of improved strategic outcomes. The broader
literature on scenario use, surveyed by Burt and van der Heijden,
finds consistent reports of improved strategic conversation and
early identification of threats, but little rigorous evidence that
scenario-using firms outperform non-using firms on financial
measures. A persistent critique, raised in the Technological
Forecasting and Social Change literature, is that the conventional
intuitive-logics method struggles with paradoxical trends --
developments that are systematically excluded as logically
impossible during the scenario-building process but that
nonetheless occur. The implication is not that scenario planning
fails, but that its evidence base is weaker than its adoption rate
would suggest. The strongest defensible claim, consistent with the
evidence, is that scenario planning improves the quality of
strategic thinking and the robustness of decisions against
underconsidered possibilities -- not that it improves the accuracy
of prediction or, by itself, financial performance.

## Implications

### For Strategic Decision-Makers

The primary implication for executives and strategists is a shift in
the objective of planning. The traditional objective is to predict the
future accurately and optimize the strategy for that prediction. The
scenario planning objective is to construct a strategy that is robust
across a range of plausible futures, accepting that the specific
future cannot be reliably predicted. This shifts the planning
question from "what will happen?" to "what could happen, and which
choices survive across the branches?" The practical consequence is
that organizations that practice scenario planning invest less in
precision they cannot achieve and more in resilience and optionality
they can. Schoemaker described three strategic postures managers
adopt under uncertainty: the zero-future posture (defer all major
decisions until the fog lifts), the single-bet posture (commit
strongly to one forecasted future), and the scenarios posture
(explore many futures and design for robustness). The scenarios
posture is the most demanding -- it requires the courage to admit
collective ignorance, which conflicts with a leadership culture that
prizes omniscience -- but it is the only one that does not rely on
the assumption that the future is predictable. For organizations
operating in genuinely turbulent environments (energy, technology,
geopolitics, regulated industries), the evidence suggests the
scenarios posture produces fewer catastrophic strategic failures.

### For Capital Allocation and Investment

Scenario planning has direct implications for how capital is
allocated under uncertainty. The wind-tunneling process separates
strategy components into robust and fragile. The robust components
can be funded with confidence. The fragile components -- those that
depend on a specific scenario to pay off -- should not be funded as
if they were certain. The real options integration provides the
valuation framework: a fragile investment is an option, and its value
depends on the ability to exercise or abandon it as the scenario
becomes clear. The implication is that scenario-aware capital
allocation favors investments that retain flexibility over those
that commit irrevocably to a single future. This is consistent with
the broader principle that under uncertainty, the value of
optionality rises. The connection to expected value thinking is
direct: scenarios provide the possible outcomes, and the
decision-maker assesses the expected value of each strategy across
the scenario set, weighting by judgment (not formal probability,
which is often unavailable) and by the reversibility of each
commitment. A strategy that has positive expected value across all
scenarios is robust; one that has positive expected value in only
one is a gamble on that scenario.

### For Organizational Learning and Culture

Scenario planning is as much an organizational intervention as an
analytical method. Wack reported that at Shell, only half the time in
a scenario project was spent building the scenarios; the other half
was spent traveling and presenting the scenarios to managers across
the business to embed the reperception. The implication is that
scenarios fail when they remain the property of a planning group.
They succeed when they change the conversation throughout the
organization. This requires a culture that tolerates ambiguity and
dissent -- a culture in which it is acceptable to say "I do not know
which future will arrive" without it being read as weakness. The
organizational learning literature, drawing on Shell's experience,
identifies scenario planning as a tool for what it calls
"decentered" planning: planning that does not converge prematurely
on a single answer but holds multiple possibilities open. The
implication for management is that the scenario exercise itself --
the workshop, the debate, the confrontation of assumptions -- may
deliver more value than the scenarios it produces, because it
surfaces and challenges the implicit forecasts that the organization
is already running. This connects scenario planning to pre-mortem
analysis: both are structured methods for forcing the consideration
of unwelcome futures before they arrive.

### For Forecasting and Prediction Practice

Scenario planning's relationship to forecasting is complementary, not
antagonistic. The method does not claim that forecasting is useless;
it claims that forecasting is most useful when its limits are
understood. In domains where the future is a continuation of
identifiable trends, forecasting and sensitivity analysis are the
right tools. Scenario planning is recommended for the cases where the
assumption of trend continuation is known to be fragile -- where a
shift in an alliance, a regulation, or a technology could invalidate
the trend entirely. The implication for forecasters and analysts is
that they should classify their decision context by uncertainty
type: where uncertainty is resolvable (risk, in the Knightian sense),
forecast; where it is irreducible (uncertainty, in the Knightian
sense), build scenarios. The inside-outside view distinction is
relevant here. The outside view -- reference class forecasting,
base rates, statistical aggregation -- is the forecasting tool. The
inside view -- detailed causal reasoning about the specific case --
is what scenario narratives provide. The evidence from
superforecasting research (Tetlock) is that the outside view improves
calibration; the evidence from scenario planning is that the inside
view, properly disciplined, improves the identification of
underconsidered possibilities. The two are not in conflict; they
address different failure modes of judgment. The mature practitioner
uses both.

### For Policy and Long-Horizon Planning

Scenario planning has migrated from corporate strategy into public
policy, infrastructure planning, and climate adaptation. Governments
and international bodies use it to reason about futures that unfold
over decades -- energy transitions, demographic shifts, climate
impacts -- where the uncertainty is structural and the decisions are
irreversible. The implication for policy is that long-horizon planning
should not seek to optimize for a single projected future but to build
adaptive capacity across a set of them. This is a different posture
from the cost-benefit optimization that dominates short-horizon
policy analysis. The scenario approach is more honest about the
limits of prediction in complex systems, and it produces plans that
are less likely to fail catastrophically when the future diverges
from the projection. The cost is that scenario-based planning is
harder to communicate to stakeholders who want a single answer, and
it is harder to evaluate, because success is defined as robustness
rather than accuracy. The implication for the design of planning
institutions is that they should be evaluated on the quality of
their strategic conversation and the robustness of their decisions,
not on the accuracy of their point forecasts.
