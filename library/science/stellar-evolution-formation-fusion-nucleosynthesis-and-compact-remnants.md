# Stars and Stellar Evolution -- Mass, Composition, and Companions Decide How a Star Makes Elements and What It Leaves Behind

A star is a self-gravitating ball of gas whose long life is a negotiation between gravity, which compresses it, and the pressure maintained by nuclear reactions in its core. Its initial mass, its composition, and whether it has a binary companion decide how long it shines, which elements it forges, and whether it ends as a white dwarf, a neutron star, or a black hole. The path from molecular-cloud collapse to a compact remnant is understood in outline from theory and large observational samples, but the explosion mechanism, mass loss, binary interaction, and the sites of several element-producing processes still carry unresolved uncertainties [1][2][6][9].

## Background

The question of what keeps a star shining set the agenda of stellar astrophysics. Bethe's 1967 Nobel lecture recounts the first scientific answer, given by Helmholtz about a century earlier, which relied on gravity: matter falling onto the Sun releases potential energy, and about half of the energy released as the Sun was assembled is radiated away. At the present solar luminosity that reservoir lasts about 30 million years, long enough for nineteenth-century physicists but not for biologists, who argued after Darwin that evolution required more time [1]. Radioactive dating of the Earth and of meteorites later placed the age of the Sun at about 5 billion years, within about 10 percent, so gravitation alone could not be the source [1]. Eddington's favoured alternative, complete annihilation of matter, would have supplied the Sun for 1500 billion years, but annihilation had never been observed, and from the early 1930s it was generally assumed that stellar energy is nuclear [1].

The nuclear explanation developed in steps. Atkinson and Houtermans concluded in 1929 that, at the temperatures inside a star, nuclei can penetrate one another's Coulomb barrier and react. Particle accelerators began to observe such reactions in 1933, and they obeyed the barrier-penetration theory of Gamow, Condon, and Gurney. In 1938 Gamow and Teller revised the theory of thermonuclear reaction rates [1]. Bethe's own contribution of 1938 was the carbon-nitrogen cycle, in which carbon acts only as a catalyst while four protons and two electrons become one helium-4 nucleus. About 2 MeV of each cycle's energy is carried away by two neutrinos and about 25 MeV is released usefully; von Weizsacker found the cycle independently [1]. The proton-proton reaction, first proposed by von Weizsacker and calculated by Critchfield and Bethe, is so slow that it can never be observed in a laboratory, yet it fits the observed energy production of the Sun because stars offer almost unlimited time and a large supply of energetic protons [1].

A second line of work asked how the final states of stars can be stable at all. Chandrasekhar's 1983 Nobel lecture describes Eddington's paradox: a white dwarf such as the companion of Sirius has a mean density of 10^5 to 10^7 g/cm3, and it seemed that such a star would need energy in order to cool. R. H. Fowler resolved the paradox in 1926 by applying Fermi statistics, introducing electron degeneracy into stellar structure for the first time [2]. Chandrasekhar's own step was to show that the relativistic limit of a degenerate star fixes a maximum mass, so that stars above it cannot end as white dwarfs unless they eject mass [2].

The same period assigned the production of the elements to stars. Bethe's lecture calls the later development of a massive star more speculative but considers the theory of Hoyle and collaborators likely to be correct: the centre of the star heats until newly formed carbon nuclei react with each other, nuclei such as magnesium-24 and silicon-28 form, and the chain of stable nuclei is built up to iron; ejected in supernova explosions, this material enriches the interstellar gas, so that most stars including the Sun are "at least second generation stars" [1]. Bethe also reports Fowler's calculation that temperatures and densities in the Big Bang did not allow the production of elements beginning with carbon [1]. Modern galactic chemical evolution models retain this division: elements with mass number 12 and above are made in stars [11].

The origin of stars themselves was a separate question. Larson's review traces the idea that stars form by gravitational condensation of diffuse matter to Newton in 1692, but notes that convincing evidence that stars are forming now by the condensation of interstellar gas became available only in the half century before 2003, and physical understanding only in recent decades [3]. Jeans's analysis identified a critical length above which density perturbations grow under gravity. The similarity solution found by Penston and by Larson in 1969 describes the runaway growth of a central density peak in a collapsing isothermal sphere, and Shu's 1977 similarity solution describes accretion from a singular isothermal sphere onto a central point mass [3].

The empirical frame for all of this is the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram (HRD), which relates stellar spectral type, temperature, or colour to luminosity or absolute magnitude [4]. Bethe describes the main sequence running from hot, luminous stars to cool, faint ones, red giants at the upper right, and white dwarfs at the lower left, and observes that after assembly by gravity stars spend most of their lives on the main sequence, then become red giants and probably white dwarfs [1]. Gaia Data Release 2 in 2018 turned the diagram from a schematic into a precision measurement for a very large sample, resolving the thin main sequence, the red clump, and a split of the white-dwarf sequence into hydrogen and helium atmospheres [4].

Several recent results show how the field now draws on independent messengers: the detection of neutrinos from the CNO cycle in the Sun in 2020 [5], the interpretation of the kilonova that followed a neutron-star merger observed in gravitational waves in 2017 [12], three-dimensional neutrino-driven supernova simulations [9], and the discovery of a 33 solar-mass dormant black hole in Gaia astrometry in 2024 [14]. This topic covers the physics that links these results. The cosmological side of nucleosynthesis, including Big Bang abundances, is treated in `library/science/cosmology-big-bang-dark-energy.md`.

## Core Concepts

### Hydrostatic equilibrium and energy transport

Stellar structure calculations integrate two fundamental equations, hydrostatic equilibrium and radiative energy transport, together with an equation of state; Bethe names Eddington, Chandrasekhar, and Stromgren as pioneers of this work [1]. Hydrostatic equilibrium requires that at each radius the pressure gradient balance the weight of the overlying matter, so pressure rises toward the centre [2]. In ordinary stars the pressure is the sum of gas pressure and radiation pressure, a decomposition due to Eddington [2]. Composition enters through the mass fractions of hydrogen (X), helium (Y), and all heavier elements (Z). Bethe quotes Z between 0.02 and 0.04 for all but the oldest stars and X of about 0.65 for the Sun as of 1967 [1].

The virial theorem provides a quick estimate of interior conditions. For a self-gravitating gas the average thermal energy per unit mass is one half of the average potential energy, so when a star contracts, half of the released energy heats it and half is radiated [1]. This is an interpretation of the cited relation, not a statement from the source: a star that radiates energy therefore contracts and grows hotter instead of cooling, which is what allows a contracting core to reach nuclear ignition temperatures. A more careful integration of the equilibrium equations by Demarque and Percy gives a central temperature of the Sun of 15.7 million kelvin [1].

The equations imply that luminosity depends steeply on mass. For a factor of 10 in mass, Bethe reports that luminosity increases by a factor of about 3000, so that the energy production per gram is about 300 times larger [1]. Massive stars therefore exhaust their fuel quickly: Larson notes that the most luminous stars have lifetimes shorter than 10 million years [3]. A star's position in the HRD is set mainly by its initial mass, chemical composition, and age, with rotation, winds, magnetic fields, convective overshooting, and departures from local thermal equilibrium playing further roles [4].

### From molecular cloud to protostar

Stars form in giant molecular clouds, which have sizes up to about 100 parsecs and masses up to about a million solar masses, and which contain clumps and dense cores of about 0.1 parsec and about one solar mass; the clouds are cold, with typical temperatures of 10 to 20 kelvin [3]. They turn only a few percent of their mass into stars before dispersing, and the ages of the stars in a cloud span no more than about 10 million years, comparable to a crossing time, which led Larson to describe clouds as transient structures and star formation as a fast process [3].

Thermal pressure sets a minimum mass for collapse, the Jeans mass. In the densest regions the Jeans mass is of the order of one solar mass, in rough agreement with the observed masses of prestellar cores [3]. Once collapse starts, thermal emission from dust keeps the gas near 10 kelvin while the density rises by many orders of magnitude, and the collapse is strongly non-uniform, with a runaway central density peak. Rotation and magnetic fields do not prevent the formation of this peak [3]. When the centre becomes opaque to its own dust emission at a density near 10^-13 g/cm3, a first hydrostatic core of about 0.01 solar masses forms. A second collapse follows when the temperature exceeds about 2000 kelvin and molecular hydrogen dissociates, and it ends with a stellar core of only about 0.001 solar masses and a radius near one solar radius [3].

The protostar therefore begins as an embryo and acquires more than 99 percent of the final mass of a one-solar-mass star by accretion from the surrounding envelope. In the standard isothermal model the accretion rate at 10 kelvin is 1.53 x 10^-6 solar masses per year, which builds a one-solar-mass star in 650,000 years; more realistic collapse calculations give a higher initial rate that declines as the envelope is depleted, with half of the envelope accreted in about 100,000 years and nearly all within a million years [3]. Deuterium burning begins at about 0.2 solar masses and the protostar radius settles near 4 solar radii [3]. Observers classify the stages as Class 0 through Class III, from a phase of rapid accretion lasting a few times 10^4 years to a weak-line T Tauri star without significant circumstellar material [3]. The young star then contracts for a few tens of millions of years until its centre is hot enough to burn hydrogen and it settles on the main sequence [3].

Two complications matter for later evolution. The angular momentum in a typical prestellar core is about three orders of magnitude larger than a single star can hold, and Larson argues that the more plausible disposal route is orbital motion in binary or multiple systems, since observations show that about two-thirds of all stars are in binaries and no completely satisfactory steady mechanism for transporting angular momentum out of a disk is known [3]. Massive stars add a second problem: in spherical infall, radiation pressure on the envelope can exceed gravity when the protostellar mass reaches about 10 solar masses, so formation of the most massive stars, which occurs in dense cluster centres, remains poorly understood and may involve disk accretion or mergers [3].

### Hydrogen fusion on the main sequence

Nuclei carry positive charge and must penetrate each other's Coulomb barrier, so the reaction rate depends steeply on temperature, and reactions between the lightest, least-charged nuclei occur most easily [1]. Two channels convert hydrogen to helium. The proton-proton chain begins with the very slow reaction of two protons to form deuterium, a positron, and a neutrino, and it is followed by the fusion of helium-3 nuclei or the capture of helium-4 on helium-3, with the branch depending on temperature [1]. The CNO cycle uses carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts and has a much stronger temperature dependence because it involves more highly charged nuclei [1]. In Bethe's Figure 4, which assumes a hydrogen mass fraction of 0.5 and a heavy-element fraction of 0.02, the proton-proton reaction dominates at low temperature, the carbon-nitrogen cycle at high temperature, and the crossing point is at 13 million kelvin, where the energy production is 7 erg per gram per second; the average over the whole Sun is smaller and compatible with 2 erg per gram per second [1].

The Borexino collaboration's 2020 paper states the present division of labour: the proton-proton chain produces about 99 percent of the energy of the Sun and the CNO cycle of the order of 1 percent, with a large uncertainty tied to poorly known solar metallicity [5]. For stars like the Sun but heavier than about 1.3 solar masses the CNO cycle dominates, whereas the proton-proton chain prevails in lighter, cooler stars; the authors state that the CNO cycle is believed to be the primary route for converting hydrogen to helium in the Universe [5].

The main sequence is the long phase in which stars spend most of their lives [1], with core fusion supplying the energy that is radiated. Gaia observations show that the main sequence of nearby stars is extremely thin, so thin that its remaining width is largely explained by parallax uncertainty, and that unresolved binary stars lie up to 0.75 magnitude above it, with two identical stars giving a shift of 0.753 magnitude [4]. Interpretation: this thinness is consistent with a main-sequence position controlled by a small number of parameters, chiefly mass, composition, and age, the same parameters that [4] gives as the principal determinants of a star's position in the HRD.

### Leaving the main sequence

When hydrogen is depleted at the centre, not enough energy is produced there to sustain the pressure of the outer layers, so gravity makes the centre collapse and reach higher temperature and density. The temperature also rises farther out where hydrogen remains, and a shell of hydrogen burning away from the centre produces most of the energy; the shell gradually moves outward and becomes thinner, while the region outside it expands and the star becomes a red giant. Bethe states that this result follows clearly from the many numerical computations but that the physical reason is not clear. He presents Iben's calculation for a star of 3 solar masses, in which, during the period of shell-source development, the central temperature rises from about 25 million to about 100 million kelvin and the radius increases from about 2 to about 30 solar radii [1]. The outer convective envelope can contain as much as 80 percent of the mass and mixes material to the surface, an effect Bethe illustrates with the lithium contents of the two red giants of the binary Capella [1].

At a central temperature of about 100 million kelvin the helium at the centre begins to react. The triple-alpha reaction, in which three helium-4 nuclei form carbon-12 and which requires about 110 million kelvin, is possible only because two resonances raise its rate: two alpha particles have nearly the energy of the unstable beryllium-8 nucleus, and beryllium-8 plus helium-4 has nearly the energy of an excited state of carbon-12. The first resonance was suggested by Salpeter and the second by Hoyle [1]. Bethe notes that early investigators had trouble bridging the gap between helium-4 and carbon-12 because nuclei of mass 5 and mass 8 are unstable [1]. The product carbon can capture a further alpha particle to make oxygen-16, and helium burns later in a shell, repeating the pattern seen for hydrogen [1].

These stages leave identifiable marks in the HRD. In Gaia data, the red clump consists of low-mass stars that burn helium in their cores, and its colour depends on metallicity and age. In metal-poor globular clusters the equivalent stars form a horizontal branch. The red-giant-branch bump marks a brief interruption of the luminosity rise of a star burning a hydrogen shell, and the asymptotic-giant-branch bump marks the start of the phase in which stars burn a helium shell [4]. Hot subdwarfs are considered to be red giants that lost their outer hydrogen before the core began to fuse helium, possibly through interaction with a low-mass companion [4].

### Degeneracy and the Chandrasekhar limit

Electron degeneracy supplies the pressure that supports white dwarfs without any thermal energy source. A nonrelativistic degenerate star behaves as a polytrope of index 3/2, with a radius that is inversely proportional to the cube root of its mass; for one solar mass and two nucleons per electron, the mean density comes out near 7 x 10^5 g/cm3, of the order of observed white dwarfs [2]. At the centre of a one-solar-mass white dwarf the density is about 4.2 x 10^6 g/cm3 and electrons at the Fermi threshold move at about 0.63 times the speed of light, so special relativity is needed; in the relativistic limit the structure becomes a polytrope of index 3, which has a uniquely determined maximum mass [2].

Chandrasekhar draws two conclusions. First, there is an upper limit to the mass of stars that can end as degenerate configurations. Second, stars above it have end states that this argument cannot predict [2]. His lecture adds a condition on radiation pressure: degeneracy is possible only if radiation pressure is below 9.2 percent of the total, and because radiation pressure grows with mass, stars above about 7 to 8 solar masses on the standard model cannot develop degeneracy at any stage and cannot become white dwarfs unless they eject a large fraction of their mass [2]. The limiting mass is stated as about 1.4 solar masses in Chandrasekhar's lecture [2], and about 1.5 solar masses in Burrows and Vartanyan's review [9]; this entry does not resolve the difference between the two quoted values.

### Fates by initial mass

The fate of a star depends chiefly on its mass and composition at birth and on its history of mass loss. For single stars mass loss occurs through winds that depend mostly on metallicity [6]. Heger and colleagues assumed that the core structure and remnant are determined mainly by the helium-core mass at death. A larger helium core means higher entropy, a larger iron core, and a shallower density gradient, which makes explosion harder [6]. The thresholds below are model estimates for non-rotating single stars from [6], and the authors state that no two groups presently agree, in detail, on the final evolution of any massive star, including explosion energy, remnant mass, and rotation rate [6].

| Initial mass (solar masses) | Core outcome | Typical remnant | Source |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| below about 9 (estimates range 6 to 11) | envelope shed, degenerate core never reaches iron | white dwarf | [6][9] |
| about 9 to 10 | degenerate oxygen-neon core; electron-capture collapse or envelope loss | neutron star or white dwarf | [6][11] |
| above about 10 | iron-core collapse | neutron star, or black hole by fallback or direct collapse | [6] |
| about 100 to 140 (metal-poor, little mass loss) | pulsational pair instability | violent pulsations, not complete disruption | [6] |
| about 140 to 260 (metal-poor, little mass loss) | pair-instability explosion | no remnant | [6] |
| above about 260 (metal-poor, little mass loss) | direct collapse | black hole | [6] |

Fallback black holes are estimated to form for helium cores of about 8 solar masses, corresponding to about 25 solar masses on the main sequence, and direct collapse to black holes for helium cores above 15 solar masses, corresponding to about 40 solar masses without mass loss; these are uncertain estimates from the literature cited by [6]. Burrows and Vartanyan describe a consensus that most stars with initial masses below about 20 solar masses leave neutron stars and most above about 30 solar masses leave black holes, while noting that their own simulations did not explode 12 and 15 solar-mass models and that this result may be model-dependent [9].

### How massive stars explode

When a massive star forms a core near the Chandrasekhar mass after about 10 to 40 million years, the core implodes in less than one second to neutron-star density and rebounds, launching a shock wave that in all credible models stalls at a radius of about 100 to 200 kilometres, sapped by a burst of electron neutrinos and by the dissociation of infalling nuclei [9]. The delayed neutrino-heating mechanism, in which neutrinos from the proto-neutron star deposit energy behind the shock, with the aid of turbulent convection and the breaking of spherical symmetry, is emerging as a robust solution, but the authors state that complexity and chaos in the dynamics prevent unambiguous victory [9]. A proto-neutron star radiates about 3 x 10^53 erg in neutrinos while a typical supernova has about 10^51 erg of kinetic energy, which might suggest a mechanism tolerating less than 1 percent error; the authors state that this is not true, because the efficiency of energy deposition in the gain region is about 4 to 10 percent [9]. Spherical simulations seldom explode. In the authors' suite of two-dimensional simulations of progenitors from 9 to 26.99 solar masses, all but the 12 and 15 solar-mass models exploded, with energies between 0.09 and 2.3 times 10^51 erg and the shock relaunched between about 150 and 400 milliseconds after bounce [9].

The same explosion looks different depending on the star's envelope at the time of explosion. Type II-P supernovae retain a massive hydrogen envelope, Type II-L and IIb retain less than about 2 solar masses of hydrogen, and Type Ib and Ic have lost it [6][9]. When the proto-neutron star exceeds the maximum neutron-star mass, it collapses to a black hole, but a collapsing Chandrasekhar core can never go directly to a black hole: a proto-neutron star always forms first, and neutrino losses of 0.1 to 0.4 solar masses times c squared lead the authors to expect some form of explosion even when a black hole forms [9].

### Thermonuclear supernovae

A different class of explosion involves white dwarfs. In a Type Ia supernova a carbon-oxygen white dwarf undergoes a runaway thermonuclear explosion that converts matter to iron-group elements and releases about 10^51 erg of kinetic energy; the light curve is powered by the decay of nickel-56 to cobalt-56 (half-life 6.1 days) and then to iron-56 (half-life 77.3 days), with 0.3 to 0.8 solar masses of iron-56 ultimately produced in each event and a peak luminosity of order 10^43 erg/s [10]. The progenitor system remains unresolved: the companion may be another white dwarf (double-degenerate) or a non-degenerate star (single-degenerate), and direct searches for companions to normal Type Ia events have found none [10]. This is in contrast to core-collapse supernovae, for which neutrino emission and remnant pulsars give clear evidence of massive-star progenitors [10].

### Binary stars alter the script

A single-star description is incomplete for massive stars. Sana and colleagues' survey of O-type stars found an intrinsic binary fraction of 0.69 and concluded that 71 percent of stars born as O-type interact with a companion, more than half of them before leaving the main sequence [7]. Roughly 20 to 30 percent merge with their companion and 40 to 50 percent either lose their envelope or accrete substantial mass, so "only a minority of massive stars evolve undisturbed towards their supernova explosion" [7]. The 33 percent of O stars predicted to be stripped before exploding provide a natural channel for hydrogen-deficient Types Ib, Ic, and IIb [7].

## Nucleosynthesis and Galactic Chemical Evolution

Stellar evolution matters beyond the stars themselves because each evolutionary channel returns processed material to the interstellar medium on its own timescale. Kobayashi, Karakas, and Lugaro built galactic chemical evolution (GCE) models for all stable elements from carbon to uranium, combining theoretical nucleosynthesis yields with the event rates of every enrichment source: asymptotic-giant-branch (AGB) stars, core-collapse supernovae including hypernovae, electron-capture supernovae, Type Ia supernovae, neutron-star mergers, and magneto-rotational supernovae [11]. Their summary assigns hydrogen and most helium to the Big Bang and everything with mass number 12 or above to stars; the lithium abundance in their model is very uncertain, and beryllium and boron are supposed to be produced by cosmic rays, which the model does not include [11].

### Light elements and the s-process: AGB stars

Stars with initial masses of roughly 0.9 to 8 solar masses pass through a thermally pulsing AGB phase in which the third dredge-up brings carbon-12 and slow-neutron-capture (s-process) products to the surface; stars above about 4 solar masses also undergo hot bottom burning [11]. The neutron source for the main s-process component is mainly the reaction of carbon-13 with an alpha particle in a carbon-13 pocket, and the authors report that there is still not full agreement on which processes drive the mixing that forms the pocket [11]. In their model at an age of 9.2 billion years, AGB stars supply 49 percent of carbon, 51 percent of fluorine, and 74 percent of nitrogen, and they are the main source of the s-process elements at the barium and lead peaks [11]. These fractions are model outputs; the authors note that Wolf-Rayet stars, which are not included, may matter for fluorine [11]. Stars of this mass range end as white dwarfs, consistent with the white-dwarf threshold near 9 solar masses in [6].

### Alpha elements and the delay of iron

The alpha elements (oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon, sulfur, argon, and calcium) are mainly produced in massive stars and released by core-collapse supernovae, but Type Ia supernovae contribute 22 percent of silicon, 29 percent of sulfur, 34 percent of argon, and 39 percent of calcium in the model [11]. About half of the iron-peak elements (chromium, manganese, iron, nickel, cobalt, copper, and zinc) come from Type Ia supernovae, the explosions of carbon-oxygen white dwarfs in binaries; in classical work most iron was attributed to them, but in this model the fraction is only 60 percent for iron, with the rest mainly from hypernovae [11]. The authors explain the decline of the alpha-to-iron ratio with metallicity by the delayed enrichment of iron from Type Ia supernovae, which have a longer timescale than core-collapse supernovae, and they note that the [alpha/Fe] ratios can therefore be used to constrain the sources [11]. Observationally, the cosmic Type Ia rate is consistent with a power-law delay-time distribution proportional to 1/t [10].

### Heavy elements and the r-process

Neutron-rich nucleosynthesis beyond iron is divided between the s-process in AGB stars and the rapid neutron-capture (r-process). Kobayashi and colleagues cite detailed simulations in which electron-capture supernovae and neutrino-driven winds cannot make elements heavier than mass number about 110, and they find that neutron-star mergers can produce r-process elements up to thorium and uranium but on timescales too long to explain observations at low metallicity; the observed europium trend can be explained if about 3 percent of 25 to 50 solar-mass hypernovae are magneto-rotational supernovae that make r-process elements [11]. The kilonova AT 2017gfo that followed the gravitational-wave event GW170817 provided an observational test of the neutron-star-merger channel, as described in the Evidence section [12]. In supernova simulations, the inner ejecta begin very neutron-rich with an electron fraction of 0.1 to 0.2, but the bulk reaches an electron fraction near 0.5, and the authors of [9] conclude that none of the ejecta in modern simulations can be the site of all of the r-process, though the first r-process peak is not excluded [9].

### Uncertainty in the chemical accounting

The GCE predictions depend on the input yields, so uncertainties are hard to quantify. Roughly, alpha-element abundances vary by about 0.2 dex with the assumed mass loss, convection, rotation, and magnetic fields; carbon, nitrogen, and odd-Z element abundances depend on rotation at the level of about 0.5 dex; and the model overproduces silver by a factor of 6, underproduces gold by a factor of 5, and overproduces uranium [11]. The authors suggest that these problems may require revising nuclear reaction rates [11].

## Compact Remnants

Each evolutionary branch ends in one of three kinds of object, and recent measurements have tightened the observational picture of each.

### White dwarfs

Burrows and Vartanyan describe white dwarfs as about half as massive as the Sun and about a hundred times smaller [9]. In Gaia Data Release 2, 26,264 white dwarfs with parallax errors below 5 percent form a sequence concentrated along the 0.6 solar-mass hydrogen cooling track, consistent with a strong peak of the white-dwarf mass distribution near 0.6 solar masses; the sequence splits into a hydrogen-atmosphere and a helium-atmosphere branch, which is the first time that split appears in an HRD [4]. The planetary-nebula phase that precedes the white-dwarf stage is short, about 10,000 years, so central stars of planetary nebulae are rare in the diagram [4].

### Neutron stars

Burrows and Vartanyan describe neutron stars as the size of a city with average masses about 50 percent larger than the Sun; radio pulsars move with speeds averaging about 350 km/s, which the authors call most naturally explained by recoil during the supernova from asymmetric ejection of matter or asymmetric neutrino emission [9]. Chandrasekhar noted that measured masses of pulsars from supernovae were consistently close to 1.4 solar masses [2]. The upper end of the mass range is constrained by the pulsar PSR J0740+6620, a 2.8-millisecond pulsar with a white-dwarf companion whose Shapiro delay gives a mass of 2.08 (+0.07, -0.07) solar masses and a 95.4 percent lower bound of 1.95 solar masses [13]. Burrows and Vartanyan give a maximum neutron-star gravitational mass between 2.1 and 2.4 solar masses, depending on the poorly constrained nuclear equation of state [9].

### Black holes

Chandrasekhar's lecture states that black holes will form as one of the natural end products of the evolution of massive stars, and that an isolated stationary black hole is described by the Kerr family, which is determined by mass and angular momentum [2]. Observationally, Galactic stellar black holes in X-ray binaries have masses generally below 10 solar masses, with Cygnus X-1 at about 20 solar masses, and gravitational-wave detections of merging black holes extend from above 30 up to 85 solar masses [14]. Burrows and Vartanyan describe a possible mass gap between the heaviest neutron stars and black holes of about 10 solar masses as suggested by the data but not proven [9]. The Gaia BH3 black hole, at 32.70 +/- 0.82 solar masses, lies in the mass range of the gravitational-wave black holes, and its metal-poor companion is cited by its discoverers as support for the idea that high-mass black holes are remnants of low-metallicity stars [14]. The relation between mass loss and metallicity underlies this link: low-metallicity stars lose less mass and keep larger helium cores and hydrogen envelopes at death [6].

## Evidence

The claims above rest on different kinds of evidence: neutrino counting, archival imaging of exploding stars, spectroscopic surveys, precision astrometry and timing, and numerical simulation. Eight studies show how each kind was obtained and what it established.

### Neutrinos from the CNO cycle in the Sun (Borexino, 2020)

The Borexino detector at Gran Sasso holds about 280 tonnes of liquid scintillator in a nylon vessel viewed by 2212 photomultiplier tubes [5]. The analysis used Phase-III data from July 2016 to February 2020, 1072 days of live time and an exposure of 1072 days times 71.3 tonnes [5]. The principal obstacle was background from bismuth-210, whose rate the team limited through the rate of its daughter polonium-210 in a region of the scintillator they call the Low Polonium Field, a region made possible by thermal stabilisation of the detector [5]. The result is a CNO neutrino interaction rate of 7.2 (+3.0, -1.7) counts per day per 100 tonnes and a significance of 5.1 sigma for the existence of CNO neutrinos; a simplified counting analysis alone would give only about 3.5 sigma [5]. The measured rate is compatible with both high-metallicity and low-metallicity solar models (0.5 and 1.3 sigma respectively), so it does not yet resolve the solar metallicity problem, although combined with other Borexino fluxes the low-metallicity hypothesis is disfavoured at 2.1 sigma [5]. The paper presents this as experimental confirmation of the catalytic hydrogen-burning cycle proposed in the 1930s by Bethe and von Weizsacker, and it notes that neutrinos are the only direct probe of the deep interior of the star [5].

### A census of supernova progenitors (Smartt, 2015)

Smartt reviewed searches of archival pre-explosion images for the progenitor stars of core-collapse supernovae in a time- and volume-limited sample, covering events from 1999 to 2013 within about 28 megaparsecs; the sample yielded 18 detections of progenitors and 27 upper limits [8]. The Type II-P progenitors were red supergiants with estimated initial masses from about 8 up to about 15 to 18 solar masses [8]. No progenitor was found above a luminosity of about log(L/Lsun) = 5.1, corresponding to roughly 16 to 18 solar masses depending on the models [8]. For a Salpeter initial mass function one would expect about 13 such progenitors above the limit; finding none or one has a Poisson probability of 3 x 10^-5 [8]. A maximum-likelihood fit gives a maximum mass of 16.5 (+2.5, -2.5) solar masses for two sets of evolutionary models and 18.5 (+3, -4) for a third, both at 95 percent confidence [8]. Smartt examined circumstellar dust, selection bias, and luminosity-analysis errors and concluded that it does not seem likely that they explain the deficit; he noted that explosion modelling by several groups predicts that stars above about 18 solar masses tend to produce failed supernovae and form black holes, and that this is in reasonable agreement with the observed luminosity limits [8]. For Type Ibc supernovae the sample contains 14 upper limits and one candidate progenitor system for a Type Ib event, and the observed rate of Ibc events is about a factor of two too high for single Wolf-Rayet stars alone, so binary systems are likely to produce the bulk, if not all, of the Ibc progenitors [8].

### Binary interaction among O stars (Sana et al., 2012)

Sana and colleagues carried out a spectroscopic survey of 71 single and multiple O-type objects in six nearby Galactic open clusters, in which 40 were spectroscopic binaries, an observed fraction of 0.56 [7]. They corrected for observational biases with simulations and obtained an intrinsic binary fraction of 0.69 +/- 0.09, a preference for close pairs, and a mass-ratio distribution consistent with uniform [7]. They then integrated these distributions through models of binary evolution and concluded that 71 percent of O stars interact with a companion, that 20 to 30 percent merge, that 33 percent are stripped of their envelope before exploding, and that the interaction and merger rates are two and three times larger than previous estimates [7]. The predicted stripped fraction of 33 percent is close to the observed share of hydrogen-poor events, 37 percent of all core-collapse supernovae [7].

### The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram from Gaia DR2 (2018)

The Gaia Collaboration built diagrams from parallax and three-band photometry by selecting sources with relative parallax error below 10 percent, tight photometric errors, an astrometric-quality filter, and, for field stars, low interstellar extinction, E(B-V) below 0.015 [4]. The full colour-magnitude diagram contained 65,921,112 stars, the low-extinction field diagram 4,276,690, and composite diagrams were assembled for 32 open clusters and 14 globular clusters [4]. The diagrams show a very thin main sequence, an evolution of the turn-off and red clump with age and metallicity, a binary sequence, hot subdwarfs, the path of post-AGB stars and planetary-nebula central stars toward the white-dwarf sequence, and the split of that sequence into hydrogen and helium atmospheres, which the authors checked against spectroscopic classifications of 5,237 white dwarfs from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey [4]. Comparison of the cluster sequences with isochrones shows good agreement for the upper and lower main sequence, with a disagreement near absolute magnitude 10 that the authors attribute either to the background subtraction at faint magnitudes or to the boundary conditions used for very-low-mass stars [4]. The old open cluster M67 has a turn-off hook and gap that distinguish diffusive from non-diffusive models, and the authors note that convection in the stellar core, mass loss, rotation, and magnetic fields remain poorly constrained and are often only parametrised in stellar models [4].

### The kilonova after GW170817 (Kasen et al., 2017)

The gravitational-wave detection of a neutron-star merger was followed by the optical and infrared transient AT 2017gfo. Its optical emission faded within days while the infrared (1 to 3 micrometres) stayed bright for nearly two weeks [12]. Kasen and colleagues computed radiative-transfer models in which radioactive heating from newly synthesised r-process nuclei powers the light, taking into account that lanthanides raise the opacity by about a factor of 100 [12]. The observed optical and infrared luminosities imply two ejecta components: a "blue" component of about 0.025 solar masses of light r-process material and a "red" component of about 0.04 solar masses of heavier r-process material [12]. The authors concluded that neutron-star mergers may be the dominant contributors to r-process production in the Galaxy, that a merger rate of at least 10^3 per cubic gigaparsec per year (about 8 x 10^-5 per year in the Milky Way) slightly exceeds the range inferred from Galactic binary pulsars, and that identification of individual elements such as neodymium is debatable given uncertainties in the atomic line data [12].

### Mass of the heaviest known pulsar (Fonseca et al., 2021)

The authors combined timing observations of the 2.8-millisecond pulsar PSR J0740+6620 from the Green Bank Telescope and the CHIME telescope. The pulsar is in a 4.77-day, nearly circular orbit with a white-dwarf companion, and the orbit is seen almost edge-on (inclination 87.56 degrees), so the general-relativistic Shapiro delay of the pulses passing near the companion can be measured [13]. The model-averaged pulsar mass is 2.08 (+0.07, -0.07) solar masses at 68.3 percent credibility, with a 95.4 percent lower bound of 1.95 solar masses, and the companion mass is 0.253 solar masses [13]. A distance of 1.14 (+0.17, -0.15) kiloparsecs, derived from the orbital-period change that results from the pulsar's transverse motion, matched the optical properties of an ultra-cool helium-atmosphere white dwarf [13]. The measurement sets a lower limit on the maximum mass of a neutron star, and thus on the nuclear equation of state [13].

### A 33 solar-mass dormant black hole (Gaia Collaboration, 2024)

During validation of preliminary Gaia data release 4 astrometry, the team found a source whose astrometric orbit of 11.6 years implied a dark companion with mass function 32.03 +/- 0.64 solar masses, the largest among about 1.5 million orbital solutions, with no other above 20 solar masses [14]. Radial velocities from Gaia and from three ground-based spectrographs matched the predicted orbit to within 0.5 km/s, which excluded a spurious solution [14]. The companion is an old, very metal-poor giant with [Fe/H] = -2.56 +/- 0.11 and mass 0.76 +/- 0.05 solar masses, giving a black-hole mass of 32.70 +/- 0.82 solar masses [14]. The authors conclude that the discovery strongly supports the scenario that high-mass black holes are remnants of low-metallicity stars, while stating that they cannot exclude a merger of two lighter black holes and that formation as an isolated binary appears unlikely [14].

### Simulations of the explosion mechanism (Burrows and Vartanyan, 2021)

The review combines the authors' two-dimensional simulations, run with the FORNAX code from progenitors of 9 to 26.99 solar masses, with a survey of three-dimensional work by other groups [9]. For their suite, models explode between about 150 and 400 milliseconds after bounce, with explosion energies of 0.09 to 2.3 times 10^51 erg, neutron-star gravitational masses of about 1.2 to 2.0 solar masses, and a trend of energy increasing with progenitor mass that matches the trend inferred from observed Type II-P supernovae [9]. The result supports the neutrino-driven mechanism, but the authors warn that progenitor models up to collapse have yet to converge between groups, and that effects of three-dimensional stellar evolution and rotation have not been fully assessed [9].

## Implications

### For theorists and modelers of stellar structure

The evidence above shows which parts of stellar theory are validated and which still depend on adjustable inputs. Fusion physics on the main sequence is well anchored: measured cross-sections, laboratory-calibrated reaction chains, and now neutrino detection of both the proton-proton and CNO channels agree with the solar picture, with only the abundance of heavy elements in the solar interior left open [1][5]. Mass loss is much less constrained. Heger and colleagues adopt the simplest assumption, mass-loss rates that scale as the square root of metallicity, and call it almost certainly naive, citing for example an exponent of 0.69 for hot stars; they add that mass loss from red supergiants is not well determined even at solar metallicity [6]. Because the mass of the final helium core depends on mass loss, the thresholds for black-hole formation and the existence of a mass gap shift with it [6][9]. The practical implication is that modelers should report the result as a function of explicit assumptions, such as mass-loss law, rotation, and treatment of convection, rather than quoting a single threshold mass. Gaia cluster sequences supply calibration data for this work: the Gaia authors argue that calibrating the effects of core convection, mass loss, rotation, and magnetic fields on star-cluster photometry is mandatory and will complement asteroseismology [4].

For explosion modelers the lesson is that the spherical star is a poor guide. Multidimensional turbulence and symmetry breaking seem to be required for explosion in most cases, since spherical simulations seldom explode, and chaos implies distribution functions of explosion energy, remnant mass, and nucleosynthesis even for one progenitor, whose widths are not yet known [9]. For population modelers the lesson is that binarity must be included from the start, since only a minority of massive stars evolve undisturbed [7].

### For observers and survey designers

Each question in the previous sections has a characteristic probe, and the evidence shows the probes are complementary. Neutrinos probe the core, as Borexino shows for the Sun [5]. Archival pre-explosion imaging probes the last stage of a star's life, as in the progenitor census, whose conclusions depend on survey depth and completeness: Smartt cites estimates that more than 80 percent of expected core-collapse supernovae are missed in optical and near-infrared searches in dusty luminous infrared galaxies and 10 to 15 percent in normal galaxies [8]. Gravitational waves combined with electromagnetic follow-up measure the sites of heavy-element production [12]. Pulsar timing and astrometric orbits give masses of compact remnants from orbital dynamics [13][14]. Nebular spectra of supernovae constrain nucleosynthesis yields and thereby progenitor masses [8]. Gaia Data Release 4 is expected to reveal further binary systems hosting black holes, which would enlarge the sample of dormant black holes beyond the three found so far in Gaia astrometry [14]. The author's assessment is that a claim about a population, such as the black-hole mass distribution, should be tested by at least two probes with different selection biases: the Gaia discoverers describe black holes in wide astrometric binaries and those in the extremely short-period binaries detected by gravitational waves as two ends of the black-hole population, and note that X-ray binaries are limited to short periods [14].

### For users of supernovae in cosmology and galactic archaeology

Type Ia supernovae calibrate cosmic distances and the expansion history, so their stellar-evolution context directly affects cosmological inference. Jha and colleagues note that the standardising variable, the light-curve width, correlates with host-galaxy properties, with brighter, slower events found in younger, less massive, and more strongly star-forming galaxies; the authors call this observation "potentially alarming" because the fundamental standardising variable then depends on the age of the progenitor system, which implies that its distribution should evolve with redshift, even though in current samples the relation between luminosity and light-curve shape does not show significant dependence on host-galaxy properties [10]. A delay-time distribution proportional to 1/t favours double-degenerate progenitors, but the progenitor question is still open [10]. For galactic archaeology, abundance patterns serve as clocks, since the decline of alpha-to-iron ratios marks the arrival of Type Ia iron, and GCE models give a framework for interpreting millions of stellar spectra, with the caveat that predictions inherit the yield uncertainties of 0.2 to 0.5 dex described earlier [11]. The treatment of the cosmological use of supernovae, including the Hubble tension, is in `library/science/cosmology-big-bang-dark-energy.md`; the author's assessment is that any cosmological analysis that propagates supernova systematics should include the progenitor-age dependence discussed here.

### For educators and general readers

Popular statements about stars often compress ranges into single numbers. Several corrections follow from the evidence. Stars below about 9 solar masses end as white dwarfs rather than core-collapse supernovae [6], and the white-dwarf mass distribution peaks near 0.6 solar masses [4]; by interpretation, this is the fate expected for the Sun. The elements are not made in one place: about half of the carbon and about three quarters of the nitrogen come from AGB stars in one model, oxygen and magnesium mainly from core-collapse supernovae, half of the iron-peak elements from Type Ia explosions, and the heaviest elements from neutron-star mergers and possibly rare magneto-rotational supernovae [11]. The phrase that we are made of star material is therefore accurate but incomplete: it hides the fact that different atoms in the body were processed in different kinds of stars on different timescales, and in the model of [11] some, such as hydrogen, came from the Big Bang. The author's assessment is that explaining stellar evolution as a family of mass- and metallicity-dependent branches, not a single life cycle, is more accurate and also more useful for understanding why astronomers still disagree on thresholds.

For methodology, the topic is an example of how a field converges: Eddington's paradox was resolved by a quantum-statistical idea, the CN cycle was confirmed more than 80 years after its proposal by detecting its neutrinos, and the red-supergiant deficit remains a live disagreement on which two independent lines of evidence, direct imaging and chemical-evolution fitting, bear [1][2][5][8][11]. That summary is the author's interpretation of the cited work.

## Criticisms and Open Problems

The sources agree on the outline of stellar evolution and disagree, in published form, on several quantitative and mechanistic points.

**Missing high-mass progenitors and what they imply.** Smartt interprets the absence of progenitors above about 18 solar masses together with explosion models that predict failed supernovae and black-hole formation in that range [8]. Burrows and Vartanyan report that in their simulations explodability does not seem to be a function of the compactness parameter, that both high- and low-compactness models explode, and that only very high compactness structures may fizzle into black holes; they also state that their finding of no explosion for 12 and 15 solar-mass models may be model-dependent [9]. The two treatments are not reconciled in the sources. Kobayashi and colleagues add a third constraint from chemical evolution, finding that stars above 30 solar masses can become failed supernovae if hypernovae contribute significantly at 20 to 50 solar masses [11].

**The explosion mechanism itself.** Heger and colleagues state that no two groups agree in detail on the final evolution of any massive star, including explosion energy, remnant mass, and rotation rate [6]. Burrows and Vartanyan argue that the neutrino mechanism seems to work to zeroth order but that mapping progenitor properties to observables such as explosion energy, neutron-star mass, nucleosynthesis, and kicks remains to be done, and that chaos and remaining microphysics uncertainties complicate it [9]. Kobayashi and colleagues note that the ejected iron mass in explosion simulations is not as large as observed [11].

**Type Ia progenitors.** The identity of the companion and the explosion mechanism remain unresolved. Direct companion searches and radio and X-ray non-detections constrain single-degenerate models, three hypervelocity stars found in Gaia data have been proposed as surviving white-dwarf companions, and early light-curve bumps are not uniquely interpretable as companion interaction [10]. Jha and colleagues note that the direct evidence for white-dwarf explosions is limited and not necessarily simply interpretable [10].

**Where the heaviest elements form.** Kasen and colleagues argue that neutron-star mergers may be the dominant source of r-process elements in the Galaxy, with a required merger rate slightly above the range inferred from Galactic binary pulsars [12]. Kobayashi and colleagues find that mergers alone do not explain europium at low metallicity because of their long delay times, and invoke a second source [11]. Burrows and Vartanyan conclude that ordinary neutrino-driven supernova ejecta cannot be the site of all of the r-process [9]. The sources therefore agree that mergers produce r-process material and disagree about whether they are sufficient.

**Black-hole formation and the mass spectrum.** Gaia BH3 supports the connection between low-metallicity progenitors and high-mass black holes, but its discoverers cannot exclude formation by merger of two lighter black holes, and they note that the maximum metallicity for the formation of high-mass black holes is a matter of active debate, with some models predicting 30 solar-mass black holes even at solar metallicity [14]. The mass gap between neutron stars and black holes is suggested but not proven [9], and Fonseca and colleagues note that the high-mass compact object in GW190814 could be the heaviest neutron star known, so the boundary between the two classes is not yet fixed by observation [13].

**Solar composition.** The Borexino CNO measurement cannot distinguish between high- and low-metallicity solar models, so the conflict between low spectroscopic abundances and the helioseismology-based interior remains open [5].

**Massive-star formation.** Larson concludes that it is not clear, observationally or theoretically, whether there is any upper limit to the mass with which stars can form, and the formation of the most massive stars remains poorly understood [3].

**Limits of observational sampling.** The Gaia authors state that they selected the most precise data without trying to reach completeness, so their diagrams demonstrate structure but do not give a complete census of stellar types [4].

## Common Pitfalls

- Quoting a single mass threshold, such as 8, 20, or 30 solar masses, as a law. The sources give ranges that depend on metallicity, mass loss, rotation, and the model used [6][9].
- Treating massive stars as single. Sana and colleagues report that only a minority evolve undisturbed [7].
- Using "supernova" for one mechanism. Core-collapse explosions are powered by gravitational energy released in the core, whereas Type Ia explosions are thermonuclear [9][10].
- Reading a composite HRD of clusters as the track of one star. Gaia's composite diagram colours different clusters by age; each star traces its own path, which is an interpretation of how [4] presents the data.
- Treating the element-source fractions of one chemical-evolution model as universal facts. The 49 percent of carbon from AGB stars is a model output at a specified age [11].
- Confusing a star's initial mass with the mass at death. Winds and binary interaction change it, which is why black-hole masses relate to progenitor metallicity [6][14].
