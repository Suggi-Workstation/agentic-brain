# Framing Effects -- Why the Same Information Produces Radically Different Decisions

Framing effects are the systematic influence that the presentation of
information -- rather than its objective content -- exerts on human
decisions. When logically identical options are described differently,
people reliably reverse their preferences: they become risk-averse when
outcomes are framed as gains and risk-seeking when the same outcomes are
framed as losses. First formalized by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman
in their 1981 Science paper "The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology
of Choice," framing effects violate a foundational axiom of rational
choice theory -- descriptive invariance, the principle that preferences
should depend only on outcomes, not on how they are described. The
phenomenon is among the most robust and most replicated findings in
behavioral science, and it carries direct consequences for medicine,
public policy, marketing, finance, and any field where decisions are
shaped by the words used to present them.

## Background

The intellectual origins of framing effects lie in the collision between
expected utility theory and the accumulating empirical evidence of human
irrationality that Kahneman and Tversky assembled throughout the 1970s.
Expected utility theory, formalized by John von Neumann and Oskar
Morgenstern in 1944, held that rational agents evaluate outcomes in terms
of their effect on total wealth, weight each outcome by its objective
probability, and choose the option that maximizes expected utility. A
core axiom of this framework was descriptive invariance (sometimes called
procedural invariance): the preference between two options should depend
only on the outcomes and probabilities, not on how the problem is
described. Two formulations of the same problem that are logically
equivalent should produce the same choice.

By the late 1970s, Kahneman and Tversky had already undermined this
assumption through their 1979 prospect theory, which showed that people
evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point
rather than in absolute terms, weight probabilities nonlinearly, and feel
losses roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains. If the reference
point is malleable -- if the same outcome can be framed as either a gain
or a loss depending on how it is described -- then prospect theory
predicts that framing should reverse preferences. The value function is
concave for gains (producing risk aversion) and convex for losses
(producing risk-seeking), so the identical outcome coded as a gain versus
a loss should pull preferences in opposite directions.

The 1981 Science paper made this prediction concrete and demonstrated it
empirically. Tversky and Kahneman presented the now-famous "Asian disease
problem" to 152 participants. Participants were told to imagine that the
United States was preparing for an outbreak of an unusual disease
expected to kill 600 people, and asked to choose between two programs.
When the programs were described in terms of lives saved (a gain frame),
72 percent chose the certain option -- "200 people will be saved" -- over
the risky gamble. When the identical programs were described in terms of
deaths (a loss frame), 78 percent chose the risky gamble -- a one-third
chance that no one would die and a two-thirds chance that 600 would die
-- over the certain "400 people will die." The objective outcomes were
identical. Only the frame differed. Yet preference reversed
dramatically.

This was not a one-off curiosity. Tversky and Kahneman demonstrated
preference reversals in monetary gambles both hypothetical and real, in
questions about the loss of human lives, and in problems involving
compound outcomes and mental accounting. They drew an explicit analogy
to perceptual illusions: just as the apparent height of a mountain peak
changes with your vantage point, the attractiveness of an option changes
with the decision frame. Unlike perceptual illusions, however, there is
no objective standard to resolve the inconsistency. The framing effect
raised a deep problem for rational choice theory: if preferences depend
on frames, and frames are arbitrary, then preferences themselves are
not stable properties of the agent but artifacts of presentation.

The 1981 paper launched a research program that has continued for over
four decades. Richard Thaler extended the work into consumer behavior
and mental accounting. The Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth (1998) typology
distinguished three kinds of framing -- risky choice, attribute, and
goal framing -- bringing order to a literature that had used the term
loosely. Thaler and Cass Sunstein's 2008 book "Nudge" translated framing
and default effects into a policy framework of "libertarian paternalism,"
showing that choice architecture could steer people toward better
outcomes without restricting their freedom. The framing effect has been
replicated in laboratories and field studies across cultures, age
groups, professional populations, and domains ranging from medicine to
finance to environmental policy. It remains one of the most cited and
most practically consequential findings in the behavioral sciences.

## Core Concepts

### The Gain-Loss Asymmetry and Prospect Theory

The engine beneath the framing effect is the value function from
prospect theory. The value function has three properties that combine to
make framing decisive. First, outcomes are evaluated as gains or losses
relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms. Second, the
function is concave for gains and convex for losses, producing risk
aversion in the gain domain and risk-seeking in the loss domain. Third,
the function is steeper for losses than for gains (loss aversion), so
losses loom larger than equivalent gains.

The framing effect exploits the first property directly. The Asian
disease problem works because "200 people will be saved" and "400 people
will die" describe the identical outcome, but the first formulation sets
the reference point at 600 deaths (so saving 200 is a gain) while the
second sets it at zero deaths (so 400 deaths is a loss). Once the
reference points differ, the concavity and convexity of the value
function in each domain produce opposing risk attitudes. A sure gain is
attractive because the value function is concave for gains -- the
certain 200 lives saved feels better than the gamble's expected 200. A
sure loss is unattractive because the value function is convex for
losses -- the certain 400 deaths feels worse than the gamble's expected
400, so people take the risk to avoid the sure loss.

This mechanism means that framing effects are not random noise or
careless responding. They are the predictable output of a cognitive
architecture that evaluates changes relative to a reference point and
that treats gains and losses asymmetrically. Any intervention that
shifts the reference point -- any change in how the status quo is
described -- will shift the frame and can reverse preferences. This is
why framing is so powerful and so difficult to eliminate: it operates
on the same machinery that produces all of prospect theory's predictions.

### Risky Choice Framing

Risky choice framing is the classic paradigm, exemplified by the Asian
disease problem. It involves a choice between a sure option and a risky
gamble, where the two are logically equivalent but described in terms of
either gains or losses. The robust finding across hundreds of
replications is that the gain frame produces risk aversion (preference
for the sure option) and the loss frame produces risk-seeking
(preference for the gamble).

The effect size is substantial. In the original 1981 study, the
preference reversal was 50 percentage points (72 percent versus 22
percent choosing the sure option). A replication by Mandel (2001) found
68.1 percent choosing the sure option in the survival frame versus 22.8
percent in the mortality frame -- nearly identical to the original. A
systematic review by Kuhberger (2023), synthesizing decades of risky
choice framing studies, confirmed the effect is reliable and of medium
magnitude, though it varies with problem description, probability
levels, and population.

Risky choice framing is the most studied variant because it most
directly tests the predictions of prospect theory. It demonstrates that
the curvature of the value function is not merely a laboratory
abstraction but a description of how people actually choose when the
stakes are framed differently. The practical implication is immediate:
anyone presenting a choice between a certain and a risky option has the
power to shift preferences simply by choosing whether to describe
outcomes as gains or losses.

### Attribute Framing

Attribute framing, distinguished by Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth (1998),
involves describing a single attribute or characteristic of an object or
event in either positive or negative terms. Unlike risky choice
framing, there is no gamble involved -- just an evaluation. The classic
example is ground beef described as "75 percent lean" versus "25 percent
fat." Participants rate the "75 percent lean" beef as better tasting,
higher quality, and less greasy than the "25 percent fat" beef, even
though the descriptions are logically identical. The positive frame
(lean) produces a more favorable evaluation than the negative frame
(fat).

Attribute framing operates through a simpler mechanism than risky
choice framing. The negative descriptor activates negative associations
and encodes the information in a less favorable light, while the
positive descriptor does the reverse. There is no reference point shift
and no risk attitude reversal -- just a direct effect of semantic
framing on evaluation. This makes attribute framing the most practically
pervasive variant: it appears in product descriptions ("90 percent fat
free" versus "10 percent fat"), performance reviews ("passed 85 percent
of tests" versus "failed 15 percent"), and news reporting ("employment
rose to 95 percent" versus "unemployment stands at 5 percent").

The Levin et al. meta-analysis found that attribute framing produces
reliable, medium-to-large effect sizes and that the effect is robust
across populations and domains. Unlike risky choice framing, attribute
framing does not require a gamble or a reference point -- it operates
purely on how a single piece of information is encoded, which makes it
both more common and harder to detect in everyday life.

### Goal Framing

Goal framing, the third type in the Levin et al. (1998) typology,
involves persuading people to adopt a behavior by framing the
consequences of acting or not acting in terms of gains or losses. A
message encouraging women to perform breast self-examination might be
gain-framed ("performing self-examination helps you discover tumors
early, increasing your chances of successful treatment") or loss-framed
("not performing self-examination means you may miss tumors, decreasing
your chances of successful treatment"). The two messages advocate the
same behavior but frame the stakes differently.

The empirical pattern for goal framing is more nuanced than for the
other two types. For prevention behaviors (where the outcome of acting
is relatively certain), gain-framed messages tend to be more persuasive
-- emphasizing the benefits of sunscreen use or regular exercise works
better than emphasizing the costs of not doing them. For detection
behaviors (where the outcome is uncertain -- a mammogram might find
something or might not), loss-framed messages tend to be more
persuasive. This interaction, documented extensively by Rothman and
Salovey (1997), reflects the fit between the certainty of the frame and
the certainty of the outcome: certain gains match gain frames, and
uncertain threats match loss frames.

Goal framing is the variant most directly relevant to public health and
marketing communication. The practical lesson is that the same
behavioral recommendation can be made more or less effective depending on
whether it is framed as a gain or a loss and whether the target behavior
is prevention or detection. This is not manipulation in the pejorative
sense -- it is using evidence about how people process information to
make beneficial messages more effective.

### Default Effects and Choice Architecture

Default effects are a distinct but closely related phenomenon in which
the pre-selected option in a choice set dramatically influences the
final choice, even when people are free to change it. Johnson and
Goldstein (2003) documented the canonical evidence: in countries with
opt-in organ donation systems (citizens must actively choose to become
donors), consent rates ranged from 4 percent (Denmark) to 28 percent
(Netherlands). In countries with opt-out systems (citizens are
automatically enrolled and must actively opt out), consent rates ranged
from 86 percent (Sweden) to nearly 100 percent (Austria and France).
The default option did not just nudge behavior -- it effectively
determined the outcome.

Default effects operate through a combination of mechanisms that overlap
with framing. The default serves as an implicit reference point, so
deviating from it feels like a loss (loss aversion). The default
carries an implicit endorsement -- if the institution pre-selected it,
it must be the recommended option. And the default requires no cognitive
effort to maintain, while changing it requires System 2 engagement,
which is costly and often skipped. These mechanisms make defaults
extraordinarily powerful: in the organ donation case, a change in the
default produced a 60-plus percentage point swing in consent rates, far
larger than any informational campaign could achieve.

Thaler and Sunstein's "Nudge" (2008) generalized this insight into a
framework of choice architecture: because people are predictably
influenced by defaults, framing, and starting points, the design of the
choice environment is itself a policy decision. There is no neutral
frame -- some default must be chosen, and that choice will shape
behavior. The framework of "libertarian paternalism" argues that
choice architects should use this influence to steer people toward
outcomes they would choose for themselves if they were fully attentive
and informed, while preserving their freedom to choose otherwise.

### Emphasis Framing versus Equivalence Framing

An important theoretical distinction, drawn by Druckman (2001),
separates equivalence framing from emphasis framing. Equivalence
framing (the classic Tversky and Kahneman paradigm) presents logically
identical information in different ways -- "saves 200 lives" versus
"400 will die" -- and demonstrates that the formulation alone reverses
preferences. Emphasis framing, by contrast, highlights different aspects
of a choice that are not logically identical but are both true -- a
political speech emphasizing economic growth versus emphasizing social
inequality. The two types operate through different mechanisms and have
different implications.

Equivalence framing is the stronger test of rationality because it
involves truly identical information. A preference reversal under
equivalence framing is a clear violation of descriptive invariance.
Emphasis framing is more common in real-world communication (politics,
media, advertising) and is arguably less irrational: emphasizing
different true aspects of a complex situation can legitimately shift
what people care about. The distinction matters for evaluating the
normative status of framing effects. Equivalence framing reveals a
genuine cognitive flaw -- the same information should produce the same
preference. Emphasis framing reveals the normal operation of salience
and attention, which is harder to call irrational.

Research by Aaroe (2011) and others, replicated during the COVID-19
pandemic, shows that the two types interact: the strongest media effects
occur when equivalence framing (gain versus loss) and emphasis framing
(domain emphasis) are combined and resonate with existing mental
schemata. This integrated view explains why framing effects are so
pervasive in real communication: professional communicators
instinctively combine both types, and audiences process them together.

### Fuzzy-Trace Theory as a Competing Explanation

Prospect theory is the dominant explanation for risky choice framing,
but it is not the only one. Fuzzy-trace theory, developed by Charles
Brainerd and Valerie Reyna, offers a competing account. Fuzzy-trace
theory proposes that people encode information at two levels: a verbatim
representation (the exact numbers and probabilities) and a gist
representation (the fuzzy, qualitative meaning -- "some people will be
saved" or "some people will die"). Decisions are based primarily on the
gist representation, not the verbatim one.

Under fuzzy-trace theory, the framing effect occurs because the gain
frame encodes the gist as "some will be saved" (a good outcome,
favoring the sure option) while the loss frame encodes the gist as "some
will die" (a bad outcome, favoring the gamble to avoid it). The key
prediction that distinguishes fuzzy-trace theory from prospect theory
concerns incomplete descriptions. Kuhberger and Tanner (2010) showed
that removing the zero complement of a risky option (stating only "a
one-third chance that 600 will be saved" without stating "a two-thirds
chance that no one will be saved") eliminates the framing effect.
Fuzzy-trace theory predicts this because the incomplete description
makes both options simplify to the same gist. Prospect theory does not
predict this, because the zero outcome adds zero value to the gamble
and should not change the prediction.

Replications by Kutscher and Feldman (2019) with Chinese samples
confirmed the Kuhberger and Tanner finding and showed it holds across
varying probability and expected-value conditions -- results that favor
fuzzy-trace theory. The debate is unresolved and reflects a deeper
question about whether decision-making is primarily computational
(prospect theory) or primarily intuitive and gist-based (fuzzy-trace
theory). The two accounts may both be partially correct, operating at
different levels of the cognitive process.

## Evidence

The empirical foundation for framing effects spans over four decades of
experimental and field research across multiple disciplines, making it
one of the most thoroughly documented phenomena in behavioral science.

### The Original Asian Disease Experiment

Tversky and Kahneman's 1981 Science paper presented the Asian disease
problem to 152 participants at Stanford University and the University of
British Columbia. In the gain frame, 72 percent chose the certain option
(Program A: 200 people saved) over the risky gamble (Program B: one-third
chance of 600 saved, two-thirds chance of none). In the loss frame, only
22 percent chose the certain option (Program C: 400 deaths) while 78
percent chose the gamble (Program D: one-third chance of no deaths,
two-thirds chance of 600 deaths). The 50-point preference reversal was
statistically significant and replicated across monetary and
life-and-death domains. The study also demonstrated framing effects in
compound outcomes, introducing the concept of mental accounting to
explain why people treat the same loss differently depending on which
mental account it is coded in.

### The McNeil Medical Framing Study

McNeil, Pauker, Sox, and Tversky (1982), publishing in the New England
Journal of Medicine, demonstrated framing effects among physicians,
patients, and graduate students facing a real medical decision: surgery
versus radiation therapy for lung cancer. When outcomes were framed in
terms of survival rates ("of 100 people having surgery, 90 live through
the post-operative period"), surgery was strongly preferred. When the
identical outcomes were framed in terms of mortality rates ("of 100
people having surgery, 10 die during surgery or the post-operative
period"), preference for surgery dropped substantially -- a swing of
roughly 34 percentage points in physicians' recommendations.

This study is particularly important because it showed that framing
effects are not limited to hypothetical classroom problems. The
participants were physicians making recommendations about a real
disease, and the framing shifted their clinical recommendations by over
a third. The findings challenged the assumption that framing only
affects the uninformed -- expert physicians were as susceptible as
patients and students. The study has been cited over a thousand times and
established medical decision-making as a major domain for framing
research.

### The Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth Meta-Analysis

The Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth (1998) typology, published in the
Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, synthesized the framing
literature and identified three distinct types: risky choice framing,
attribute framing, and goal framing. Their meta-analysis found that
all three produce reliable effects but with different magnitudes and
mechanisms. Attribute framing produced the largest and most consistent
effects. Risky choice framing produced reliable but somewhat smaller
effects, moderated by problem domain and probability levels. Goal
framing showed the most variable effects, interacting with the type of
behavior (prevention versus detection).

This meta-analysis was critical because it brought theoretical order
to a fragmented literature. Before 1998, "framing effect" was used to
describe many different phenomena, making it difficult to compare
results across studies. The typology clarified that the three types
operate through different mechanisms and should be studied separately.
Subsequent meta-analyses by Kuhberger (1998, 2023) and Steiger and
Kuhberger (2015) confirmed the reliability of each type while refining
the estimates of effect size and moderators.

### Default Effects in Organ Donation

Johnson and Goldstein (2003), publishing in Science, provided the
definitive evidence for default effects in the real world. Comparing
organ donation consent rates across European countries, they found
that opt-in countries (where citizens must actively register as donors)
had consent rates between 4 and 28 percent, while opt-out countries
(where citizens are presumed donors unless they opt out) had consent
rates between 86 and nearly 100 percent. A complementary experimental
study showed the same pattern in a controlled setting: when
participants were asked to make an organ donation decision, those given
an opt-in default donated at 42 percent while those given an opt-out
default donated at 82 percent.

This evidence is powerful because it isolates the default as the causal
variable. The countries had similar cultures, similar medical systems,
and similar attitudes toward organ donation as measured by surveys. The
only systematic difference was the default rule, and it produced a
60-plus percentage point difference in a life-or-death decision. The
finding directly validates the prospect-theory prediction that
reference points and loss aversion dominate decision-making when the
costs of switching are non-trivial, and it provided the empirical
foundation for Thaler and Sunstein's nudge framework.

### Moderators and Boundary Conditions

Research has identified several factors that moderate the size of
framing effects. A study by Guo, Trueblood, and Diederich (2017) and
follow-up work by Diederich and Wyszynski (2017) found that time
constraints amplify framing effects: under severe time pressure (one
second), the tendency to take more risk in the loss frame versus the
gain frame was significantly stronger than under relaxed time limits
(three seconds). This finding supports the dual-process account -- when
System 2 does not have time to engage, System 1's frame-dependent
intuitions dominate.

Aaroe (2011), studying framing during the COVID-19 pandemic, found
that framing effects were strongest for health-related outcomes
compared to economic outcomes and most pronounced when the type of
intervention entailed the highest risk for the emphasized domain. The
gain-versus-loss framing effect was moderated by domain emphasis and
prior beliefs -- participants who believed they had already been
infected showed weaker framing effects for the health domain, arguably
because they felt less vulnerable. This research integrated equivalence
and emphasis framing in a single design and showed that the strongest
effects emerge from their interaction.

Individual differences also matter. Studies of older versus younger
adults (Mikels and Reed, 2009; Mayhorn et al., 2002) found minimal age
differences in framing susceptibility, suggesting the effect is a robust
feature of human cognition rather than one that declines with cognitive
aging. However, expertise in the specific domain can reduce framing
effects -- experienced traders show smaller framing effects in financial
decisions than novices, paralleling the finding that market experience
reduces the endowment effect.

### The Replication Landscape

A systematic review by Kuhberger (2023), synthesizing the accumulated
evidence, confirmed that risky choice framing effects are reliable and
of medium magnitude across hundreds of studies. The review noted that
effect sizes vary with problem description, with studies using the
original unaltered Asian disease problem tending to produce larger
effects than studies using modified versions. Some replication
attempts have produced smaller effects than the original, but
methodological differences -- especially whether participants saw one
problem or many, and whether the original wording was preserved --
explain much of the variation. The core finding -- gain frames produce
risk aversion, loss frames produce risk-seeking -- has survived decades
of scrutiny and is considered one of the most robust findings in
behavioral decision-making.

## Implications

### For Medical Decision-Making

The McNeil et al. (1982) finding that framing shifts physician
recommendations by over a third has profound implications for medical
practice. When a physician tells a patient that a surgery has a "90
percent survival rate," the patient is more likely to choose surgery
than if the physician says it has a "10 percent mortality rate." The
information is identical, but the frame changes the decision. This is
not a hypothetical concern: it is a daily reality in every clinical
encounter where treatment options are discussed. Physicians are
constantly framing choices, often without realizing it, and the frames
they choose systematically influence the treatments patients receive.

The implication is not that physicians should avoid framing -- some
frame must be chosen. The implication is that physicians should be
trained to recognize framing effects, to present information in
balanced ways (offering both survival and mortality statistics), and to
use decision aids that present information in multiple frames so
patients can see how their preferences shift. Informed consent
procedures that present only one frame are not fully informative,
because they exploit the patient's framing susceptibility rather than
mitigating it. The author's assessment is that standardized,
multi-frame presentation of treatment outcomes should be a
requirement for high-stakes medical decisions, not a recommendation.

### For Public Policy and Nudge Design

Framing effects are the intellectual foundation of nudge theory and
choice architecture. The Johnson and Goldstein (2003) organ donation
evidence shows that a single default rule can shift a life-or-death
decision by 60-plus percentage points -- an effect larger than any
informational campaign. The Save More Tomorrow program, developed by
Thaler and Benartzi, exploits framing and default effects to increase
retirement savings: by asking employees to commit to future
contribution increases (which feel costless because the sacrifice is in
the future) and by making enrollment the default, participation rates
rose from roughly 40 percent to over 90 percent.

The policy implication is that the design of choice environments is
itself a policy decision with consequences as large as any tax or
mandate. Because some default must be chosen, the question is not
whether to nudge but which way to nudge. Thaler and Sunstein's framework
of libertarian paternalism argues that choice architects should set
defaults and frames that steer people toward outcomes they would choose
for themselves if fully attentive and informed, while preserving their
freedom to choose otherwise. The controversy is not about whether
framing works -- it does -- but about who gets to set the frames and
whether the nudged outcome genuinely reflects the individual's
preferences or the choice architect's.

### For Marketing and Communication

Framing is the single most leveraged tool in marketing communication.
"Save $50" is more motivating than "spend $450" because the discount
frame codes the purchase price against a higher reference point, making
the saving feel like avoiding a loss. "90 percent fat free" sells better
than "10 percent fat" because the positive frame produces a more
favorable product evaluation. "Limited time offer" creates urgency by
framing inaction as a loss of the opportunity. Every pricing, packaging,
and advertising decision is a framing decision, and the frames that
marketers choose systematically influence what consumers buy.

The goal-framing research (Rothman and Salovey, 1997) provides specific
guidance: gain-framed messages work better for prevention behaviors
(sunscreen, exercise, dental hygiene), while loss-framed messages work
better for detection behaviors (screening tests, where the outcome is
uncertain). Public health campaigns that ignore this distinction --
using loss frames for prevention or gain frames for detection --
leave persuasive power on the table. The practical lesson for
communicators is to match the frame to the behavior: certain outcomes
deserve gain frames, uncertain threats deserve loss frames.

### For Negotiation and Persuasion

In negotiation, framing determines what feels like a gain and what
feels like a loss. Concessions framed as losses from one's current
position are psychologically far more painful than identical concessions
framed as forgone gains. A negotiator who frames their offer as a gain
for the other party ("you will receive X") elicits more cooperation than
one who frames it as a reduction from their own position ("I will give
up Y"). The first offer in a negotiation anchors the entire range, and
the frame in which it is presented shapes how the other party
evaluates subsequent proposals.

The implication for anyone who persuades for a living -- salespeople,
lawyers, politicians, managers -- is that the frame is often more
important than the substance. Two offers with identical objective terms
can be received very differently depending on how they are framed. The
ethical line is the same as in policy: framing to help the audience make
a decision aligned with their interests is legitimate persuasion;
framing to exploit the audience's cognitive vulnerabilities against
their interests is manipulation. The cognitive mechanism is identical;
the ethical assessment depends on intent and outcome.

### For Investing and Financial Decisions

Framing effects are a primary driver of investor error. The disposition
effect -- selling winners too early and holding losers too long -- is
partly a framing effect: realizing a loss frames the decision as
accepting a loss (painful, so postponed), while realizing a gain frames
it as locking in a win (pleasurable, so rushed). Myopic loss aversion,
documented by Benartzi and Thaler (1995), is a framing effect driven by
evaluation frequency: investors who check portfolios daily experience
far more loss frames than those who check annually, making equities feel
riskier and driving them toward lower-return assets.

The implication for investors is to reframe deliberately. Evaluating
a position in terms of long-term expected return rather than
short-term paper gains and losses shifts the frame from loss-averse
daily tracking to rational long-term assessment. Value investors like
Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger explicitly train themselves to
reframe market declines as opportunities rather than losses, which is
an application of framing awareness to investment discipline. The
practical prescription is to control the frame: choose evaluation
horizons, reference points, and information presentations that
minimize the influence of loss-averse framing on consequential
financial decisions.
