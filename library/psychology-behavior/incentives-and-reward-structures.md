# Incentives -- Why Reward Structures Shape Surprising Behavior and Often Backfire

Incentives are the most powerful predictor of human behavior, yet they
are also the most systematically underestimated force in human affairs.
Charlie Munger's dictum -- "show me the incentive and I will show you the
outcome" -- captures a truth that economics, psychology, and
organizational theory have independently converged on: people respond to
reward structures with remarkable, often surprising, and frequently
counterproductive creativity. The cobra effect, Goodhart's Law, the
principal-agent problem, and the crowding out of intrinsic motivation are
not isolated curiosities but manifestations of a single underlying
mechanism -- the gap between what we measure and what we value, exploited
by agents whose interests diverge from the designer's intent.

## Background

The study of incentives sits at the intersection of economics and
psychology, two disciplines that long treated the subject differently
before converging on a shared understanding. Economics developed the
formal apparatus: principal-agent theory, contract theory, and mechanism
design. Psychology contributed the empirical foundations: the distinction
between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, the crowding-out effect, and
the cognitive mechanisms by which incentives reshape not just behavior
but thought itself.

The economic lineage begins with Adam Smith, who recognized that
self-interest, properly channeled, could produce collective benefit. But
the modern formal treatment emerged in the 1970s. Stephen Ross and Barry
Mitnick both claimed authorship of the principal-agent problem, though
the most cited reference is Michael Jensen and William Meckling's 1976
paper "Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership
Structure," which outlined how the separation of ownership and control
creates agency costs. Sanford Grossman and Oliver Hart (1983) provided
the rigorous mathematical treatment, showing that when an agent's effort
is unobservable, the principal must design a compensation scheme that
balances incentives against the risk imposed on the agent. Oliver Hart
and Bengt Holmstrom (1987) synthesized the field in their "Theory of
Contracts," distinguishing hidden-action models (moral hazard) from
hidden-information models (adverse selection) and establishing that the
optimal incentive scheme is always second-best -- never the first-best
solution that complete information would allow.

The psychological lineage is younger but equally consequential. Edward
Deci's 1971 experiments at the University of Rochester demonstrated that
paying people to do activities they already enjoyed reduced their
subsequent interest in those activities when the payment stopped. This
"undermining effect" challenged the behaviorist orthodoxy that rewards
always reinforce behavior. Deci and Richard Ryan developed
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) from this foundation, arguing that
humans have three basic psychological needs -- autonomy, competence, and
relatedness -- and that external interventions can support or undermine
these needs depending on how they are perceived. Cognitive Evaluation
Theory, a sub-theory of SDT, specifies the conditions under which
rewards diminish intrinsic motivation: when they are perceived as
controlling rather than informational, they reduce the agent's sense of
autonomy and thereby crowd out intrinsic drive.

The bridge between these lineages was built by behavioral economists like
Bruno Frey, who formalized the "crowding-out effect" in economic terms,
showing that external interventions via rewards or regulations can
systematically diminish intrinsic motivation under specific, identifiable
conditions. The result is a unified picture: incentives are not simple
levers that can be pulled to produce predictable behavior. They are
complex interventions that interact with pre-existing motivation, alter
perceived autonomy, create strategic responses the designer never
anticipated, and can produce outcomes diametrically opposite to those
intended.

Charles Goodhart's 1975 observation about monetary policy -- "when a
measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" -- and Donald
Campbell's 1976 corollary -- "the more any quantitative social indicator
is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to
corruption pressures" -- provide the meta-principle that unifies these
strands. Any incentive system selects a proxy for the true goal. Agents
optimize the proxy. The proxy decouples from the goal. The result is
the cobra effect, the teaching-to-the-test phenomenon, the financial
crisis driven by misaligned banker compensation, and every other instance
of incentives producing surprising and often destructive behavior.

Charlie Munger's contribution was the synthesis: in his 1995 speech "The
Psychology of Human Misjudgment," he ranked incentive-caused bias as the
first and most powerful of his 25 cognitive tendencies, arguing that
agents inside an incentive structure do not merely act differently --
they think differently. The distortion is invisible from the inside. A
surgeon paid per procedure genuinely believes the procedures are
necessary. A banker chasing quarterly bonuses genuinely believes the
risk is manageable. The incentive does not corrupt judgment in the sense
of conscious deception; it reshapes perception so that the self-serving
action appears correct.

## Core Concepts

### The Iron Law of Incentives

The foundational principle, articulated in its bluntest form by Charlie
Munger, is that incentives dominate behavior more reliably than any other
psychological force. Munger placed "Reward and Punishment Super-Response
Tendency" first among his 25 causes of human misjudgment, arguing that
incentive-caused bias is the single most underestimated force in human
affairs. The principle is not that people are cynical or mercenary. It
is that incentive structures reshape what people attend to, what they
perceive as important, and ultimately what they believe to be true.

The iron law has three corollaries that explain why incentive design
fails so often. First, people optimize the measured proxy, not the
underlying goal -- this is Goodhart's Law. Second, the optimization is
rational from the agent's perspective; the behavior is not a moral
failure but a correct response to the reward structure as designed.
Third, the agent's perception adapts to the incentive, making the
distortion invisible from inside the system. Munger's key insight was
this psychological layer: agents do not just act differently when
incentives diverge, they think differently. The surgeon paid per
operation, the banker compensated for short-term returns, the teacher
evaluated on test scores -- each genuinely perceives the world through
the lens the incentive provides.

This concept connects to System 1 versus System 2 thinking. Incentive
structures operate largely through System 1, the fast, automatic mode
of cognition. The agent does not consciously calculate "how can I game
this metric?" Instead, the incentive shapes attention, salience, and
what feels like the right thing to do. The conscious System 2 reasoning
that might question the alignment comes later, if at all, and must
overcome the comfortable certainty that System 1 provides.

### Perverse Incentives and the Cobra Effect

A perverse incentive is a reward structure designed to encourage a
specific behavior that instead promotes actions undermining the intended
objective, often yielding outcomes worse than inaction. The canonical
example is the cobra effect, named after a purported incident in British
colonial India: concerned about the venomous cobra population in Delhi,
the British administration offered a bounty for every dead cobra.
Initially effective, the program eventually incentivized enterprising
locals to breed cobras for the bounty. When the government discovered
the breeding operations and scrapped the program, breeders released
their now-worthless snakes, and the cobra population exceeded its
original level.

The cobra effect is the most dramatic form of perverse incentive because
the incentive directly rewards making the problem worse. But the pattern
is universal: the Hanoi rat bounty under French colonial rule, where rat
catchers cut tails off live rats and bred more in basements; the
Bogota one-car-per-day driving ban that caused families to buy second
cars; the US Endangered Species Act motivating preemptive habitat
destruction by landowners who feared losing development rights. Each
instance follows the same structure: a noble goal, a measurable proxy,
a financial reward attached to the proxy, and a population of rational
agents optimizing the proxy at the expense of the goal.

The cobra effect is distinguished from a mere Goodhart failure by the
directionality of the outcome. Goodhart's Law describes the decoupling
of measure from goal under optimization pressure. The cobra effect is what
happens when the decoupled metric creates incentives that actively worsen
the original problem. Goodhart's Law is the gap between measure and
reality. The cobra effect is the gap turned into a weapon against the
goal.

### Goodhart's Law and Campbell's Law

Charles Goodhart, an economist at the Bank of England, observed in 1975
that "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
The principle was originally about monetary policy: when a government
targets a specific monetary aggregate, banks and consumers rearrange
their behavior to optimize against the target, and the target loses its
predictive value. But the law generalizes to any system where a proxy
measure is selected as a target and agents respond to the incentive it
creates.

Donald Campbell's 1976 corollary extends this to social indicators: "The
more any quantitative social indicator is used for social
decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures,
and the more apt it will be to distort the social processes it is
intended to monitor." Campbell's Law explains why standardized test
scores, used as targets for school funding and evaluation, produce
teaching-to-the-test, narrowed curricula, and in extreme cases outright
cheating scandals. The indicator does not merely fail to measure quality
-- it actively degrades the educational processes it was meant to
monitor.

The mechanism is straightforward. Any goal has multiple dimensions.
A measure captures some of them imperfectly. When the measure becomes a
target, agents optimize the measured dimensions and neglect the
unmeasured ones. If the unmeasured dimensions matter, the outcome
degrades even as the measured indicator improves. A hospital rewarded
for short length-of-stay discharges patients early, potentially harming
outcomes. A software team rewarded for lines of code produces verbose,
low-quality code. A sales team rewarded for deal count signs
low-quality contracts that haunt the company later. In each case, the
measured metric rises while the true goal suffers.

### The Principal-Agent Problem

The principal-agent problem, formalized by Jensen and Meckling (1976)
and developed rigorously by Grossman and Hart (1983) and Hart and
Holmstrom (1987), describes the conflict that arises when one party (the
principal) delegates work to another (the agent) whose interests diverge
from the principal's. The core difficulty is asymmetric information: the
agent has information the principal lacks -- about effort expended, about
risks taken, about private costs and benefits. The principal cannot
perfectly observe the agent's action, so compensation must be tied to
observable outcomes, which are influenced by factors beyond the agent's
control.

The standard examples are corporate management (agent) versus
shareholders (principal), elected officials (agent) versus citizens
(principal), and brokers (agent) versus clients (principal). In each
case, the agent's interests diverge from the principal's: managers prefer
perquisites and low effort; politicians prefer re-election; brokers
prefer high transaction volume. The deviation of the agent's actions from
the principal's interest is called agency cost, and it is the price of
delegation under asymmetric information.

Hart and Holmstrom's framework distinguishes two variants. In hidden
action models (moral hazard), the agent chooses an effort level the
principal cannot observe; compensation must be tied to a noisy outcome
signal, imposing risk on the risk-averse agent. In hidden information
models (adverse selection), the agent has private information about their
type before the contract is written. In both cases, the optimal contract
is second-best: it never achieves the first-best outcome that complete
information would allow, because the principal must trade incentive
intensity against the risk premium the agent demands for bearing
outcome-based compensation risk.

The Informativeness Principle, developed by Holmstrom (1979), states that
any signal that reveals information about the agent's effort should be
included in the compensation contract. Relative Performance Evaluation --
comparing an agent to peers -- filters out common noise and sharpens
incentives. But the principle also reveals a fundamental tension: the
more precisely effort is measured, the more risk is imposed on the agent,
and the more expensive the contract becomes. Incentive design is always a
balancing act, never a free lunch.

### Intrinsic Versus Extrinsic Motivation

Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory distinguishes two fundamental
types of motivation. Intrinsic motivation is the drive to engage in an
activity for its inherent satisfaction -- the curiosity, mastery, and
autonomous interest that make a child spend hours building sandcastles or
a scientist pursue a problem for decades. Extrinsic motivation is the
drive to engage in an activity to obtain an external reward or avoid an
external punishment -- the bonus, the grade, the promotion, the threat of
firing.

The distinction matters because the two types interact in
counterintuitive ways. Deci's 1971 experiment found that paying
participants to solve puzzles reduced the time they spent on the puzzles
during a free-choice period when payment was no longer offered. Lepper,
Greene, and Nisbett (1973) replicated this with children: nursery school
children who were offered a reward for drawing -- an activity they
previously enjoyed -- showed less interest in drawing afterward than
children who were not rewarded. This "overjustification effect" suggests
that external rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation by shifting the
perceived locus of causality from internal to external.

Self-Determination Theory identifies three basic psychological needs whose
satisfaction fosters intrinsic motivation and well-being: autonomy (the
experience of choice and self-direction), competence (the experience of
effective interaction with the environment), and relatedness (the
experience of connection to others). External interventions support
intrinsic motivation when they are perceived as informational -- affirming
competence or supporting autonomy. They undermine it when perceived as
controlling -- reducing the agent's sense of volition and replacing
intrinsic drive with external compliance.

The practical implication is profound. Daniel Pink's 2009 book "Drive"
popularized the finding that for tasks requiring even rudimentary
cognitive skill, contingent-if-then rewards (do this, get that) reduce
performance and crowd out intrinsic motivation, while autonomy,
mastery, and purpose sustain engagement over the long term. The lesson is
not that extrinsic motivators are useless -- they can be effective for
mechanical, algorithmic tasks -- but that they carry hidden costs that
compound in knowledge work, creative work, and any domain where the
quality of engagement matters as much as the quantity of output.

### The Crowding-Out Effect

Bruno Frey formalized the crowding-out effect as the phenomenon where
external interventions -- monetary rewards, regulations, performance
evaluations -- diminish intrinsic motivation. The effect is not
universal; it depends on specific conditions. Crowding out is more
likely when the external intervention is perceived as controlling rather
than supportive, when the task is inherently interesting rather than
boring, and when the agent had high initial intrinsic motivation.

Frey and Jegen (2001) synthesized the empirical evidence and identified
the mechanisms. External rewards can shift the perceived locus of
control from internal to external, reducing the agent's sense of
self-determination. Regulations can signal distrust, implying the agent
would not behave well without oversight, which undermines the intrinsic
motivation that previously drove good behavior. The effect can spill
over: a reward in one domain can reduce intrinsic motivation in
adjacent, unrewarded domains.

The classic real-world illustration is the Israeli daycare study
(Gneezy and Rustichini, 2000): when daycares introduced a fine for late
pickups, late pickups increased rather than decreased. The fine replaced
the moral obligation to arrive on time with a market transaction -- the
price of being late was now simply a fee, and parents paid it willingly.
When the fine was removed, late pickups remained elevated, because the
intrinsic motivation that had previously suppressed lateness did not
return. This demonstrates that crowding out can be irreversible: once
intrinsic motivation is displaced, it may not recover even if the
external intervention is removed.

### Incentive-Caused Bias and Perceptual Distortion

Munger's deepest contribution to incentive theory is the claim that
incentives do not merely change behavior -- they change perception. The
person inside an incentive structure experiences their own judgment as
objective, careful, even conservative. The incentive structure has shaped
their clinical judgment so thoroughly that the distortion is invisible
from the inside. This is not conscious corruption. It is the natural
operation of cognitive processes adapting to the reward environment.

The mechanism connects to cognitive dissonance and confirmation bias.
When an agent's livelihood depends on a particular action being correct,
the agent's cognitive system works to make that action appear correct.
The surgeon paid per procedure does not consciously think "I will perform
unnecessary surgeries for money." The surgeon's perception of surgical
necessity adapts to the reward structure, and procedures that would seem
marginal under a different compensation scheme now seem clearly
indicated. The banker compensated for loan volume does not consciously
decide to make bad loans. The banker's perception of creditworthiness
adapts, and marginal borrowers now appear creditworthy.

This perceptual adaptation is why incentive-caused bias is so dangerous
and so resistant to reform. The agents inside the system are not lying;
they are reporting their honest perception, which has been shaped by the
incentive structure. Externally imposed reforms that change the
incentive structure are met with genuine confusion and resistance, not
because the agents are dishonest but because their adapted perception
tells them the new structure is wrong.

## Evidence

### Deci's Original Experiments and the Meta-Analytic Record

Edward Deci's 1971 experiment at the University of Rochester established
the empirical foundation for the undermining effect. Participants were
paid for one session of a puzzle-solving task and not paid for another
session. During a free-choice period after each session, participants who
had been paid spent less time on the puzzles than participants who had
not been paid. Deci interpreted this as evidence that the external reward
reduced intrinsic motivation by shifting the perceived locus of
causality from internal (doing the puzzle for its own sake) to external
(doing the puzzle for money).

The finding was controversial. Subsequent meta-analyses produced mixed
results. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999) conducted a meta-analytic review
of 128 experiments and concluded that tangible rewards expected and
contingent on performance reliably undermined intrinsic motivation,
while verbal rewards and unexpected tangible rewards did not. Cameron
and colleagues (2001) challenged this conclusion, arguing that the
undermining effect was limited to specific reward types and dependent
measures. Deci, Ryan, and Koestner (2001) responded that Cameron's
analysis inappropriately combined different reward types and failed to
account for moderating variables. The controversy persists, but the
consensus in the psychological literature is that the crowding-out effect
is real, robust for controlling contingent rewards, and absent or even
reversed for informational or unexpected rewards.

### The Israeli Daycare Study

Gneezy and Rustichini (2000) provided one of the most cited real-world
demonstrations of crowding out. They studied ten Israeli daycares that
introduced a fine for late pickups. After the fine was introduced, the
number of late pickups increased. Parents interpreted the fine as a price
for being late rather than a punishment, and the moral cost of lateness
was replaced by a monetary cost they were willing to pay. Critically,
when the fine was removed after 12 weeks, late pickups remained at the
elevated level. The intrinsic motivation that had previously suppressed
lateness -- the social pressure to be on time, the concern for the
teachers -- did not return. The crowding out was effectively permanent.

The study has been debated on methodological grounds, but its central
finding -- that introducing a monetary price for a behavior previously
governed by social norms can permanently erode those norms -- has been
replicated in multiple contexts. Blood donation provides a parallel
case: in some studies, paying donors reduced donation rates because it
crowded out the altruistic motivation that previously drove giving.

### Jensen and Meckling on Agency Costs

Jensen and Meckling's 1976 paper "Theory of the Firm" established the
modern framework for analyzing agency costs. They defined agency costs
as the sum of the principal's monitoring expenditures, the agent's
bonding expenditures, and the residual loss -- the reduction in the
principal's welfare due to the divergence between the agent's decisions
and the principal's optimal decisions. Their key empirical claim was
that agency costs are endemic to any situation where ownership and
control are separated, and that the magnitude of agency costs is a
function of the incentive structure, the cost of monitoring, and the
agent's risk preferences.

The framework has been applied extensively to corporate governance.
Executive compensation is the canonical application: stock options and
performance shares are designed to align manager incentives with
shareholder interests. But the history of executive compensation
provides as many examples of incentive failure as of incentive success.
Backdated stock options, earnings manipulation to hit bonus targets, and
excessive risk-taking driven by convex compensation structures (heads I
win, tails the shareholder loses) all illustrate how incentive design can
produce agency costs rather than reduce them. The 2008 financial crisis
provided a large-scale demonstration: banker compensation structures
rewarded short-term gains and socialized long-term losses, creating
perverse incentives that contributed to systemic risk.

### The Cobra Effect in Practice

The cobra effect has been documented across domains. In colonial Hanoi,
the French rat bounty led to rat breeding. In US environmental policy, the
Endangered Species Act created perverse incentives for landowners to
destroy habitat preemptively before endangered species could be
discovered on their property. In healthcare, fee-for-service reimbursement
rewards providers for the volume of procedures rather than patient
outcomes, contributing to overtreatment and rising costs. In education,
high-stakes testing has produced teaching-to-the-test, narrowed
curricula, and in the Atlanta Public Schools scandal, outright
falsification of student test scores by educators whose bonuses depended
on the results.

A 2024 paper in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (John et al.) synthesized
the cross-disciplinary evidence under the concept of "proxy failure."
The authors argued that whenever incentivization is based on an
imperfect proxy of the underlying goal, a pressure arises that tends to
make the proxy a worse approximation of the goal. They documented this
pattern in neuroscience (dopamine signaling and reward learning),
economics (monetary policy and performance metrics), and ecology
(peacock tails and sexual selection), arguing that proxy failure is an
inherent risk in any goal-oriented system, not a contingent accident of
bad design.

### Munger's Empirical Synthesis

Charlie Munger's 1995 speech at Harvard Law School, "The Psychology of
Human Misjudgment," provided an empirical synthesis drawn not from
controlled experiments but from decades of observation across business,
law, and investing. Munger argued that incentive-caused bias is the most
powerful and most underestimated of all the psychological tendencies that
distort human judgment. He cited cases from his own experience at
Berkshire Hathaway and from legal and business history, arguing that
incentive structures shape not just behavior but perception -- the agent
inside the system genuinely perceives the incentive-aligned action as
correct.

Munger's contribution is less a formal theory than a practitioner's
synthesis, but its influence is substantial. The speech has become a
standard reference in value investing and behavioral finance, and the
incentive-caused bias framework is widely used to analyze corporate
governance failures, regulatory capture, and professional misconduct.
The strength of Munger's account is its insistence on the perceptual
dimension: incentives do not just change what people do, they change
what people think is right.

## Implications

### For Investors and Business Decision-Makers

Understanding incentives is essential for analyzing any business. The
principal-agent problem is endemic to public companies: managers (agents)
and shareholders (principals) have divergent interests, and compensation
structures determine the direction and magnitude of the resulting agency
costs. An investor evaluating a company must examine not just the
financials but the incentive structure: how are executives compensated,
what metrics drive their bonuses, and do those metrics align with
long-term shareholder value or short-term stock price?

The analysis extends to corporate culture. A company that rewards
salespeople for deal volume will generate high volumes of low-quality
deals. A company that rewards engineers for lines of code will generate
verbose, unmaintainable code. A company that rewards managers for
quarterly earnings will generate short-term thinking at the expense of
long-term investment. The incentive structure is a more reliable
predictor of organizational behavior than stated values or mission
statements. As Munger put it, the incentive structure shapes the
outcome more reliably than the stated intentions.

For investors, this means that analyzing compensation disclosures, the
metrics tied to performance bonuses, and the presence of clawback
provisions provides insight into the true risk profile of an investment.
Bankers paid for loan origination volume without retention risk generated
the subprime mortgage crisis. Pharmaceutical executives compensated for
drug launches without liability for downstream safety failures produced
the opioid epidemic. In each case, the incentive structure was visible
in the compensation disclosures, and the resulting behavior was
predictable from the structure.

### For Policy Design and Public Administration

Policy design is incentive design. Every regulation, subsidy, tax, and
penalty creates an incentive structure, and the designers' intentions are
irrelevant to the outcomes the structure produces. The cobra effect is
the cautionary tale: a well-intentioned bounty for dead cobras created
cobra farms. But the lesson generalizes to every domain of public policy.

The crowding-out effect has particular relevance for policy. When a
monetary incentive is introduced for a behavior previously governed by
intrinsic motivation or social norms, the policy designer must weigh the
direct incentive effect against the potential erosion of the pre-existing
motivation. The Israeli daycare study shows that this erosion can be
irreversible. A policy that introduces a fine for late pickups may
increase lateness permanently. A policy that pays blood donors may
reduce donation rates. A policy that pays students for grades may reduce
intrinsic interest in learning.

The design principle that emerges from this evidence is to distinguish
leading from lagging indicators, to tie rewards to verified outcomes
rather than easily manufactured outputs, and to build short feedback
loops so gaming is detected before it compounds. The designer must ask,
before any incentive system is launched: "What is the cheapest way
someone could earn this reward without achieving the goal?" If that path
exists and pays well, people will find it. The Endangered Species Act
failed to ask this question and produced preemptive habitat destruction.
The cobra bounty failed to ask it and produced cobra farms.

### For Organizational Design and Management

Organizational incentive design is the practical art of aligning
individual interests with collective goals. The principal-agent
framework provides the theoretical foundation, but the practical
challenges are substantial. Hart and Holmstrom's Informativeness
Principle suggests using all available performance signals, but the
risk imposed on agents by outcome-based compensation creates a cost that
limits how aggressively incentives can be applied. The optimal contract
is always second-best.

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory provides a complementary perspective:
compensation is a hygiene factor, not a motivator. Adequate and fair
compensation prevents dissatisfaction, but it does not create sustained
engagement. Motivators -- achievement, growth, autonomy, responsibility --
are what sustain performance over time. The implication for incentive
design is that the hygiene layer (fairness, stability, legibility) must
be protected first. A system that produces extreme variance, visible
gaming, or unexplained outcomes undermines trust even when headline
financial results appear strong.

The crowding-out literature adds a further constraint: incentive systems
that are perceived as controlling rather than supportive will erode
intrinsic motivation, and the erosion may be irreversible. The design
challenge is to create incentive structures that are perceived as
informational (affirming competence, supporting autonomy) rather than
controlling (imposing external compliance). This is easier to state than
to achieve, but the principle is clear: the worst outcome is an
incentive system that purchases short-term compliance at the cost of
long-term engagement.

### For Personal Decision-Making

The incentive framework applies to personal decisions as much as to
organizational ones. Understanding one's own incentive structures --
what one is rewarded for, what one is punished for, and how those
rewards and punishments shape perception -- is a form of
self-awareness that most people lack. The incentive-caused bias
operates on individuals as much as on organizations: the person whose
income depends on a particular belief will find that belief increasingly
self-evident.

The practical implication is to examine one's own incentive structures
with the same skepticism one would apply to a company's compensation
disclosures. What am I rewarded for believing? What would I have to
give up if I changed my mind? What does my social environment reward?
The answers reveal the perceptual distortions that incentive-caused
bias imposes, and awareness of those distortions is the first step
toward mitigating them. Decision journals -- recording reasoning before
outcomes are known -- are one tool for counteracting the perceptual
adaptation that incentives produce.

## Common Pitfalls

### Measuring the Proxy Instead of the Goal

The most common incentive design error is selecting a proxy that is
easily measurable but poorly correlated with the true goal. Lines of
code, test scores, patient throughput, and deal count are all proxies
that are easy to measure and easy to game. The design question is not
"what can we measure?" but "what do we actually want?" If the answer to
the second question cannot be measured directly, the incentive system
must use multiple complementary proxies, accept measurement imperfection,
and build in feedback loops that detect gaming.

### Ignoring the Crowding-Out Risk

Introducing monetary incentives for behavior previously driven by
intrinsic motivation or social norms risks crowding out the pre-existing
motivation. The risk is not hypothetical; it is documented across
education, healthcare, environmental policy, and blood donation. Before
adding a monetary incentive, the designer must assess whether the
behavior is currently driven by intrinsic motivation and whether the
monetary incentive will be perceived as controlling. If both are true,
the crowding-out risk is high and the intervention may produce the
opposite of its intended effect.

### Assuming Agents Will Not Find the Cheap Path

Every incentive system has a cheap path -- a way to earn the reward
without achieving the goal. The designer's job is to identify this path
before launch, not after agents have found it. Red-team testing --
asking "how would I game this?" -- is the design discipline that
separates effective incentive systems from cobra effects. If the cheap
path exists and pays well, agents will find it, optimize it, and
potentially undermine the goal more thoroughly than inaction would have.
