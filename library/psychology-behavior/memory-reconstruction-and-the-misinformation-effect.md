# Memory Is Reconstructed -- Later Information Can Rewrite Event Recall

Remembering an event is a reconstruction from encoded details, retrieval cues, prior knowledge, and information acquired afterward, not a literal replay of a fixed record [3][5][11]. The misinformation effect occurs when misleading post-event information lowers accuracy for the original event or is later reported as part of that event [2][5][15]. The effect is well established, but it does not prove that every error reflects erasure of the original trace or that every confident memory is false; mechanisms and measurement conditions remain contested [6][12][15].

## Background

Scientific concern about suggestive questioning predates the modern term "misinformation effect," but Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer's 1974 experiments provided a compact demonstration of how language presented after an event can change a later report. Participants watched films of automobile accidents and answered questions containing different collision verbs. The wording changed speed estimates, and in a second experiment the verb manipulation changed later reports of broken glass even though no broken glass appeared in the film [1]. The work did not establish that all autobiographical memory is unstable. It demonstrated a narrower causal point: information embedded in a question can influence what a witness subsequently reports about a recently observed event [1].

Loftus, Miller, and Burns then formalized the post-event misinformation paradigm in 1978. Across five experiments and a pilot study, undergraduates saw an auto-pedestrian accident, received information that was consistent, misleading, or irrelevant, and then completed recognition tests. Misleading information reduced accuracy on yes-no and two-alternative forced-choice tests, and its influence varied with the timing of presentation [2]. This sequence became the field's basic three-stage design: witness an event, encounter post-event information, and complete a memory test. Researchers can compare misled items with control items, measure endorsement of the suggested detail, measure loss of access to the original detail, or use source tests that ask where each detail came from [2][5][10]. Those measures overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

The experiments challenged a recording-device model of episodic memory. The National Research Council's review of eyewitness identification describes perception as selective, working memory as limited and vulnerable to interference, long-term memory as plastic, and retrieval as a process that can omit, update, or add content [11]. The same flexibility that lets a person connect a present cue with earlier knowledge also creates an opportunity for later information to enter an event representation or to compete with it at retrieval. "Reconstructive" therefore means that a report is assembled from available information under current retrieval conditions. It does not mean that memory is arbitrary or that external events leave no durable traces [3][11].

Source-monitoring research supplied a framework for explaining why a familiar detail can be remembered while its origin is not. Johnson, Hashtroudi, and Lindsay argued that people attribute memories to sources by evaluating qualities such as perceptual detail, spatial and temporal context, emotional information, and evidence of reflective processing. These judgments use flexible criteria and can fail when two sources share content, when contextual detail is weak, or when retrieval is hurried [3]. A witness may therefore remember a red bag vividly but misattribute the detail to the witnessed event rather than to a later question, news account, or co-witness. The error can concern source without requiring the conclusion that all content from the original event was destroyed [3][6].

The domain boundary is important. The misinformation effect is a cognitive-memory phenomenon: it asks how later information changes recall of a prior episode. Propaganda, false news, and disinformation systems concern how messages are produced, distributed, and believed at population scale. Those systems can supply post-event information, but their institutional design belongs in communication rather than in the mechanism of episodic reconstruction. Legal admissibility rules, lineup doctrine, and remedies likewise belong in law. This topic uses eyewitness research as the strongest applied evidence while remaining focused on encoding, retrieval, source monitoring, report criteria, and confidence [5][11].

The field has also become more cautious about strong claims. A misinformation response can reflect several processes: weakened access to the original detail, integration of later information, competition between traces, source confusion, guessing under uncertainty, or strategic compliance with what a participant thinks the experimenter expects [5][6][15]. Test format matters because a binary choice may make the suggested answer especially available, whereas open recall, a multi-option test, and an explicit source judgment impose different demands. A contemporary replication by Wiechert and colleagues reproduced the effect online and still found it when a four-option test and confidence-based point betting reduced demand concerns, although the effect was smaller [15]. Their results confirm susceptibility while leaving the mechanism disputed.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors. The first is to treat memory as a faithful recording unless deliberate lying is proven. The second is to treat any inconsistency as evidence that a memory has been rewritten. The research supports neither position. It shows experimentally that specified forms of post-event information can cause measurable changes in later memory performance, with effect size and expression depending on the original encoding, the post-event source, repetition, delay, warning, test design, and characteristics of the rememberer [6][9][10][13]. A disciplined account asks which information entered at each stage and which measure was used before drawing a conclusion about what changed.

## Core Concepts

### The Three-Stage Misinformation Paradigm

The standard paradigm separates an original event from later information and the final test. In the first stage, participants observe a video, slide sequence, narrative, or live event containing critical details. In the second, they encounter a description, question, interview, social discussion, or co-witness account that contains accurate, neutral, or misleading versions of selected details. In the third, researchers test memory for the original event [2][5][10]. Random assignment or within-person control items lets researchers estimate how much performance changes because of the misleading information rather than because of ordinary forgetting alone.

The dependent measure determines what the result can establish. Original-detail accuracy asks whether the participant can recover what was present. Misinformation endorsement asks whether the suggested alternative is selected or reported. A source-monitoring test asks whether the participant assigns a detail to the event, the later account, both, or neither. Free recall measures what a person volunteers, while recognition tests require choices among supplied alternatives. A decline in original-detail accuracy can occur without explicit endorsement of the suggested item, and endorsement can reflect source error or response strategy as well as alteration of an event representation [2][3][6]. The author's synthesis is that a study should be described by its exact measure rather than reduced to the claim that misinformation "changed memory."

### Reconstruction Across Encoding, Retention, and Retrieval

Encoding begins with selection. Attention does not preserve every feature of an event, and details that are peripheral, brief, expected, or observed under divided attention can enter memory weakly or not at all [11]. Later information has more leverage when the original detail is poorly specified because the rememberer has less event-specific evidence with which to detect a discrepancy. This does not imply that a weak original trace is blank. It means the relative evidence for the original and later alternatives may be closer at retrieval [3][10][11].

Retention is not passive storage. New experiences create associations, activate related concepts, and can alter which retrieval routes are available. The National Research Council concluded that long-term memory remains plastic and that later sensory, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral events can contaminate or reshape retrieved content [11]. Loftus's review similarly describes misinformation research as an investigation into impairment after misleading information and the conditions under which people are susceptible or resistant [5]. These sources support updating and interference as broad principles; they do not establish one universal molecular process for every misinformation error.

Retrieval is cue-dependent and constructive. A question supplies categories, vocabulary, assumptions, and a decision rule. If a detail from a later narrative is fluent and fits the event, it may be retrieved alongside original details. The rememberer then evaluates content under a criterion shaped by instructions, confidence, expected specificity, and the available response options [3][6]. Each retelling can also become new post-event information for the next retrieval because the person may remember the prior report as well as the original event [8][11]. This feedback makes an early, uncontaminated account evidentially distinct from a later, repeated account.

Some research links retrieval to reconsolidation, in which reactivated memory becomes temporarily open to modification, but the misinformation literature does not permit every post-retrieval error to be assigned to reconsolidation. Chan, Thomas, and Bulevich found that an immediate cued-recall test before misinformation increased later suggestibility, a reversed testing effect they interpreted through enhanced learning of misinformation and vulnerability of initially recalled details [8]. Other conditions produce the familiar benefit of retrieval practice. The author's assessment is that retrieval can strengthen access to an event while also changing what is attended to and learned next; whether it protects or harms depends on the sequence, cue, warning, and later exposure [8].

### Source Monitoring and Discrepancy Detection

Source monitoring treats memory attribution as an inference. Perceptual detail may support an external event source; records of deliberate thought may support an imagined source; knowledge of when, where, and from whom a detail was acquired may separate an event from a later account. Because these attributes overlap and decay at different rates, a person can retain content while losing diagnostic source features [3]. Familiarity without source context is especially hazardous: repetition can make a detail easy to retrieve even when the person cannot identify where it was encountered [3][9].

Discrepancy detection is a first line of resistance. If the later statement conflicts sharply with a strong original memory, the witness may notice the conflict and tag the statement as post-event information. If the conflict is subtle, the original trace is weak, or the sources are similar, the discrepancy may go unnoticed. Warning studies indicate that directing attention to the possibility and source of misleading information can increase scrutiny, but this protection is conditional rather than automatic [6][7]. The practical mechanism is not simply "knowing that misinformation exists." It is preserving or reinstating enough source-specific evidence to reject the wrong origin.

Warnings can act at more than one stage. A prewarning given before post-event information can increase attention to source and discrepancy during encoding. A postwarning given before the final test cannot undo initial exposure, but it can encourage deliberate retrieval and source checking. Karanian and colleagues found that both prewarnings and postwarnings reduced misinformation errors; their imaging results linked warning to greater reinstatement of original visual information and less reinstatement of the misleading auditory source during retrieval [7]. This is evidence that warnings can change reconstructive retrieval, not proof that warnings recover every lost detail.

### Competing Mechanisms and the Meaning of an Error

One family of accounts proposes that later information integrates with or alters the original representation. A second proposes that the original and misleading traces coexist but compete, with the later or more accessible trace winning at test. A third emphasizes source misattribution: the suggested content is remembered, but its origin is assigned incorrectly. A fourth emphasizes social compliance or demand: the participant reports the suggestion without privately remembering it as witnessed [2][3][5][15]. These mechanisms can operate together, and no single test cleanly separates all of them.

The modified recognition test was developed to reduce a simple response-choice explanation by presenting the original item against a novel alternative rather than against the suggested item. Small misinformation effects under conservative tests and improvement after source warnings support more than pure compliance, while changes across test formats show that reporting conditions still matter [6]. Wiechert and colleagues' contemporary replication reached a similar graded conclusion: demand effects contributed to the observed effect but did not fully explain it, and an order-reversal experiment favored some memory-based accounts while leaving an alternative order explanation open [15]. The correct conclusion is mechanistic uncertainty constrained by reproducible behavioral effects.

### Moderators of Susceptibility

Original-memory quality is a central moderator. Stronger encoding supplies more event-specific evidence, improves discrepancy detection, and gives source monitoring more diagnostic content [3][10][11]. Delay can weaken access to original detail and increase reliance on later information, although delay also interacts with test and repeated retrieval. Stress, attention, age, and cognitive ability have been studied, but their effects are not reducible to one vulnerability score. Brassil, O'Mahony, and Greene's 2024 systematic review found only nine eligible adult studies covering 23 associations and judged the measures too heterogeneous for a single meta-analysis. Higher reasoning, perceptual, or memory abilities were generally associated with lower susceptibility, but no trait conferred immunity [10].

Repetition strengthens misinformation even when the number of distinct sources does not. O'Donnell and colleagues manipulated both variables in two experiments and supplemented them with meta-analyses. Repeating a misleading detail increased suggestibility, whereas presenting it through multiple sources did not add an independent effect under their conditions [9]. The result cautions against assuming that apparent consensus is always the operative memory mechanism. Repetition can increase retrieval fluency without preserving reliable source information [3][9].

Social transmission nevertheless matters. Gabbert, Memon, and Allan showed pairs of witnesses different versions of the same event and allowed some pairs to discuss it. Seventy-one percent of discussing witnesses later reported at least one item acquired from the partner's account [4]. That result combines cognitive and social processes: the co-witness can supply new content, credibility and conversational norms can affect reporting, and the later source can be confused with the event. It does not show that every agreement between witnesses is conformity, because co-witnesses can also exchange accurate information [4].

Culture and context may moderate reporting and endorsement, but generalization requires care. Anakwah and colleagues compared mock witnesses in Ghana and the United Kingdom. The groups did not differ in misinformation endorsement during free recall, while the Ghana sample endorsed more misleading central details in recognition; individual cultural orientation also related to some outcomes [13]. The study provides evidence that test format and cultural context can interact. Its two samples do not justify a universal claim about collectivistic or individualistic people.

### Confidence and Accuracy Are Conditional, Not Opposites

Confidence is a judgment about a memory response, not a direct readout of historical truth. Post-event information can change both content and the criteria used to express confidence. O'Donnell and colleagues found that misinformation undermined how diagnostic confidence was for critical items even while confidence tracked accuracy for neutral items [9]. Repetition, confirmatory feedback, and repeated questioning can make later confidence differ from the confidence attached to an initial, uncontaminated response [9][11].

It is also inaccurate to claim that eyewitness confidence is always unrelated to accuracy. Wixted and Wells reviewed identification research and concluded that an immediate confidence statement can be highly informative when it follows an initial, fair, uncontaminated test administered without influence. Under non-pristine conditions, even high confidence can be compromised [12]. The apparent contradiction dissolves when timing and procedure are specified. Confidence attached to a first response under controlled conditions and confidence measured after suggestion, repetition, or feedback are different evidential objects.

### Correction, Warning, and Active Scrutiny

Postwarnings are among the best studied corrective interventions. Blank and Launay meta-analyzed 25 studies containing 155 effect sizes and found that postwarnings reduced the misinformation effect to less than half its unwarned size on average. More informative "enlightenment" procedures were especially effective, and the average benefit arose from better performance on misled items with little cost to control performance [6]. Reduction is not elimination, and averages conceal variation by warning, test, and study design.

Newer experiments refine that result. Karanian and colleagues found benefits from both prewarnings and postwarnings and tied those benefits to source-specific reinstatement [7]. Torrance and colleagues found that prewarnings, postwarnings, and repeated warnings improved memory accuracy for misinformation-related questions, while repeated warnings could lower confidence in accurate, consistent details, a possible "tainted truth" cost [14]. Active scrutiny is therefore useful when it directs attention to provenance and discrepancy, but indiscriminate skepticism can reduce confidence in correct information as well as false information [6][14].

## Evidence

### Foundational Experiments Established a Causal Effect

Loftus and Palmer's first experiment manipulated one verb in a question about a filmed collision. The verbs produced systematically different speed estimates. In the second experiment, participants later answered whether they had seen broken glass; those who had received the stronger collision wording reported broken glass more often, although the film contained none [1]. Randomly varying the question while holding the film constant supports a causal influence of post-event wording on later report. The study does not by itself distinguish altered visual memory from inference, response bias, or source confusion.

Loftus, Miller, and Burns expanded the design across five experiments and a pilot study. Participants saw an accident, encountered consistent, misleading, or irrelevant information, and completed recognition tests. Misleading information reduced correct responding on yes-no and two-alternative forced-choice measures. The influence was larger when misleading information appeared near the final test, and the authors reported results inconsistent with a simple demand-characteristics explanation [2]. This program established that the phenomenon was not limited to speed estimation or one question wording.

### Social and Retrieval Conditions Can Amplify Contamination

Gabbert, Memon, and Allan used a co-witness design in which each person in a pair saw a different version of the same simulated crime. After discussion, 71 percent of witnesses reported at least one detail that they had obtained from the partner rather than from their own video [4]. The method demonstrates a realistic transmission route: information can move between witnesses without an investigator inserting it. Because the outcome is a report after social interaction, cognitive source error and social conformity are both plausible contributors.

Chan, Thomas, and Bulevich tested the expectation that recalling an event before misinformation should protect it. Immediate cued recall instead increased later suggestibility in younger and older adults. Their analyses supported two contributors: prior testing increased learning of the misinformation, and initially recalled details became especially vulnerable to later interference [8]. The result is a boundary condition on the general testing effect. Retrieval usually benefits retention, but retrieval followed by misleading input can alter what is learned and later selected.

O'Donnell and colleagues separated repetition from source variability. In two experiments, repeating misinformation increased suggestibility, while delivering it through multiple visibly or textually distinct witnesses did not produce an additional source-variability effect. Their meta-analysis of comparable studies supported a moderate repetition effect and essentially no independent effect of source variability in the smaller source analysis [9]. This evidence favors retrieval fluency or repeated encoding over a simple "more people said it" mechanism under the tested conditions.

### Reviews Show Both Robustness and Heterogeneity

Loftus's 2005 review summarized three decades of research and defined the effect as impairment in memory after exposure to misleading information. The review documented susceptibility and resistance across many conditions while emphasizing unresolved questions about the fate of original information [5]. Blank and Launay's later meta-analysis focused on one intervention rather than the overall effect. Across 25 postwarning studies and 155 effect sizes, warnings reduced the effect substantially but did not erase it [6]. Together, the reviews support a robust phenomenon with modifiable expression, not a fixed error rate.

Brassil, O'Mahony, and Greene's systematic review illustrates why broad claims about individual vulnerability remain premature. From 6,395 retrieved records, their eligibility process produced a small set of adult studies with heterogeneous paradigms and measures. Higher general reasoning, perceptual, and memory abilities usually correlated with less susceptibility, but associations were sometimes weak or unreplicated, and no cognitive profile provided immunity [10]. The evidence supports variation among people while warning against a diagnostic label based on one trait.

Anakwah and colleagues added a cross-cultural comparison using Ghanaian and United Kingdom samples. Both groups encountered misleading post-event information. The samples did not differ in misinformation endorsement during free recall, but the Ghana sample endorsed more misleading central items on the recognition test, and some individual cultural-orientation measures related to performance [13]. The mixed pattern is important: culture did not produce one uniform susceptibility effect across measures.

### Contemporary Studies Replicate the Effect and Narrow Mechanisms

Wiechert and colleagues conducted a contemporary online replication of the 1978 traffic-sign paradigm. Participants watched a simulated accident and later encountered co-witness statements containing consistent, misleading, or no sign information. Misled participants identified the original sign less often than controls. The effect remained with a four-option test and confidence-based point betting, although it became smaller, indicating that demand characteristics contribute but are not sufficient [15]. Their order-reversal experiment did not produce the same effect and therefore constrained, but did not settle, competing trace accounts.

Karanian and colleagues combined behavioral experiments with functional imaging. Both prewarnings and postwarnings reduced errors for misleading trials. During retrieval, warning was associated with stronger reinstatement of visual information from the witnessed event and weaker reinstatement of auditory information from the later narrative; the degree of reinstatement related to performance [7]. The study supplies convergent behavioral and neural evidence for source-selective retrieval, while its laboratory task does not establish the size of warning benefits in field investigations.

Torrance and colleagues tested warning timing and frequency in two experiments. Prewarnings and postwarnings both reduced the negative impact of misinformation. Repeated warnings also improved accuracy and calibration for misinformation-related items, but could lower confidence for accurate details that had appeared consistently in both event and narrative [14]. The cost matters because a warning can change a witness's reporting criterion as well as memory search.

### Official Synthesis Places the Effect Within the Whole Memory System

The National Research Council reviewed basic and applied eyewitness science and concluded that memory can be compromised at encoding, storage, and retrieval. Its account emphasizes limited attention, interference, plasticity, source-memory failure, and contamination by the procedures used to elicit recall [11]. This broader evidence prevents the misinformation effect from being isolated as a laboratory trick. Post-event suggestion operates on a memory system already constrained by what was perceived, attended, and retained.

At the same time, Wixted and Wells's review shows why "memory is reconstructive" cannot justify dismissing every confident witness. Under an initial, fair, uncontaminated identification procedure with an immediate confidence statement, adult confidence can be strongly related to accuracy. That diagnostic relation weakens under unfair, influenced, delayed, or repeated conditions [12]. Evidence evaluation must therefore preserve procedural history rather than apply a global rule that confidence either proves or never informs accuracy.

The cumulative finding is graded. Controlled experiments, reviews, a modern replication, social-transmission studies, and intervention studies all show that later information can change event reports [1][2][4][5][6][7][8][9][15]. The evidence is weaker for any claim that one mechanism always causes the change, that all original information is permanently overwritten, or that one demographic or cognitive profile predicts an individual's error [3][10][13][15].

## Implications

### Preserve the Earliest Account and Its Conditions

For investigators, auditors, journalists, and researchers, the first implication is temporal: obtain and preserve an account before the witness encounters avoidable post-event information. The National Research Council recommends procedures that reduce contamination and document the witness's own responses and confidence at the time they are given [11]. A later statement may still contain useful information, but it should not be treated as an interchangeable copy of the first. The record should identify when the account was taken, who was present, which prompts were used, what materials the witness had seen, and whether other witnesses had been consulted.

Prompt design should minimize new content. Open requests such as "Describe what you remember" provide fewer suggested details than questions that presuppose an object, action, or identity. Follow-up questions may be necessary, but the exact wording and sequence should be recorded because the prompt is part of the evidence-generating process [1][2][11]. The goal is not to avoid all questions. It is to distinguish information produced by the witness from information introduced by the interviewer.

Witnesses should be separated before substantive discussion when independent reports matter. Gabbert and colleagues show that co-witness conversation can transfer details into later reports [4]. Separation does not imply that witnesses are untrustworthy, and agreement after contact does not prove contamination. It preserves the independence needed to use agreement as corroboration. If discussion already occurred, that exposure should be documented rather than hidden.

### Treat Repetition as Exposure, Not Independent Confirmation

Repeated claims, questions, or retellings can increase familiarity and suggestibility [3][9]. The author's synthesis is that evidence systems should count unique, independent sources rather than appearances. Three reports that repeat one witness's mistaken description are one informational lineage, not three independent observations. Provenance records should identify who first supplied a detail and how later recipients encountered it.

The same rule applies to organizations. Incident reviews, medical handoffs, quality investigations, and workplace complaints often contain successive summaries. A clean record should separate direct observation, contemporaneous notes, later inference, and information learned from others. This does not make later synthesis worthless. It prevents fluency created by repetition from being mistaken for independent support [3][9].

### Use Warnings to Improve Source Discrimination

Warnings work best as instructions for a concrete retrieval operation: some later information may be inaccurate; answer from the original event; consider where each detail came from; use "do not know" when source evidence is insufficient. Blank and Launay's meta-analysis and Karanian and colleagues' experiments show that warnings can reduce misinformation errors, especially when they increase understanding or source-specific retrieval [6][7]. A vague statement that "memory is unreliable" supplies no method and may simply reduce confidence.

Timing changes the possible mechanism. A prewarning can promote scrutiny while the post-event account is encoded. A postwarning can promote source monitoring at the final test. Repeated warnings can recruit both operations, but Torrance and colleagues show a possible cost: witnesses may become underconfident about accurate information that also appeared in the questioned source [14]. The author's synthesis is that warnings should be specific enough to support provenance checks but not framed as an instruction to distrust everything.

Correction should also avoid overclaiming. Telling a person that one source was unreliable can reduce later endorsement, but it does not establish that the original detail has been fully restored [6][7]. A corrected report should distinguish three outcomes: the person accurately retrieves the original detail, rejects the misleading detail without recovering the original, or remains uncertain. Combining all three as "correction succeeded" conceals different memory states.

### Interpret Confidence With the Procedural History

A confidence statement should be timestamped and tied to the exact response and procedure. High confidence expressed immediately after a fair, uncontaminated initial test can be informative, whereas confidence after repeated interviews, suggestion, feedback, or source mixing has a different meaning [12]. The fact that confidence can be contaminated does not make it useless. The fact that initial confidence can be diagnostic does not make later confidence self-validating.

For any important memory claim, ask four questions. First, was this the initial test of the detail? Second, were the prompts neutral and the alternatives fair? Third, had the person encountered the same detail from other sources? Fourth, was confidence recorded before feedback? These questions convert a vague debate about whether confidence "tracks truth" into a test of the conditions under which the confidence judgment was produced [9][11][12].

### Build Corroboration Outside the Memory Loop

Because a memory report can be altered by later information, corroboration should come from evidence not generated by the same chain. Contemporaneous video, documents, device records, physical traces, and genuinely independent witnesses provide different error structures. Two witnesses who discussed the event are not fully independent, and a document written from one witness's account is not external confirmation of that account [4][11].

The author's synthesis is that corroboration quality depends on causal independence, not just agreement. This principle prevents a misinformation loop in which a first report shapes a news story, the story shapes later witness memories, and those later memories are then cited as confirmation of the first report. Mapping provenance exposes the loop without presuming which report is correct.

### Apply the Finding Beyond Eyewitnesses Without Erasing Boundaries

Everyday memory is exposed to captions, photographs, messages, social accounts, and repeated narratives after an event. The laboratory mechanism therefore has plausible relevance to family recollection, organizational history, journalism, and online communication [5][11]. Yet the effect should not be exported without specifying the original event, the misleading content, the delay, and the test. Believing a false public claim that one never witnessed is not the same outcome as misremembering a detail of a personally observed episode.

This boundary separates the present topic from the broader psychology of misinformation belief. Repetition can affect both event memory and judged truth, but the dependent variables and source histories differ. A topic about propaganda asks who created and distributed a message and why audiences believe or share it. A misinformation-effect analysis asks whether exposure changed recall of a prior event. Confusing the two encourages unsupported claims that every false belief is a false memory or that every memory discrepancy results from organized deception.

### Design Better Research and Reviews

Researchers should report original-detail accuracy, misinformation endorsement, source attribution, confidence, and "do not know" responses separately when the design permits. They should state whether tests were open recall, recognition, forced choice, or source judgments. They should also disclose the interval between stages, whether participants were tested before misinformation, the number and credibility of sources, and the exact warning [2][6][8][9][15]. These features are not procedural trivia; they define which cognitive process a result can support.

Samples and moderators require the same precision. Brassil and colleagues found heterogeneous measures and a small eligible evidence base for cognitive abilities [10]. Anakwah and colleagues found a cultural difference on recognition but not free recall [13]. The author's assessment is that future synthesis should not collapse across test formats or treat one sample contrast as a stable trait. Preregistered replications, open materials, and designs that compare rival mechanisms can distinguish robust performance changes from the story attached to them.

### Use a Four-Stage Evaluation Framework

The author's synthesis from the evidence is a four-stage audit for consequential event memories [1][2][3][6][11]. At encoding, record what could reasonably have been seen or heard, under what attention, duration, distance, stress, and visibility. During retention, map every plausible exposure to questions, co-witnesses, media, documents, and repeated claims. At retrieval, record prompts, options, warnings, source instructions, confidence, and whether the response was volunteered or selected. At evaluation, compare the report with independent evidence and state whether an inconsistency is best described as ordinary forgetting, misinformation endorsement, source uncertainty, changed reporting criterion, or unresolved discrepancy.

This framework does not diagnose the hidden state of one person's memory with certainty. It prevents the worst inferential errors: assuming a report is a recording, assuming every inconsistency proves fabrication, treating repeated exposure as independent confirmation, and treating a warning as complete repair. The central practical lesson is procedural. Memory evidence becomes more interpretable when its information history is preserved.
