# Decision Fatigue and Ego Depletion -- Fatigue Is Real, but a Universal Depletable Resource Is Not Established

Decision fatigue and ego depletion describe plausible changes after sustained choice or self-control, but they do not identify one proven, domain-general resource that predictably runs down with use. The post-replication evidence is conditional: effort and subjective fatigue can change, several large protocols produced near-zero or small performance effects, one intensive multisample protocol produced a moderate inhibition aftereffect, and field decisions sometimes vary with time or sequence, yet the cause and generality of these patterns remain contested [5][8][9][10][17][18][27]. Practical claims should therefore separate experienced fatigue, measured performance, and causal mechanism rather than treating the popular willpower-battery metaphor as established fact.

## Background

The modern debate began with the strength model of self-control. Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven, and Tice proposed that choice, active response, emotion regulation, and other acts of volition draw on a common limited resource. Across four experiments, an initial act such as resisting tempting food, making an attitude-relevant choice, or suppressing emotion was followed by less persistence, poorer anagram performance, or greater passivity on a different task [1]. The term "ego depletion" named the temporary state inferred from this sequential pattern: Task 1 was assumed to consume strength, and lower performance on Task 2 was interpreted as evidence that less strength remained [1]. The theory was attractive because one simple mechanism appeared to connect many failures of self-control.

Decision fatigue entered this framework as a related claim about choice. Vohs and colleagues compared participants who made repeated choices with participants who considered the same options without choosing. The four core laboratory study series reported less physical stamina, reduced persistence, more procrastination, and lower quality or quantity of arithmetic work after choice; later experiments separately tested deliberation versus choosing or implementation and prompt action versus passivity [2]. A field study related shoppers' reported amount of prior choosing to subsequent task performance [2]. The authors argued that choosing and self-control used the same limited resource [2]. Later conceptual work defined decision fatigue more broadly as impaired decision-making or behavioral regulation following repeated decisions, while also noting that the literature had not clearly established its consequences or measurement boundaries [13].

The two concepts overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Ego depletion is a hypothesized aftereffect of prior self-control and is usually tested with a sequential-task experiment. Decision fatigue refers to changes after repeated or demanding decisions and is often inferred from time of day, decision order, default use, deferral, or declining decision quality [2][5][13][17]. Mental fatigue is distinct: Pignatiello and colleagues describe it as impaired cognitive processing, whereas their decision-fatigue concept also includes impaired behavioral and emotional regulation; prolonged attention or other cognitive work can produce fatigue without repeated choices [13][19]. Calling all three states "depletion" can hide differences in antecedent task, subjective experience, measured outcome, and proposed mechanism.

The early literature expanded quickly. A 2010 meta-analysis synthesized 83 studies and estimated an average effect of d = 0.62, while also reporting changes in effort, perceived difficulty, negative affect, subjective fatigue, and other proposed correlates [3]. This medium-to-large estimate encouraged applications far beyond the original laboratory tasks. It also made the model unusually consequential: if many kinds of self-control consumed one common stock, then prior effort could be used to predict eating, persistence, moral conduct, spending, work, and judgment through the same mechanism.

The replication crisis changed the evidentiary standard. Carter and colleagues searched for published and unpublished tests, used narrower inclusion rules, examined small-study effects, and concluded that the common laboratory methods provided very little evidence for a depletion effect [4]. A 23-laboratory preregistered replication with 2,141 participants then estimated d = 0.04 with a 95% confidence interval from -0.07 to 0.15, consistent with no effect for the standardized protocol that was tested [5]. These results did not logically prove that no depletion effect can occur under any procedure. They did show that the original literature and its broad resource interpretation could not be treated as settled.

The dispute exposed a conceptual problem in addition to a replication problem. Lurquin and Miyake argued that many tasks used to induce or measure depletion lacked independent validation as measures of self-control. They also noted that the strength model had not specified how the first and second tasks consume the putative resource, when the remainder becomes low enough to impair the second task, or whether particular task pairs draw on the same common process [6]. Kurzban and colleagues separately emphasized that resource accounts need independent measures or discriminating predictions rather than post hoc appeals to reserves [12]. If a null result can always be explained by an insufficiently depleting first task, an insensitive second task, unusual motivation, or an unobserved reserve, the theory becomes difficult to falsify [6][12]. Better sample sizes do not by themselves solve an underspecified construct.

Later evidence remained mixed rather than returning to the original consensus. Dang's updated meta-analysis found an overall random-effects estimate of g = 0.38 and a trim-and-fill estimate of g = 0.24, but the evidence was heterogeneous and varied by task [7]. Friese and colleagues reviewed arguments from advocates and skeptics and concluded that the available evidence was inconclusive despite hundreds of published studies [8]. Two large projects in 2021 reached different but compatible limits: one found d = 0.10 across 12 laboratories, while another tested 3,531 participants across 36 laboratories but, after 1,068 preregistered exclusions, found a nonsignificant confirmatory estimate of d = 0.06 in 2,461 participants and stronger Bayesian support for the null than for its informed alternative [9][10]. Dang and colleagues later tested a 30-40 minute antisaccade manipulation across 14 samples and 2,078 participants. No-Go errors were higher after depletion than after long and short controls, d = 0.35 and d = 0.31, with I-squared equal to zero, while Go errors and Go reaction time did not differ [27]. The pooled program mixed preregistered and nonpreregistered stages, and the task combined inhibitory and visual-processing demands [27]. Together, these estimates reject a stable domain-general d = 0.62 and replace it with strong protocol and outcome dependence.

Field evidence on decision fatigue developed along a parallel track. Studies reported sequence or time-of-day patterns in judicial and medical decisions, but these designs generally used time, order, or proximity to a break as a proxy for fatigue rather than measuring the proposed state directly [14][16]. Simulation using the reported decision-time distributions showed that duration-dependent selective dropout, rational stopping behavior, censoring, and autocorrelation could account for large parts, though not every aspect, of one famous judicial pattern without requiring mental depletion; the original case-level data were unavailable for reanalysis [15]. Recent reviews of clinical decision fatigue found inconsistent definitions, heterogeneous methods, and a minority or plurality rather than a uniform majority of quantitative results supporting the hypothesis [17]. A 2025 Registered Report using high-resolution medical-triage decisions found Bayes factors greater than 22 in favor of no fatigue-related difference in all four confirmatory tests [18].

The history therefore supports a disciplined reframing. Repeated choice and control can feel effortful, and subjective fatigue is a real reportable state [3][10][12]. Performance aftereffects range from null to moderate across tasks and outcomes [5][9][10][27]. What did not survive is the strong inference that such feelings or later errors demonstrate consumption of a single common resource. The live scientific questions concern which tasks produce which aftereffects, how large they are, whether they generalize, what role motivation and attention play, and when field sequence patterns have causal rather than procedural explanations [6][8][10][17][18][27].

## Core Concepts

### Four Distinct Claims Must Be Separated

The phrase "decision fatigue" often combines at least four claims. First, repeated decisions may increase subjective tiredness or effort. Second, later behavior may change, for example through deferral, default selection, impulsivity, slower work, or lower persistence. Third, those behavioral changes may reflect reduced decision quality rather than an adaptive change in strategy. Fourth, a common resource may have been consumed and caused the change. Evidence for one claim is not automatically evidence for the next [6][13][17].

Subjective fatigue is an experience. It can be measured with self-report and can change even when objective performance remains stable. Performance is task-specific: speed, accuracy, persistence, inhibition, risk preference, and default choice are different outcomes. Decision quality is normative and requires a criterion for what counts as better. Mechanism is causal and requires evidence distinguishing resource loss from motivation, attention, learning, strategy, opportunity cost, boredom, general time-on-task, and physical fatigue [11][12][17]. A sound study states which level it tests rather than moving silently between them.

The author's synthesis is that these levels form a ladder of increasing inference. A person who reports fatigue establishes an experienced state, not a depleted resource. A correlation between time and default choice establishes a sequence pattern, not necessarily deteriorating quality. An experiment in which one task changes another establishes an aftereffect under that procedure, not necessarily a domain-general mechanism. A resource theory requires an independently measurable resource or a sufficiently precise pattern of predictions that competing models do not make [6][12].

### The Sequential-Task Paradigm

The canonical ego-depletion experiment uses two tasks. Participants in the depletion condition perform an initial activity intended to require self-control, while controls perform an easier or noncontrol version. Both groups then perform a second task intended to require self-control. Worse performance by the depletion group on Task 2 is the depletion effect [1][5]. Common first tasks have included emotion suppression, food temptation, thought suppression, crossing out letters under changing rules, or Stroop interference; second tasks have included persistence, anagrams, handgrip endurance, Stroop performance, antisaccades, and other inhibition or control measures [1][5][7][9].

This design is simple, but its interpretation is not. Task 1 may differ between groups in difficulty, boredom, frustration, expectancy, demand characteristics, practice, or perceived purpose as well as self-control. Task 2 may contain measurement noise or may not depend on the same process as Task 1. If the resource is not directly observed, the causal chain is inferred from the between-group difference. Independent validation is therefore essential: the manipulation and outcome tasks should measure the specified self-control operation, the task pair should have evidence of a shared process, and Task 1 demand should be quantified with objective performance measures where possible [6].

Task heterogeneity creates another problem. If many combinations of manipulations and outcomes are treated as interchangeable tests of one effect, genuine differences between tasks can inflate heterogeneity and invite post hoc classification. Dang's 2018 analysis found that estimates differed across manipulation types. The attention-video estimate fell from g = 0.21 to an adjusted g = 0.13 with a 95% confidence interval from -0.02 to 0.28 after trim-and-fill, while working-memory and multiple-depletion estimates were already nonsignificant before correction [7]. A task taxonomy specified after results are known cannot provide a clean confirmatory test. The appropriate unit of evidence is the preregistered task-and-outcome combination, not an abstract label applied after the fact.

### The Strength or Resource Model

The strength model states that acts of self-control draw on a common limited capacity and that prior exertion temporarily reduces capacity for later control [1][3]. Its central appeal is domain generality: resisting food could impair puzzle persistence because both require the same underlying stock. The model also uses a muscle analogy, in which exertion produces temporary fatigue but repeated training may improve capacity over longer periods [3][12].

The principal unresolved question is what "resource" denotes. A literal metabolic account requires a physiological substrate and quantitative relation between use and performance. A functional account can treat the resource as an abstract capacity, but it still needs rules for measuring availability, consumption, recovery, and output. Without such rules, motivation can be invoked to release a reserve, positive affect can replenish it, and a null result can be classified as insufficient exertion without independent evidence [6][12]. This flexibility weakens the model's discriminatory power.

The replication record does not support a robust large effect across ordinary sequential-task procedures [4][5][10]. It also does not exclude every resource-like limit. Executive control, working memory, sustained attention, and response selection impose real simultaneous-processing constraints, but their existence does not validate one common self-control reservoir [11][12]. A mechanism can be capacity-limited without being depleted by prior use, and performance can decline without capacity loss. The model must therefore compete with alternatives at the level of predictions, not metaphors.

### Motivation-and-Attention Process Models

Inzlicht and Schmeichel proposed that prior control changes what people want to do and what they attend to. After effortful control, motivation may shift away from obligations and toward gratification or leisure, while attention becomes less oriented to control-relevant cues [11]. Lower Task 2 performance can then occur because allocation changes rather than because an inner stock is exhausted. The model predicts sensitivity to incentives, task meaning, affect, and attentional priority [11].

This process account helps explain why people sometimes perform adequately after prior exertion when stakes or incentives change. It also explains why fatigue can be accompanied by a desire to disengage rather than inability. However, motivation is not a universal escape from measurement. It needs operational definitions, independent measures, and temporal predictions just as a resource does. Direct mediation evidence was sparse when the model was proposed, and Inzlicht and Schmeichel recommended measuring and manipulating motivation and attention with methods beyond a simple retrospective self-report [11].

The distinction between capacity and allocation is central. Capacity asks what a person could do under sufficiently strong reasons and appropriate conditions. Allocation asks how much control the person deploys to the current task. Poorer performance after sustained work can arise because capacity fell, because the expected value of effort fell, because attractive alternatives gained priority, or because the control policy changed. Observed disengagement alone cannot identify which route occurred [11][12].

### Opportunity-Cost and Effort Models

Kurzban and colleagues modeled mental effort as the experienced output of opportunity-cost computations. Executive processes can support only a limited set of simultaneous activities, so allocating them to one task prevents their use elsewhere. As the relative value of alternatives rises, effort feels more aversive and continued allocation becomes less attractive [12]. On this account, effort is not a fuel that is consumed. It is a signal about competing uses of scarce processing capacity.

The opportunity-cost model makes fatigue sensitive to context. The same task should feel more effortful when attractive alternatives are available, when rewards for the current task decline, or when time spent on the task increases the value of switching. It also permits rapid recovery when incentives or tasks change because no stock must be replenished [12]. The model does not imply that all fatigue is imaginary. Subjective effort can be functional even when no consumable resource is involved: it can regulate allocation and prevent one goal from monopolizing control.

Evans, Boggero, and Segerstrom compared self-regulatory fatigue with physical fatigue and argued for an integrative view. Physical fatigue itself is not explained by substrate exhaustion alone; performance reflects physiological state, protective regulation, expectations, motivation, and context. They proposed that self-regulatory fatigue may likewise integrate multiple inputs and occur while some capacity remains [19]. This analogy is more precise than a battery metaphor because it allows real constraints and strategic regulation to coexist.

### Beliefs, Expectancies, and Learned Policies

Job, Dweck, and Walton reported that participants who viewed willpower as nonlimited did not show the same postexertion decline as participants who viewed it as easily depleted, and experimental manipulation of those beliefs changed the pattern [20]. The historical finding is compatible with expectancy, motivation, allocation, or learned-policy accounts, but its specific moderation claim is not robustly established. A preregistered replication with 187 participants found neither an overall depletion effect nor the predicted moderation [22]. Miyake and Carruth reviewed 13 individual-difference studies: five reported some moderation evidence, four of those were ambiguous, eight found no moderation, and only one produced an unambiguous result in the predicted direction [23]. They also noted that Job and colleagues' original Study 1 appears to have treated repeated trials as independent observations [23].

Belief effects do not establish that fatigue is merely imagined. A belief can influence how strongly a real signal changes behavior, how effort is interpreted, or when a person decides to disengage. It can also interact with task demands and social expectations. Broader correlational research links self-control beliefs with outcomes across several domains, but a 2024 review judged causal experiments limited and mixed and the direction of association unresolved [26]. Exploratory moderator analyses in the 2021 multisite test found no moderation by willpower beliefs, trait self-control, or action orientation; only reported fatigue moderated performance [10]. The 2025 intensive antisaccade program likewise found no moderation by lay willpower theory in the five samples that measured it [27]. The defensible inference is that beliefs may matter, but the Job-style laboratory moderation is not a reliable boundary condition and positive thinking does not abolish limits.

### Decision Fatigue as a Field Construct

In field research, decision fatigue is commonly operationalized through decision order, time within a shift, cumulative cases, or position before versus after a break [14][16][17][18]. These proxies are practical, but they are not direct measures of fatigue. Time also changes hunger, circadian state, workload, queue composition, time pressure, learning, patient behavior, staffing, and selection into which cases remain. Decision order can correlate with case difficulty or duration. Breaks can be chosen in response to the cases already completed or anticipated next [15][17].

The outcome also needs interpretation. More defaults may indicate avoidance, but a default can be safer or more appropriate. Faster decisions may reflect degraded scrutiny or improved learning. More conservative choices may reflect fatigue, case mix, or rational response to diminishing time. A quality claim needs external criteria such as guideline adherence, diagnostic accuracy, patient outcome, or independently rated appropriateness [17][18]. Without such a criterion, a temporal change is only a change.

The famous parole-board study illustrates the identification problem. Danziger, Levav, and Avnaim-Pesso analyzed 1,112 rulings and found that favorable decisions declined within sessions and rose after breaks. They interpreted the pattern cautiously through mental depletion while acknowledging that they did not measure judges' mental resources [14]. Gloeckner later showed through simulation that longer favorable rulings, stopping decisions, selective dropout, censoring, and autocorrelation could generate large parts of a similar pattern for a rational judge without depletion [15]. The original case-level data were unavailable, and the simulation did not reproduce every aspect [15]. The critique did not prove that judges never tire; it showed that the observed curve did not uniquely identify fatigue.

### Replication, Effect Size, and Heterogeneity

A claim can fail in several ways. The average effect may be near zero, a small average may conceal reliable effects for some tasks, or heterogeneous estimates may reflect noise, bias, and genuine moderation together. Publication bias can inflate an average when small significant studies are more likely to appear than small null studies. Flexible exclusion, task selection, and analysis can produce additional researcher degrees of freedom [4][6][8].

The post-crisis record therefore needs more than a vote count. Hagger's 2016 project tested one standardized protocol and found d = 0.04 [5]. Dang's 2021 project found d = 0.10 for both error rate and reaction time. In a nonpreregistered robustness analysis excluding 340 chance-level performers, error rate remained d = 0.10 and reaction time increased to d = 0.16 [9]. Vohs and colleagues tested 3,531 participants with two paradigmatic protocols; after 1,068 preregistered exclusions, the confirmatory analysis used 2,461 participants and found d = 0.06, while the no-exclusion exploratory analysis found d = 0.08 [10]. Dang's intensive 2025 protocol found No-Go error effects of d = 0.35 and d = 0.31 against long and short controls, while Go errors and Go reaction time were null [27]. These results are not contradictory if effects are task-, outcome-, and analysis-dependent; they are contradictory to treating one large universal effect as established.

The author's synthesis is that heterogeneity is now part of the phenomenon to be explained. A useful theory should predict which first tasks change which later outcomes, through which measured mediator, over what interval, and under which incentives. It should also predict null effects. Until those predictions are prospectively tested, an effect in one paradigm, whether small or moderate, should not be generalized to every decision context, and a null result should not be generalized to prove that sustained cognitive work has no consequences.

## Evidence

### Original Laboratory Findings Established a Research Program

Baumeister and colleagues' 1998 paper reported four experiments built around cross-domain aftereffects. Resisting chocolates while eating radishes preceded shorter persistence on unsolvable puzzles; making an attitude-relevant choice preceded reduced persistence; suppressing emotion preceded worse solvable-anagram performance; and a high-regulation task preceded greater passive responding [1]. The design's key contribution was not simply that hard work felt tiring. It showed how unrelated tasks could be arranged to test a common-resource hypothesis [1].

Vohs and colleagues extended the logic to decision-making. Across the four core laboratory study series, participants made choices about consumer goods or courses or engaged with comparable materials without choosing. Those assigned to choose later showed less physical stamina, reduced persistence, more procrastination, or lower quality and quantity of arithmetic calculations [2]. Later experiments separately distinguished choosing from deliberating or implementing and measured prompt action versus passivity. A field study associated shoppers' reported prior choosing with subsequent performance [2]. The set supplied the empirical foundation for calling repeated choice depleting [2].

These studies were influential but were not definitive. Small samples, varied outcomes, and an unobserved mediator left room for alternative explanations. The designs established that sequential-task effects were testable; they did not measure a resource or establish that every observed aftereffect had the same cause [6][12]. Their historical importance and their evidentiary limits can both be true.

### Meta-Analysis First Strengthened and Then Destabilized the Claim

Hagger and colleagues' 2010 meta-analysis combined 83 studies and reported d = 0.62, with additional effects on effort, difficulty, negative affect, fatigue, and proposed moderators [3][6]. This estimate made the effect appear both substantial and broadly reproducible. The analysis included varied manipulation and outcome tasks, however, and later critics argued that published-only evidence, broad inclusion, and small-study effects could overstate the mean [4][7].

Carter and colleagues assembled published and unpublished experiments, narrowed the set of tasks considered valid tests, and applied methods designed to detect or correct small-study effects. Their abstract conclusion was that common laboratory methods provided very little evidence that the depletion effect was real and strongly challenged a limited-resource interpretation [4]. The exact corrected estimate depended on statistical method, and subsequent authors disputed whether PET-PEESE was appropriate under the observed heterogeneity [7][8]. Thus, the correction debate did not produce one uncontested number.

Dang's updated meta-analysis makes the disagreement concrete. Across 142 included effect sizes spanning its eligible depletion-task categories, the random-effects estimate was g = 0.38 with substantial heterogeneity; trim-and-fill reduced it to g = 0.24. A more restricted post hoc set of tasks judged reliable produced g = 0.42. The attention-video estimate became nonsignificant after trim-and-fill, while working-memory and multiple-depletion estimates were already nonsignificant before correction [7]. These estimates support neither the original stable d = 0.62 nor a simple claim that every estimate equals zero. They show that inclusion criteria, task validity, heterogeneity, and bias correction govern the answer.

### Large Preregistered and Multisample Tests Bound Effects by Protocol

The 2016 Registered Replication Report coordinated 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants around a standardized sequential-task protocol. The pooled estimate was d = 0.04, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.07 to 0.15 [5]. The result was consistent with no effect for that protocol and excluded the historical meta-analytic estimate as a plausible description of the tested procedure. Critics argued that the computerized letter-e response-inhibition task may not have induced sufficient fatigue or self-control, which returned the debate to manipulation validity [6][8].

Dang and colleagues later selected a Stroop-to-antisaccade sequence based on a prior preregistered study. Across 12 laboratories and 1,775 participants, both error rate and reaction time produced small significant full-sample effects of d = 0.10 [9]. In a nonpreregistered analysis excluding 340 chance-level performers, error rate remained d = 0.10 and reaction time increased to d = 0.16 [9]. This project is evidence that a small sequential aftereffect can be detected under some procedures. It does not recover the original large effect or identify a common consumable resource.

Vohs and colleagues used a paradigmatic replication strategy in 36 laboratories and recruited 3,531 participants. Each laboratory implemented one of two manipulation-outcome procedures. Preregistered exclusions removed 1,068 participants, leaving 2,461 in the confirmatory performance analysis; its estimate was d = 0.06 and nonsignificant, and an informed-prior Bayesian analysis made the data about 4.4 times more likely under the null than under the specified alternative [10]. Exploratory analysis without the exclusions yielded d = 0.08 and a Bayesian comparison about equally compatible with null and alternative models [10]. Participants in the depletion conditions reported more fatigue, but confirmatory performance evidence did not establish the predicted depletion effect [10].

Dang and colleagues' 2025 multisample program tested a substantially more intensive task. Participants completed 30-40 minutes of antisaccades or a duration-matched prosaccade control, followed by a Go-NoGo outcome task. Across 14 samples and 2,078 participants, No-Go errors were higher after depletion than after the long control, d = 0.35 with a 95% confidence interval from 0.25 to 0.45, and after the short control, d = 0.31 with a 95% confidence interval from 0.18 to 0.44; I-squared was zero in both comparisons [27]. Go errors and Go reaction time did not differ. The initial study and five direct replications were preregistered, but short-control extensions and two online samples were exploratory or not preregistered [27]. The authors also acknowledged that antisaccade inhibition was confounded with vector-inversion and visual-processing demands [27]. The result supplies a reproducible task-specific aftereffect, not a direct measurement of a common resource.

Taken together, the four multisite programs place tested effects from near zero to moderate [5][9][10][27]. They demonstrate why method details matter: manipulation intensity, tasks, exclusion rules, outcome measures, and analysis plans changed estimates. The strongest shared conclusion is not that all effort has no aftereffects. It is that one large, robust, domain-general decrement and one resource mechanism cannot be presumed.

### Conceptual Reviews Found the Theory Underspecified

Lurquin and Miyake examined whether the experimental tasks and theoretical models supported falsifiable predictions. They found that many tasks lacked independent evidence that they required self-control and that the model did not specify how the tasks consumed the putative resource, when the remainder would impair performance, or whether a given task pair used the same process [6]. Their argument matters because replication is interpretable only when a protocol is accepted as a valid test before outcomes are known.

Friese and colleagues reviewed the main empirical threats and then evaluated six arguments that could be offered in defense of ego depletion, asking whether proponents or skeptics would find the evidence compelling. The arguments concerned meta-analysis and replication limitations, manipulation and outcome weaknesses, moderator evidence, reverse effects, file-drawer estimates, and everyday-life evidence [8]. They concluded that neither side had a compelling case: the available evidence was inconclusive [8]. This verdict is narrower than declaring the effect nonexistent and more restrictive than preserving the original model by default.

Process and opportunity-cost theories supply alternatives with different causal language. Inzlicht and Schmeichel proposed shifts in motivation and attention after prior control [11]. Kurzban and colleagues proposed that subjective effort reflects the opportunity cost of allocating limited computational processes, not consumption of those processes [12]. Evans and colleagues used physical-fatigue research to argue that physiological limits, protective regulation, expectations, and motivation can jointly shape performance [19]. These are models, not settled replacements, but their existence means that a Task 1 to Task 2 decrement is mechanistically underdetermined.

The dispute also has a current pro-theory account. Baumeister and colleagues' 2024 narrative review states that the theory has shifted from literal exhaustion to conservation and argues that longer, stronger manipulations, effective fatigue checks, engaged participants, and nonboring controls explain many failures [24]. The authors call ego depletion "undeniably real," but that is their position rather than a consensus established by systematic review [24]. Their methodological boundary condition anticipates the stronger 2025 antisaccade result [27], while the null and small multisite estimates, outcome specificity, mixed preregistration, and unresolved mechanism prevent that result from establishing one common resource [5][9][10][27].

Ordali and colleagues provide a candidate physiological pathway without proving the resource model. After about 45 minutes of demanding control tasks, Study 1 found increased frontal sleep-like delta activity and less peaceful Hawk-and-Dove choices in 44 participants; a second sample of 403 replicated the behavioral pattern and also found altered punishment choices [25]. The authors did not demonstrate that the EEG change caused the behavior, most economic-game outcomes and Go-NoGo performance did not change, and general mental fatigue could not be excluded [25]. The study supports task-specific fatigue and a candidate neural correlate, not a universal willpower stock.

### Field Patterns Are Suggestive but Vulnerable to Confounding

The parole-board study found a large decline in favorable rulings as sessions progressed and a reset after breaks [14]. Because the study was observational and had no direct fatigue measure, it could not isolate mental depletion from scheduling, case mix, duration, and stopping behavior. Gloeckner's simulation showed that longer favorable cases and selective dropout near breaks could produce much of the pattern without irrational fatigue [15]. The case is an important warning: a dramatic sequence plot can be real while its popular causal explanation is wrong.

Linder and colleagues examined 21,867 acute respiratory-infection visits to 204 clinicians in 23 practices. Antibiotic prescribing increased across four-hour morning and afternoon sessions, including for diagnoses where antibiotics were sometimes or never indicated [16]. The pattern was consistent with decision fatigue, but the authors explicitly identified scheduled time as a proxy, a single health system as the setting, and unmeasured confounding or general fatigue as limitations [16]. It therefore provides field plausibility rather than direct identification of repeated decisions as the cause.

Maier and colleagues' 2025 preregistered systematic review searched eight databases and included 82 healthcare studies. Across 130 quantitative cases, 58, or 45 percent, supported a decision-fatigue hypothesis; 42 did not, and 30 were inconclusive. Meta-analysis was not possible because definitions, designs, outcomes, and operationalizations were heterogeneous [17]. Grignoli and colleagues separately included 43 clinical papers, 25 empirical, in a scoping review and meta-synthesis of definitions, related constructs, determinants, and proposed outcomes [21]. Only one quantitative sequential cross-sectional study directly measured physician decision fatigue; all quantitative studies were cross-sectional, no experiment or randomized trial was found, methodological quality was not formally assessed, and the review could not support causal inference [21]. The two reviews show an active field with nonuniform evidence rather than a settled occupational law.

Andersson and colleagues supplied a stronger null test through a Stage 2 Registered Report using medical-triage judgments by specialized nurses. They compared decisions under higher and lower fatigue proxies in overlap and around-break samples, testing urgency ratings and convergence toward personal defaults with Bayesian mixed models. All four confirmatory tests produced one-sided Bayes factors greater than 22 in favor of the null [18]. The study does not exclude every context-specific fatigue effect, and the authors note decision support and organization-specific factors as limits. It does cast doubt on a practically large domain-general effect in a demanding real setting [18].

### What Survived

Three findings survive with different confidence. First, sustained or effortful control can increase reported fatigue and aversiveness under some procedures [3][10][19][25][27]. Second, sequential performance effects can occur, but large studies place tested effects from near zero to moderate and show dependence on manipulation, outcome, exclusion, and analysis [5][9][10][27]. Third, field decisions sometimes change with time or sequence, but causal attribution to decision fatigue requires protection against case ordering, time pressure, general fatigue, and stopping-rule artifacts [15][16][17][18].

What did not survive is the unrestricted inference from any later error to a depleted common resource. The resource has not been independently measured, the task family is heterogeneous, newer positive effects remain task- and outcome-specific, and alternative models predict disengagement or reallocation without literal consumption [6][11][12][25][27]. The author's assessment is therefore conditional: decision fatigue is a useful description of a possible pattern, and ego depletion is a contested family of experimental aftereffects, but neither term should serve as a mechanism without additional evidence.

## Implications

### For Individual Decisions

A person should take subjective fatigue seriously without treating it as a diagnostic meter of lost willpower. Feeling effortful disengagement may indicate sustained conflict, unattractive incentives, boredom, opportunity costs, sleep or physical fatigue, or a demanding control policy [11][12][19]. The immediate practical question is not "How much resource remains?" but "Which function is changing -- attention, motivation, speed, accuracy, persistence, or willingness to choose?" Different failures call for different responses.

The author's synthesis is to separate state management from decision design. Rest, food, sleep, and workload matter for many reasons, but the depletion literature does not justify one universal replenishment formula. For consequential choices, externalize criteria, reduce irrelevant options, record reasons, and schedule independent review. These practices reduce reliance on fluctuating attention and motivation whether the underlying state is depletion, opportunity-cost reallocation, or general fatigue. Their value follows from robustness across mechanisms, not from proof of the strength model.

The popular recommendation to eliminate trivial choices may reduce clutter, but it should not be sold as conserving a quantified daily decision budget. Choice can be effortful when trade-offs are complex, goals conflict, information is uncertain, or options require active comparison [2][13]. Repetition can also produce learning and faster performance. The relevant design variable is the demand imposed by a choice and its alternatives, not a simple count of how many decisions occurred.

### For Organizations and High-Stakes Work

Organizations should not infer fatigue from clock time alone. A late-shift change can reflect workload, case mix, queue composition, deadlines, staffing, learning, or selection into which cases remain [15][17][18]. Monitoring should pair temporal patterns with case-level controls, direct or validated fatigue measures, and externally defined quality criteria. If the outcome is merely more conservative or more default-like, the organization must still establish whether that change is inappropriate.

The author's synthesis is that reversible safeguards are preferable to mechanism-specific claims. Predefined criteria, checklists, decision support, second review for irreversible actions, explicit deferral rules, and protected breaks can reduce vulnerability to several failure modes. A checklist preserves task-relevant information under attentional load; a second reviewer reduces dependence on one person's current allocation; a deferral rule distinguishes justified delay from passive avoidance. These mechanisms should be tested against actual outcomes rather than assumed effective because they sound consistent with fatigue theory.

High-volume systems should examine sequences, but sequence analysis must include stopping rules. In the parole simulation, favorable rulings took longer on average than unfavorable rulings, so cases expected to require more time could be deferred near a break and become selectively absent from late ordinal positions [15]. Break timing may be endogenous rather than random. The minimum causal question is whether comparable cases assigned under plausibly exogenous timing receive different decisions. Without that comparison, a within-day curve is a surveillance signal, not proof of degraded judgment.

### For Research Practice

Future experiments should specify the causal chain before data collection. The first and second tasks should be independently validated as measures of the stated operation, the task pair should have evidence of shared process, and Task 1 demand should be quantified objectively where possible [6]. The author's proposed measurement plan is to distinguish effort, fatigue, frustration, boredom, motivation, and expectancy rather than collapse them, while the outcome task should have adequate reliability. The theory should state which mediator changes and what pattern would count against it [6][11].

Preregistration is especially important because the design space is large. Researchers can choose among many Task 1 and Task 2 combinations, exclusion rules, transformations, outcome indices, and moderator analyses. Confirmatory exclusions and effect directions should be fixed in advance, while exploratory analyses should be labeled. Multisite studies should report both average effects and heterogeneity and should avoid interpreting any significant average as proof of one universal mechanism [5][9][10][27].

Resource, process, and opportunity-cost models should be compared rather than tested in isolation. A decisive study would manipulate variables on which the models diverge: incentive value, attractive alternatives, recovery interval, task meaning, or independently measured capacity. It would measure motivation and attention during the proposed causal sequence, not only after performance is known. Null predictions are essential. A model that predicts an effect whenever one appears and an unmeasured boundary condition whenever it does not cannot accumulate strong evidence [6][12].

Field research should use designs that constrain confounding: random or quasi-random case assignment, predeclared windows around breaks, within-worker comparisons, externally rated case difficulty, and outcomes tied to appropriateness. Registered Reports and out-of-sample validation can prevent a temporal pattern discovered in one dataset from being described as a prior hypothesis in the same data. Andersson and colleagues demonstrate this logic by exploring pilot data, locking a protocol, and testing new target data [18].

### For Interpreting Replication-Crisis Findings

A failed replication does not erase an entire psychological domain, and a positive replication does not restore every original claim. The 2016, 2021 Dang, 2021 Vohs, and 2025 Dang programs tested different procedures and produced principal or primary-outcome estimates of d = 0.04, d = 0.10, d = 0.06, and d = 0.31-0.35 [5][9][10][27]. The correct response is to model task- and outcome-specific effects and uncertainty, not to average away every difference or select the preferred protocol after seeing its result.

Effect size matters more than the binary label "real." An effect near d = 0.10 may be scientifically informative yet too small or unstable to support strong advice about daily scheduling. A modest average can matter at scale, but practical significance requires baseline risk, outcome cost, intervention effect, and implementation burden. Those quantities are not supplied by the existence of a statistically significant sequential-task effect.

The author's assessment is that the replication crisis improved the question. The early question was whether self-control uses a finite resource. The better question is which combinations of effort, attention, motivation, expectation, physical state, task duration, and task structure produce reliable changes in a specified outcome. That question admits multiple mechanisms, null effects, moderate protocol-specific effects, and bounded generalization. It is less elegant than a battery metaphor and more compatible with the evidence [6][8][19][25][27].

### For Investing and Other Sequential Judgment

Investors, clinicians, judges, editors, and managers all make sequences of consequential judgments, but evidence from one setting does not automatically transfer to another. The author's synthesis is to treat fatigue as one item in a causal checklist rather than a diagnosis. When a later decision differs from an earlier one, compare case quality, stakes, available time, prior outcomes, incentives, default structure, and information load before attributing the difference to depletion.

For investment process, the reversible safeguard is to define valuation assumptions and disconfirming evidence before reacting to a sequence of opportunities. A late-session rejection may be prudent selectivity or passive defaulting; a rapid purchase may reflect insight or reduced scrutiny. A written criterion and delayed review create evidence about the process. This application is an interpretation based on the general control and fatigue literature, not a direct finding from investment trials.

The deepest practical lesson is epistemic. Feeling depleted is evidence about experience, not proof of a mechanism. A declining metric is evidence about an outcome, not proof that decisions caused the decline. A protocol-specific experimental effect is evidence about that task and outcome, not a license to generalize to every kind of choice. Preserving these distinctions prevents a useful warning about sustained effort from hardening into an unsupported universal law.
