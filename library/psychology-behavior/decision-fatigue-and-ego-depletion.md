# Decision Fatigue and Ego Depletion -- Fatigue Is Real, but a Universal Depletable Resource Is Not Established

Decision fatigue and ego depletion describe plausible changes after sustained choice or self-control, but they do not identify one proven, domain-general resource that predictably runs down with use. The strongest post-replication conclusion is narrower: effort and subjective fatigue can change, small performance effects sometimes occur under particular tasks, and field decisions sometimes vary with time or sequence, yet the size, cause, and generality of these patterns remain contested [5][8][9][10][17][18]. Practical claims should therefore separate experienced fatigue, measured performance, and causal mechanism rather than treating the popular willpower-battery metaphor as established fact.

## Background

The modern debate began with the strength model of self-control. Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven, and Tice proposed that choice, active response, emotion regulation, and other acts of volition draw on a common limited resource. Across four experiments, an initial act such as resisting tempting food, making an attitude-relevant choice, or suppressing emotion was followed by less persistence, poorer anagram performance, or greater passivity on a different task [1]. The term "ego depletion" named the temporary state inferred from this sequential pattern: Task 1 was assumed to consume strength, and lower performance on Task 2 was interpreted as evidence that less strength remained [1]. The theory was attractive because one simple mechanism appeared to connect many failures of self-control.

Decision fatigue entered this framework as a related claim about choice. Vohs and colleagues compared participants who made repeated choices with participants who considered the same options without choosing. Four laboratory studies reported less physical stamina, persistence, arithmetic performance, or prompt action after choice, and a field study related shoppers' reported amount of prior choosing to subsequent task performance [2]. The authors argued that choosing and self-control used the same limited resource [2]. Later conceptual work defined decision fatigue more broadly as impaired decision-making or behavioral regulation following repeated decisions, while also noting that the literature had not clearly established its consequences or measurement boundaries [13].

The two concepts overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Ego depletion is a hypothesized aftereffect of prior self-control and is usually tested with a sequential-task experiment. Decision fatigue refers to changes after repeated or demanding decisions and is often inferred from time of day, decision order, default use, deferral, or declining decision quality [2][5][13][17]. Mental fatigue is broader still: it can arise from vigilance, sustained attention, conflict, workload, or prolonged cognitive activity without repeated choices [13][19]. Calling all three states "depletion" can hide differences in antecedent task, subjective experience, measured outcome, and proposed mechanism.

The early literature expanded quickly. A 2010 meta-analysis synthesized 83 studies and estimated an average effect of d = 0.62, while also reporting changes in effort, perceived difficulty, negative affect, subjective fatigue, and other proposed correlates [3]. This medium-to-large estimate encouraged applications far beyond the original laboratory tasks. It also made the model unusually consequential: if many kinds of self-control consumed one common stock, then prior effort could be used to predict eating, persistence, moral conduct, spending, work, and judgment through the same mechanism.

The replication crisis changed the evidentiary standard. Carter and colleagues searched for published and unpublished tests, used narrower inclusion rules, examined small-study effects, and concluded that the common laboratory methods provided very little evidence for a depletion effect [4]. A 23-laboratory preregistered replication with 2,141 participants then estimated d = 0.04 with a 95% confidence interval from -0.07 to 0.15, consistent with no effect for the standardized protocol that was tested [5]. These results did not logically prove that no depletion effect can occur under any procedure. They did show that the original literature and its broad resource interpretation could not be treated as settled.

The dispute exposed a conceptual problem in addition to a replication problem. Lurquin and Miyake argued that many tasks used to induce or measure depletion lacked independent validation as measures of self-control. They also noted that the strength model had not specified the resource, its unit, the dose consumed by a task, or the relation between the resource and performance precisely enough to yield unambiguous predictions [6]. If a null result can always be explained by an insufficiently depleting first task, an insensitive second task, unusual motivation, or an unobserved reserve, the theory becomes difficult to falsify [6][12]. Better sample sizes do not by themselves solve an underspecified construct.

Later evidence remained mixed rather than returning to the original consensus. Dang's updated meta-analysis found an overall random-effects estimate of g = 0.38 and a trim-and-fill estimate of g = 0.24, but the evidence was heterogeneous and varied by task [7]. Friese and colleagues reviewed arguments from advocates and skeptics and concluded that the available evidence was inconclusive despite hundreds of published studies [8]. Two large preregistered projects in 2021 reached different but compatible limits: one found a small effect of d = 0.10 across 12 laboratories, while another found a nonsignificant confirmatory estimate of d = 0.06 across 36 laboratories and stronger Bayesian support for the null than for its informed alternative [9][10]. These estimates rule out the easy assumption that the historical d = 0.62 is a stable domain-general effect.

Field evidence on decision fatigue developed along a parallel track. Studies reported sequence or time-of-day patterns in judicial and medical decisions, but these designs generally used time, order, or proximity to a break as a proxy for fatigue rather than measuring the proposed state directly [14][16]. Reanalysis showed that at least one famous judicial pattern could be generated by case duration, stopping rules, and selective attrition within sessions without requiring mental depletion [15]. Recent reviews of clinical decision fatigue found inconsistent definitions, heterogeneous methods, and a minority or plurality rather than a uniform majority of quantitative results supporting the hypothesis [17]. A 2025 Registered Report using high-resolution medical-triage decisions found Bayes factors greater than 22 in favor of no fatigue-related difference in all four confirmatory tests [18].

The history therefore supports a disciplined reframing. Repeated choice and control can feel effortful, and subjective fatigue is a real reportable state [3][10][12]. What did not survive is the strong inference that such feelings or later errors demonstrate consumption of a single common resource. The live scientific questions concern which tasks produce which aftereffects, how large they are, whether they generalize, what role motivation and attention play, and when field sequence patterns have causal rather than procedural explanations [6][8][10][17][18].

## Core Concepts

### Four Distinct Claims Must Be Separated

The phrase "decision fatigue" often combines at least four claims. First, repeated decisions may increase subjective tiredness or effort. Second, later behavior may change, for example through deferral, default selection, impulsivity, slower work, or lower persistence. Third, those behavioral changes may reflect reduced decision quality rather than an adaptive change in strategy. Fourth, a common resource may have been consumed and caused the change. Evidence for one claim is not automatically evidence for the next [6][13][17].

Subjective fatigue is an experience. It can be measured with self-report and can change even when objective performance remains stable. Performance is task-specific: speed, accuracy, persistence, inhibition, risk preference, and default choice are different outcomes. Decision quality is normative and requires a criterion for what counts as better. Mechanism is causal and requires evidence distinguishing resource loss from motivation, attention, learning, strategy, opportunity cost, boredom, general time-on-task, and physical fatigue [11][12][17]. A sound study states which level it tests rather than moving silently between them.

The author's synthesis is that these levels form a ladder of increasing inference. A person who reports fatigue establishes an experienced state, not a depleted resource. A correlation between time and default choice establishes a sequence pattern, not necessarily deteriorating quality. An experiment in which one task changes another establishes an aftereffect under that procedure, not necessarily a domain-general mechanism. A resource theory requires an independently measurable resource or a sufficiently precise pattern of predictions that competing models do not make [6][12].

### The Sequential-Task Paradigm

The canonical ego-depletion experiment uses two tasks. Participants in the depletion condition perform an initial activity intended to require self-control, while controls perform an easier or noncontrol version. Both groups then perform a second task intended to require self-control. Worse performance by the depletion group on Task 2 is the depletion effect [1][5]. Common first tasks have included emotion suppression, food temptation, thought suppression, crossing out letters under changing rules, or Stroop interference; second tasks have included persistence, anagrams, handgrip endurance, Stroop performance, antisaccades, and other inhibition or control measures [1][5][7][9].

This design is simple, but its interpretation is not. Task 1 may differ between groups in difficulty, boredom, frustration, expectancy, demand characteristics, practice, or perceived purpose as well as self-control. Task 2 may contain measurement noise or may not depend on the same process as Task 1. If the resource is not directly observed, the causal chain is inferred from the between-group difference. Independent validation is therefore essential: the manipulation should demonstrably alter the proposed mediator, and the outcome should validly measure the claimed capacity [6].

Task heterogeneity creates another problem. If many combinations of manipulations and outcomes are treated as interchangeable tests of one effect, genuine differences between tasks can inflate heterogeneity and invite post hoc classification. Dang's 2018 analysis found that estimates differed across manipulation types and that some tasks did not retain an effect after bias correction [7]. A task taxonomy specified after results are known cannot provide a clean confirmatory test. The appropriate unit of evidence is the preregistered task-and-outcome combination, not an abstract label applied after the fact.

### The Strength or Resource Model

The strength model states that acts of self-control draw on a common limited capacity and that prior exertion temporarily reduces capacity for later control [1][3]. Its central appeal is domain generality: resisting food could impair puzzle persistence because both require the same underlying stock. The model also uses a muscle analogy, in which exertion produces temporary fatigue but repeated training may improve capacity over longer periods [3][12].

The principal unresolved question is what "resource" denotes. A literal metabolic account requires a physiological substrate and quantitative relation between use and performance. A functional account can treat the resource as an abstract capacity, but it still needs rules for measuring availability, consumption, recovery, and output. Without such rules, motivation can be invoked to release a reserve, positive affect can replenish it, and a null result can be classified as insufficient exertion without independent evidence [6][12]. This flexibility weakens the model's discriminatory power.

The replication record does not support a robust large effect across ordinary sequential-task procedures [4][5][10]. It also does not exclude every resource-like limit. Working memory, sustained attention, response selection, sleep, and physical energy impose real constraints, but their existence does not validate one common self-control reservoir. A mechanism can be capacity-limited without being depleted by prior use, and performance can decline without capacity loss [11][12]. The model must therefore compete with alternatives at the level of predictions, not metaphors.

### Motivation-and-Attention Process Models

Inzlicht and Schmeichel proposed that prior control changes what people want to do and what they attend to. After effortful control, motivation may shift away from obligations and toward gratification or leisure, while attention becomes less oriented to control-relevant cues [11]. Lower Task 2 performance can then occur because allocation changes rather than because an inner stock is exhausted. The model predicts sensitivity to incentives, task meaning, affect, and attentional priority [11].

This process account helps explain why people sometimes perform adequately after prior exertion when stakes or incentives change. It also explains why fatigue can be accompanied by a desire to disengage rather than inability. However, motivation is not a universal escape from measurement. It needs operational definitions, independent measures, and temporal predictions just as a resource does. Self-reported motivation after a task may reflect performance, beliefs, or demand characteristics rather than the mediator that caused behavior [6][11].

The distinction between capacity and allocation is central. Capacity asks what a person could do under sufficiently strong reasons and appropriate conditions. Allocation asks how much control the person deploys to the current task. Poorer performance after sustained work can arise because capacity fell, because the expected value of effort fell, because attractive alternatives gained priority, or because the control policy changed. Observed disengagement alone cannot identify which route occurred [11][12].

### Opportunity-Cost and Effort Models

Kurzban and colleagues modeled mental effort as the experienced output of opportunity-cost computations. Executive processes can support only a limited set of simultaneous activities, so allocating them to one task prevents their use elsewhere. As the relative value of alternatives rises, effort feels more aversive and continued allocation becomes less attractive [12]. On this account, effort is not a fuel that is consumed. It is a signal about competing uses of scarce processing capacity.

The opportunity-cost model makes fatigue sensitive to context. The same task should feel more effortful when attractive alternatives are available, when rewards for the current task decline, or when time spent on the task increases the value of switching. It also permits rapid recovery when incentives or tasks change because no stock must be replenished [12]. The model does not imply that all fatigue is imaginary. Subjective effort can be functional even when no consumable resource is involved: it can regulate allocation and prevent one goal from monopolizing control.

Evans, Boggero, and Segerstrom compared self-regulatory fatigue with physical fatigue and argued for an integrative view. Physical fatigue itself is not explained by substrate exhaustion alone; performance reflects physiological state, protective regulation, expectations, motivation, and context. They proposed that self-regulatory fatigue may likewise integrate multiple inputs and occur while some capacity remains [19]. This analogy is more precise than a battery metaphor because it allows real constraints and strategic regulation to coexist.

### Beliefs, Expectancies, and Learned Policies

Beliefs about willpower can affect behavior. Job, Dweck, and Walton reported that participants who viewed willpower as nonlimited did not show the same postexertion decline as participants who viewed it as easily depleted, and experimental manipulation of those beliefs changed the pattern [20]. Such results are inconsistent with a simple resource model in which beliefs have no role. They are compatible with expectancy, motivation, allocation, or learned-policy accounts.

Belief effects do not establish that fatigue is merely imagined. A belief can influence how strongly a real signal changes behavior, how effort is interpreted, or when a person decides to disengage. It can also interact with task demands and social expectations. The correct inference is that subjective theories are candidate moderators and mechanisms, not that positive thinking abolishes limits [10][20]. The 2021 multisite paradigmatic test did not find willpower beliefs to moderate the confirmatory depletion effect, illustrating that moderator findings also require replication [10].

### Decision Fatigue as a Field Construct

In field research, decision fatigue is commonly operationalized through decision order, time within a shift, cumulative cases, or position before versus after a break [14][16][17][18]. These proxies are practical, but they are not direct measures of fatigue. Time also changes hunger, circadian state, workload, queue composition, time pressure, learning, patient behavior, staffing, and selection into which cases remain. Decision order can correlate with case difficulty or duration. Breaks can be chosen in response to the cases already completed or anticipated next [15][17].

The outcome also needs interpretation. More defaults may indicate avoidance, but a default can be safer or more appropriate. Faster decisions may reflect degraded scrutiny or improved learning. More conservative choices may reflect fatigue, case mix, or rational response to diminishing time. A quality claim needs external criteria such as guideline adherence, diagnostic accuracy, patient outcome, or independently rated appropriateness [17][18]. Without such a criterion, a temporal change is only a change.

The famous parole-board study illustrates the identification problem. Danziger, Levav, and Avnaim-Pesso analyzed 1,112 rulings and found that favorable decisions declined within sessions and rose after breaks. They interpreted the pattern cautiously through mental depletion while acknowledging that they did not measure judges' mental resources [14]. Gloeckner later showed through simulation that differences in case duration, stopping decisions, and censoring could generate a similar pattern for a rational judge without depletion [15]. The critique did not prove that judges never tire; it showed that the observed curve did not uniquely identify fatigue.

### Replication, Effect Size, and Heterogeneity

A claim can fail in several ways. The average effect may be near zero, a small average may conceal reliable effects for some tasks, or heterogeneous estimates may reflect noise, bias, and genuine moderation together. Publication bias can inflate an average when small significant studies are more likely to appear than small null studies. Flexible exclusion, task selection, and analysis can produce additional researcher degrees of freedom [4][6][8].

The post-crisis record therefore needs more than a vote count. Hagger's 2016 project tested one standardized protocol and found d = 0.04 [5]. Dang's 2021 project tested another protocol and found d = 0.10, rising to d = 0.16 after excluding participants judged likely to have responded randomly [9]. Vohs and colleagues tested two paradigmatic protocols and found d = 0.06 in confirmatory analyses and d = 0.08 in exploratory full-sample analyses [10]. These results are not contradictory if the effect is small, task-dependent, or sensitive to analysis; they are contradictory to treating a large universal effect as established.

The author's synthesis is that heterogeneity is now part of the phenomenon to be explained. A useful theory should predict which first tasks change which later outcomes, through which measured mediator, over what interval, and under which incentives. It should also predict null effects. Until those predictions are prospectively tested, a small result in one paradigm should not be generalized to every decision context, and a null result should not be generalized to prove that sustained cognitive work has no consequences.

## Evidence

### Original Laboratory Findings Established a Research Program

Baumeister and colleagues' 1998 paper reported four experiments built around cross-domain aftereffects. Resisting chocolates while eating radishes preceded shorter persistence on unsolvable puzzles; making an attitude-relevant choice preceded reduced persistence; suppressing emotion preceded worse solvable-anagram performance; and a high-regulation task preceded greater passive responding [1]. The design's key contribution was not simply that hard work felt tiring. It showed how unrelated tasks could be arranged to test a common-resource hypothesis [1].

Vohs and colleagues extended the logic to decision-making. Across four laboratory studies, participants made choices about consumer goods or courses or engaged with comparable materials without choosing. Those assigned to choose later showed less stamina, persistence, prompt action, or arithmetic performance. A field study associated shoppers' reported prior choosing with subsequent performance [2]. The set distinguished active choice from merely considering options and supplied the empirical foundation for calling repeated choice depleting [2].

These studies were influential but were not definitive. Small samples, varied outcomes, and an unobserved mediator left room for alternative explanations. The designs established that sequential-task effects were testable; they did not measure a resource or establish that every observed aftereffect had the same cause [6][12]. Their historical importance and their evidentiary limits can both be true.

### Meta-Analysis First Strengthened and Then Destabilized the Claim

Hagger and colleagues' 2010 meta-analysis combined 83 studies and reported d = 0.62, with additional effects on effort, difficulty, negative affect, fatigue, and proposed moderators [3][6]. This estimate made the effect appear both substantial and broadly reproducible. The analysis included varied manipulation and outcome tasks, however, and later critics argued that published-only evidence, broad inclusion, and small-study effects could overstate the mean [4][7].

Carter and colleagues assembled published and unpublished experiments, narrowed the set of tasks considered valid tests, and applied methods designed to detect or correct small-study effects. Their abstract conclusion was that common laboratory methods provided very little evidence that the depletion effect was real and strongly challenged a limited-resource interpretation [4]. The exact corrected estimate depended on statistical method, and subsequent authors disputed whether PET-PEESE was appropriate under the observed heterogeneity [7][8]. Thus, the correction debate did not produce one uncontested number.

Dang's updated meta-analysis makes the disagreement concrete. Across 142 effects including all depletion types, the random-effects estimate was g = 0.38 with substantial heterogeneity; trim-and-fill reduced it to g = 0.24. A more restricted post hoc set of tasks judged reliable produced g = 0.42, while some individual tasks did not retain significance after correction [7]. These estimates support neither the original stable d = 0.62 nor a simple claim that every estimate equals zero. They show that inclusion criteria, task validity, heterogeneity, and bias correction govern the answer.

### Preregistered Multisite Tests Bounded the Average Effect

The 2016 Registered Replication Report coordinated 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants around a standardized sequential-task protocol. The pooled estimate was d = 0.04, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.07 to 0.15 [5]. The result was consistent with no effect for that protocol and excluded the historical meta-analytic estimate as a plausible description of the tested procedure. Critics argued that the initial letter-crossing task may not have induced sufficient fatigue or self-control, which returned the debate to manipulation validity [6][8].

Dang and colleagues later selected a Stroop-to-antisaccade sequence based on a prior preregistered study. Across 12 laboratories and 1,775 participants, they reported a small significant effect of d = 0.10. Excluding participants who may have responded randomly on the outcome task increased the estimate to d = 0.16 [9]. This project is evidence that a small sequential aftereffect can be detected under some procedures. It does not recover the original large effect or identify a common consumable resource.

Vohs and colleagues used a paradigmatic replication strategy in 36 laboratories with 3,531 participants. Each laboratory implemented one of two manipulation-outcome procedures. The preregistered confirmatory estimate was d = 0.06 and nonsignificant; an informed-prior Bayesian analysis made the data four times more likely under the null than under the specified alternative. Exploratory analysis without preregistered exclusions yielded d = 0.08 and a Bayesian comparison about equally compatible with null and alternative models [10]. Participants in the depletion conditions reported more fatigue, but confirmatory performance evidence did not establish the predicted depletion effect [10].

Taken together, the three multisite projects place the typical effect for their protocols near zero to small [5][9][10]. They also demonstrate why method details matter: tasks, exclusion rules, outcome measures, and analysis plans changed estimates. The strongest shared conclusion is not that all effort has no aftereffects. It is that a large, robust, domain-general decrement cannot be presumed.

### Conceptual Reviews Found the Theory Underspecified

Lurquin and Miyake examined whether the experimental tasks and theoretical models supported falsifiable predictions. They found that many tasks lacked independent evidence that they required self-control and that the models did not clearly specify the resource, consumption conditions, or expected task pairings [6]. Their argument matters because replication is interpretable only when a protocol is accepted as a valid test before outcomes are known.

Friese and colleagues considered six major lines of argument from both defenders and skeptics, including limitations of meta-analysis, weaknesses in manipulations and outcomes, moderator evidence, reverse effects, file-drawer estimates, and everyday-life evidence. They concluded that neither side had a compelling case: the available evidence was inconclusive [8]. This verdict is narrower than declaring the effect nonexistent and more restrictive than preserving the original model by default.

Process and opportunity-cost theories supply alternatives with different causal language. Inzlicht and Schmeichel proposed shifts in motivation and attention after prior control [11]. Kurzban and colleagues proposed that subjective effort reflects the opportunity cost of allocating limited computational processes, not consumption of those processes [12]. Evans and colleagues used physical-fatigue research to argue that physiological limits, protective regulation, expectations, and motivation can jointly shape performance [19]. These are models, not settled replacements, but their existence means that a Task 1 to Task 2 decrement is mechanistically underdetermined.

### Field Patterns Are Suggestive but Vulnerable to Confounding

The parole-board study found a large decline in favorable rulings as sessions progressed and a reset after breaks [14]. Because the study was observational and had no direct fatigue measure, it could not isolate mental depletion from scheduling, case mix, duration, and stopping behavior. Gloeckner's simulation showed that longer favorable cases and selective dropout near breaks could produce much of the pattern without irrational fatigue [15]. The case is an important warning: a dramatic sequence plot can be real while its popular causal explanation is wrong.

Linder and colleagues examined 21,867 acute respiratory-infection visits to 204 clinicians in 23 practices. Antibiotic prescribing increased across four-hour morning and afternoon sessions, including for diagnoses where antibiotics were sometimes or never indicated [16]. The pattern was consistent with decision fatigue, but the authors explicitly identified scheduled time as a proxy, a single health system as the setting, and unmeasured confounding or general fatigue as limitations [16]. It therefore provides field plausibility rather than direct identification of repeated decisions as the cause.

Maier and colleagues' 2025 preregistered systematic review searched eight databases and included 82 healthcare studies. Across 130 quantitative cases, 58, or 45 percent, supported a decision-fatigue hypothesis; 42 did not, and 30 were inconclusive. Meta-analysis was not possible because definitions, designs, outcomes, and operationalizations were heterogeneous [17]. Grignoli and colleagues separately reviewed 43 clinical papers, 25 empirical, and emphasized the need for a coherent definition and validated measurement [21]. Both reviews show an active field with nonuniform evidence rather than a settled occupational law.

Andersson and colleagues supplied a stronger null test through a Stage 2 Registered Report using medical-triage judgments by specialized nurses. They compared decisions under higher and lower fatigue proxies in overlap and around-break samples, testing urgency ratings and convergence toward personal defaults with Bayesian mixed models. All four confirmatory tests produced one-sided Bayes factors greater than 22 in favor of the null [18]. The study does not exclude every context-specific fatigue effect, and the authors note decision support and organization-specific factors as limits. It does cast doubt on a practically large domain-general effect in a demanding real setting [18].

### What Survived

Three findings survive with different confidence. First, sustained or effortful control can increase reported fatigue and aversiveness under some procedures [3][10][19]. Second, sequential performance effects can occur, but the best large studies place tested averages near zero to small and show dependence on protocol and analysis [5][9][10]. Third, field decisions sometimes change with time or sequence, but causal attribution to decision fatigue requires protection against case ordering, time pressure, general fatigue, and stopping-rule artifacts [15][16][17][18].

What did not survive is the unrestricted inference from any later error to a depleted common resource. The resource has not been independently measured, the task family is heterogeneous, and alternative models predict disengagement or reallocation without literal consumption [6][11][12]. The author's assessment is therefore conditional: decision fatigue is a useful description of a possible pattern, and ego depletion remains a contested experimental effect, but neither term should serve as a mechanism without additional evidence.

## Implications

### For Individual Decisions

A person should take subjective fatigue seriously without treating it as a diagnostic meter of lost willpower. Feeling effortful disengagement may indicate sustained conflict, unattractive incentives, boredom, opportunity costs, sleep or physical fatigue, or a demanding control policy [11][12][19]. The immediate practical question is not "How much resource remains?" but "Which function is changing -- attention, motivation, speed, accuracy, persistence, or willingness to choose?" Different failures call for different responses.

The author's synthesis is to separate state management from decision design. Rest, food, sleep, and workload matter for many reasons, but the depletion literature does not justify one universal replenishment formula. For consequential choices, externalize criteria, reduce irrelevant options, record reasons, and schedule independent review. These practices reduce reliance on fluctuating attention and motivation whether the underlying state is depletion, opportunity-cost reallocation, or general fatigue. Their value follows from robustness across mechanisms, not from proof of the strength model.

The popular recommendation to eliminate trivial choices may reduce clutter, but it should not be sold as conserving a quantified daily decision budget. Choice can be effortful when trade-offs are complex, goals conflict, information is uncertain, or options require active comparison [2][13]. Repetition can also produce learning and faster performance. The relevant design variable is the demand imposed by a choice and its alternatives, not a simple count of how many decisions occurred.

### For Organizations and High-Stakes Work

Organizations should not infer fatigue from clock time alone. A late-shift change can reflect workload, case mix, queue composition, deadlines, staffing, learning, or selection into which cases remain [15][17][18]. Monitoring should pair temporal patterns with case-level controls, direct or validated fatigue measures, and externally defined quality criteria. If the outcome is merely more conservative or more default-like, the organization must still establish whether that change is inappropriate.

The author's synthesis is that reversible safeguards are preferable to mechanism-specific claims. Predefined criteria, checklists, decision support, second review for irreversible actions, explicit deferral rules, and protected breaks can reduce vulnerability to several failure modes. A checklist preserves task-relevant information under attentional load; a second reviewer reduces dependence on one person's current allocation; a deferral rule distinguishes justified delay from passive avoidance. These mechanisms should be tested against actual outcomes rather than assumed effective because they sound consistent with fatigue theory.

High-volume systems should examine sequences, but sequence analysis must include stopping rules. Cases near the end of a session may be a selected subset because easy cases finish earlier, hard cases take longer, or staff defer work unlikely to fit before a break [15]. Break timing may be endogenous rather than random. The minimum causal question is whether comparable cases assigned under plausibly exogenous timing receive different decisions. Without that comparison, a within-day curve is a surveillance signal, not proof of degraded judgment.

### For Research Practice

Future experiments should specify the causal chain before data collection. The first task must have an independently justified relation to self-control, decision demand, or fatigue. Manipulation checks should distinguish effort, fatigue, frustration, boredom, motivation, and expectancy rather than collapsing them. The second task should measure a defined outcome with adequate reliability. The theory should state which mediator changes and what pattern would count against it [6].

Preregistration is especially important because the design space is large. Researchers can choose among many Task 1 and Task 2 combinations, exclusion rules, transformations, outcome indices, and moderator analyses. Confirmatory exclusions and effect directions should be fixed in advance, while exploratory analyses should be labeled. Multisite studies should report both average effects and heterogeneity and should avoid interpreting a significant small average as proof of one universal mechanism [5][9][10].

Resource, process, and opportunity-cost models should be compared rather than tested in isolation. A decisive study would manipulate variables on which the models diverge: incentive value, attractive alternatives, recovery interval, task meaning, or independently measured capacity. It would measure motivation and attention during the proposed causal sequence, not only after performance is known. Null predictions are essential. A model that predicts an effect whenever one appears and an unmeasured boundary condition whenever it does not cannot accumulate strong evidence [6][12].

Field research should use designs that constrain confounding: random or quasi-random case assignment, predeclared windows around breaks, within-worker comparisons, externally rated case difficulty, and outcomes tied to appropriateness. Registered Reports and out-of-sample validation can prevent a temporal pattern discovered in one dataset from being described as a prior hypothesis in the same data. Andersson and colleagues demonstrate this logic by exploring pilot data, locking a protocol, and testing new target data [18].

### For Interpreting Replication-Crisis Findings

A failed replication does not erase an entire psychological domain, and a positive replication does not restore every original claim. The 2016, 2021 Dang, and 2021 Vohs projects tested different procedures and produced estimates of d = 0.04, d = 0.10, and d = 0.06 in their principal analyses [5][9][10]. The correct response is to model task-specific effects and uncertainty, not to average away every difference or select the preferred protocol after seeing its result.

Effect size matters more than the binary label "real." An effect near d = 0.10 may be scientifically informative yet too small or unstable to support strong advice about daily scheduling. A modest average can matter at scale, but practical significance requires baseline risk, outcome cost, intervention effect, and implementation burden. Those quantities are not supplied by the existence of a statistically significant sequential-task effect.

The author's assessment is that the replication crisis improved the question. The early question was whether self-control uses a finite resource. The better question is which combinations of effort, attention, motivation, expectation, physical state, and task structure produce reliable changes in a specified outcome. That question admits multiple mechanisms, null effects, and bounded generalization. It is less elegant than a battery metaphor and more compatible with the evidence [6][8][19].

### For Investing and Other Sequential Judgment

Investors, clinicians, judges, editors, and managers all make sequences of consequential judgments, but evidence from one setting does not automatically transfer to another. The author's synthesis is to treat fatigue as one item in a causal checklist rather than a diagnosis. When a later decision differs from an earlier one, compare case quality, stakes, available time, prior outcomes, incentives, default structure, and information load before attributing the difference to depletion.

For investment process, the reversible safeguard is to define valuation assumptions and disconfirming evidence before reacting to a sequence of opportunities. A late-session rejection may be prudent selectivity or passive defaulting; a rapid purchase may reflect insight or reduced scrutiny. A written criterion and delayed review create evidence about the process. This application is an interpretation based on the general control and fatigue literature, not a direct finding from investment trials.

The deepest practical lesson is epistemic. Feeling depleted is evidence about experience, not proof of a mechanism. A declining metric is evidence about an outcome, not proof that decisions caused the decline. A small experimental effect is evidence about a protocol, not a license to generalize to every kind of choice. Preserving these distinctions prevents a useful warning about sustained effort from hardening into an unsupported universal law.
