# Sunk Costs Distort the Next Decision When the Past Becomes a Reason to Continue

A sunk cost is an investment of money, time, effort, or identity that cannot be recovered, while the sunk-cost effect is the increased tendency to continue because that investment has already been made [1][8]. Escalation of commitment is the broader process in which negative feedback is followed by further commitment to a chosen course; it can include sunk-cost sensitivity, but also self-justification, project completion, reputation, incentives, and features of how the next choice is framed [2][5][7].

## Background

The normative benchmark is prospective. If two available actions have the same future consequences regardless of how much was spent yesterday, yesterday's expenditure should not change which action is better today. Past investment can explain how the decision maker arrived at the present state, but an unrecoverable cost does not itself alter the marginal benefits, marginal costs, opportunity costs, or probabilities attached to the alternatives now available [8][9]. This benchmark does not say that all persistence after a loss is irrational. New evidence may favor continuation, abandoning may incur a real switching cost, learning may make the incumbent path more productive, or another investment may preserve a valuable option. A sunk-cost error is present only when the past expenditure influences the choice even though it provides no relevant information and changes none of those future consequences [8][9].

The modern research program developed along two related lines. Staw's 1976 business simulation studied escalation after adverse feedback. In a role-playing exercise with 240 business students, participants committed the most additional resources when they were personally responsible for the initial decision and it had produced negative consequences [2]. The result directed attention toward self-justification: withdrawing can amount to admitting that one's earlier judgment was wrong, whereas reinvestment keeps open the possibility of vindication [2][4]. Bazerman, Giuliano, and Appelman later found escalation in both individual and group decisions and reported that dissonance-related responses varied with responsibility and explained additional variance in allocations [3].

Arkes and Blumer's 1985 program focused directly on sunk costs. They defined the effect as a greater tendency to continue after an investment of money, effort, or time and argued that a desire not to appear wasteful was central [1]. Their field study found that theater patrons who had paid more for a season subscription attended more performances during the first six months, and their scenarios showed that prior investment could affect both continuation and estimated chances of success [1]. These studies made the phenomenon concrete, but they also joined several psychological processes that later work would try to separate: feeling a loss, closing a mental account, avoiding waste, justifying personal responsibility, and changing beliefs about success [1][4][6].

Brockner's 1992 review treated escalation as a class of situations rather than one isolated fallacy. In the standard structure, resources have been committed, feedback is unfavorable or ambiguous, and the decision maker can withdraw or commit more [4]. That structure matters because a continuation choice can reflect several causes at once. The amount already spent may be salient; personal responsibility may threaten self-image; public withdrawal may threaten reputation; organizational rewards may favor apparent consistency; and incomplete work may create a powerful goal to finish [4][5][7]. The phrase "throwing good money after bad" describes the outcome, not a complete causal diagnosis.

This distinction became important when researchers separated utilization decisions from progress decisions. A utilization decision asks whether to use something already purchased, such as attending a prepaid event. A progress decision asks whether to add resources to an unfinished project whose benefits remain contingent on completion [6]. In progress settings, the percentage complete can rise with the percentage of budget spent, so sunk cost and proximity to completion are easily confounded. Garland and Conlon showed that decision makers could persist because a project was close to completion even when sunk-cost information was held apart; they described completion as a substituted goal that can displace the original objective [7]. Completion can be economically relevant if the remaining cost is small and the completed asset has value, but the desire to finish can also survive after the original value case has collapsed [7].

Meta-analytic work supports the existence of the general phenomenon while rejecting a single universal mechanism. Sleesman and colleagues synthesized the organizational escalation literature and organized determinants across project, psychological, social, and structural perspectives [5]. Roth, Robbert, and Straus analyzed 98 sunk-cost effect sizes and found clear aggregate evidence, but the magnitude and moderators differed between utilization and progress decisions [6]. More recent incentivized studies have also produced mixed patterns: Ronayne, Sgroi, and Tuckwell found persistence with a dominated lottery after participants had earned it through effort, whereas Negrini, Riedl, and Wibral found no preregistered sunk-cost bias in their investment task and observed a reverse effect under some conditions [8][9]. The defensible conclusion is therefore conditional: prior investment can bias choice, but task design, decision type, responsibility, measurement, and genuine forward-looking information determine what an observed continuation decision means [5][6][8][9].

## Core Concepts

### Sunk cost, sunk-cost effect, and escalation are not synonyms

A sunk cost is a property of a resource already spent: it is unrecoverable under the alternatives now being considered. The sunk-cost effect is a behavioral relationship in which that irrelevant prior investment increases attachment to an option [1][8]. Escalation of commitment is a dynamic pattern: after choosing a course and receiving negative feedback, a decision maker devotes more resources or remains committed despite deteriorating prospects [2][4]. Escalation may contain a sunk-cost effect, but it need not. A manager can escalate because compensation rewards project size, because evidence is genuinely ambiguous, because stopping is reputationally costly, or because a nearly complete project still has positive prospective value [4][5][7].

The distinctions change measurement. To identify a sunk-cost effect, a comparison must vary past investment while holding the future alternatives and relevant information constant [8][9]. To identify irrational escalation, the evidence must show not merely that investment continued after bad news, but that prospective value did not justify it. Without those controls, persistence is behavior consistent with a bias, not proof of the bias. This is why field evidence is valuable but difficult: draft position, acquisition price, salary, and prior expenditure may also carry information about quality, expectations, contractual constraints, or switching costs [14][15].

### Prospective choice and the counterfactual reset

The clean decision question is: "If I inherited this position today with none of the history, which option would I choose from this point forward?" This counterfactual reset removes ownership of the original decision while preserving every current consequence. The author's synthesis is that four quantities then become visible: the incremental resources required, the distribution of future benefits, the opportunity cost of tying up those resources, and the cost of switching or stopping. Prior expenditure belongs in the calculation only if it changes one of those quantities through information, capability, contract, or path dependence [8][9].

Suppose a project has consumed 90 units and requires 10 more. "We have already spent 90" is not a reason to spend the next 10. If completion yields 30 units of expected benefit and stopping yields zero, continuing may be rational because 30 exceeds the remaining 10. If completion yields 5, continuing destroys another 5 regardless of the 90 already lost. The same history therefore permits opposite choices depending on the prospective comparison. The bias lies neither in quitting nor persisting as such; it lies in allowing the irrecoverable 90 to substitute for the marginal case.

### Waste aversion and mental accounting

Arkes and Blumer proposed that people continue partly because abandoning makes the prior expenditure feel wasted [1]. A norm such as "do not waste" is often adaptive before or during a purchase: it can deter careless acquisition, encourage maintenance, and motivate use of durable resources. Applied after recovery is impossible, however, the same norm can create more waste. Eating unwanted food, attending an unsafe event, or funding a negative-value project does not recover the original expenditure; it adds discomfort, risk, time, or money to it [1].

Mental accounting explains how the past remains psychologically active. Decision makers can treat an expenditure and its hoped-for benefit as one account that remains open until the benefit is consumed or the loss is acknowledged. Continuing promises to close the account without recording failure, while stopping crystallizes a negative balance [6]. The account can be monetary, temporal, relational, or reputational. Yet the strength of this mechanism is context dependent. Petrov and colleagues' registered replication found sunk-cost effects for both money and time in one replicated scenario and failed to reproduce an intervention that was supposed to strengthen sunk-time effects by making time more money-like [10]. The evidence therefore supports sensitivity to multiple invested resources, not a simple rule that time is always treated differently or that accounting prompts reliably intensify it [10].

### Loss framing, reference points, and risk seeking

When the original investment becomes the reference point, stopping can be represented as accepting a sure loss, while continuing offers a chance to erase it. Prospect-theory reasoning predicts that choices framed in the loss domain can become more risk seeking, so an unattractive additional gamble may look preferable to closing the account [1][6]. This account helps explain why the decision can feel like "recover or lose" even though the past cost will remain lost under either action.

Prospect theory is not a complete explanation of escalation. It describes how outcomes relative to a reference point can alter risk attitude, but it does not by itself identify why a particular project, public commitment, or self-concept becomes the relevant account. Personal responsibility, social evaluation, and completion cues can shift that reference point or add separate motives [4][5][7]. The useful role of loss framing is diagnostic: when the language of a decision centers on "getting back to break-even," "recovering what we put in," or "not taking the loss," the analysis may be anchored to a past reference point rather than to current alternatives.

### Self-justification, dissonance, and responsibility

The self-justification account begins with authorship. A person who selected an unsuccessful course has both a material problem and a threat to the belief that the choice was competent. Continuing can postpone that conflict and preserve a narrative in which the decision will ultimately be vindicated [2][4]. Staw's original experiment linked the largest allocations to the combination of personal responsibility and adverse consequences [2]. Bazerman and colleagues found escalation in groups as well as individuals and tied allocation behavior to dissonance-related responses [3]. Sleesman and colleagues' meta-analysis found that the literature contains multiple determinants consistent with self-justification, rather than reducing escalation to sunk expenditure alone [5].

Responsibility does not require one named individual. A team can defend a jointly chosen strategy, a department can protect its program, and successors can inherit an identity built around an earlier commitment. Shared responsibility can diffuse blame, but it can also create collective rationalization because admitting error challenges group competence and cohesion [3]. The key diagnostic is whether the same evidence would be interpreted differently by someone who did not authorize, champion, or publicly defend the prior decision.

### Completion effects and goal substitution

An unfinished project creates a salient gap between the present state and a concrete end state. As completion approaches, finishing can become rewarding in its own right, even if the project's original benefit is no longer worth obtaining [7]. Garland and Conlon separated percentage completion from sunk expenditure and found substantial completion effects, supporting a goal-substitution explanation: "finish the project" can replace "create value" as the operative objective [7].

Completion is not inherently irrational. Near completion, the remaining cost often falls while the available benefit remains, making continuation prospectively attractive. The measurement problem is that this legitimate marginal logic and a psychological completion drive point in the same direction. A valid review must estimate the remaining cost and realizable benefit independently, then ask whether closeness itself changes willingness after those quantities are held fixed. Percentage complete is not a value metric; a project can be 95 percent complete and still require the most difficult, uncertain, or expensive work.

### Reputation, consistency, and identity

Persistence can signal reliability. Dorison and colleagues found across three preregistered experiments that observers viewed escalators as more trustworthy, entrusted them with 29 percent more money, and that escalators returned 15 percent more in a later trust task [12]. Their public-versus-private comparison did not show more escalation under observation, which the authors interpreted as consistent with internalized reputational norms [12]. This evidence identifies a reason that apparently wasteful persistence may survive: "staying the course" can communicate loyalty and dependability even when changing course would improve the focal project's economics.

Reputation and identity broaden the cost structure. A leader may fear being labeled inconsistent; an employee may associate persistence with grit; a group may treat withdrawal as betrayal; an investor may treat selling as an admission that the thesis was wrong. Those are real psychological or social consequences, but they should be named rather than smuggled into the project's value case. Kristal and Dorison found in preregistered experiments that a public, conditional precommitment to change course at a concrete future state made later de-escalation appear more trustworthy, although it could reduce perceived confidence or motivation at project inception [16]. The result shows that reputation costs are designable, not fixed [16].

### Action, inaction, and default framing

Negative feedback creates pressure to respond, but the apparent response depends on framing. Feldman and Wong varied whether continuing or stopping was described as the active intervention. Across four experiments, escalation was higher when continuation was framed as action than when de-escalation was framed as action; their mini-meta-analysis estimated an effect of d = 0.37 [11]. Some apparent commitment is therefore action orientation: after failure, doing something can feel more responsible than allowing the status quo to continue [11].

This finding also warns against a common intervention error. Making cancellation administratively difficult casts continuation as the default and stopping as a conspicuous act. Conversely, requiring a fresh authorization makes continued funding the action that must be justified. Neither arrangement guarantees a correct decision, but the choice architecture changes which alternative receives the psychological burden of proof [11].

### Persistence can be rational

The label "sunk-cost fallacy" should not be applied whenever history and continuation occur together. Past investment can reveal private information: a team that has learned through development may now possess capabilities that outsiders lack. Continued investment may preserve option value while uncertainty resolves. Switching can require retraining, migration, contract termination, relationship repair, or temporary loss of service. Reputation can matter to future cooperation. A sequence of negative results can also be compatible with a positive expected-value process if failures were anticipated and the posterior probability remains adequate [8][9][15].

The rationality test is counterfactual and marginal. Would an otherwise identical decision maker without the historical investment choose the same continuation given the current assets, knowledge, contracts, capabilities, switching costs, and probabilities? If yes, persistence can be justified without invoking the sunk amount. If no, and the decisive reason is "because we already invested," prior cost is distorting the choice. The author's synthesis is that good analysis does not command "quit sooner"; it commands "price the future without pretending that additional sacrifice redeems the past."

## Evidence

The foundational evidence combines scenarios, field behavior, and organizational simulations. Arkes and Blumer reported that higher-paying theater subscribers attended more performances during an initial six-month period and that sunk-cost scenarios increased continuation and perceived success [1]. Staw's simulated investment decision showed the highest further allocation when participants were personally responsible for a choice that produced negative consequences [2]. Bazerman and colleagues extended the escalation paradigm to both individuals and groups; 183 participants completed a role-playing exercise, and dissonance-related measures helped account for allocation behavior beyond the experimental manipulations [3]. Together, these studies support waste avoidance and self-justification, but their differing tasks also show why "the" sunk-cost effect is not one invariant response.

The larger literature was assessed quantitatively. Sleesman and colleagues' 2012 meta-analysis reviewed determinants of escalation across theoretical perspectives and found support for multiple project, psychological, social, and structural antecedents [5]. Roth and colleagues' 2015 meta-analysis classified 98 effect sizes by decision type. It found clear evidence for a sunk-cost effect overall, while showing that magnitude and moderators depended on whether participants were deciding to use a purchased resource or to invest further in an unfinished project [6]. These syntheses support the general phenomenon while undermining claims that one mechanism or one effect size transfers unchanged across settings.

Completion research exposes a major confound. In many project scenarios, both the fraction of budget spent and the fraction of work completed rise together. Garland and Conlon manipulated them separately and found that closeness to completion could drive continuation even when sunk costs did not [7]. Their result does not disprove sunk-cost effects; it shows that a continuation decision can be misclassified when the experiment or case study has not isolated the history of expenditure from the prospective distance to a valued end state. It also explains why a project near completion can attract support for two different reasons: rationally, because little remains to be spent; psychologically, because finishing has become an independent goal [7].

Incentivized experiments have sharpened the identification problem. Ronayne and colleagues required participants to earn a lottery through a real-effort task, then unexpectedly offered a switch to a lottery with a higher chance of the same payoff. Twenty-three percent kept the earned but dominated lottery, compared with 7 percent who merely received the inferior lottery without effort; the authors concluded that endowment explained only part of the observed persistence [8]. They also developed the SCE-8 scenario scale and found that cognitive reflection and crystallized knowledge predicted behavior in their design [8]. This study provides evidence with real effort and incentives, but no single laboratory task can establish prevalence across organizational settings.

Negrini and colleagues reached a different result in a repeated, incentivized investment task. Their preregistered hypotheses predicted more additional investment after larger initial costs, but they did not observe the predicted bias and under some conditions found a reverse sunk-cost pattern: larger initial investment reduced continuation [9]. Participants nevertheless reproduced conventional sunk-cost responses in hypothetical scenarios [9]. The contrast matters methodologically. Scenario responses can capture beliefs about what one would do or culturally familiar narratives, while consequential repeated choices also involve learning, confusion, risk preference, and budget constraints. Evidence for a bias in one measurement mode does not validate every operationalization.

Time investment is similarly conditional. Petrov and colleagues conducted a high-powered registered replication and extension of Soman's money-versus-time studies with 821 online participants [10]. They reproduced a stronger money-versus-time contrast in one study, but in another found sunk-cost effects for both resources; they also failed to replicate the claim that making time easier to account for in monetary terms strengthens the sunk-time effect [10]. The evidence rejects both extremes: time is not immune to sunk-cost reasoning, and monetary and temporal investment are not guaranteed to produce identical effects.

Field studies supply realism but face confounding. Staw and Hoang used NBA draft order as a proxy for organizational investment and reported effects on playing time, trade, and survival after controlling for performance and other factors [14]. Camerer and Weber reanalyzed those data with alternative economic explanations and concluded that evidence of escalation persisted but was weaker in magnitude and duration [15]. The central lesson is not that field evidence is absent, but that prior investment often carries information and contractual consequences, so causal identification must rule out rational state dependence [14][15].

Social evidence shows that escalation can be reinforced even when observers understand the poor prospects. Dorison and colleagues' three preregistered studies, totaling 3,888 participants, found that escalators were trusted more by observers and behaved more trustworthily in a subsequent interaction [12]. The result supplies a social payoff that a narrow project ledger misses. It does not make continued funding value creating, but it explains why leaders can face a conflict between economic adaptation and a reputation for follow-through [12]. Kristal and Dorison's later preregistered program found that conditional precommitments can preserve trust when leaders de-escalate, providing evidence that the social penalty can be altered before the decision point [16].

Intervention evidence is not uniformly robust. Simonson and Staw had reported that accountability for process reduced escalation while accountability for outcomes increased it. Schulz-Hardt and colleagues conducted two close replications, strengthened the accountability manipulation in the second, and found no effects in either experiment [13]. By contrast, Feldman and Wong found across four experiments that reframing de-escalation as the active response reduced relative escalation, with a mini-meta-analytic effect of d = 0.37 [11]. These findings favor modest, testable safeguards over a promise that one debiasing instruction will reliably remove the problem.

Overall, the evidence supports five bounded conclusions. Prior investment can affect later choice [1][6][8]. Escalation has several determinants beyond sunk expenditure [4][5]. Completion, ownership, information, and switching costs can confound diagnosis [7][9][15]. Effects vary with task and measurement, and failed replications are part of the record [9][10][13]. Finally, organizational and reputational environments can reward persistence, so changing individual awareness alone may leave the incentive to escalate intact [12][16].

## Implications

### For individual decisions

The first safeguard is to separate the decision record into past and future columns. Past expenditure is recorded for learning and accountability, but the choice calculation contains only future cash flows, time, effort, probabilities, switching costs, and opportunity costs. The author's synthesis is that a useful written prompt has three lines: "What must be spent from today? What can still be gained from today? What better use of the next unit is being displaced?" If the argument for continuation cannot be restated without "already," "so far," or "too much to quit," it likely depends on history rather than marginal value [1][8][9].

A second safeguard is the outsider test. Ask what recommendation would be made by a qualified person who inherited the position but did not make the original choice. This does not make the outsider automatically correct; insiders possess information and capabilities that must be retained in the comparison. Its purpose is to expose self-justification. A material difference between the insider's and outsider's answer should trigger a search for the exact forward-looking fact that explains it [2][4].

A third safeguard is to distinguish use, progress, and completion. A prepaid ticket decision is not the same as a research project requiring another funding round. For a utilization decision, the relevant comparison is usually between the future experience of using and not using the resource. For a progress decision, estimate remaining cost, probability of technical completion, probability of commercial or personal benefit after completion, and the value of stopping or redirecting assets. Treat percentage complete as descriptive, not dispositive [6][7].

### For teams and organizations

Organizations should define continuation criteria before outcomes threaten reputations. A project charter can state measurable milestones, ranges of acceptable cost and delay, disconfirming evidence, and the authority to stop, pause, redesign, or scale. Precommitment changes the later meaning of withdrawal: following a previously declared rule can signal procedural integrity rather than inconsistency. Kristal and Dorison's evidence indicates that a public commitment tied to a concrete future state can protect trust when de-escalation occurs, although organizations must manage its possible cost to perceived confidence at launch [16].

Stage gates should require a fresh authorization rather than allow funding to continue by default. This reverses the action frame identified by Feldman and Wong: continuation becomes the choice that must be actively defended, while stopping at a failed gate becomes execution of the agreed process [11]. The review should include a base-rate forecast, an updated expected-value range, current alternatives, and a reconciliation of original assumptions with observed results. A gate is ineffective if success criteria are rewritten after every miss or if the project sponsor alone controls the evidence.

Decision ownership and evaluation should be partially separated. The original sponsor supplies context and is accountable for the quality of the initial process, but an independent reviewer or committee assesses continuation. The purpose is not to punish error. If stopping reliably destroys careers while rescue attempts preserve them, the incentive system rewards escalation regardless of training [4][5][12]. Postmortems should therefore distinguish a reasonable ex ante decision that received bad luck from a poor process, and distinguish disciplined de-escalation from abandonment without analysis.

Accountability must be used cautiously. It is plausible that explaining one's process improves decisions, but the two-study replication by Schulz-Hardt and colleagues did not reproduce earlier process-versus-outcome accountability effects [13]. Organizations should not treat "make someone accountable" as an evidence-settled cure. They can instead audit concrete process artifacts: assumptions written before the outcome, forecast ranges, milestone definitions, alternatives considered, and whether new evidence changed estimates in the predicted direction. These records are observable and reviewable even if the psychological effect of an accountability label varies.

### For capital allocation and strategy

Capital-allocation reviews should compare every incumbent project with the best current alternative, not merely with its own prior plan. Annual budget increments can hide escalation because each request looks small relative to the total already committed. Zero-basing the marginal decision asks whether the next dollar belongs in this project, a redesigned version, another project, debt reduction, or distribution. Expected-value trees help make uncertain continuation explicit by separating probabilities, payoffs, decision nodes, and information that may arrive before the next irreversible commitment.

A real-options perspective can justify staged persistence without invoking sunk costs. A limited experiment may purchase information, preserve a license, or keep a platform alive until uncertainty resolves. The condition is that the next stage has positive value as an option after its cost and probability of useful learning are counted. "We might learn something" is not enough; the review should state what signal is expected, how it changes the next decision, and the maximum price worth paying for it. This prevents option language from becoming a rhetorical shield for indefinite continuation.

Acquisitions, products, and transformation programs require special attention to identity. Public announcements, executive sponsorship, and named strategic themes can fuse a project with leadership competence. Dorison and colleagues' trust findings imply that abrupt reversal may carry a genuine reputation cost [12]. The response is not to hide that cost inside optimistic project forecasts. It is to communicate the decision rule, identify what changed, preserve transferable assets, and explain why updating is evidence of reliability. Conditional commitments established in advance can make that message more credible [16].

### For interpreting field evidence

Analysts should resist labeling any correlation between prior investment and continued use as a fallacy. A high acquisition price may reflect higher expected quality. An early draft pick may receive more development because private scouting information remains informative. A long relationship may contain accumulated trust and coordination capital. A mature software system may have high migration cost. Each produces persistence associated with history, but the cause can be forward-looking [14][15].

A stronger design seeks variation in sunk expenditure that does not change quality, information, contracts, or switching costs. It measures the future alternatives directly and tests whether the historical amount predicts choice after those variables are held constant. Where that is impossible, claims should say "consistent with a sunk-cost effect" rather than asserting one. Camerer and Weber's reanalysis of NBA evidence illustrates why: plausible alternative explanations weakened but did not eliminate the original pattern [15].

### For learning from failed projects

Ignoring sunk costs in the current choice does not mean forgetting them. Past expenditure is essential for estimating process quality, calibration, and future costs. The separation is temporal: do not spend the next unit to justify the last one, but do study the last unit to improve the next forecast. A postmortem can ask which signals arrived late, which assumptions were untested, whether milestone definitions moved, and which incentives suppressed exit. That information belongs in future priors and governance, not as a debit that the current project must repay.

The worst outcome is a system in which stopping destroys the evidence needed to learn while continuation conceals it. Preserve data, decision logs, prototypes, contracts, and technical lessons even when the focal objective is abandoned. This reduces the felt choice between "complete success" and "total waste." Some residual value may be real, but it should be valued explicitly rather than used to claim that every prior expenditure will be recovered.

### A practical decision test

The author's synthesis is a six-question test:

1. Which past resources are genuinely unrecoverable under every current option?
2. What incremental costs, benefits, probabilities, and opportunity costs begin now?
3. Would a new owner without responsibility for the original choice continue?
4. Is persistence explained by value, learning, option value, or switching cost, or by waste, vindication, completion, and reputation?
5. What evidence would cause a pause, redesign, or stop, and was that threshold set before the next result?
6. Has the decision been framed so that both continuing and stopping are active alternatives requiring the same standard of proof?

This test does not presume that exit is brave or continuation is foolish. It makes the governing reason falsifiable. Persistence is defensible when the marginal case survives after the history is removed; escalation is suspect when the history is the case.
