# Cognitive Biases -- Systematic Errors That Shape Every Human Decision

Cognitive biases are systematic patterns of deviation from rationality in
human judgment and decision-making. First catalogued by psychologists
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in their heuristics-and-biases research
program starting in the early 1970s, these biases arise from mental
shortcuts (heuristics) that the brain uses to process the overwhelming
volume of information it encounters. While heuristics are generally
adaptive -- enabling fast decisions with limited cognitive effort -- they
produce predictable, recurring errors that affect everything from
investment decisions to medical diagnoses to everyday social judgments.
Understanding cognitive biases is not about eliminating them, which is
impossible, but about recognizing when they are likely to operate and
building systems to counteract their worst effects.

## Background

### The Heuristics and Biases Program

Before Kahneman and Tversky, the dominant model of human decision-making
was Expected Utility Theory -- the idea that people are rational actors
who weigh probabilities and outcomes to maximize expected value. Herbert
Simon challenged this with his concept of bounded rationality in the
1950s: humans have limited time, information, and cognitive resources,
so they satisfice rather than optimize.

Kahneman and Tversky operationalized bounded rationality through their
heuristics and biases research program. In their landmark 1974 paper
"Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," published in
Science, they identified three foundational heuristics --
representativeness, availability, and anchoring -- and demonstrated how
each produces systematic and predictable biases. The program expanded
over the following decades to catalogue over 200 distinct cognitive
biases, organized into categories spanning judgment, decision-making,
memory, and social cognition.

### System 1 and System 2

A unifying framework for understanding cognitive biases is Kahneman's
dual-process model, popularized in "Thinking, Fast and Slow" (2011).
System 1 operates automatically, quickly, with little or no effort and
no sense of voluntary control. It is the source of most heuristic
processing. System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental activities
that demand it, including complex computation and deliberate reasoning.

Cognitive biases arise when System 1 generates an intuitive answer that
System 2 fails to override. The brain defaults to the path of least
resistance. This is not a design flaw -- most of the time, System 1's
quick judgments are good enough. The problem is that certain situations
reliably produce errors, and these errors are not random: they are
systematic, predictable, and shared across populations.

## Core Biases by Category

The biases below are organized by the underlying heuristic or cognitive
process that produces them. Each category describes a class of errors
that share a common mechanism.

### Biases from the Representativeness Heuristic

The representativeness heuristic leads people to judge probability by
how much A resembles B -- by similarity rather than by base rates or
statistical reasoning.

**Base Rate Neglect.** People ignore the underlying probability of an
event when given specific descriptive information. In the classic
"Linda problem," participants judged it more likely that Linda is a
bank teller AND a feminist than that she is a bank teller alone,
violating the conjunction rule of probability. The description of Linda
resembled a feminist, overriding the statistical impossibility that a
conjunction could be more probable than either constituent.

**The Gambler's Fallacy.** The belief that past random events influence
future ones: after seeing five reds on a roulette wheel, the gambler
believes black is "due." In reality, each spin is independent. This bias
extends to investing (believing a stock must rebound after a decline),
sports (the "hot hand" fallacy is the inverse), and everyday reasoning
about luck and probability.

**Stereotyping.** Judging an individual based on the categories they
appear to belong to -- a direct application of the representativeness
heuristic to social cognition. While stereotypes sometimes contain
statistical truth about group averages, they systematically fail when
applied to individuals and override more diagnostic individual
information.

### Biases from the Availability Heuristic

The availability heuristic leads people to judge frequency or probability
by how easily examples come to mind. Events that are vivid, recent, or
emotionally charged are judged more likely than they actually are.

**Availability Bias (Salience).** After a plane crash, people
overestimate the danger of flying and underestimate the danger of
driving, despite driving being far more dangerous per mile. Media
coverage amplifies this effect -- news reports make rare but dramatic
events disproportionately available to memory.

**Recency Bias.** Recent events are weighted more heavily than older ones
in judgment and forecasting. In investing, this leads to chasing recent
performance and extrapolating short-term trends. In management,
employees are evaluated on their most recent work rather than their
overall performance.

**Survivorship Bias.** Focusing on the survivors of a process while
overlooking those who did not make it. The classic example: studying
successful companies to find their "secrets" while ignoring the
thousands of companies that followed the same playbook and failed. The
author's own observations suggest this is one of the most pervasive and
difficult-to-notice biases because the failures are invisible by
definition.

### Biases from Anchoring and Adjustment

Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of
information offered (the "anchor") when making decisions. Once an anchor
is set, subsequent judgments are made by adjusting away from it, and
adjustments are typically insufficient.

**Anchoring Effect.** In a classic experiment, participants spun a wheel
that landed on a random number, then estimated the percentage of African
nations in the UN. Those who spun a 10 estimated 25%; those who spun a
65 estimated 45%. The completely irrelevant number served as an anchor.
In negotiation, the first offer anchors the entire negotiation range. In
pricing, the original price anchors perceptions of a discount's value.

**Framing Effect.** How a choice is presented -- as a gain or a loss --
dramatically affects the decision, even when the objective outcomes are
identical. Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated that people are risk-averse
when options are framed as gains but risk-seeking when the same options
are framed as losses. A treatment with a "90% survival rate" is more
appealing than one with a "10% mortality rate."

**Default Effect.** People tend to stick with the default option, even
when the stakes are high. Organ donation rates are dramatically higher
in countries with opt-out defaults than opt-in defaults, despite
comparable attitudes toward donation. The default serves as an implicit
anchor and an endorsement, and changing requires System 2 effort.

### Biases from Confirmation and Belief Perseverance

These biases protect existing beliefs from disconfirming evidence. They
operate selectively on how people search for, interpret, and remember
information.

**Confirmation Bias.** The tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and
recall information that confirms pre-existing beliefs while ignoring or
discounting contradictory evidence. This is arguably the most dangerous
cognitive bias because it compounds: each piece of confirming evidence
strengthens the belief, making it harder to overturn. In investing, it
produces the classic pattern of falling in love with a stock and
filtering out bad news. In science, it is the reason for double-blind
studies and independent replication.

**Hindsight Bias.** After an event occurs, people believe they "knew it
all along" and overestimate the predictability of the outcome. This
erodes the ability to learn from surprises because the unexpected
becomes retrospectively obvious. In organizations, it produces blame for
decisions that turned out badly even when the decision was reasonable
given the information available at the time.

**Overconfidence Effect.** The systematic overestimation of one's own
abilities, knowledge, or the accuracy of one's predictions. Studies
consistently show that when people say they are 90% confident, they are
correct roughly 50-70% of the time. Overconfidence is particularly
strong for difficult tasks and for experts operating outside their
domain of expertise. It is a primary driver of entrepreneurial failure
(most founders believe they will succeed while most startups fail) and
investment underperformance (most active managers believe they can beat
the market while most cannot).

### Social and Group Biases

Cognitive biases also arise from social dynamics and group membership.

**Groupthink.** The tendency for cohesive groups to prioritize consensus
over critical evaluation. Dissenting views are suppressed or
self-censored, alternatives are not fully explored, and the group
converges on a decision that no individual member would have made alone.
Irving Janis documented groupthink in major foreign policy failures
including the Bay of Pigs invasion and the escalation of the Vietnam War.

**Bandwagon Effect.** The tendency to adopt beliefs or behaviors because
many other people hold them, regardless of their merit. This is distinct
from groupthink -- it does not require group membership, only
observation of others. In financial markets, this drives bubbles and
crashes. In technology, it drives adoption of inferior standards.

**In-group Bias.** The tendency to favor members of one's own group over
outsiders on dimensions ranging from resource allocation to perceived
trustworthiness. This bias operates automatically and can be triggered
by even arbitrary group assignments (the "minimal group paradigm").
It underlies discrimination, political polarization, and intergroup
conflict.

**Fundamental Attribution Error.** The tendency to attribute others'
behavior to their character or disposition while attributing one's own
behavior to situational factors. When someone cuts you off in traffic,
they are reckless and selfish. When you cut someone off, you are late
and stressed. This asymmetry makes conflict intractable because each
side interprets the same event through fundamentally different causal
frameworks.

### Memory Biases

Human memory is not a recording device; it is reconstructed each time
it is accessed, and the reconstruction process introduces systematic
distortions.

**Peak-End Rule.** People judge an experience largely by how they felt at
its peak (the most intense point) and at its end, rather than by the
average or sum of every moment. A painful medical procedure with an
extended but less painful ending is remembered as less painful than a
shorter procedure that ends abruptly at peak pain, even though the total
pain experienced is greater.

**Rosy Retrospection.** People remember past events more positively than
they experienced them at the time. Vacations, jobs, and relationships
are recalled as better than moment-by-moment ratings would indicate. This
bias likely serves an adaptive function -- filtering out quotidian
negativity makes past experience a more useful guide for future action.

**Source Amnesia.** Remembering information but forgetting where it came
from, making it impossible to evaluate its reliability. This is a key
mechanism by which misinformation persists: people remember the claim
but not that it came from an unreliable source, and the claim gradually
acquires the status of common knowledge.

## Evidence and Research Foundation

The empirical foundation for cognitive biases rests on decades of
controlled experiments showing that these errors are systematic,
predictable, and resistant to correction through education alone.

Kahneman and Tversky's 1974 Science paper presented experiments
demonstrating representativeness, availability, and anchoring biases
across diverse populations including statistically trained
participants. The biases persisted even when participants were warned
about them -- knowing about a bias does not inoculate against it.

Subsequent research has confirmed and extended the catalogue. A 2024
systematic review of cognitive biases in fact-checking identified 221
distinct biases from the literature, of which 39 were directly relevant
to truthfulness assessment. The field has expanded from the original
three heuristics to cover biases in memory, social cognition, attention,
and perception.

The dual-process model (System 1 / System 2) has received substantial
empirical support from cognitive psychology and neuroscience. fMRI
studies show distinct neural correlates for intuitive versus deliberate
processing, and individual differences in System 2 engagement predict
susceptibility to cognitive biases.

An alternative perspective, ecological rationality (Gigerenzer et al.),
argues that many "biases" are not errors at all but adaptive responses
that perform well in natural environments and only fail in the
artificial contexts of laboratory experiments. Under this view, what
appears as a bias in a contrived probability problem may represent an
optimal strategy in the information environment humans evolved in. Both
perspectives agree that the patterns are real and predictable; they
disagree on whether to call them errors.

## Implications

### For Decision-Making

The primary implication is that unaided human judgment is systematically
unreliable in specific, predictable ways. The solution is not to
eliminate intuition -- it is fast, cheap, and often correct -- but to
identify the situations where intuition is likely to fail and deploy
System 2, checklists, or statistical models as a backstop.

### For Investing

Value investing can be understood as a systematic framework designed to
counter specific cognitive biases: price anchoring (ignore the market
price anchor and compute intrinsic value independently), confirmation
bias (seek out disconfirming evidence, require a margin of safety),
overconfidence (diversify, acknowledge what you do not know), and loss
aversion (deploy capital when others are fearful). The entire Graham-
Buffett framework is, in a real sense, applied cognitive bias mitigation.

### For Organizations

Organizations amplify certain biases (groupthink, information cascades,
incentive-driven myopia) and can mitigate others through structured
decision processes. Pre-mortems (imagining a future failure and working
backward to its causes), red teams (dedicated critics), and blind
evaluation (removing identifying information before judging) are
organizational techniques derived from cognitive bias research.

### For Everyday Life

Understanding cognitive biases provides a diagnostic toolkit for
examining one's own thinking. The most important meta-skill is
recognizing when one is in a high-bias situation: when stakes are high,
information is ambiguous, emotions are engaged, or social pressure is
present. In these situations, slowing down and applying structured
reasoning -- even something as simple as asking "what would convince me
I am wrong?" -- can substantially improve decision quality.
