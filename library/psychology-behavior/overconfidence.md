# Overconfidence -- Why Humans Systematically Overestimate Themselves and How It Warps Every Decision

Overconfidence is the cognitive bias in which people hold unwarranted
certainty in their own abilities, knowledge, and judgments. It is not
a single error but a family of three distinct distortions: people
overestimate their own performance, overplace themselves relative to
others, and overestimate the precision of their beliefs. Across
laboratories, markets, and battlefields, overconfidence has been blamed
for stock market bubbles, entrepreneurial failure, wars, and
disastrous corporate acquisitions, earning Scott Plous's verdict that
no problem in judgment and decision making is more prevalent or more
potentially catastrophic.

## Background

The systematic study of overconfidence began in the 1960s and 1970s
as psychologists moved beyond the assumption of rational economic man.
Before Kahneman and Tversky reframed decision research, the dominant
model in economics held that people weigh outcomes by their
probabilities and arrive at optimal choices. Early calibration
experiments shattered that assumption. When researchers asked
participants to answer factual questions and then state how confident
they were that each answer was correct, people who reported being 100
percent certain were right only about 80 percent of the time. This
gap between subjective confidence and objective accuracy, documented
across hundreds of studies, became one of the most robust findings in
the psychology of judgment.

The intellectual lineage runs from early psychophysics through the
heuristics-and-biases program that Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky
launched in the 1970s. The concept of calibration -- whether stated
probabilities match observed frequencies -- came from signal
detection theory and meteorology, where forecasters needed to know
whether a 70 percent chance of rain actually rained seven times in
ten. When behavioral researchers applied the same standard to human
judgment, they found systematic miscalibration: people placed
inappropriately tight confidence intervals around their estimates
and assigned too much probability to answers they believed correct.

The field matured through a series of conceptual refinements. For
decades, researchers treated overconfidence as a single phenomenon
measured in different ways, which produced apparently contradictory
results. Some studies found people overestimated their absolute
performance; others found they ranked themselves above average;
still others found their confidence intervals were too narrow. The
breakthrough came when Don Moore and Paul Healy published "The Trouble
With Overconfidence" in 2008 in Psychological Review, arguing that the
literature had conflated three distinct constructs. They proposed a
taxonomy -- overestimation, overplacement, and overprecision -- that
has since become the standard framework. The taxonomy resolved many
contradictions: people can overestimate their own performance while
underplacing themselves relative to others on the same task, because
the two judgments depend on different information about self and
others.

Parallel literatures developed in finance and management. Ulrike
Malmendier and Geoffrey Tate's work on CEO overconfidence, beginning
in the mid-2000s, showed that classifying a chief executive as
overconfident based on their personal investment in company stock
predicted a 65 percent increase in the odds of making an acquisition
and a tendency to destroy value in those deals. In evolutionary
biology, Dominic Johnson and James Fowler's 2011 Nature paper
demonstrated that overconfidence could be evolutionarily stable --
populations of overconfident individuals could outcompete accurate
ones under a wide range of conditions, helping to explain why a bias
that produces so many catastrophic outcomes persists in the human
repertoire.

The problem that overconfidence solves and creates is this: humans
must act under irreducible uncertainty, with limited information
about their own abilities, the abilities of competitors, and the
accuracy of their beliefs. Confidence serves as the motivational fuel
for action, but unwarranted confidence becomes the engine of
disastrous decisions. Understanding overconfidence means understanding
both why it exists -- its evolutionary and psychological roots -- and
why it fails -- the systematic distortions it produces in modern
environments that differ sharply from the ancestral conditions under
which it evolved.

## Core Concepts

### The Three Faces of Overconfidence

The Moore and Healy (2008) taxonomy distinguishes three forms of
overconfidence that the earlier literature had conflated. Each is a
distinct psychological phenomenon with different causes and
consequences.

Overestimation is the tendency to believe one's own performance,
ability, or prospects are better than they objectively are. A student
who expects to score 90 percent on a test and scores 70 percent has
overestimated. Overestimation is measured absolutely, without
reference to others. It is most pronounced on difficult tasks, where
people have the most room to be wrong and the least feedback to
correct themselves. The hard-easy effect captures this pattern: people
overestimate performance on hard tasks and underestimate it on easy
ones, because their self-assessments regress toward a moderate
central estimate while actual performance swings more widely.

Overplacement, sometimes called the better-than-average effect, is
the belief that one ranks higher relative to others than one actually
does. In a classic result, more than 80 percent of drivers rate
themselves as above average in skill. Overplacement is a comparative
judgment -- it requires estimating both one's own position and the
distribution of others. Because people generally know more about
themselves than about others, their estimates of others are more
regressive, pulled toward the center of the distribution. On easy
tasks where most people perform well, this regression makes people
overplace themselves; on difficult tasks where most perform poorly,
the same mechanism produces underplacement.

Overprecision is the tendency to hold excessive certainty in the
accuracy of one's beliefs, producing confidence intervals that are
too narrow. When asked to give a 90 percent confidence interval for
the date of a historical event, people produce ranges that capture
the true answer only about 50 percent of the time. Overprecision
reflects a failure to appreciate how much one does not know -- an
unwarranted belief that one's best guess is close to the truth. Of
the three forms, overprecision is the most directly relevant to
forecasting and risk assessment, because it causes people to assign
too little probability to outcomes far from their central estimate.

### The Dunning-Kruger Effect

A closely related phenomenon, popularized by Justin Kruger and David
Dunning in 1999, is that the least competent individuals tend to
overestimate their ability the most. Their account proposed a dual
burden: the skills needed to perform well on a task are the same
skills needed to accurately evaluate that performance. Incompetent
people therefore lack not only the ability to succeed but the
metacognitive insight to recognize their failure, leading them to
hold inflated self-assessments.

The Dunning-Kruger effect has generated significant scientific
controversy. Critics, including Krueger and Mueller (2002), argued
that the pattern is largely a statistical artefact of regression
toward the mean combined with the better-than-average effect: if
everyone estimates their ability near the population mean, those
with low actual ability will necessarily show larger overestimation
gaps. Subsequent work using independent measures of performance and
self-estimation to control for regression found that the effect
shrinks substantially and may be smaller than originally reported.
A 2020 Registered Report in Royal Society Open Science found that
metacognitive bias was positively associated with performance --
poor performers were actually less confident, not more -- and
concluded the classic pattern is largely a regression artefact.
The debate remains open, but the practical implication stands: low
performers are poorly calibrated, whether because of genuine
metacognitive failure or statistical structure.

### Evolutionary Origins

If overconfidence produces so many catastrophic outcomes, why does it
persist? Johnson and Fowler (2011) offered an evolutionary answer.
Their mathematical model showed that overconfidence can be
evolutionarily stable -- meaning a population of overconfident
individuals can resist invasion by accurate strategists -- as long
as the resources at stake in conflicts exceed twice the cost of
competition. Under these conditions, overconfident individuals
challenge more often, win more conflicts they would otherwise avoid,
and accumulate resources at a rate that outweighs the occasional
costly loss.

The evolutionary account explains overconfidence as a former
adaptation that has become maladaptive in many modern environments.
In ancestral small-group settings with repeated interactions and
visible consequences, exaggerated confidence could deter rivals,
attract allies, and motivate persistence in valuable endeavors.
Robert Trivers argued that self-deceptive overconfidence would be
especially effective because genuine belief generates more
convincing signals than conscious bluffing, reducing the behavioral
leakage that would reveal deception to opponents. The modern
environment differs sharply: consequences are often delayed, hidden,
or socialized across large organizations, so the calibration
mechanisms that might have checked overconfidence in ancestral
groups no longer operate reliably.

### The Inside View and the Planning Fallacy

Kahneman and Tversky identified the planning fallacy in 1979:
people generate optimistic forecasts for task completion by
extrapolating from an inside view -- a detailed, scenario-based
simulation of the focal project -- while neglecting the outside view
derived from distributions of outcomes in comparable past projects.
A student estimating when they will finish their thesis focuses on
the specific steps ahead and arrives at a best-case timeline,
ignoring the fact that most theses take far longer than their authors
predict.

The inside view is a manifestation of overconfidence because it
relies on the belief that one's specific case will proceed as
planned, despite abundant evidence that comparable cases did not.
The planning fallacy is robust across domains: construction projects,
software development, infrastructure budgets, and academic writing
all show systematic underestimation of time and cost, often by 30 to
70 percent. The mechanism connects directly to overprecision --
people place too much confidence in their specific plan and too
little weight on the distribution of historical outcomes.

### The Illusion of Control

Overconfidence is reinforced by the illusion of control, the
tendency to believe one can influence outcomes that are determined
by chance. Ellen Langer identified this phenomenon in 1975,
showing that people behave as if uncontrollable events are
controllable when the situation contains features associated with
skill, such as choice, competition, or familiarity. The illusion
of control amplifies overestimation because it leads people to
attribute expected positive outcomes to their own skill rather than
to luck, and to discount the role of random factors in determining
results.

### Debiasing Techniques

Several evidence-based techniques reduce overconfidence, though none
eliminates it. Reference class forecasting, developed by Bent
Flyvbjerg from Kahneman and Tversky's outside-view concept, replaces
the inside view with base rates from comparable past projects.
Instead of estimating a project's cost and timeline from its
specific plan, one identifies a reference class of similar projects,
gathers their actual outcomes, and uses that distribution as the
forecast. The premortem, articulated by Gary Klein, asks decision
makers to assume a project has failed and then generate reasons why.
This prospective-hindsight technique has been shown to reduce
overconfidence by forcing consideration of failure modes that
optimistic planning ignores. Calibration training, in which people
receive feedback on the accuracy of their probability assessments
over many trials, improves overprecision specifically, though the
gains are domain-specific and decay without continued practice.

## Evidence

### Calibration Studies and the Overprecision Effect

The foundational evidence for overprecision comes from calibration
research spanning five decades. In the standard paradigm,
participants answer factual questions and state their confidence
that each answer is correct. The aggregate result is remarkably
consistent: people who are 100 percent confident are correct only
about 80 percent of the time, and those who are 90 percent
confident are correct about 75 percent of the time. This gap --
subjective confidence consistently exceeding objective accuracy --
constitutes the overprecision effect. Lichtenstein, Fischhoff, and
Phillips reviewed more than 100 calibration studies in their 1982
meta-analysis and found the effect across domains ranging from
general knowledge to medical diagnosis. The effect is strongest
for difficult items and attenuates, though does not disappear, for
easy ones, consistent with the hard-easy effect.

Calibration research also established that overprecision is not
uniform across populations. Experts in their domains show better
calibration than novices, and meteorologists, who receive rapid
feedback on their forecasts, achieve near-perfect calibration for
short-term weather predictions. This contrast is informative: it
shows that overprecision is not an immutable flaw but a consequence
of insufficient feedback and learning. Where the environment provides
frequent, clear, outcome-based feedback, calibration improves.

### The Better-Than-Average Effect

Evidence for overplacement comes from studies of comparative
self-assessment. The driving study, replicated many times, finds
that 80 percent or more of respondents rate themselves as above-average
drivers, a statistical impossibility. Alicke (1985) demonstrated that
the effect generalizes across traits and abilities: people rate
themselves above average on positive dimensions and below average
on negative ones. Moore and Healy (2008) showed that overplacement
is not universal but depends on task difficulty. In their experiments,
participants overplaced themselves on easy tasks -- where most people
perform well -- but underplaced themselves on difficult tasks, where
most perform poorly. This reversal supports the differential
information account: people know more about themselves than about
others, so their estimates of others regress more toward the
population mean, producing overplacement when the mean is high and
underplacement when it is low.

The better-than-average effect has been documented across cultures,
though its magnitude varies. Cross-cultural research suggests the
effect is stronger in individualistic Western societies and weaker,
though still present, in collectivistic East Asian societies.
Interestingly, the effect reverses for some traits: people
underplace themselves on rare positive attributes and rate
themselves below average on common negative ones, a pattern
consistent with the statistical structure of regression rather than
with a pure motivational account.

### CEO Overconfidence and Corporate Investment

Malmendier and Tate (2008) provided some of the most cited evidence
for overconfidence in a real-world, high-stakes setting. They
classified CEOs as overconfident when they held company stock options
past their optimal exercise date -- a behavioral marker indicating
the executive believed their company was undervalued relative to the
market. They found that the odds of undertaking an acquisition were
65 percent higher for overconfident CEOs, and the effect was largest
for diversifying acquisitions -- deals that take the firm outside its
core competence. Critically, the market reacted more negatively to
announcements of acquisitions by overconfident CEOs, and these deals
were more likely to destroy shareholder value.

Their 2015 Journal of Economic Perspectives review with Geoffrey
Tate synthesized a decade of follow-on research. The evidence
showed that overconfident CEOs overinvest when they have abundant
internal cash flow but are constrained by external financing, they
prefer internal funding to external equity issuance because they
believe their stock is undervalued, and their acquisitions deliver
worse returns than those of non-overconfident peers. The
Malmendier-Tate framework is significant because it connects
overconfidence to measurable corporate outcomes through observable
behavioral proxies, moving the bias from the laboratory to the
boardroom with quantifiable financial consequences.

### Entrepreneurial Entry and Market Bubbles

Overconfidence has been documented as a driver of entrepreneurial
market entry, where it produces both the beneficial effect of
encouraging risk-taking and the detrimental effect of leading most
entrants to fail. Camerer and Lovallo (1999) showed that when
business entry decisions depended on skill rather than chance, far
too many firms entered, because each entrant overestimated their
relative ability. The result: excess entry, market crowding, and
predictable failure for the majority. Subsequent experimental and
field studies, including Rotondi (2024) and Chwolka (2023),
confirmed that overconfidence primarily drives entry by individuals
already embedded in relevant networks, suggesting the bias interacts
with opportunity structure.

Overconfidence is also implicated in asset price bubbles. The
Chicago Fed's review of bubble research noted that bubbles are not
rare black-swan events but occur relatively frequently, and that
overvaluation can lead to overinvestment in the bubble sector.
Daniel Kahneman and other behavioral finance researchers argue that
overconfidence, combined with the illusion of control and
self-attribution bias, generates the pattern by which rising prices
reinforce confident predictions, drawing more capital into inflated
assets until the correction arrives. The dot-com bubble of the late
1990s and the housing bubble of the mid-2000s both featured
participants expressing high confidence in continued price
appreciation while discounting the historical base rates of
mean-reversion.

### Debiasing Evidence

Flyvbjerg (2006, 2008) demonstrated that reference class forecasting
substantially reduced cost and time overruns in large infrastructure
projects. His analysis of 258 major transportation projects found
that standard inside-view forecasting underestimated costs by an
average of 30 to 50 percent, while reference-class-based estimates
were far more accurate. The method has since been adopted by
governments including the UK and Denmark for major project
appraisal. Klein's premortem, tested in organizational settings,
reduced overconfidence in project success predictions by 30 percent
in one frequently cited study, though the magnitude of the effect
varies with implementation. Calibration training studies show that
repeated feedback on probability assessments can narrow the gap
between stated confidence and observed accuracy, but gains are
domain-specific and require sustained practice to maintain.

## Implications

### For Investors and Financial Decision-Makers

Overconfidence is among the most damaging biases in investing
because it produces excessive trading, under-diversification, and
the refusal to acknowledge error. Overconfident investors trade more
frequently because they believe their analysis gives them an edge,
and the additional trading costs reduce net returns. They concentrate
portfolios in a small number of positions because they are
confident in their picks, taking on uncompensated risk. They hold
losing positions too long because selling would require admitting
the original decision was wrong. The behavioral finance literature
documents that individual investors, particularly men, trade more
and earn lower returns than less active investors, a pattern
directly attributable to overconfidence in stock-picking ability.

For institutional investors, the Malmendier-Tate evidence on CEO
overconfidence has practical application. Overconfident CEOs
overpay for acquisitions, prefer debt to equity, and overinvest when
cash flow is abundant. An investor evaluating a company led by a
highly confident, acquisition-oriented CEO can anticipate value
destruction and adjust expectations accordingly. The behavioral
signals -- late option exercise, optimistic guidance, a
preferences for internal funding -- provide observable proxies for
the bias. This connects overconfidence directly to capital
allocation decisions and the assessment of management quality,
making it a practical tool for fundamental investors.

### For Entrepreneurs and Business Leaders

Overconfidence is a double-edged force in entrepreneurship. On one
side, it provides the motivation and resolve needed to start
ventures that face long odds of success. Without some degree of
overestimation, few people would undertake the enormous effort and
risk of founding a company. On the other side, it leads to
predictable failure when founders underestimate the difficulty,
overestimate market demand, and underprepare for the challenges of
execution. The evidence on excess entry shows that overconfidence
produces more firms than markets can sustain, with most entrants
failing.

For business leaders, the practical response is to separate the
motivational function of confidence from the forecasting function.
Confidence in one's ability to execute can coexist with calibrated
estimates of the probability of success. The tools that achieve this
separation -- reference class forecasting for projections, premortems
for risk identification, decision journals for calibration tracking
-- do not eliminate confidence but redirect it from forecasting to
execution. A founder who uses base rates to understand that most
comparable ventures fail, while maintaining full confidence in their
ability to outwork the odds, holds a more adaptive posture than one
who simply assumes success is likely.

### For Forecasters and Decision-Makers Under Uncertainty

Philip Tetlock's research on forecasting, summarized in
Superforecasting, found that the most accurate forecasters are not
the most confident but the most calibrated -- those who assign
probabilities that match observed frequencies. Overconfidence
produces the opposite pattern: excessive certainty in specific
outcomes, too-narrow confidence intervals, and too little weight on
alternative scenarios. The practical implication for anyone making
consequential decisions under uncertainty is to treat confidence as
a hypothesis to be tested rather than a fact to be acted on.

The reference class and premortem techniques, while powerful, have
limits. Reference class forecasting requires a sufficient base of
comparable historical data, which may not exist for genuinely novel
projects. Premortems generate failure-mode lists but provide no sense
of magnitude -- whether the identified risks add up to a 2 percent
problem or a 40 percent one. The most effective approach combines
both: use the reference class to establish the scale of uncertainty,
then use the premortem to identify specific failure modes within
that range. This outside-in sequence leverages the strengths of
each method while compensating for their individual weaknesses.

### For Organizational Design

Organizations can either amplify or mitigate overconfidence through
their structure and incentives. Cultures that reward bold claims and
punish admission of error produce systematic overconfidence because
individuals learn that optimistic projections are rewarded and
honest assessments are penalized. The Volkswagen emissions scandal,
in which engineers designed software to cheat emissions tests under
pressure to meet unrealistic performance targets, illustrates how
organizational incentives can convert individual overconfidence into
institutional fraud. Cultures that separate forecasting from
execution, require pre-mortems before major commitments, and reward
honest post-mortems create conditions where overconfidence is
checked rather than amplified.

The governance implication is that overconfidence is not purely an
individual problem. Organizations that rely on individual vigilance to
counteract bias will fail, because the social pressures that produce
overconfidence operate on the same individuals who are supposed to
check it. Structural safeguards -- independent risk assessment,
reference-class-based project appraisal, and decision journals that
survive the optimism of the planning phase -- are necessary because
they do not depend on the biased individual recognizing their own
bias in the moment.

## Common Pitfalls

A common error in applying overconfidence research is assuming that
all confidence is unwarranted. Confidence based on genuine expertise,
repeated feedback, and strong evidence is well-calibrated, not
overconfident. The meteorologist who predicts rain with 70 percent
confidence and is correct 70 percent of the time is not overconfident
-- they are calibrated. The distinction matters because telling
experts their confidence is a bias can discourage appropriate
conviction and produce the opposite error of chronic underconfidence.

A second pitfall is conflating the three forms of overconfidence.
A person who accurately estimates their own performance but ranks
themselves too highly relative to others shows overplacement without
overestimation. A person with realistic relative rankings but
excessively narrow confidence intervals shows overprecision without
overplacement. Debiasing techniques that target one form may not
affect the others, because the underlying mechanisms differ. The
taxonomy is not academic hair-splitting -- it determines which
intervention will work.
