# The EU AI Act Regulates Uses and Models Through Layered Duties, Not a Single Risk Ladder

The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act combines prohibited practices, requirements for high-risk uses, transparency duties, and a separate regime for general-purpose AI models; it does not assign every AI product to one simple tier. Compliance follows the actor, intended purpose, model or system classification, lifecycle stage, and application date, while Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 changed material parts of the original 2024 timetable and enforcement architecture.[1][2][4]

## Background

The AI Act began as a European Commission proposal in April 2021 and became Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 after negotiation by the European Parliament and the Council. It was published in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024 and entered into force on 1 August 2024. The regulation creates harmonised rules for placing AI systems on the EU market, putting them into service, and using them in the Union. Its stated purposes join internal-market uniformity, human-centric and trustworthy AI, protection of health, safety, fundamental rights, democracy, the rule of law, and the environment, and support for innovation.[1][3]

The law follows the European Union's product-regulation method in important respects. It allocates obligations to economic operators, defines essential requirements for specified systems, uses technical documentation and conformity assessment, requires post-market monitoring, and gives market-surveillance authorities corrective and sanctioning powers. Yet it also reaches beyond conventional product safety. Annex III identifies uses in areas such as biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice, and democratic processes where an AI system can materially affect fundamental rights or access to important opportunities. Article 5 separately prohibits enumerated practices whose legal defect is not cured by better documentation or testing.[1][3]

The familiar description of four risk levels - unacceptable, high, transparency, and minimal or no risk - is a useful orientation but not a complete legal decision tree. Article 5 defines prohibited practices. Article 6 and Annexes I and III define high-risk systems. Article 50 creates transparency duties for certain systems whether or not they are high-risk. Chapter V regulates general-purpose AI models, including a more demanding subset with systemic risk. One provider can therefore face model-level duties for a general-purpose model, system-level transparency duties for an application built on that model, and high-risk requirements if the application's intended purpose falls within Article 6.[1][3]

The original regulation deliberately phased application. Definitions, AI-literacy duties, and most prohibitions began applying on 2 February 2025. Governance provisions and obligations for general-purpose AI model providers began applying on 2 August 2025, while the Commission's power to enforce those model obligations began on 2 August 2026. Article 50 transparency duties and many other provisions also began applying on 2 August 2026. The initial text gave other high-risk provisions longer transition periods, but the final timetable now has to be read through the 2026 amendment.[1][4][6]

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was adopted on 8 July 2026, published on 24 July, and entered into force on 27 July 2026. It postponed the Annex III high-risk regime to 2 December 2027 and the regime for high-risk AI embedded in products covered by Annex I to 2 August 2028. It also extended selected simplified obligations from small and medium-sized enterprises to small mid-cap companies, simplified the AI-literacy obligation, changed database-registration rules, expanded testing opportunities, added a guarded legal basis for processing special-category personal data to detect and correct bias, added prohibitions concerning non-consensual sexually explicit or intimate material and child sexual abuse material, and enlarged the AI Office's supervision of specified AI systems.[2][4][12]

The amendment did not repeal the risk-based structure or make compliance optional until 2027. Prohibited practices already applicable, general-purpose model duties, transparency requirements, governance rules, and enforcement powers continue on their own dates. The two new prohibitions and a transition for machine-readable marking by certain synthetic-content systems already on the market apply on 2 December 2026. High-risk dates were moved because the high-risk regime depends heavily on standards, conformity-assessment capacity, guidance, and operator preparation; the postponement affects that part of the architecture rather than the Act as a whole.[2][4][10][12]

The Act also has material territorial reach. It applies to providers that place AI systems or general-purpose AI models on the Union market, deployers located in the Union, and providers or deployers outside the Union where output produced by the system is used in the Union, subject to the regulation's exclusions and qualifications. The legal inquiry therefore does not end with the developer's incorporation or the location of a server. Market access, deployment, intended purpose, and use of output can connect an AI value chain to the Union framework.[1]

The historical sequence matters because statements that were accurate in July 2024 can now be legally stale. A compliance analysis dated after 27 July 2026 should identify whether it describes the original act, the amending regulation, or the consolidated text. It should then map each obligation to its own application date. Treating "the AI Act applies in August 2026" as a single event obscures obligations that applied earlier, high-risk rules that apply later, and the difference between a rule's application and the availability of enforcement powers.[1][2][4]

## Core Concepts

### Classification begins with practices, intended purpose, and legal function

The first classification question is whether the conduct falls within an Article 5 prohibition. The original set covers specified forms of harmful manipulation or deception, exploitation of vulnerabilities, social scoring that leads to defined detrimental or unfavourable treatment, certain criminal-risk prediction based solely on profiling or personality traits, untargeted scraping of facial images to create or expand facial-recognition databases, emotion recognition in workplaces and education subject to stated exceptions, biometric categorisation that infers specified sensitive characteristics, and real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement except under narrow conditions and safeguards. The Commission's 2025 guidelines explain its interpretation but are non-binding; authoritative interpretation remains with the Court of Justice.[1][8]

The 2026 Omnibus added prohibitions targeting systems that generate or manipulate non-consensual sexually explicit or intimate material and child sexual abuse material. Those additions apply from 2 December 2026 and include qualifications stated in the amended text. Their placement in Article 5 is significant: they are bans, not high-risk requirements that a provider can satisfy through conformity assessment. A defensible classification record should quote the relevant paragraph, test every element and exception, and retain the factual basis for the conclusion.[1][2][4]

If no prohibition applies, Article 6 supplies two routes into the high-risk regime. Under Article 6(1), an AI system is high-risk when it is a safety component of a product, or is itself a product, covered by listed Union harmonisation legislation and that product must undergo third-party conformity assessment. Under Article 6(2), systems in Annex III are high-risk subject to the Act's classification qualifications. Annex III concerns intended uses, not technology labels: the same model can support a low-impact drafting tool and a high-risk employment, education, credit, law-enforcement, or migration application. Classification must therefore be maintained when the intended purpose, context, or design changes.[1][11]

Article 6 permits a provider to conclude that some Annex III systems are not high-risk when they do not pose a significant risk of harm to health, safety, or fundamental rights and do not materially influence decision-making, subject to specified conditions. Profiling of natural persons remains high-risk. A provider relying on the exception must document the assessment and can be reviewed by a market-surveillance authority. This is not a general "low impact" self-exemption; it is a reasoned classification under statutory criteria, backed by records and exposed to correction and penalties if misused.[1]

Transparency duties form another axis. Article 50 applies to specified systems that interact directly with natural persons, generate synthetic content, perform emotion recognition or biometric categorisation, or generate or manipulate deepfake and certain public-interest text content. Duties include disclosure to people, machine-readable marking and detectability for synthetic output, and context-appropriate labelling by deployers, with exceptions and qualifications. These rules began applying on 2 August 2026, while certain systems placed on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026 to meet the Article 50(2) marking duty.[1][4][10][11]

Systems outside the prohibitions, high-risk categories, and Article 50 duties generally face no mandatory system-specific requirement under the AI Act beyond provisions that apply more broadly, such as the revised AI-literacy duty where relevant. Other Union or national law can still govern data protection, consumer protection, employment, discrimination, intellectual property, product safety, cybersecurity, or sectoral activity. "Minimal risk under the AI Act" does not mean unregulated, lawful for every purpose, or safe in fact.[1][3]

### High-risk requirements create an evidence-bearing lifecycle

Articles 8 through 15 state the core requirements for high-risk AI systems. They cover continuous risk management; data and data governance; technical documentation; automatic record-keeping; transparency and instructions for deployers; human oversight; and appropriate accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity. These requirements are interconnected. Risk management identifies and evaluates foreseeable risks across the lifecycle; data governance addresses the relevance, representativeness, quality, provenance, and processing of data where applicable; documentation explains how the system was built and assessed; logs preserve operational evidence; instructions define proper use; human oversight makes intervention possible; and testing supports claims about performance and controls.[1][11]

A risk-management file should not be a static launch memo. The Act describes an iterative process planned and run throughout the system's lifecycle. Providers must consider risks under intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, test controls, and incorporate information from post-market monitoring. Testing should establish that the system performs consistently for its intended purpose and complies with the requirements. The compliance object is therefore a maintained system and control process, not only a frozen model binary.[1]

Provider obligations translate those requirements into accountable acts. A high-risk provider must ensure conformity, identify itself, operate a quality-management system, keep required documentation and logs, complete the applicable conformity assessment, draw up an EU declaration of conformity, affix CE marking where required, register the system, take corrective action, report required incidents, and cooperate with competent authorities. Conformity assessment may rely on internal control in some cases and involve a notified body in others, depending on the category, harmonised standards, and applicable product legislation.[1][11]

Documentation serves several audiences and functions. Technical documentation allows assessment of design, intended purpose, development, data, performance, risk controls, and compliance. Instructions give deployers enough information to use the system lawfully and understand its capabilities, limitations, human-oversight measures, input requirements, and expected performance. Automatically generated logs support traceability. A quality-management system assigns procedures and responsibilities across design, testing, supplier control, change management, corrective action, monitoring, and record retention. Evidence should be internally consistent: a risk identified in analysis should appear in testing, instructions, monitoring indicators, and escalation rules rather than disappear between documents.[1]

Post-market monitoring closes the lifecycle. Article 72 requires providers to establish and document a system that actively and systematically collects, documents, and analyses relevant performance data throughout the high-risk system's lifetime. Article 73 requires reporting of serious incidents to market-surveillance authorities under specified deadlines and conditions. Providers must investigate, correct, withdraw, disable, or recall systems where appropriate and notify relevant parties. Monitoring without a decision owner, threshold, or corrective path is therefore incomplete because the law connects collected evidence to action.[1][11]

### Duties follow operator roles rather than a generic company label

A provider develops an AI system or general-purpose model, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts a system into service under its name or trademark. A deployer uses an AI system under its authority, other than personal non-professional use. Importers and distributors perform market-access and supply-chain functions. An authorised representative carries specified duties for a provider outside the Union. These roles can coexist inside one corporate group and can change when an actor rebrands, substantially modifies, or changes the intended purpose of a system.[1]

Deployers of high-risk systems must take appropriate technical and organisational measures to follow the instructions, assign competent and properly resourced human oversight, ensure input data under their control is relevant and sufficiently representative for the intended purpose, monitor operation, retain logs under their control for the required period, suspend use and notify relevant actors when risk appears, and report serious incidents through the specified chain. Additional duties can include workplace notice, registration by public authorities, and fundamental-rights impact assessment for designated public or essential-service uses.[1][11]

This division prevents the provider from controlling facts it cannot observe and prevents the deployer from treating procurement as a transfer of all responsibility. The provider controls design, validation, documentation, and instructions. The deployer controls the actual setting, operator training, input practices, local workflow, oversight, and response to operational signals. Contracts can allocate information and support, but they cannot erase statutory duties. A practical compliance interface should specify what evidence the provider supplies, what telemetry and incidents the deployer returns, who preserves logs, and how either party triggers suspension or corrective action.[1]

Importers verify before market placement that required conformity assessment and documentation exist, the CE marking and declaration of conformity are present where applicable, and an authorised representative has been appointed when required. They must identify themselves, preserve records, protect compliance during storage or transport, stop placement when they have sufficient reason to suspect non-conformity or falsification, and cooperate with authorities. Distributors perform analogous checks appropriate to their position and must not make a non-conforming high-risk system available.[1][11]

Article 25 addresses shifts in responsibility. An importer, distributor, deployer, or other third party can become the provider of a high-risk system if it places its name or trademark on the system, makes a substantial modification while the system remains high-risk, or changes the intended purpose of another system so that it becomes high-risk. The original provider must then supply necessary information and technical access in specified circumstances, subject to protections for intellectual property and confidential business information. Change control is therefore a legal classification control, not only an engineering practice.[1][11]

### General-purpose model duties are separate from high-risk system duties

Chapter V regulates general-purpose AI models because one model can be integrated into many downstream systems and its provider may hold information that downstream system providers need. Article 53 requires model technical documentation, information and documentation for downstream providers, a policy to comply with Union copyright law and rights reservations, and a sufficiently detailed public summary of training content using the Commission template. Providers established outside the Union generally need an authorised representative before placing the model on the Union market, subject to the Act's terms.[1][6]

A qualified free and open-source release can receive exemptions from some Article 53 documentation duties when its parameters, including weights, architecture, and information on model use, are publicly available and other conditions are met. The exemption does not apply to a general-purpose model with systemic risk. "Open source" is therefore not a universal exclusion from Chapter V; the provider must test the precise statutory conditions and preserve its reasoning.[1][6]

A general-purpose model is classified as presenting systemic risk when it has high-impact capabilities evaluated through appropriate technical tools and indicators, including a statutory presumption tied to cumulative training computation, or when the Commission designates it under the Act's criteria. Providers must notify the AI Office when the threshold or designation conditions are met, subject to the procedure for arguing that a presumed model does not present systemic risk. The regime focuses on model capabilities and reach rather than on one downstream use.[1]

Article 55 adds duties for systemic-risk model providers: perform standardised model evaluations, including adversarial testing; assess and mitigate possible systemic risks at Union level; track, document, and report serious incidents and possible corrective measures; and ensure adequate cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure. The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice offers voluntary compliance paths. Its Transparency and Copyright chapters address Article 53, while Safety and Security addresses Article 55. Following the code can supply a structured demonstration, but the legal obligation remains in the regulation and alternative adequate means remain possible.[1][6][7]

The model and system regimes can stack. A company supplying a general-purpose model can owe Chapter V duties. A downstream company integrating that model into recruitment software can become the high-risk system provider and owe Chapter III duties. An employer using that software can be a deployer and owe Article 26 duties. If the model provider also supplies the finished system, or entities in the same undertaking do so, the 2026 governance changes can bring specified system oversight within the AI Office's competence. The correct compliance map follows each artifact and role through the value chain.[1][2][5]

### Enforcement is distributed but coordinated

The AI Office, national competent authorities, and the European Data Protection Supervisor divide supervision. The AI Office enforces rules for general-purpose models and, after the Omnibus changes, specified systems based on those models or integrated into very large online platforms or very large online search engines. National market-surveillance authorities enforce other AI-system rules, including prohibitions and high-risk requirements. The European Data Protection Supervisor supervises AI systems used by Union institutions, bodies, offices, and agencies.[2][5][9][10]

Authorities can request information and documentation, access data and source code under statutory conditions, conduct monitoring and investigations, demand corrective measures, and restrict, withdraw, or recall non-compliant or risky systems. The AI Office can request access for model evaluation, require measures from general-purpose model providers, and use sanctioning powers. Fundamental-rights authorities receive cooperation and information mechanisms, while the European AI Board supports consistent application across Member States.[1][5][9]

Penalty ceilings reflect the infringement category. Article 99 sets an upper limit of EUR 35 million or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices, and EUR 15 million or 3 percent for listed operator and notified-body violations, subject to the Act's rules, proportionality, and treatment of undertakings. Incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information can attract a lower category of fine. Article 101 permits fines for general-purpose model providers up to 3 percent of total worldwide annual turnover in the preceding financial year for intentional or negligent infringement of relevant duties or failure to comply with Commission measures. A ceiling is not an automatic tariff: nature, gravity, duration, cooperation, prior infringement, size, and other statutory factors affect the decision.[1][5][11]

## Evidence

### The consolidated regulation shows that one ladder is an incomplete model

The strongest evidence is the consolidated legal text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended on 27 July 2026. Its method is legislative rather than empirical: it defines actors, regulated objects, requirements, procedures, authorities, and sanctions. Reading Article 5, Article 6, Article 50, and Chapter V together establishes that the Act uses several overlapping triggers. Prohibitions attach to practices; high-risk rules attach to product-safety classification or intended uses; transparency duties attach to specified interactions and generated content; and general-purpose model duties attach at the model layer.[1]

This structure explains why the four-level pyramid is educational but legally lossy. A general-purpose model with systemic risk is not simply a fifth system-risk tier, because the model can feed many systems with different uses. A chatbot can face Article 50 disclosure without being high-risk. An Annex III system can be high-risk even when its underlying model is ordinary. A prohibited practice remains prohibited even if built with a model that independently complies with Chapter V. The finding is structural: compliance must be represented as a matrix of object, role, use, lifecycle stage, and date.[1][3]

The consolidated text also supplies evidence that compliance is operational. High-risk requirements connect risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, instructions, oversight, performance, robustness, and cybersecurity. Provider obligations connect those requirements to conformity assessment, registration, corrective action, monitoring, and incident reporting. Deployer, importer, distributor, and value-chain provisions distribute responsibility to actors that control different evidence and decisions. The text therefore rejects both a model-only safety assessment and a paperwork-only certification view.[1][11]

### The 2026 amendment is a controlled change case

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 is primary evidence of how the legislature adjusted implementation after parts of the Act had begun applying. Its recitals and operative amendments cite burdens on smaller operators, delayed support instruments and standards, overlap with sectoral product law, testing needs, bias-detection constraints, and governance alignment. The response did not discard the Act. It changed selected dates, procedures, proportionality rules, legal bases, prohibitions, and supervision.[2]

The timetable change is measurable. Annex III high-risk rules now apply on 2 December 2027; Annex I product-related high-risk rules apply on 2 August 2028. Meanwhile, general-purpose model duties applied from 2 August 2025, enforcement and Article 50 transparency began on 2 August 2026, and the new prohibitions apply on 2 December 2026. This staggered result is confirmed independently by the official Service Desk timeline, the Commission's Act overview, the Omnibus announcement, and the July 2026 enforcement release.[3][4][10][12]

The Omnibus also demonstrates that simplification can coexist with new safeguards. It extends selected simplified forms and proportionate obligations to small mid-cap companies, changes AI-literacy wording, and simplifies registration, while adding two prohibited practices and a safeguarded basis for special-category data processing in bias detection and correction. It extends AI Office oversight to specified systems and expands regulatory-sandbox and real-world-testing routes. The legal effect must be read in the regulation; the Commission's summary is useful corroboration but is not a substitute for the enacted text.[2][12]

### Implementation instruments translate duties into inspectable records

The Commission's 2025 guidelines on general-purpose AI model providers explain who may be a provider, what placing a model on the market means, when modification can create provider status, which exemptions may apply, and what the Commission expects as obligations take effect. The guidelines identify technical documentation, downstream information, copyright policy, and public training-content summaries as baseline model-provider duties, with evaluations, incident reporting, risk mitigation, and cybersecurity added for systemic-risk models. Their method is administrative interpretation; courts retain ultimate authority over the regulation.[6]

The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice provides a second implementation instrument. Independent experts prepared it through a multi-stakeholder process, and the Commission and AI Board assessed it as an adequate voluntary tool for demonstrating compliance. Its three chapters convert Article 53 and Article 55 into model-documentation, copyright, safety, and security practices. This is evidence of an implementation route, not proof that signing alone conclusively establishes compliance or that non-signatories violate the law.[7]

The prohibited-practices guidelines provide worked legal explanations and examples for Article 5. Their stated purpose is consistent and uniform application across the Union. They are useful because terms such as manipulation, exploitation of vulnerability, social scoring, and biometric practices require factual interpretation, but the Commission expressly describes the guidelines as non-binding and reserves authoritative interpretation to the Court of Justice. That limitation should travel with any compliance claim based on them.[8]

### The enforcement launch shows institutions moving from design to supervision

The Commission's 31 July 2026 release records the start of enforcement on 2 August 2026 for general-purpose model obligations, applicable prohibitions, and transparency rules. It identifies the AI Office, national authorities, and the European Data Protection Supervisor as the principal enforcement actors and describes support from a 60-member Scientific Panel. The release also confirms that the AI Office can enforce duties concerning documentation, downstream information, copyright policy, training-content summaries, and systemic-risk controls.[10]

The Commission's enforcement page describes investigative powers, model evaluation and access, corrective measures, restrictions on public availability, and penalty categories. The market-surveillance page separately records national powers to monitor remotely, access documentation, data sets, and source code under legal conditions, request corrective measures, cooperate on investigations, and impose penalties. Together, these official descriptions show that the regime has moved beyond legislative adoption into active supervisory capacity, although the long-term consistency and effectiveness of enforcement remain empirical questions.[5][9]

The evidence has boundaries. As of 28 September 2026, high-risk rules postponed to 2027 and 2028 have not produced a mature body of enforcement outcomes. Commission pages describe current policy and institutional practice but do not replace the binding text. Codes and guidelines reduce ambiguity but are not judgments. Claims about compliance cost, deterrence, innovation, or rights protection therefore remain partly prospective. What is verified now is the enacted framework, the amended calendar, published compliance instruments, and the activation of identified enforcement powers.[1][2][4][5]

## Implications

### For system and model providers: build a dated classification file before controls

A provider should begin with a versioned legal inventory, not a generic AI risk score. For each model and system, record the artifact, intended purpose, geographic route to market, provider identity, users, affected persons, Article 5 analysis, Article 6 route, Annex references, Article 50 trigger, Chapter V status, systemic-risk analysis, and applicable dates. Attach the legal text and guidance version used. Reassess when intended purpose, branding, integration, model capability, customer segment, or application date changes.[1][2]

The worst operational failure is to build an expensive control program around the wrong legal object. A model card does not complete high-risk system conformity. A high-risk technical file does not satisfy a general-purpose model provider's copyright and downstream-information duties. A transparency label does not legalise a prohibited practice. Classification should therefore produce separate workstreams that later connect through shared evidence and change control.[1][6]

For a high-risk system, the evidence architecture should mirror the statute. Maintain a risk register linked to hazards, affected groups, foreseeable misuse, test cases, controls, residual risk, instructions, and monitoring indicators. Preserve data provenance and governance decisions. Map each Article 8-15 requirement to an owner, artifact, verification method, and release gate. Ensure the declaration of conformity, registration, CE marking where applicable, and supplier evidence describe the same system version. A change log should state whether a modification affects intended purpose, performance, risk, conformity assessment, or provider status.[1]

Testing should answer claims that the provider actually makes. Accuracy needs a population, task, metric, uncertainty, and acceptance threshold. Robustness needs defined perturbations, failures, and recovery behaviour. Cybersecurity needs a threat model tied to the system architecture. Human oversight needs evidence that operators can understand outputs, detect anomalies, intervene, and stop or override the system under realistic time and workload constraints. Post-market monitoring should specify signals, denominators, review frequency, escalation thresholds, responsible decision-makers, and corrective options.[1]

General-purpose model providers need two connected but distinct packages. The first supports Article 53: model technical documentation, downstream integration information, copyright policy, and public training-content summary. The second, when systemic-risk rules apply, supports Article 55: evaluation protocols, adversarial testing, systemic-risk taxonomy, mitigation evidence, serious-incident process, and cybersecurity controls for model and infrastructure. Code-of-Practice adoption can organise this work, but governance should track the binding article behind each commitment and retain evidence for deviations or alternative compliance.[1][6][7]

### For deployers and purchasers: procurement is the start of responsibility

A deployer should not ask only whether a vendor says the product is compliant. It should identify the provider and exact system version; obtain instructions, performance limits, intended-purpose boundaries, conformity documents, monitoring interfaces, logging behaviour, and incident contacts; and test whether the local use matches the provider's assessed use. Procurement should reserve audit access, change notice, security information, incident cooperation, data-retention terms, and exit or suspension rights that allow the deployer to perform its own duties.[1]

Local workflow can change legal and factual risk. A recruiting system used to prioritise applications, a credit model used as the dominant basis for a decision, or a biometric tool placed in a new setting can have consequences not represented by a vendor benchmark. The deployer controls input quality, operator training, human-oversight conditions, decision policy, affected-person communication, and monitoring of actual outcomes. It should document who can override, what independent evidence is required, how appeals and corrections feed back into monitoring, and what event triggers suspension.[1][11]

Public authorities and designated essential-service deployers should plan for fundamental-rights impact assessment and registration duties where applicable. The assessment should identify the process in which the system is used, categories of affected persons, possible effects on rights, oversight measures, complaint and remedy paths, and residual risks. It should connect to data-protection assessment where relevant without assuming that one document automatically satisfies the other legal regime.[1]

### For importers, distributors, and integrators: make chain-of-custody legal

Importers and distributors need release checks that block market placement or availability when required evidence is missing or appears false. Product identifiers, provider and representative details, declarations, certificates, instructions, storage or transport conditions, and record-retention duties should be maintained in a system that can answer an authority quickly. Commercial pressure to ship is not a defence to a statutory stop obligation.[1][11]

Integrators should treat substantial modification and changed intended purpose as formal gates. Before rebranding, fine-tuning, connecting new data, changing decision thresholds, adding autonomy, or moving a system into another use, determine whether provider status transfers or a previously non-high-risk system becomes high-risk. Record why a change is or is not substantial, what evidence was revalidated, and whether the provider's instructions still cover the final system. Contract clauses should require necessary technical cooperation while respecting protected intellectual property and confidential information.[1]

### For regulators and courts: consistency requires shared evidence without erased jurisdiction

The distributed enforcement model creates a coordination problem. The AI Office has central competence for general-purpose models and specified related systems; national authorities supervise other systems; sectoral, data-protection, consumer, labour, and fundamental-rights bodies retain adjacent mandates; and the European Data Protection Supervisor covers Union bodies. A single deployment can therefore produce evidence relevant to several authorities. Common identifiers, interoperable incident records, reasoned referrals, and clear lead-authority rules can reduce duplication without collapsing legally distinct powers.[1][5][9]

Enforcement should preserve the difference between rule, guidance, code, and adjudication. Commission guidelines can promote consistency but do not bind the Court of Justice. Voluntary codes can demonstrate a route to compliance but do not amend the regulation. Harmonised standards can provide presumptions within their scope but do not legalise a prohibited use. Public decisions should identify the binding provision, factual findings, evidence, operator role, applicable date, and corrective rationale so that later actors can distinguish precedent from policy explanation.[1][7][8]

The postponed high-risk dates create an implementation window, not an evidence-free pause. Authorities can use it to complete guidance, standards, notified-body capacity, sandboxes, templates, and cross-border procedures. Providers can use it to test control systems before legal application. The success criterion is not the number of documents produced but whether an authority can reconstruct classification, conformity, deployment, monitoring, incident response, and change history for the same system.[2][4][12]

### For boards, investors, and analysts: model exposure by duty and remedy

A board should ask which products and business processes fall under which provisions, when each duty applies, which entity is the operator, what evidence is missing, and what business action an authority could require. Fine ceilings are only one exposure. A restriction, withdrawal, recall, forced corrective measure, loss of market access, documentation request, model evaluation, or required interface change can affect revenue and operations more directly than a monetary penalty.[1][5]

Investors should distinguish four kinds of regulatory sensitivity. First is prohibited-use exposure, where a business line may be legally unavailable. Second is high-risk compliance exposure, including conformity, documentation, monitoring, and deployer friction. Third is general-purpose model exposure, where downstream information, copyright policy, training-content disclosure, evaluations, systemic-risk controls, and AI Office access matter. Fourth is transparency exposure, where product design and generated-content workflows must support disclosure and marking. A company can have several exposures at once.[1][3]

The 2026 Omnibus should not be modelled as deregulation in one scalar. It delayed high-risk application and reduced selected administrative burdens, but it also added prohibitions, a bias-detection legal basis with safeguards, extended AI Office oversight, and preserved other application dates. The economic effect depends on the company's role and products. A small integrator may benefit from simplified documentation; a synthetic-content provider may face a new prohibition or marking transition; a general-purpose model provider remains under duties already in application; a high-risk vendor gains preparation time but not exemption.[2][4][12]

### For researchers and public reporting: state the legal date and level of authority

Every account of the AI Act should state an as-of date and distinguish the original regulation from the consolidated post-Omnibus text. It should identify whether a claim comes from enacted law, a Commission guideline, a voluntary code, an authority's enforcement summary, or a court decision. It should also distinguish entry into force, application, enforcement power, transitional compliance, and later high-risk dates. These are different legal events.[1][2][4]

Risk labels should not replace legal analysis. "Limited risk" is common explanatory language, but Article 50 contains specific triggers and duties. "Unacceptable risk" refers to enumerated prohibitions, not any system an observer considers dangerous. "High risk" follows Article 6 and the annexes, not a company's internal severity score. "Systemic risk" in Chapter V concerns designated general-purpose models and is not interchangeable with high-risk system status. Precise terminology keeps factual reporting separate from policy advocacy.[1][3]

The author's synthesis is a five-question audit. What regulated object is involved: practice, system, model, or generated content? Which operator controls the relevant act: provider, deployer, importer, distributor, representative, or public authority? Which legal trigger applies: prohibition, Article 6, Article 50, Chapter V, or another law? What lifecycle evidence must exist: classification, risk management, testing, documentation, conformity, monitoring, incident response, or corrective action? Which version and date govern? If those questions cannot be answered from current records, the organisation does not yet have an auditable compliance position.[1][2][5]
