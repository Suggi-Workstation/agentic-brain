# International Law and Treaties -- The Decentralized Framework of Global Order

International law is the body of rules that governs relations between sovereign states, created not by a single legislature but through the consent of the states it binds. Its primary instruments are treaties -- written agreements between states governed by international law -- supplemented by customary practice, general principles, and judicial decisions. The system lacks a central enforcement authority, relying instead on state consent, institutional pressure, and the strategic costs of noncompliance. Understanding how international law is made, interpreted, and enforced -- and why it frequently fails to constrain powerful states -- is essential to understanding the architecture of global order.

## Background

The intellectual lineage of international law stretches back to the Roman
concept of jus gentium, the law of nations, which governed relations
between Romans and foreigners. But the modern system traces its
foundations to the seventeenth-century Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius,
whose 1625 work De Jure Belli ac Pacis (On the Law of War and Peace)
argued that states, like individuals, are bound by natural law
principles that constrain their conduct even in the absence of a
sovereign above them. Grotius drew on the Roman idea that promises
must be kept -- pacta sunt servanda -- and extended it to the
relations between states, establishing the principle that treaties,
once concluded in good faith, create binding obligations.

The system developed incrementally over the following centuries. The
1648 Peace of Westphalia, which ended the Thirty Years' War,
established the principle of state sovereignty -- that each state
possesses exclusive authority over its territory and domestic affairs.
This sovereignty became the foundational axiom of international law:
states are equal, independent, and not subject to any higher
authority. Every subsequent development in the system has had to
reckon with this tension between the ambition of universal rules and
the reality of sovereign independence.

The nineteenth century saw the rapid growth of treaty-making as
European states expanded trade and diplomatic relations. The Congress
of Vienna (1815) and the Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907 began to
codify rules on diplomacy, warfare, and dispute settlement,
establishing the precedent that multilateral conferences could create
binding legal obligations. The Hague Conferences also proposed the
creation of a permanent international court, an idea that would not
be realized until the establishment of the Permanent Court of
International Justice in 1922 under the League of Nations.

The catastrophic failure of the League of Nations to prevent the
Second World War led to the founding of the United Nations in 1945.
The UN Charter became the constitutional document of the modern
international legal order, codifying principles such as the sovereign
equality of states, the prohibition on the use of force, and the
peaceful settlement of disputes. Article 92 of the Charter
established the International Court of Justice (ICJ) as the principal
judicial organ of the UN, and the ICJ Statute -- particularly its
Article 38 -- became the authoritative statement of the sources of
international law: treaties, customary international law, general
principles recognized by civilized nations, and, as subsidiary means,
judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified
publicists.

The most significant twentieth-century development in treaty law was
the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT), adopted in 1969
and entering into force in 1980. The product of two decades of work
by the International Law Commission (ILC), the VCLT codified the
rules governing how treaties are formed, interpreted, amended, and
terminated. It codified pacta sunt servanda in Article 26, declared
that treaties conflicting with peremptory norms (jus cogens) are void
in Article 53, and established the rules on reservations, invalidity,
and termination. Though some major states -- including the United
States -- have not ratified the VCLT, most of its provisions are
recognized as customary international law and bind all states.

The post-1945 era also saw the proliferation of specialized
international institutions. The General Agreement on Tariffs and
Trade (GATT), established in 1947, evolved into the World Trade
Organization (WTO) in 1995, creating a dispute settlement system that
became the most effective enforcement mechanism in international
economic law. The European Court of Human Rights, the Inter-American
Court of Human Rights, and the International Criminal Court (ICC)
extended international law into domains previously reserved to
domestic jurisdiction. Each of these institutions operates within the
framework of treaties -- the ICC was created by the Rome Statute, the
WTO by the Marrakesh Agreement -- demonstrating that treaties remain
the primary instrument through which states construct international
legal obligations.

## Core Concepts

### Sources of International Law

The formal sources of international law are enumerated in Article
38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, which
directs the Court to apply: (a) international conventions, whether
general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the
contesting states; (b) international custom, as evidence of a general
practice accepted as law; (c) the general principles of law
recognized by civilized nations; and (d), as subsidiary means for the
determination of rules of law, judicial decisions and the teachings
of the most highly qualified publicists. This list is hierarchical in
practice: treaties are the primary source, custom fills gaps and
binds non-parties, general principles provide fallback rules, and
judicial decisions and scholarship serve as evidence of what the law
is rather than independent sources of obligation.

The Article 38 framework is widely accepted but not exhaustive. The
ILC's 2018 conclusions on the identification of customary
international law clarified the criteria for establishing custom, and
modern scholarship recognizes additional sources such as soft law,
resolutions of international organizations, unilateral state acts,
and jus cogens norms that sit above the Article 38 hierarchy. These
developments reflect the reality that international law has evolved
beyond its classical formulation while retaining consent as its
foundational basis.

### Treaties and the Law of Treaties

A treaty is defined by Article 2(1)(a) of the VCLT as an
international agreement concluded between states in written form and
governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument
or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular
designation. Treaties may be bilateral (binding two states) or
multilateral (binding multiple parties). The terminology varies --
conventions, covenants, protocols, agreements, and exchanges of
notes are all treaties -- but the legal principles governing them are
uniform.

The law of treaties, codified in the VCLT, governs the entire
lifecycle of a treaty. Treaties are formed through negotiation,
signature, and ratification, with ratification being the act by
which a state expresses its definitive consent to be bound. The
principle of pacta sunt servanda, codified in Article 26, provides
that every treaty in force is binding on the parties and must be
performed by them in good faith. This is the foundational rule of
treaty law and arguably the oldest rule in the entire international
legal system. A state may not invoke the provisions of its internal
law as justification for failure to perform a treaty (Article 27).

Treaties are interpreted according to the rules in Articles 31-33 of
the VCLT, which provide that a treaty shall be interpreted in good
faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the
terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object
and purpose. The preparatory work of the treaty (travaux
preparatoires) and the circumstances of its conclusion may be used as
supplementary means of interpretation where the text is ambiguous or
obscure, or leads to a manifestly absurd or unreasonable result. This
textual approach, prioritizing the ordinary meaning of the words,
distinguishes international treaty interpretation from some domestic
traditions that emphasize legislative intent or purpose more heavily.

### Customary International Law

Customary international law (CIL) arises from the conjunction of two
elements: state practice (the objective element) and opinio juris
(the subjective element). State practice refers to the general and
consistent practice of states, while opinio juris is the belief that
such practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of
law requiring it. The ICJ articulated this dual requirement in the
North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969), holding that not only must
the acts concerned amount to a settled practice, but they must also
be carried out in such a way as to be evidence of a belief that this
practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law.

Customary law is significant because it binds all states, not only
those that have consented to a particular treaty. A state that has
not ratified the VCLT is nonetheless bound by those provisions that
have become customary law. Similarly, the prohibition on the use of
force, codified in Article 2(4) of the UN Charter, is also a
customary norm binding on all states regardless of UN membership.
This creates a tension with the principle of sovereignty: states can
find themselves bound by rules to which they never expressly
consented. The persistent objector rule mitigates this tension
partially, allowing a state that has consistently and openly objected
to a rule during its formation to remain unbound, but this exception
is narrow and rarely successfully invoked.

### Sovereignty and State Consent

Sovereignty is the foundational principle of international law. Each
state is sovereign, equal, and independent, and no state is subject
to the jurisdiction of another state's courts or the authority of a
superior government. The UN Charter enshrines the sovereign equality
of all its members. This principle has a direct structural
consequence: international law is created by the consent of states,
not imposed by a legislature. Treaties bind only those states that
ratify them (pacta tertiis nec nocent nec prosunt -- treaties neither
bind nor benefit third parties), and customary law is theoretically
grounded in the implied consent of states through their practice and
opinio juris.

This consent-based structure is both the strength and the weakness of
international law. It ensures legitimacy, because states are bound
only by rules they have accepted. But it also means that enforcement
depends on the willingness of states to comply, since there is no
central authority to compel obedience. The tension between
sovereignty and the ambition of universal rules is the defining
structural feature of the system and the source of most of its
enforcement failures.

### Jus Cogens and Peremptory Norms

Jus cogens (compelling law) refers to peremptory norms of general
international law from which no derogation is permitted. Article 53
of the VCLT provides that a treaty is void if, at the time of its
conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general
international law. Article 64 provides that if a new peremptory norm
emerges, any existing treaty conflicting with it becomes void and
terminates. The VCLT did not provide a closed list of peremptory
norms but gave a method for their identification: a norm is jus
cogens if it is accepted and recognized by the international
community of states as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is
permitted.

Commonly cited examples of jus cogens norms include the prohibition
on genocide, the prohibition on torture, the prohibition on slavery,
the prohibition on aggression, and the prohibition on crimes against
humanity. These norms represent the highest hierarchy in
international law and represent a significant qualification of state
consent: a state cannot contract out of a jus cogens norm by treaty,
and a treaty attempting to do so is void ab initio. The ILC's 2022
Draft Conclusions on Peremptory Norms clarified the criteria for
identification and the consequences of peremptory norms beyond treaty
law, including their applicability to customary law and unilateral
state acts.

### International Institutions and Dispute Settlement

International law is administered and enforced through a network of
institutions, the most prominent being the United Nations and its
organs. The UN Security Council, under Chapter VII of the Charter,
has the authority to determine threats to international peace and
security and to impose binding measures including sanctions (Article
41) and military action (Article 42). The Security Council is the
only international body with the legal authority to compel member
states to use force, though in practice this authority has been
constrained by the veto power of the five permanent members.

The International Court of Justice adjudicates disputes between
states, but its jurisdiction is consensual: a state must consent to
be sued, either through a treaty compromissory clause, an ad hoc
agreement, or a declaration under the Optional Protocol. The ICJ's
judgments are binding on the parties to the case but have no formal
stare decisis effect (Article 59 of the ICJ Statute). Enforcement of
ICJ judgments depends on the Security Council, which may make
recommendations or decide on measures to give effect to the judgment
(Article 94 of the UN Charter) -- but this route is again subject to
the veto.

The WTO dispute settlement system, established under the Dispute
Settlement Understanding (DSU) of 1994, is the most effective
enforcement mechanism in international economic law. It features
compulsory jurisdiction, ad hoc panels, an Appellate Body, and a
reverse consensus rule under which panel and Appellate Body reports
are adopted unless all members vote against adoption. This system
adjudicated over 600 disputes from 1995 onward, but has been in
crisis since December 2019 when the United States blocked
appointments to the Appellate Body, rendering it unable to hear
appeals. Over 50 WTO members have joined the Multi-Party Interim
Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA) as a provisional bypass, but
the crisis illustrates the vulnerability of even the most effective
international institutions to the obstruction of a single powerful
state.

## Evidence

### The North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (1969)

The North Sea Continental Shelf cases (Federal Republic of Germany v.
Denmark; Federal Republic of Germany v. Netherlands) are the
foundational modern authority on the formation of customary
international law. The dispute concerned the delimitation of the
continental shelf between Germany and its neighbors. Denmark and the
Netherlands argued that the equidistance principle in Article 6 of
the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Continental Shelf was binding on
Germany as customary law. Germany, which had not ratified the
Convention, disputed this.

The ICJ held that Article 6 had not attained customary law status at
the relevant time. In doing so, it articulated the dual requirement
for the formation of custom: state practice and opinio juris. The
Court held that the number of ratifications (39 states) was not
adequately representative or widespread, particularly given that
many ratifying states had no coastlines or no immediate interest in
continental shelf delimitation. The Court emphasized that the
practice of states whose interests were specially affected was
particularly relevant. On opinio juris, the Court examined 15 cases
of delimitation by equidistance and found that the states involved
had not acted out of a sense of legal obligation but for practical
or negotiational reasons. The Court's formulation -- that states
must feel that they are conforming to what amounts to a legal
obligation -- remains the authoritative statement of the opinio
juris requirement.

### The Nicaragua v. United States Case (1986)

In Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua
(Nicaragua v. United States), the ICJ addressed the relationship
between treaty law and customary law, and the content of the
prohibition on the use of force. The United States had withdrawn from
the Court's jurisdiction after the Court ruled it had jurisdiction,
and did not participate in the merits phase. The Court held that
even where a treaty (the UN Charter) and customary law address the
same subject matter, the customary norm continues to exist
independently and can bind states that are not parties to the treaty
or in situations not covered by the treaty.

The Court found that the prohibition on the use of force was both a
treaty norm (Article 2(4) of the UN Charter) and a customary norm
binding on all states. It held that the United States had violated
this prohibition by mining Nicaraguan harbors and supporting
contra forces. The case demonstrated that customary international
law can operate independently of treaty obligations and that even
the most powerful states can be found in violation of international
law -- while also demonstrating the enforcement problem, since the
United States refused to comply with the judgment and the Security
Council was unable to act due to the US veto.

### UN Security Council Sanctions Practice

The UN Security Council's sanctions practice provides empirical
evidence of the enforcement mechanisms available under Chapter VII
of the UN Charter. Since 1966, the Security Council has established
31 sanctions regimes under Article 41, ranging from comprehensive
economic sanctions against Rhodesia and apartheid South Africa to
targeted measures such as arms embargoes, travel bans, and financial
restrictions against specific individuals and entities. The evolution
from comprehensive to targeted (smart) sanctions reflects a learning
process driven by the humanitarian costs of comprehensive sanctions,
particularly in Iraq in the 1990s.

Research analyzing Security Council resolutions between 2000 and 2021
found that 40.4 percent of 1,333 resolutions addressed or discussed
organized crime in relation to a crisis, with measures taken under
Chapter VII in a significant number of cases. This demonstrates that
the Council has expanded its enforcement activities far beyond the
inter-state conflicts it was originally designed to address,
exercising functions of law enforcement and regulation that the
Charter framers did not anticipate. The appointment of independent
expert panels and monitoring mechanisms has become a regular feature,
providing the investigative capacity that member states often lack.
However, enforcement remains constrained by the political will of
the Permanent Five, inadequate compliance by member states, and the
absence of an independent UN enforcement capacity.

### The WTO Appellate Body Crisis

The WTO dispute settlement system provides the most instructive case
study of the tension between institutional effectiveness and state
sovereignty. From 1995 to 2019, the system adjudicated over 600
disputes, with the Appellate Body issuing over 150 appeal reports.
The reverse consensus rule made adoption of reports nearly automatic,
creating a binding and predictable system that stood in stark
contrast to the diplomatic, consensus-dependent dispute resolution
of the GATT era.

In December 2019, the Appellate Body ceased to function after the
United States blocked the appointment of new members, reducing the
body below the three-member quorum required to hear appeals. Since
then, cases appealed into the void have no resolution, and over 50
members have joined the MPIA as a provisional alternative. The crisis
illustrates a fundamental vulnerability: even the most
well-designed international institution depends on the continued
consent and cooperation of its most powerful members, and a single
state can disable the enforcement mechanism of a system binding 160+
economies. The paralysis has also driven a trend toward forum
shopping, as members increasingly turn to alternative dispute
resolution mechanisms outside the WTO framework, and toward
bilateral and regional agreements that bypass the multilateral
system entirely. Research analyzing the 32 cases stuck in appellate
limbo found that the absence of a functioning appellate tier erodes
the credibility of the entire dispute settlement process, since
panel rulings can no longer be definitively resolved and remain
exposed to potential non-compliance without consequence.

## Implications

### For Statecraft and Foreign Policy

International law is the operating system of diplomacy. Every
diplomatic interaction -- from treaty negotiation to border
delimitation to trade disputes -- takes place within a framework of
legal rules that states invoke, interpret, and sometimes violate. For
practitioners of statecraft, understanding international law is not
optional: it is the language in which claims are made and defended.
A state that violates international law without a legal
justification incurs reputational costs that can affect its ability
to form alliances, attract investment, and secure cooperation on
issues it cares about. The prohibition on the use of force, for
instance, does not prevent states from using force, but it forces
them to frame their actions in legal terms -- self-defense,
humanitarian intervention, Security Council authorization -- which
constrains the range of justifications available and creates a
record against which behavior can be judged.

The enforcement gap is real but not total. States comply with
international law most of the time, not because they fear punishment
but because compliance is usually in their interest. Treaties
coordinate expectations, reduce transaction costs, and create
patterns of reciprocal obligation. The WTO system, despite its
current crisis, demonstrated for 25 years that a rules-based
dispute settlement mechanism can produce high rates of compliance
even among states with asymmetric power. The lesson for statecraft
is that international law works best when it aligns compliance with
self-interest and creates institutions that make defection costly.

### For Investors and Markets

International law shapes the environment in which capital flows.
Bilateral investment treaties (BITs) and the investment chapters of
free trade agreements create enforceable rights for foreign
investors, often through investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS)
mechanisms that allow investors to sue host states directly in
international arbitration. Over 3,000 BITs exist, and the body of
ISDS jurisprudence has grown into a significant field of
international law. For investors, these treaties provide protection
against expropriation, unfair treatment, and denial of justice --
protections that would be unavailable in domestic courts of the host
state, particularly where the judiciary is weak or politically
dependent.

Trade law is equally consequential for markets. WTO rules on
subsidies, anti-dumping, safeguards, and technical barriers to trade
determine the competitive conditions under which firms operate. The
dispute settlement system, when functional, provides a predictable
mechanism for challenging trade barriers, and its paralysis since
2019 has increased uncertainty for firms reliant on the rules-based
trading system. The proliferation of bilateral and regional trade
agreements -- CPTPP, USMCA, RCEP -- reflects a partial migration of
trade governance away from the multilateral system, creating a
patchwork of overlapping obligations that investors and traders must
navigate.

### For International Relations Theory

International law sits at the intersection of law and politics, and
its study illuminates central debates in international relations
theory. Realists view international law as epiphenomenal -- a
reflection of the balance of power rather than an independent
constraint on state behavior. The enforcement gap, the Security
Council veto, and the compliance record of great powers provide
evidence for this view. The US refusal to comply with the Nicaragua
judgment, the Russian veto blocking action on Ukraine, and the US
blockade of the WTO Appellate Body all illustrate that powerful
states can disregard international law with relative impunity when
their interests are at stake.

Constructivists and institutionalists offer a counter-narrative:
international law shapes state identity and interests, creates
patterns of expectation that structure behavior, and provides a
framework for cooperation that would be impossible in its absence.
The high baseline compliance rate, the willingness of states to
invoke legal justifications even for controversial actions, and the
expansion of international law into new domains (human rights,
environmental law, criminal accountability) suggest that law is more
than a reflection of power. The truth likely lies between these
positions: international law constrains most states most of the
time, but its constraint on the most powerful is weakest, and the
system's legitimacy depends on managing this asymmetry.

### For the Rule of Law Globally

The aspiration of international law is to extend the rule of law --
the principle that power is exercised under and subject to law --
to the international sphere. This aspiration is incomplete. There is
no international legislature, no compulsory universal jurisdiction,
no international police force. The ICC can prosecute individuals for
genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes, but only when
national courts are unwilling or unable to do so, and only for
crimes committed on the territory of states parties or by nationals
of states parties. Major powers -- the United States, China, Russia,
India -- are not parties to the Rome Statute, limiting the Court's
reach.

The development of jus cogens and obligations erga omnes (obligations
owed to the international community as a whole) represents a partial
answer to the consent problem. These doctrines hold that certain
norms are so fundamental that they bind all states regardless of
consent and that any state has standing to invoke their violation.
This creates a hierarchy in international law that did not exist in
the classical system, elevating the prohibition on genocide and
torture above ordinary treaty obligations. The long-term implication
is a gradual shift from a purely consent-based system toward one in
which certain core norms are non-derogable -- a development that
strengthens the rule of law but creates tension with sovereignty
that remains unresolved.

### For Legal Practitioners and Compliance

International law creates obligations that domestic legal systems
must incorporate and enforce. Monist states (like the Netherlands)
treat ratified treaties as directly binding in domestic courts,
while dualist states (like the United Kingdom) require implementing
legislation before treaty obligations become domestically
enforceable. For legal practitioners, this distinction determines
whether an individual can invoke a treaty in a domestic court or
whether the treaty operates only at the state-to-state level. The
European Convention on Human Rights, for instance, is directly
enforceable in the domestic courts of Council of Europe member
states, while the International Covenant on Civil and Political
Rights requires implementing legislation in most common law
jurisdictions.

Compliance professionals in multinational corporations must navigate
an increasingly dense web of international obligations: sanctions
regimes imposed by the Security Council, export controls under
treaties like the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the Chemical
Weapons Convention, anti-corruption standards under the OECD
Anti-Bribery Convention, and environmental obligations under
instruments like the Paris Agreement. Violations can trigger both
domestic criminal liability (under implementing statutes) and
international consequences (asset freezes, trade restrictions). The
cost of noncompliance has risen as sanctions have become more
targeted and as monitoring mechanisms have grown more sophisticated,
making international law literacy a core competency for corporate
legal and compliance functions.
