# Securities Regulation -- The Disclosure Philosophy That Made Modern Capital Markets Possible

Securities regulation is the legal framework that governs how companies
raise capital from the public and how securities are traded in secondary
markets. Built on a disclosure philosophy rather than merit review, it
does not guarantee a good investment -- it guarantees that investors
receive truthful, complete information before they commit their money.
This regime, established by the Securities Act of 1933 and the
Securities Exchange Act of 1934, created the Securities and Exchange
Commission (SEC) and remains the foundation of American capital markets,
influencing securities laws globally.

## Background

Before the Great Depression, securities markets in the United States
operated with minimal federal oversight. Companies issued stock based on
promotional claims with no legal requirement to disclose financial
condition, business risks, or conflicts of interest. Brokers sold
securities to the public on promises of large profits, often with little
or no substantive basis. The market operated under the principle of
caveat emptor -- let the buyer beware.

This regulatory vacuum ended catastrophically with the stock market
crash of October 1929. The ensuing Great Depression revealed widespread
fraud, insider manipulation, and information asymmetries that had
enabled a speculative frenzy. Congressional hearings in the early 1930s,
led by Ferdinand Pecora, documented these abuses in detail and built
public demand for federal intervention.

The response came in two landmark statutes drafted largely by James M.
Landis, Benjamin V. Cohen, and Thomas G. Corcoran under President
Franklin D. Roosevelt. The Securities Act of 1933, signed into law on
May 27, 1933, regulated the primary market -- the initial issuance of
securities to the public. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934, enacted
the following year, regulated secondary trading between investors and
created the Securities and Exchange Commission as an independent federal
agency.

The core innovation was a departure from state-level "blue sky laws,"
which often applied merit review -- a government judgment about whether
an investment was good or bad. The federal approach, championed by
Roosevelt and his advisors, replaced merit review with mandatory
disclosure. The theory, articulated by Supreme Court Justice Louis
Brandeis in his 1914 book "Other People's Money," was that "sunlight is
said to be the best of disinfectants." If companies were forced to
disclose material facts, investors could make their own informed
decisions, and fraud would wither under scrutiny.

Subsequent legislation expanded the framework. The Trust Indenture Act
of 1939 regulated debt securities. The Investment Company Act of 1940
and Investment Advisers Act of 1940 brought mutual funds and financial
advisors under federal oversight. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002,
passed after the Enron and WorldCom scandals, imposed stricter corporate
governance and internal control requirements. The Dodd-Frank Act of
2010, responding to the 2008 financial crisis, expanded SEC authority
over derivatives, hedge funds, and credit rating agencies.

## Core Concepts

### The Disclosure Philosophy

The organizing principle of U.S. securities regulation is not that the
government decides which investments are worthy. It is that companies
must provide investors with full and fair disclosure of material
information. "Material" means information a reasonable investor would
consider important in making an investment decision. The SEC does not
approve or disapprove securities; it reviews registration statements for
completeness and accuracy.

This philosophy has a specific strength: it scales with markets.
Regulators do not need to evaluate every business model, technology, or
industry. They only need to ensure that whatever a company says about
itself is truthful, complete, and not misleading. This is why the same
basic framework can accommodate a railroad, a software startup, and a
biotech firm -- the SEC does not judge the business, only the
disclosure.

### The Two-Act Architecture

The 1933 Act and the 1934 Act divide the securities world into primary
and secondary markets, creating a comprehensive regulatory architecture.

The 1933 Act -- often called the "truth in securities" law -- governs
the initial offering and sale of securities. Its core requirement is
that any security offered to the public must be registered with the SEC
unless a specific exemption applies. Registration requires filing a
registration statement that includes a prospectus -- a detailed document
describing the company's business, financial condition, management, and
risks. Section 5 of the 1933 Act makes it unlawful to offer or sell
unregistered securities. Section 11 imposes liability on issuers,
underwriters, and directors for material misstatements or omissions in
registration statements. Section 12 provides liability for offers and
sales made through prospectuses containing material misstatements.

The 1934 Act -- the Exchange Act -- governs secondary trading of
securities already issued. It established the SEC and gave it broad
authority to regulate securities exchanges, broker-dealers, and
self-regulatory organizations (SROs) such as FINRA. Its four main
components are: (1) periodic reporting requirements for public
companies, (2) regulation of securities exchanges and market
participants, (3) anti-fraud provisions, and (4) regulation of proxy
solicitations and tender offers.

This two-act design reflects a practical reality: the risks to investors
differ between buying newly issued securities from the company (primary
market) and trading existing securities with other investors (secondary
market). The 1933 Act ensures investors know what they are buying at the
point of issuance. The 1934 Act ensures they continue to receive current
information and that trading markets operate fairly.

### Periodic Disclosure: 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K

Section 13 of the Exchange Act requires public companies to file
periodic reports that keep their disclosures current. These filings form
the backbone of the public information infrastructure that analysts,
investors, journalists, and competitors rely on.

Form 10-K is the annual report. It contains audited financial
statements, a detailed description of the business, risk factors,
management's discussion and analysis (MD&A), and disclosures about legal
proceedings, executive compensation, and related-party transactions.
Filing deadlines vary by company size: large accelerated filers (public
float over $700 million) have 60 days after fiscal year end; smaller
companies have up to 90 days.

Form 10-Q is the quarterly report. It provides unaudited financial
statements and an updated MD&A. The shorter filing cycle (40 or 45 days
after quarter end depending on company size) means investors receive a
near-continuous stream of financial information.

Form 8-K is the current report, filed when specific material events
occur. Triggering events include bankruptcy, acquisition or disposition
of assets, changes in control, departure of directors or officers,
amendments to articles of incorporation, and changes in the company's
certifying accountant. Most 8-K items must be filed within four business
days. This mechanism prevents companies from sitting on material
developments until the next quarterly filing.

### The SEC: Structure and Powers

The SEC is an independent federal agency governed by five commissioners
appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, with no more
than three from the same political party. Its staff of approximately
4,500 is organized into divisions: Corporation Finance (reviews
corporate filings), Enforcement (investigates and prosecutes
violations), Trading and Markets (oversees exchanges and broker-dealers),
Investment Management (regulates mutual funds and advisors), and
Economic and Risk Analysis (provides data and economic analysis).

The SEC's enforcement powers fall into three categories. First,
administrative proceedings: the SEC can bring cases before its own
administrative law judges, seeking cease-and-desist orders, civil
penalties, and bars from the securities industry. Second, civil actions
in federal court: the SEC can seek injunctions, disgorgement of illicit
profits, and civil monetary penalties. Third, criminal referrals: the
SEC can refer cases to the Department of Justice for criminal
prosecution, which can result in imprisonment.

A 2025 Supreme Court ruling in SEC v. Jarkesy held that when the SEC
seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, defendants have a Seventh
Amendment right to a jury trial in federal court, limiting the SEC's
ability to use its in-house administrative proceedings for
penalty-seeking enforcement actions.

### Insider Trading and Rule 10b-5

Insider trading -- buying or selling securities based on material,
non-public information in breach of a duty of trust or confidence --
is among the most aggressively enforced areas of securities law. The
Exchange Act does not explicitly define insider trading; the prohibition
has been developed primarily through SEC enforcement actions and court
decisions.

The SEC brings insider trading cases under two theories. The classical
theory applies when a corporate insider (officer, director, employee)
trades in their own company's securities based on material non-public
information, breaching a fiduciary duty to shareholders. The
misappropriation theory, recognized by the Supreme Court in United
States v. O'Hagan (1997), applies when an outsider trades based on
information misappropriated from its source in breach of a duty -- for
example, a lawyer trading on a client's merger plans, or a printing
press employee reading advance copies of a financial publication.

Rule 10b-5, promulgated under Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act, is the
primary anti-fraud weapon. It prohibits "any device, scheme, or artifice
to defraud" and any "untrue statement of a material fact" or omission
that makes statements misleading. The scope of Rule 10b-5 is broad: it
applies to any purchase or sale of any security, and courts have
interpreted it to cover insider trading, market manipulation, and
corporate misrepresentations.

Rule 10b5-1 provides an affirmative defense for insiders who trade under
pre-established written plans. If a plan is adopted in good faith when
the insider is not aware of material non-public information, trades
executed under the plan are shielded from insider trading liability even
if the insider later learns material information before the trade
executes. In 2022, the SEC adopted amendments imposing cooling-off
periods (90 to 120 days), limits on multiple overlapping plans, and
certification requirements to close perceived loopholes.

### State Blue Sky Laws

Federal securities law did not displace state regulation. Every state
has its own securities laws, known as blue sky laws, which predate the
federal statutes. The name derives from a judicial description of
speculative schemes with "no more basis than so many feet of blue sky."
State laws vary considerably. Some apply merit review where federal law
uses disclosure. Some, like New York's Martin Act, give enforcement
authority exclusively to the state Attorney General, denying private
rights of action that exist under federal law. The National Securities
Markets Improvement Act of 1996 preempted many state registration
requirements for securities listed on national exchanges, but state
antifraud authority remains.

## Evidence and Historical Impact

The empirical case for securities regulation rests on before-and-after
comparisons and studies of market responses to disclosure mandates.

Before the 1933 and 1934 Acts, the U.S. securities markets were
notorious for information asymmetry. A 1933 congressional report
documenting practices leading to the 1929 crash found that underwriters
routinely allocated hot IPO shares to insiders at below-market prices,
company insiders traded on information not available to public
shareholders, and financial statements were unaudited and often
fabricated. After the Acts, the percentage of NYSE-listed firms
publishing audited financial statements rose from approximately 50
percent before 1933 to nearly 100 percent by 1936. The cost of equity
capital for firms subject to the new disclosure regime declined, as
reduced information asymmetry translated into lower risk premiums
demanded by investors.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 provides a more recent natural
experiment. After Enron and WorldCom collapsed in accounting frauds that
destroyed hundreds of billions in market value, SOX imposed CEO/CFO
certification requirements, enhanced internal control audits under
Section 404, and created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board
(PCAOB) to regulate auditors. Studies by researchers at the SEC and
academic economists found that SOX significantly reduced earnings
restatements and improved investor confidence, though the compliance
costs fell disproportionately on smaller public companies. The number of
U.S. public companies declined in the years following SOX, with some
attributing the decline to regulatory burden and others to broader
market trends.

The SEC enforcement track record is substantial. In fiscal year 2024,
the SEC filed 583 total enforcement actions and obtained orders for $8.2
billion in financial remedies, including disgorgement and civil
penalties. High-profile cases include the $1.8 billion penalty against a
major investment bank for misleading investors in residential
mortgage-backed securities, and a string of insider trading actions
using data analytics to detect suspicious trading patterns.

The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 added accounting and
record-keeping requirements to the Exchange Act, responding to
revelations that hundreds of U.S. companies had made questionable
payments to foreign officials. The globalization of securities
regulation further supports the disclosure model's effectiveness.
The U.S. approach has
influenced securities laws in jurisdictions from the European Union
(Market Abuse Regulation, Prospectus Regulation) to Japan (Financial
Instruments and Exchange Act) to emerging markets building their first
stock exchanges. Research comparing countries with strong securities
regulation to those with weak enforcement consistently finds that
stronger investor protection correlates with larger, more liquid capital
markets and lower cost of capital for firms -- a finding documented in
La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer, and Vishny's influential
cross-country studies of law and finance.

## Implications

Securities regulation is not merely a set of compliance requirements. It
is the legal infrastructure on which public capital markets depend, and
its design choices have far-reaching consequences.

For investors, the disclosure regime reduces information asymmetry
between corporate insiders and outside shareholders. A retail investor
buying shares in a public company can access years of audited financial
statements, risk factor disclosures, and MD&A analysis -- all filed
under penalty of perjury and antifraud liability. This does not
eliminate investment risk, but it eliminates the risk of investing
blindfolded. The private right of action under Rule 10b-5 gives
investors a mechanism to recover losses caused by fraud, creating a
powerful deterrent against misrepresentation. In effect, securities
regulation transforms the securities market from a game of poker
between unequally informed players into a more level playing field.

For companies, the regulatory framework provides both a burden and a
benefit. The compliance cost of being a public company -- estimated at
$1.5 to $4 million annually for smaller public companies, and multiples
of that for large ones -- is substantial. This creates a threshold
effect: some companies choose to remain private, raise capital through
private markets, or defer IPOs. However, the same framework provides the
credibility that makes public capital raising possible at scale. The
U.S. has the deepest and most liquid capital markets in the world, and
the disclosure regime is a significant factor: investors are willing to
commit capital at lower cost when they trust the information environment.

For the broader economy, securities regulation shapes capital allocation.
When disclosure is reliable, capital flows toward productive uses
because investors can distinguish well-managed companies from poorly
managed ones. When disclosure is unreliable, capital allocation becomes
arbitrary, and economic growth suffers. This is the macro-level payoff
of the disclosure philosophy: efficient capital allocation drives
innovation, job creation, and wealth formation.

For governance, securities regulation operates alongside corporate law
to constrain agency problems. The proxy rules under Section 14 of the
Exchange Act enable shareholders to vote on directors, executive
compensation, and fundamental corporate changes -- all based on
mandatory disclosures. The Williams Act regulates tender offers and
takeovers, ensuring that shareholders receive adequate information and
time to evaluate acquisition proposals. These provisions transform
corporate governance from an insider-controlled process into one subject
to market discipline.

The ongoing tension in securities regulation is between investor
protection and regulatory burden. Each new rule imposes compliance
costs; each disclosure gap leaves investors vulnerable. The SEC's
regulatory agenda constantly navigates this tradeoff. Recent debates
include climate risk disclosure requirements, cybersecurity incident
reporting, cryptocurrency regulation, and whether the accredited
investor definition should be expanded to allow more individuals to
invest in private offerings.
