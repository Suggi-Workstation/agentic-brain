# Environmental Law and Climate Treaties -- Why the World Keeps Negotiating Agreements It Cannot Enforce

Environmental law and climate treaties constitute the body of international legal instruments designed to address anthropogenic climate change and broader environmental degradation. From the 1992 UN Framework Convention on Climate Change through the Kyoto Protocol, the Copenhagen collapse, the Paris Agreement, and the Glasgow Climate Pact, the climate regime has evolved through three distinct architectural phases: a framework convention with non-binding aims, a protocol with top-down binding targets for developed countries, and a hybrid agreement combining universal participation with self-determined contributions. The central tension -- between the scientific imperative for rapid emissions reductions and the political reality of national sovereignty -- has shaped every design choice in this legal evolution and explains why the regime keeps producing agreements that are simultaneously landmark achievements and insufficient responses.

## Background

The international climate regime did not emerge from a vacuum. Its intellectual
roots lie in the broader development of international environmental law, which
itself was a relatively late arrival in the international legal order. Before the
1970s, environmental issues were treated as domestic matters; international law
addressed territorial sovereignty, war, trade, and human rights, but not the
global commons. The shift began with the 1972 Stockholm Conference on the Human
Environment, the first major UN conference to address environmental degradation
as an international concern. Stockholm produced a declaration of principles and
established the UN Environment Programme (UNEP), creating the institutional
scaffolding for subsequent treaty-making.

The scientific foundations for climate treaty-making were laid by the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), established in 1988 by the
World Meteorological Organization and UNEP. The IPCC's role was not to
advocate policy but to assess the scientific literature on climate change,
providing the authoritative scientific basis that treaty negotiations would
rely upon. The First Assessment Report (1990) confirmed that human activities
were increasing atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and that this would
lead to additional warming of the Earth's surface. This scientific consensus
created the political pressure that led to the negotiation of the UN Framework
Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC).

The UNFCCC was adopted on 9 May 1992 at the United Nations Headquarters in New
York and opened for signature at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in June
1992, where 154 nations signed it. The Convention entered into force on 21
March 1994 after receiving 50 ratifications. As of 2025, it has 197 parties --
near-universal participation. The UNFCCC established the basic governance
system for the climate issue: a Conference of the Parties (COP) meeting
annually, a Secretariat based in Bonn, and the objective of stabilizing
greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent
dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system. Crucially, the
UNFCCC set non-binding limits on greenhouse gas emissions for individual
countries and contained no enforcement mechanisms. Instead, it outlined how
specific international treaties called "protocols" or "agreements" could be
negotiated to specify further action toward the Convention's objective.

The UNFCCC's most significant structural feature was the principle of common
but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities (CBDRRC).
This principle, enshrined in Article 3.1 of the Convention, recognized that
all parties had a common responsibility to address climate change but that
developed countries (listed in Annex I) should take the lead, given their
historical contribution to global emissions and their greater financial and
technological capacity. Annex I parties committed to returning their
greenhouse gas emissions to 1990 levels by 2000, while non-Annex I
(developing) parties had no quantitative obligations. This bifurcation --
a firewall between developed and developing countries -- became the defining
structural feature of the climate regime and the source of its most persistent
conflicts.

The first protocol to the UNFCCC was the Kyoto Protocol, adopted at COP3 in
December 1997. Kyoto imposed legally binding, economy-wide emissions reduction
targets on Annex I parties: an average reduction of 5.2 percent below 1990
levels during the first commitment period of 2008-2012. The Protocol
established three flexible mechanisms to help parties meet their targets
cost-effectively: emissions trading, the Clean Development Mechanism (CDM),
and Joint Implementation (JI). The Protocol entered into force in February
2005 after Russia's ratification provided the required quorum. However, the
United States signed the Protocol in 1998 but never ratified it, and Canada
withdrew in 2011. The Protocol's coverage was limited to approximately 15
percent of global emissions, as the world's largest emitters -- the United
States and China -- were outside its binding obligations.

The failure to produce a successor agreement at COP15 in Copenhagen in
December 2009 marked the second major turning point in the climate regime's
evolution. The Copenhagen conference was widely expected to produce a legally
binding treaty to succeed the Kyoto Protocol's first commitment period. Instead,
the negotiations collapsed under the weight of fundamental disagreements
between developed and developing countries over burden-sharing, the consensus
decision-making rule that allowed small groups of countries to block agreement,
and the incompatible positions of the two largest emitters -- the United States
and China. The resulting Copenhagen Accord was a political agreement, not a
treaty. It was merely "noted" by the parties rather than adopted, as several
countries objected to the process by which it was negotiated. The Accord
recognized the need to limit warming below 2 degrees Celsius but contained no
binding targets, no baseline year, and no enforcement mechanism.

The Copenhagen failure taught the climate regime a painful lesson: a rigidly
differentiated, top-down approach with binding targets could not achieve
universal participation, and universal participation was essential when
developing countries -- particularly China -- had become the world's largest
emitters. This realization drove the design of the Paris Agreement, adopted at
COP21 on 12 December 2015. Paris abandoned the annex-based bifurcation in favor
of universal participation with self-differentiated contributions. It entered
into force on 4 November 2016, less than a year after adoption -- a remarkably
rapid entry into force that reflected the political momentum behind the new
architecture. The Paris Agreement has, in effect, superseded the Kyoto Protocol
as the principal regulatory instrument governing the global response to climate
change, though the Kyoto Protocol technically remained in force through its
second commitment period ending in 2020.

## Core Concepts

### The Framework Convention Architecture (UNFCCC)

The UNFCCC established the foundational architecture upon which all subsequent
climate treaties were built. It is a framework convention -- a treaty that
sets general objectives and principles and establishes institutions, leaving
the specific regulatory content to be elaborated through subsequent protocols
and agreements. This approach is standard in international environmental law;
the 1985 Vienna Convention on the Protection of the Ozone Layer similarly
served as a framework, with the 1987 Montreal Protocol providing the regulatory
substance. The UNFCCC's key architectural elements are: a supreme
decision-making body (the COP, meeting annually), a permanent Secretariat,
subsidiary bodies for scientific and technological advice (SBSTA) and for
implementation (SBI), and a financial mechanism operated by the Global
Environment Facility (GEF). The Convention also established the principle of
CBDRRC, which has shaped every subsequent negotiation.

The UNFCCC's objective, stated in Article 2, is to stabilize greenhouse gas
concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that would prevent dangerous
anthropogenic interference with the climate system. This objective is notable
for what it lacks: a specific temperature target, a timeline, or a
concentration level. These were left to be determined by subsequent
instruments. The Convention's only quantitative provision was Article 4.2(b),
which called on Annex I parties to return their emissions to 1990 levels by
2000 -- a target that was non-binding and that most parties failed to achieve.
The UNFCCC was, by design, a starting point rather than a solution. Its
genius lay in creating a durable institutional framework within which all
subsequent climate diplomacy would take place.

### The Kyoto Protocol -- Top-Down Binding Targets

The Kyoto Protocol represented the climate regime's first attempt at
substantive regulation. Its architecture was top-down: an international
negotiation produced quantified emissions limitation and reduction commitments
for Annex I parties, inscribed in Annex B of the Protocol. Each Annex I party
was assigned a specific percentage reduction target relative to its 1990
baseline, to be achieved during the commitment period of 2008-2012. The
aggregate target was a 5.2 percent reduction below 1990 levels for all Annex I
parties combined. These targets were legally binding under international law:
a party whose emissions exceeded its assigned amount was in non-compliance,
and the Protocol's compliance committee could impose consequences, though
these were primarily procedural rather than punitive.

The Protocol's three flexible mechanisms were its most innovative feature.
Emissions trading (Article 17) allowed Annex I parties to buy and sell
emission allowances among themselves, creating the first international carbon
market. The Clean Development Mechanism (CDM, Article 12) allowed Annex I
parties to earn Certified Emission Reductions (CERs) by investing in emission
reduction projects in non-Annex I countries -- creating a financial flow from
developed to developing countries while reducing the cost of compliance. Joint
Implementation (JI, Article 6) allowed Annex I parties to earn Emission
Reduction Units (ERUs) from projects in other Annex I countries. The CDM
became the largest project-based carbon market mechanism, registering over
7,800 projects by 2012 and issuing over 2 billion CERs. The European Union
Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), launched in 2005, was the largest
compliance market linked to the Kyoto framework and became the primary demand
source for CDM credits.

The Kyoto Protocol's fundamental weakness was its limited coverage. By
exempting non-Annex I countries from any quantitative obligations, it created
a system in which the world's largest emitter (China, which surpassed the
United States in annual emissions by 2006) and the largest historical emitter
(the United States, which never ratified) were both outside the binding
framework. Global emissions rose by approximately 60 percent from 1990 to
2019 despite the Protocol's existence, though it is important to note that
this increase was driven primarily by economic growth in non-Annex I countries
that had no obligations under the Protocol. The Protocol's causal impact on
global emissions was constrained by its architecture, not by non-compliance
with its targets -- Annex I parties that ratified generally met their
commitments.

### The Paris Agreement -- Hybrid Architecture and NDCs

The Paris Agreement represents a fundamental redesign of the climate regime.
It combines elements of both the Kyoto Protocol (it is a treaty under
international law, with binding procedural obligations) and the Copenhagen
Accord (it takes a bottom-up approach to the substance of climate policy,
allowing parties to self-determine their contributions). This hybrid
architecture was the result of a deliberate search for a "Goldilocks" solution
-- not too rigid like Kyoto, which could not achieve universal participation,
and not too soft like Copenhagen, which lacked legal form and accountability.

The central innovation of the Paris Agreement is the system of Nationally
Determined Contributions (NDCs). Article 4 requires each party to prepare,
communicate, and maintain successive NDCs that it intends to achieve. Parties
must pursue domestic mitigation measures with the aim of achieving the
objectives of their NDCs. NDCs must be communicated every five years, and each
successive NDC must represent a progression beyond the party's current NDC and
reflect its highest possible ambition. The NDCs are recorded in a public
registry maintained by the UNFCCC Secretariat.

The critical legal distinction is between the procedural obligation and the
substantive obligation. The procedural obligation -- to prepare, communicate,
maintain, and update NDCs -- is legally binding. The substantive obligation --
to achieve the emissions reductions specified in the NDC -- is not. Parties
are obligated to pursue domestic measures aimed at achieving their NDC
objectives, but they are not obligated to achieve those objectives. This
design choice was deliberate: making NDC targets legally binding would have
driven parties to set less ambitious targets, or to refuse participation
entirely. The United States, in particular, insisted that NDCs not be binding
in order to avoid the need for Senate ratification. The trade-off was between
universal participation with self-determined ambition and limited
participation with externally imposed obligations. The Paris Agreement chose
the former.

The Paris Agreement's long-term temperature goal, stated in Article 2, is to
hold the increase in the global average temperature to well below 2 degrees
Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit the
temperature increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius. This was the first time the
climate regime adopted a specific temperature objective. Article 4.1
establishes a collective aim of reaching global peaking of greenhouse gas
emissions as soon as possible and achieving a balance between anthropogenic
emissions by sources and removals by sinks in the second half of the century --
the "net-zero" goal. These collective goals are not binding on individual
parties; they provide a direction of travel and a yardstick for assessing
collective progress.

### Differentiation -- From Bifurcation to Self-Differentiation

The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities underwent its most
significant evolution in the Paris Agreement. Under the UNFCCC and Kyoto
Protocol, differentiation was categorical: Annex I (developed) countries had
binding targets; non-Annex I (developing) countries had none. This
bifurcation, sometimes called the "firewall," became increasingly untenable as
the economic and emissions landscape shifted. By 2012, China was the world's
largest emitter, and developing countries' collective emissions exceeded those
of developed countries. The United States' refusal to ratify the Kyoto
Protocol was attributed in large part to concern about the protocol's
differentiated obligations -- specifically, that major competitors like China
and India had no commitments.

The Paris Agreement replaced top-down differentiation with a paradigm of
universal coverage and bottom-up self-differentiation. All parties, developed
and developing alike, are subject to the same basic procedural obligations:
to prepare, communicate, and maintain NDCs. The substance of each party's
contribution is self-determined, taking into account the party's national
circumstances. This approach eliminates the annex-based firewall while
preserving the principle of CBDRRC in a more nuanced form. Only a few
obligations remain categorically differentiated: financial obligations fall
only on developed country parties, while developing countries are encouraged
to provide support voluntarily. The transparency framework provides
flexibility to developing countries that need it based on capacity
constraints, but they must explain the nature of those constraints and provide
a timeline for addressing them.

This shift from bifurcation to self-differentiation was the most contentious
issue in the Paris negotiations and remains a source of ongoing tension.
Developing countries argue that self-differentiation allows developed
countries to evade their historical responsibility; developed countries argue
that categorical differentiation is no longer justifiable given changed
economic realities. The Paris Agreement's compromise -- universal procedural
obligations with self-differentiated substantive contributions -- was
acceptable to all parties precisely because it did not definitively resolve
this tension. It managed it through a legal architecture that accommodated
both positions.

### The Ambition Mechanism and Global Stocktake

The Paris Agreement's answer to the problem of insufficient ambition is the
ambition mechanism, centered on the global stocktake. Article 14 of the
Agreement establishes a global stocktake to assess collective progress toward
the long-term goals every five years. The first global stocktake concluded in
2023 and warned that the world is not on track to meet the Paris Agreement's
long-term goals. The stocktake is designed to inform the next round of NDCs:
parties are expected to take its outcomes into account when updating their
contributions.

The five-year NDC cycle, combined with the progressive enhancement
requirement (each NDC must represent a progression beyond the previous one)
and the global stocktake, creates an iterative ratcheting mechanism. The
theory is that each cycle of NDC submission, stocktake assessment, and
enhanced ambition will drive emissions reductions upward over time. The
practice has been less encouraging: the initial round of NDCs submitted under
Paris did not put the world on a pathway to limiting warming to well below 2
degrees Celsius, much less 1.5 degrees. The policies of Paris signatories as
of late 2024 project approximately 2.3 to 2.5 degrees of warming by 2100
under full NDC implementation, and approximately 2.8 degrees under current
policies alone. The gap between the Paris Agreement's temperature goals and
the trajectory of NDCs is the regime's central unresolved problem.

### Article 6 and Carbon Markets

Article 6 of the Paris Agreement addresses voluntary cooperation between
parties in achieving their NDCs through both market and non-market approaches.
Article 6.2 allows parties to use internationally transferred mitigation
outcomes (ITMOs) toward their NDCs, establishing a framework for bilateral
and multilateral carbon market cooperation. Article 6.4 establishes a
centralized crediting mechanism -- the successor to the CDM -- supervised by a
designated body, to generate credits from emission reduction projects. Article
6.8 addresses non-market approaches to cooperation.

The rules for operationalizing Article 6 were not finalized until COP26 in
Glasgow in November 2021, six years after the Paris Agreement was adopted.
The Glasgow decisions resolved three key issues: corresponding adjustments to
prevent double-counting of emission reductions (a credit used by one party
toward its NDC cannot simultaneously be used by the transferring party);
treatment of unused CDM credits from the Kyoto Protocol (these could be used
only for the first NDC cycle, effectively limiting their use to 2025); and
the share of proceeds to fund adaptation (Article 6.4 transactions contribute
to the Adaptation Fund, while Article 6.2 transactions are encouraged but not
required to do so). The completion of the Article 6 rulebook at Glasgow
removed the last major obstacle to a functioning global carbon market under
the Paris framework, though the market's effectiveness remains to be
demonstrated.

## Evidence

### The Kyoto Compliance Record and Its Structural Limits

The Kyoto Protocol's compliance record provides the first major body of
empirical evidence on the climate regime's effectiveness. Annex I parties
that ratified the Protocol generally met their first commitment period
targets (2008-2012). The European Union-15 collectively exceeded their 8
percent reduction target. However, this compliance must be interpreted
carefully. Several factors inflated the apparent achievement: the economic
collapse of the former Soviet states reduced emissions independently of any
climate policy, the use of CDM credits allowed parties to offset domestic
emissions with reductions in developing countries (some of which have been
questioned for additionality), and the Protocol's targets were modest relative
to the scale of the problem.

The Protocol's structural limitation -- its exclusion of non-Annex I countries
-- was demonstrated by the emissions trajectory during its first commitment
period. Global greenhouse gas emissions rose by approximately 60 percent from
1990 to 2019, driven overwhelmingly by economic growth in developing
countries that had no obligations under the Protocol. China's emissions more
than tripled during this period. The Protocol thus demonstrated that a
climate treaty covering only developed countries, however well its targets
were met, could not address a global emissions problem driven increasingly by
developing country growth. This empirical finding directly motivated the
Paris Agreement's design choice for universal participation.

The Protocol's flexible mechanisms generated significant data. The CDM
registered over 7,800 projects by 2012 and issued over 2 billion CERs. The
EU ETS, linked to the Kyoto framework, became the world's largest carbon
market, trading allowances worth tens of billions of euros annually. However,
CDM projects faced persistent criticism regarding additionality -- the
requirement that credited reductions would not have occurred without the CDM
incentive. Studies found that a significant fraction of CDM projects,
particularly in the industrial gas sector (HFC-23 destruction and N2O from
adipic acid), would have been economically viable without CDM financing,
meaning the credits did not represent genuine additional reductions. The EU
prohibited the use of credits from these project types in the EU ETS from
2013 onward, acknowledging the integrity problem.

### The Copenhagen Collapse -- Anatomy of a Negotiating Failure

The Copenhagen conference (COP15, December 2009) provides the clearest case
study of why international climate negotiations fail. The conference was
expected to produce a legally binding successor to the Kyoto Protocol. Instead,
it produced the Copenhagen Accord, a political agreement that was not even
formally adopted by the COP -- it was merely "noted" because several
countries objected to the process by which it was negotiated. The failure
resulted from three converging factors.

First, the Danish presidency mismanaged the process. The Danish government
underestimated the number of participants, providing a venue too small for
the delegations and NGO observers that arrived. More fundamentally, the
Danish negotiating team was replaced mid-conference by the Prime Minister's
team, disrupting continuity. A leaked Danish draft text, seen as imposing a
developed-country agenda, alienated developing countries at the outset.

Second, the substantive positions of the two largest emitters were
fundamentally incompatible. The United States, constrained by domestic
politics -- the Senate had already indicated it would not ratify any binding
treaty -- insisted that China and other major developing countries take on
binding commitments. China, invoking CBDRRC and its developing country status
under the UNFCCC, refused binding targets. The Copenhagen Accord was
ultimately brokered by President Obama and the leaders of the BASIC countries
(Brazil, South Africa, India, China) in a closed session, a process that
excluded most UN member states and undermined the UNFCCC's multilateral
consensus model.

Third, the UNFCCC's consensus decision-making rule allowed a relatively small
group of countries to block agreement. Tuvalu's demand for immediate
consideration of a legally binding outcome, while procedurally legitimate,
contributed to days of procedural deadlock. The conference revealed that the
consensus rule, while ensuring inclusiveness, made it virtually impossible to
negotiate a complex binding agreement among 193 parties within a two-week
conference. The lesson was structural: the Paris Agreement would need a
different approach -- one that did not require consensus on specific targets
but instead required consensus only on the rules of the game.

### The Paris Agreement's Implementation Gap

The Paris Agreement provides the most significant current body of evidence on
the climate regime's performance. The agreement achieved near-universal
participation: as of 2025, 194 parties have submitted NDCs. This is its
greatest success -- the inclusion of all major emitters, including China and
India, in a single climate framework for the first time. The agreement's
rapid entry into force (November 2016, less than a year after adoption)
demonstrated strong political momentum.

However, the implementation gap between the Paris Agreement's temperature
goals and the trajectory of NDCs is stark and well-documented. The first
global stocktake, concluded in 2023, warned that the world is not on track to
meet the long-term goals of the Paris Agreement. Climate Action Tracker, a
consortium of research organizations, estimated in late 2024 that full
implementation of all current NDCs would result in approximately 2.3 to 2.5
degrees of warming by 2100, while current policies alone would result in
approximately 2.8 degrees. Only 13 of 195 parties submitted new NDCs by the
February 2025 deadline, and as of September 2025, 55 parties had submitted
their third NDCs -- far short of universal participation.

The United States' withdrawal from the Paris Agreement provides a natural
experiment in the regime's resilience. The US announced its intention to
withdraw in June 2017, with withdrawal taking effect in November 2020. The
US rejoined in February 2021 under a new administration. In January 2025,
under a subsequent administration, the US again announced withdrawal, which
took effect on 27 January 2026. The US also withdrew from the UNFCCC itself,
the bedrock treaty underlying the Paris Agreement. This sequence demonstrates
both a strength and a weakness of the Paris architecture: the agreement
survives the withdrawal of its largest historical emitter, but it cannot
prevent that withdrawal or compel re-participation. The regime's resilience
is institutional, not enforcement-based.

### Climate Litigation -- Domestic Courts as Enforcement Agents

The absence of an international enforcement mechanism for climate obligations
has driven a parallel development: climate litigation in domestic courts.
The most significant case is Urgenda Foundation v. State of the Netherlands.
In 2015, the District Court of The Hague ordered the Netherlands to reduce
greenhouse gas emissions by at least 25 percent below 1990 levels by the end
of 2020. The Court of Appeal upheld this order in 2018, and the Supreme Court
confirmed it in December 2019. The Supreme Court grounded its ruling in
Articles 2 (right to life) and 8 (right to private and family life) of the
European Convention on Human Rights, holding that the state has a positive
obligation to take preventive measures against the real and imminent threat
of dangerous climate change, even though the Netherlands' contribution to
global emissions is small. The Court relied on the international consensus
that Annex I countries should reduce emissions by 25-40 percent by 2020,
drawing on IPCC assessments and the UNFCCC framework.

In Milieudefensie et al. v. Royal Dutch Shell (May 2021), the District Court
of The Hague extended the logic of Urgenda to a private corporation. The
Court ordered Shell to reduce its global CO2 emissions (Scope 1, 2, and 3)
by at least 45 percent by 2030 relative to 2019 levels. This was the first
court order imposing a specific emissions reduction obligation on a private
company. The Court based its ruling on the unwritten standard of care under
Dutch tort law, which it interpreted in light of international human rights
instruments (Articles 2 and 8 ECHR) and the UN Guiding Principles on Business
and Human Rights. The Court referenced the Paris Agreement's temperature
goals as informing the standard of care. The Hague Court of Appeal overturned
the specific 45 percent reduction order in November 2024, finding that the
district court had imposed an overly specific obligation, but it confirmed
that Shell has a duty of care to reduce its emissions and that the Paris
Agreement's goals are relevant to determining the content of that duty.

These cases demonstrate that international climate treaties, while lacking
direct enforcement at the international level, can generate legal effects
domestically through judicial interpretation. The Paris Agreement's
temperature goal has been cited in national court decisions in the
Netherlands, Germany, and elsewhere, requiring governments to adopt more
specific climate targets. The Urgenda case has inspired similar litigation
in Belgium, France, Ireland, Switzerland (Klimaseniorinnen), and other
jurisdictions. This litigation trend represents an emerging enforcement
mechanism that operates outside the treaty regime itself but draws on its
normative content.

## Implications

### For International Law and Treaty Design

The climate regime's evolution carries profound implications for international
law and the design of multilateral treaties generally. The central lesson is
that the traditional model of international law -- a treaty with binding
obligations enforced through state consent and institutional pressure --
struggles to address collective action problems where participation must be
universal and where the costs of compliance are high and unequally
distributed. The Kyoto Protocol demonstrated that a binding treaty with
limited participation cannot solve a global problem. The Paris Agreement
demonstrated that a universal treaty with non-binding substantive targets can
achieve participation but not necessarily compliance.

The Paris Agreement's hybrid architecture -- binding procedural obligations
with self-determined substantive commitments -- may represent a new model for
international environmental agreements and potentially for other domains
facing similar structural challenges. The agreement calibrates legal
bindingness along a spectrum rather than treating it as binary: the
instrument itself is a treaty, the procedural obligations are binding, the
transparency framework is binding, but the substantive targets are not. This
calibration allowed the agreement to bridge seemingly irreconcilable
positions between states demanding binding targets and states refusing them.
The model's success or failure will determine whether it is applied to other
international environmental problems, as scholars have suggested.

The climate regime also demonstrates the importance of institutional
adaptability. The UNFCCC's framework convention approach -- establishing
institutions and principles while leaving regulatory substance to subsequent
instruments -- has proven remarkably durable. The same institutional
architecture (COP, Secretariat, subsidiary bodies) has accommodated three
fundamentally different regulatory approaches over 30 years: the
non-binding UNFCCC, the top-down Kyoto Protocol, and the hybrid Paris
Agreement. This adaptability is a feature, not a bug: it allows the regime
to learn from failure and redesign without requiring the negotiation of an
entirely new institutional framework each time.

### For Sovereignty and the Enforcement Problem

The climate regime's enforcement gap is the most consequential structural
feature for the broader international legal system. There is no international
court with compulsory jurisdiction over climate obligations. There is no
international police force to compel compliance. The Paris Agreement's
compliance mechanism, established under Article 15, is facilitative rather
than punitive -- it is designed to promote compliance through dialogue and
assistance, not to impose sanctions. The Kyoto Protocol's compliance
committee, while more developed, was still primarily procedural in its
consequences.

This enforcement gap reflects a fundamental tension between sovereignty and
the collective action requirements of climate change. No state can solve
climate change alone; every state's contribution to the problem is small
relative to the whole; and the benefits of any single state's emission
reductions are shared globally while the costs are borne locally. This is the
classic free-rider problem: each state has an incentive to let others bear
the costs of emissions reductions while it continues to emit. A binding
treaty with strong enforcement could solve this problem in theory, but no
state will accept strong enforcement over its economic policy, and the states
whose participation is most essential (the largest emitters) are the least
willing to submit to external compulsion.

The implication for sovereignty is significant. The climate regime
represents the most ambitious attempt to date to use international law to
constrain state behavior in a domain -- economic activity producing
greenhouse gas emissions -- that is deeply embedded in domestic policy and
politics. The regime's inability to enforce its targets demonstrates the
limits of the consent-based international legal order: the system can create
norms, institutions, and processes, but it cannot override the sovereign
decision-making of states that choose not to comply. The emergence of
domestic climate litigation as a quasi-enforcement mechanism represents a
partial workaround -- using domestic courts to give international norms legal
force within national legal systems -- but it depends on the existence of
independent judiciaries and justiciable human rights obligations, which are
not universally available.

### For Domestic Law and Policy

The international climate regime has had a profound effect on domestic legal
systems, often exceeding its direct international legal force. Article 4.1 of
the Paris Agreement, which establishes the collective net-zero goal, has
driven the adoption of carbon neutrality targets by over 100 countries and
thousands of non-state actors. National courts in the Netherlands and Germany
have referenced the Paris Agreement's temperature goal in decisions requiring
governments to strengthen their climate targets. The Paris Agreement has been
an important motivation for the adoption and updating of national climate
change acts, particularly in Europe but also elsewhere.

The distinction between monist and dualist legal systems determines how
international climate obligations enter domestic law. Monist states (like the
Netherlands) treat ratified treaties as directly applicable in domestic
courts, allowing litigants to invoke treaty provisions directly. Dualist
states (like the United Kingdom) require implementing legislation before
treaty obligations become domestically enforceable. This distinction has
proven consequential for climate litigation: the Urgenda and Shell cases
succeeded in part because the Dutch legal system allows international human
rights instruments (the ECHR) to be directly invoked in domestic courts,
providing the bridge between international climate norms and domestic legal
obligations. In dualist systems, the path from international treaty to
domestic enforceability is longer and more uncertain.

The domestic legal effects of the Paris Agreement have been described as
ground-breaking, particularly in comparison to the UNFCCC and Kyoto Protocol,
which had far less impact on domestic law. The Paris Agreement's specific
mitigation goals, while formulated as collective aims rather than binding
individual obligations, provide a long-term direction of travel for both
international and national climate policy and serve as yardsticks for
assessing country-level action. This normative function -- shaping
expectations and providing a standard against which domestic policy can be
evaluated -- may be the Paris Agreement's most significant legal effect, even
though it operates through influence rather than enforcement.

### For Investors, Markets, and Corporate Governance

The climate regime shapes the environment in which capital is allocated and
businesses operate. Carbon markets, created under the Kyoto Protocol and
extended under the Paris Agreement's Article 6, create direct financial
instruments -- emission allowances and credits -- that are traded on
compliance and voluntary markets. The EU ETS, the largest compliance market,
prices carbon for over 11,000 installations and airlines operating in Europe,
directly affecting the cost structure of energy-intensive industries. The
extension of carbon pricing to additional sectors and jurisdictions is
ongoing, with implications for asset valuations across the energy and
industrial sectors.

The Paris Agreement's temperature goals have become a reference point for
corporate climate commitments and for the financial sector's assessment of
climate risk. The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures
(TCFD), the Net-Zero Banking Alliance, and similar initiatives use the Paris
Agreement's 1.5-2 degree goal as the benchmark for assessing whether
corporate and portfolio emissions trajectories are consistent with the
international climate regime. The Shell case demonstrated that this benchmark
can acquire legal force through domestic litigation: a court ordered a
corporation to align its emissions with the Paris Agreement's temperature
goal, treating the agreement as informing the standard of care under tort
law. While the specific percentage reduction was overturned on appeal, the
principle that corporations have a legal duty to reduce emissions consistent
with the Paris goals was confirmed.

For investors, the climate regime creates both opportunity and risk. The
transition to a low-carbon economy, driven by the Paris Agreement's goals and
by domestic implementing legislation, creates investment opportunities in
renewable energy, energy efficiency, and carbon removal technologies. It also
creates stranded asset risk for fossil fuel investments, as policies
designed to meet Paris goals reduce demand for coal, oil, and gas. The
climate regime's enforcement gap means that the pace and completeness of the
energy transition will be determined by domestic policy choices and market
forces as much as by international legal obligations, creating uncertainty
that investors must navigate. The regime's iterative architecture -- with
NDCs updated every five years and the global stocktake assessing progress --
ensures that policy signals will continue to evolve, making climate policy a
persistent factor in investment decisions across all asset classes.
