# Administrative Law Makes Agency Power Operational but Keeps It Legally Bounded

Administrative law is the framework through which United States federal agencies turn congressional statutes into generally applicable rules, case-specific orders, licenses, sanctions, and other public actions. The Administrative Procedure Act supplies default procedures for rulemaking and adjudication, while organic statutes grant each agency its substantive authority and courts police the resulting boundaries. Agency expertise therefore operates inside a chain of legal authorization, public procedure, reasoned explanation, and judicial review rather than as an independent power to make law ([1] [2] [3]).

## Background

Administrative law developed because legislatures could establish public programs but could not themselves make every technical, recurring, or case-specific decision needed to administer them. Congress responded by creating bodies with defined missions and by delegating authority to implement statutes through rules, licensing, investigation, enforcement, and adjudication. Congress created the Interstate Commerce Commission in 1887 to oversee railroads; the commission was unique at the time and became a model for later regulatory agencies. New Deal agencies extended the administrative model across additional sectors and public programs. The expansion produced a durable constitutional problem: agencies needed enough discretion to administer complex statutes, but affected persons needed notice, fair process, and a means to challenge unlawful action ([3] [20] [26]).

Before 1946, federal administrative procedure was fragmented. An agency's enabling statute might prescribe detailed hearings, but another program might leave procedure largely to the agency. Critics objected that agencies could combine investigative, prosecutorial, legislative, and adjudicative functions without consistent safeguards. Defenders answered that courts and legislatures could not reproduce the speed, specialization, or continuing supervision that administration required. The Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 was a negotiated institutional settlement. Its stated purpose was to improve the administration of justice by prescribing fair administrative procedure, and its enduring design couples minimum procedures with room for Congress to impose program-specific requirements ([20]).

The APA did not create a single procedure for every agency action. Instead, it defined categories. A "rule" is an agency statement of general or particular applicability and future effect designed to implement, interpret, or prescribe law or policy; "rulemaking" is the process of formulating, amending, or repealing such a rule. An "order" is a final disposition in a matter other than rulemaking, including licensing, and "adjudication" is the process for formulating an order. "Agency action" is broader still, encompassing rules, orders, licenses, sanctions, relief, and failures to act. These statutory categories matter because the applicable procedure and standard of review depend on what the agency is actually doing, not merely on the label attached to the document ([1] [20]).

The APA's most common rulemaking model is informal rulemaking under 5 U.S.C. 553, usually called notice-and-comment rulemaking. The agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking, identifies its legal authority and proposed subject, receives written data and arguments from interested persons, considers relevant submissions, and publishes a final rule with a concise statement of basis and purpose. Formal rulemaking, which uses trial-like procedures under Sections 556 and 557, applies only when another statute requires rules to be made "on the record after opportunity for an agency hearing" and is rare. Congress sometimes creates hybrid processes by adding requirements beyond Section 553 for a particular program ([1] [3] [4]).

Agency adjudication also has multiple forms. Formal APA adjudication is triggered when a statute requires a decision on the record after an agency hearing. Sections 554, 556, and 557 then provide notice, an opportunity to present evidence and rebuttal, impartial presiding officers, a record-based decision, and rules separating adjudicative personnel from investigative or prosecutorial functions in the same case. Many other agency determinations occur outside this formal model. Benefits decisions, immigration matters, patent examinations, licensing decisions, grant disputes, and enforcement-related proceedings may use procedures created by an organic statute, agency regulation, or constitutional due process rather than the APA's complete formal-hearing framework ([1] [5] [6]).

This topic concerns United States federal administrative law. State administrative procedure acts often borrow from the federal model but differ in text and judicial interpretation, while other countries allocate rulemaking and adjudicative authority through different constitutional structures. Even within the federal system, the APA is a default rather than a complete code: an organic statute may alter procedures, specify a review court, define evidentiary standards, require consultation, or preclude review in limited circumstances. Sound analysis therefore begins with the governing substantive statute and then adds the APA, constitutional requirements, agency regulations, and controlling precedent ([1] [2] [3] [19]).

The central achievement of this framework is not the elimination of discretion. Administration cannot function without judgment about evidence, technical standards, enforcement priorities, and program design. The framework instead converts discretion into legally bounded discretion. Congress supplies authority and limits; agencies create a public record and explain their choices; affected parties participate or receive a hearing when applicable; and courts test authority, procedure, evidence, reasoning, and constitutional compliance under differentiated standards of review ([2] [7] [9] [11]).

## Core Concepts

### Organic statutes and delegated authority

An agency has no inherent regulatory jurisdiction. It receives power from an act of Congress commonly called an organic or enabling statute. That statute identifies the regulated subject, authorizes particular tools, sets substantive criteria, and may specify procedures and judicial review. A rule can be sensible as policy yet unlawful if the statute does not authorize it. Likewise, a statutory delegation may be broad without being unlimited: courts determine the boundaries of the delegation, while the agency chooses among lawful options within those boundaries and must explain that choice ([3] [11] [19]).

The constitutional nondelegation doctrine supplies an outer boundary by requiring Congress to state an intelligible principle that guides delegated authority. The Supreme Court has rarely invalidated federal legislation on that ground, but the related major questions doctrine has become more consequential in reviewing agency claims of authority. In West Virginia v. EPA, the Court held that an agency claiming power over a matter of vast economic and political significance must point to clear congressional authorization. The doctrine does not replace ordinary statutory interpretation in every case; it is a clear-statement approach for extraordinary assertions of regulatory power, and its trigger remains contested ([13] [14]).

FCC v. Consumers' Research reaffirmed in 2025 that the intelligible-principle test governs ordinary nondelegation challenges. The Court upheld the universal-service funding framework because Congress supplied goals and limits for the FCC's collection authority and the FCC retained final decision-making power while a private administrator performed subordinate work and provided nonbinding input. The decision did not displace the major questions doctrine; it clarified that broad implementation discretion can remain constitutional when the statute supplies determinate boundaries and the agency, rather than a private party, makes the controlling decisions ([21] [22]).

Delegation and discretion must therefore be separated from statutory ambiguity. Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo overruled the Chevron framework, under which a court often deferred to an agency's reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute. Loper Bright held that Section 706 requires courts to exercise independent judgment on questions of law. It also recognized that Congress may expressly or implicitly delegate discretionary authority, in which case a court must identify the delegation's boundaries and ensure reasoned decision-making within them. Agency expertise can still persuade a court, but ambiguity alone no longer transfers final interpretive authority from the judiciary to the agency ([11] [12]).

### Legislative rules and notice-and-comment procedure

Legislative, or substantive, rules have the force and effect of law when an agency issues them under delegated authority through the procedure Congress prescribed. Section 553 ordinarily requires three linked stages. First, notice in the Federal Register must identify the proceeding, cite legal authority, and describe the proposed terms or subjects. Second, interested persons must have an opportunity to submit written data, views, or arguments. Third, after considering the relevant material, the agency publishes a final rule with a statement of basis and purpose, ordinarily at least 30 days before the rule becomes effective. The process is deliberative rather than plebiscitary: the number of comments on each side does not decide the result; relevant evidence and reasoned analysis do ([1] [4]).

Adequate notice must expose the issues on which the final rule rests so participants can offer meaningful comments. A final rule may differ from the proposal, but a material change generally must remain a logical outgrowth of the proposal and record rather than surprise participants with an untested regulatory scheme. Agencies must respond to significant comments that challenge a central premise, identify a material alternative, or reveal a serious factual problem. They need not answer every repetitive or immaterial submission. This exchange improves the decision record and gives a reviewing court a basis for deciding whether the agency considered the relevant factors ([3] [4] [7]).

Section 553 contains important exceptions. Interpretive rules, general statements of policy, and rules of agency organization, procedure, or practice ordinarily do not require notice and comment. The section also excludes certain military or foreign-affairs functions and specified internal or benefits-related matters. An agency may invoke "good cause" to bypass prior notice when public procedure would be impracticable, unnecessary, or contrary to the public interest, but it must state its finding and reasons. Because exceptions reduce participation, courts examine whether the agency is relying on the actual character and circumstances of the action rather than an expedient label ([1] [3]).

Rulemaking is cumulative. An agency cannot treat repeal or rescission as legally costless merely because it is reducing regulation. State Farm held that rescinding an existing rule is subject to the same arbitrary-and-capricious review as adopting one. When an agency changes policy, it may choose a different lawful approach, but it must acknowledge the change, provide good reasons, and address serious reliance interests or factual findings where relevant. Administrative law permits policy change after elections and new evidence; it does not permit unexplained reversal ([7] [19]).

### Interpretive rules, policy statements, and guidance

Not every agency statement is a legislative rule. Interpretive rules advise the public how an agency reads a statute or regulation. General statements of policy describe how the agency currently intends to exercise discretionary authority, such as enforcement priorities. Manuals, compliance guides, advisory letters, and technical bulletins can fall within these categories. They are valuable because agencies can clarify complex requirements and communicate policy without the delay and rigidity of legislative rulemaking ([17] [18]).

The procedural advantage of guidance carries a legal limit: an agency cannot use a nominally nonbinding document to create obligations that only a statute or valid legislative rule can impose. Perez v. Mortgage Bankers Association held that the APA does not require notice and comment merely because an agency changes an interpretive rule; Section 553 expressly exempts interpretive rules. The Court also distinguished procedural validity from substantive validity. A guidance document may be exempt from notice and comment yet still fail if it contradicts the statute or regulation it purports to interpret, or if in practice it functions as a binding legislative rule without the required procedure ([17] [18]).

The practical boundary turns on legal effect rather than format. A statement that leaves officials free to consider individual circumstances and announces only a tentative enforcement posture is more likely to be a policy statement. A document that purports to determine rights, fixes a binding norm, or leaves regulated parties no realistic opportunity to contest its commands may be treated as a legislative rule. The author's synthesis is that agencies should make the boundary explicit: identify the source of legal obligation, state whether the document binds the public, and preserve case-specific discretion when issuing policy rather than law ([18]).

### Adjudication, hearings, and decisional independence

Rulemaking generally operates prospectively across a class; adjudication applies law or policy to particular facts and produces an order. Formal adjudication under Sections 554, 556, and 557 resembles a bench trial but remains an executive-branch process. Parties receive notice of the factual and legal issues, may present documentary or oral evidence, may submit rebuttal, and may conduct cross-examination as required for a full and true disclosure of facts. An agency head, member, or administrative law judge presides, and the decision must rest on the record ([1]).

The APA protects decisional impartiality without fully separating the adjudicator from the agency. Investigative or prosecutorial employees generally may not participate in the decision in the same or a factually related case, and presiding officers must act impartially. An administrative law judge may issue an initial decision, but the agency may review it and ordinarily retains final policymaking authority. This arrangement reflects a compromise: adjudicators need insulation from case-level pressure, while politically accountable agency leadership retains lawful control over policy within the statute ([1] [5] [6]).

Formal APA adjudication is not the dominant model. CRS describes a spectrum that includes APA-governed hearings, legally required evidentiary hearings outside the APA's formal provisions, and determinations with no required evidentiary hearing. A 2018 study cited by CRS counted at least 10,831 non-ALJ adjudicators, more than 7,000 of them patent examiners, illustrating how much federal decision-making occurs outside the familiar ALJ hearing. Procedures vary because caseload, subject matter, urgency, and the interests at stake vary. Informal does not necessarily mean casual: some non-APA hearings are more trial-like than some formal ones ([5] [6]).

### Due process and the procedure that is due

The Fifth Amendment independently constrains agency adjudication when government action deprives a person of life, liberty, or property. Due process ordinarily requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before an impartial decision-maker, but it does not require a courtroom trial for every administrative determination. In Mathews v. Eldridge, the Supreme Court balanced three considerations: the private interest affected, the risk of erroneous deprivation under existing procedure and the probable value of additional safeguards, and the government's interest, including administrative burden. Applying that test, the Court held that written pretermination procedures were constitutionally sufficient before termination of Social Security disability benefits because a later evidentiary hearing and judicial review remained available ([10]).

The Mathews framework makes procedure context-dependent. Loss of subsistence benefits, a professional license, a civil penalty, and denial of an application do not necessarily require the same sequence. Reliability also matters: documentary medical evidence may call for different safeguards than a credibility contest between witnesses. The correct question is not whether more process would be desirable in the abstract, but whether the marginal reduction in error justifies the burden in light of the protected interest. Organic statutes and agency rules may provide more procedure than the constitutional floor ([5] [10]).

### Investigation, enforcement, and forum choice

Agencies commonly investigate through reporting requirements, inspections, requests for information, and subpoenas authorized by statute. They may resolve suspected violations through warning letters, consent orders, license conditions, administrative compliance orders, civil penalties, or referrals to the Department of Justice. The available tool and forum depend on the organic statute. Enforcement discretion is broad because resources are limited, but it remains subject to statutory constraints, constitutional protections, and judicial review where the law provides a manageable standard ([2] [5] [19]).

Forum choice can determine procedure and remedy. Some statutes permit an agency to seek sanctions before an in-house adjudicator or in federal district court. SEC v. Jarkesy held that the Seventh Amendment required a jury when the SEC sought civil penalties for securities fraud because the statutory claim resembled common-law fraud and did not fall within the public-rights exception. The decision did not abolish administrative adjudication; it limited assignment of certain legal claims and remedies to agency tribunals. Its application depends on the claim, remedy, and public-rights analysis established by the governing statute and precedent ([15] [16]).

FCC v. AT&T supplied a material qualification in 2026. The Court upheld an FCC process in which the agency issued a forfeiture order without a jury because the order itself did not create a binding payment obligation or make the agency's factual findings conclusive. A recipient could refuse payment, after which the Department of Justice had to prove the violation and penalty de novo in a jury trial before collecting. The distinction from Jarkesy therefore turned on legal effect and the availability of a later de novo jury determination, not merely on whether an agency investigated, held a hearing, or announced a penalty amount ([24] [25]).

### Judicial review and differentiated standards

The APA embodies a strong default of judicial review but imposes threshold requirements. Section 702 grants review to a person suffering legal wrong or adversely affected within the relevant statute. Section 704 generally requires final agency action when no other adequate court remedy exists. Standing, ripeness, exhaustion where required, review-channel statutes, and filing deadlines can determine whether a court reaches the merits. Section 701 excludes review where another statute precludes it or where action is committed to agency discretion by law ([2] [19]).

Once review is available, Section 706 assigns different questions to different standards. Courts independently decide constitutional and statutory meaning after Loper Bright. They set aside action beyond statutory jurisdiction, contrary to constitutional right, or adopted without required procedure. They review most discretionary policy judgments under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard, asking whether the agency examined relevant data, considered important aspects of the problem, and connected the record to its conclusion. Formal adjudicative findings receive substantial-evidence review on the whole record. A court may also compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed, but ordinarily cannot dictate the substance of a discretionary choice ([2] [7] [9] [11] [19]).

McLaughlin Chiropractic Associates v. McKesson clarified in 2025 that a special statutory review route does not invariably make an agency's legal interpretation binding in a later enforcement case. Under the review structure at issue, the district court had to interpret the Telephone Consumer Protection Act independently because the parties had not received a prior, adequate, and exclusive opportunity for judicial review of the FCC's interpretation. The holding concerns the binding effect of an interpretation in enforcement; it does not eliminate the finality and review-channel requirements that govern direct challenges to agency action ([23]).

These standards allocate responsibility rather than rank institutions by general competence. Courts do not choose the best policy under arbitrary-and-capricious review, and they do not reweigh formal-hearing evidence as if deciding the case initially. Agencies, however, do not receive the last word on the existence or legal boundaries of their power. Vermont Yankee adds a complementary restraint on courts: absent constitutional or statutory authority, a reviewing court generally may not invent extra procedural requirements for agency rulemaking. Agencies must follow the procedures law requires, while courts must use the review law authorizes ([8] [9] [11]).

## Evidence

### The APA established a durable procedural baseline

The text and history of the APA provide the first evidence for the framework's design. The 1946 Act expressly defined rulemaking, adjudication, licensing, sanction, relief, and agency action; prescribed public rulemaking procedure; regulated formal hearings; and authorized judicial review. Congress later recodified those provisions in 5 U.S.C. 551-559 and 701-706 without abandoning the category-based structure. The same provisions continue to govern agencies with radically different missions, while program statutes add tailored requirements. This longevity supports a limited conclusion: the combination of common defaults and statute-specific variation has proved institutionally adaptable, not that every resulting procedure is optimal ([1] [2] [20]).

The scale of non-APA-formal adjudication confirms why a single trial model would be incomplete. CRS's review of informal adjudication reports a wide variety of adversarial hearings, inquisitorial benefits systems, interviews, inspections, and paper determinations. The cited 2018 survey's count of at least 10,831 non-ALJ adjudicators, compared with the much smaller familiar ALJ corps, indicates that most administrative justice cannot be understood by studying formal hearings alone. The evidence also cautions against assuming that procedural labels predict rigor: legally required hearings outside Sections 554, 556, and 557 can include substantial safeguards ([5] [6]).

### State Farm made explanation a legal obligation

Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm tested an agency's rescission of a passive-restraint requirement. The Supreme Court held that repeal receives the same arbitrary-and-capricious review as adoption. It faulted the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration for failing to consider a significant alternative -- an airbag-only requirement -- and for inadequately explaining parts of its analysis. The case established the practical core of reasoned decision-making: an agency must examine relevant data, consider important aspects of the problem, avoid explanations contrary to the record, and articulate a rational connection between facts and choice. The standard is narrow and does not authorize a court to substitute its preferred policy ([7]).

State Farm also shows why an administrative record matters. The agency's explanation defines the grounds a court can assess; post hoc litigation arguments cannot replace reasoning supplied when the decision was made. A participant who identifies a material defect or feasible alternative during comments therefore contributes both to agency deliberation and to the record for review. The author's synthesis is that notice and comment has two outputs: a regulation and an auditable explanation of how the agency handled material evidence and alternatives ([3] [7]).

### Vermont Yankee bounded judicial procedural innovation

Vermont Yankee Nuclear Power Corp. v. NRDC addressed the opposite institutional risk. The court of appeals had demanded procedures beyond those required by the APA and relevant statutes during nuclear rulemaking. The Supreme Court rejected that approach, holding that reviewing courts generally may not impose additional procedural devices merely because they believe those devices would improve agency decision-making. Congress can mandate more procedure, the Constitution can require it, and agencies can voluntarily provide it; courts cannot routinely create a common law of extra rulemaking procedure inconsistent with the APA's allocation ([8]).

Read with State Farm, Vermont Yankee creates a deliberate distinction. Courts may rigorously review whether the agency's explanation and record satisfy the statutory standard, but they ordinarily may not prescribe an unlegislated hearing format or cross-examination regime. The pair prevents two opposite failures: unreasoned agency choice and judicial takeover of administrative design. This is an interpretation of the cases' combined institutional effect rather than a separate holding ([7] [8]).

### Universal Camera required review of the whole evidentiary record

Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB interpreted substantial-evidence review after enactment of the APA and amendment of federal labor law. The Supreme Court held that a reviewing court must consider the record as a whole, including evidence that detracts from the agency's conclusion and the hearing examiner's findings. Substantial evidence is therefore not satisfied by isolating some supporting material while ignoring the counter-record. At the same time, review remains deferential to reasonable fact-finding and does not make the court the initial trier of fact ([9]).

This whole-record approach connects adjudicative procedure to review. Cross-examination, credibility findings, rebuttal evidence, and the presiding officer's initial decision matter because they become part of the record against which the agency's final order is tested. The agency may disagree with an initial decision, but the reviewing court can consider that disagreement and the evidentiary basis for it. Formal process therefore disciplines final agency leadership without eliminating its reviewing authority ([1] [9]).

### Mathews demonstrated calibrated constitutional procedure

Mathews v. Eldridge rejected a universal rule requiring an oral evidentiary hearing before every termination of government benefits. The Court examined the claimant's private interest, the accuracy of existing written medical procedures and potential value of oral process, and the fiscal and administrative cost of an added pretermination hearing. It concluded that the procedures, including an opportunity to submit evidence and a later hearing with judicial review, met due process for disability benefits. The holding was specific to the process and interests before the Court ([10]).

The evidence from Mathews is methodological. Due process analysis compares error risks and safeguards rather than matching every agency proceeding to a civil trial. This allows high-volume programs to use documentary or inquisitorial methods when they are reliable, while preserving stronger procedures where credibility, severe deprivation, or irreversible harm changes the balance. It also makes factual design choices -- what evidence is available, when review occurs, and whether interim harm can be repaired -- legally relevant ([5] [10]).

### Recent decisions reallocated interpretive and adjudicative authority

Loper Bright changed the law of statutory interpretation by overruling Chevron. The Court read Section 706 to require independent judicial judgment rather than mandatory deference to any reasonable agency interpretation of ambiguity. It preserved the possibility that an agency interpretation may carry persuasive weight and that statutes may delegate policy discretion. CRS identifies open implementation questions, including how lower courts will apply independent review, Skidmore-style persuasive weight, prior precedents, and express delegations. The verified conclusion is the doctrinal change; predictions about its long-term effect on agency win rates remain uncertain ([11] [12]).

McLaughlin applied independent judicial interpretation to a later enforcement proceeding involving an FCC order. The Court held that a district court was not bound by the agency's statutory reading where the governing scheme had not furnished the defendant a prior, adequate, and exclusive opportunity for review. The case shows that review-channel design and the timing of judicial access can determine whether an earlier agency interpretation controls outside a direct petition for review ([23]).

West Virginia v. EPA supplied a separate constraint through the major questions doctrine. The Court required clear congressional authorization for EPA's asserted power to restructure electricity generation under the statutory provision at issue. CRS notes that the Court did not provide a precise test identifying every major question, leaving uncertainty for agencies, Congress, and regulated parties. The case is evidence that breadth, novelty, economic significance, and the relationship between the claimed power and an agency's traditional domain can alter the interpretive inquiry ([13] [14]).

FCC v. Consumers' Research tested the older nondelegation doctrine directly. The Court reaffirmed the intelligible-principle standard and upheld the statutory and administrative structure for universal-service contributions. It found usable limits in the statute's sufficiency requirement and universal-service principles, and it treated the private administrator's role as subordinate because the FCC retained final authority. The case confirms that major questions, statutory interpretation, public delegation, and private delegation remain related but distinct inquiries ([21] [22]).

SEC v. Jarkesy constrained administrative enforcement forums. The Court held that an in-house SEC action seeking civil penalties for securities fraud implicated the Seventh Amendment jury right and did not qualify for the public-rights exception. CRS observes that the decision may affect other statutes authorizing agencies to impose civil penalties in-house, but its reach depends on whether other claims resemble common-law actions and how the public-rights doctrine applies. The verified holding is narrower than the proposition that every civil penalty requires a district-court jury ([15] [16]).

FCC v. AT&T then identified a constitutionally different penalty structure. The FCC could make preliminary findings and state a forfeiture amount without a jury because its order was nonbinding: the government could collect only after proving its case de novo before a jury in district court, and the FCC's findings had no conclusive effect there. Read together, Jarkesy and FCC v. AT&T make the consequences of the agency order and the later court proceeding decisive to Seventh Amendment analysis ([24] [25]).

Together, Loper Bright, West Virginia, and Jarkesy show that procedure, interpretation, and forum are independent legal questions. An agency may follow notice and comment yet exceed statutory authority; it may possess substantive authority yet choose an unconstitutional adjudicative forum; it may select a lawful forum yet fail to explain its action on the record. Administrative legality is conjunctive: authority, procedure, reasoning, evidence, and constitutional structure must each pass their applicable test ([11] [13] [15] [19]).

## Implications

For agencies, the framework makes authority mapping the first design task. Before drafting a rule or initiating enforcement, officials should identify the exact statutory provision authorizing the action, the object regulated, the available remedy, the required procedure, and the proper review route. Broad mission language should not be treated as a substitute for operative authority, especially where a program would be novel or economically consequential. After Loper Bright and West Virginia, a durable action needs a textually grounded account of delegation rather than reliance on ambiguity or general purpose ([11] [13]).

Procedure should then be matched to legal effect. If an agency intends to bind the public with a new norm, notice-and-comment rulemaking is ordinarily the appropriate path unless a valid exception or different statutory procedure applies. If it intends only to explain existing law or announce a tentative enforcement posture, guidance may be faster and more adaptable, but the document should not be administered as if it independently created liability. Clear statements about nonbinding effect, decisional discretion, and the source of actual legal obligations reduce both confusion and litigation risk ([1] [17] [18]).

The administrative record is a governance instrument, not merely a litigation file. A high-quality record identifies the problem, statutory criteria, evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, material comments, and reasons for the final choice. State Farm means that omitting a central alternative or relying on a conclusion unsupported by the record can invalidate policy regardless of its political support. Record discipline also improves reversibility: a later administration can change policy, but it can see what factual premises and reliance interests require acknowledgment ([7] [19]).

For adjudicators and enforcement officials, legitimacy depends on both impartiality and intelligible forum selection. Investigative staff should not control the decision in the same contested case where separation-of-functions rules apply. Parties should know the allegations, governing authority, record, and route of appeal. Jarkesy and FCC v. AT&T require agencies and Congress to examine whether a claim seeking a legal remedy resembles a common-law action, whether the agency's order itself creates a binding obligation, and whether the government must later prove its case de novo to a jury before collection. Moving every dispute to court would sacrifice administrative specialization, but assuming every congressionally assigned matter is a public right would ignore the constitutional boundary the Court enforced ([1] [6] [15] [16] [24] [25]).

For regulated persons, the first practical question is: what kind of agency action is this? A legislative rule, interpretive rule, policy statement, adjudicative order, license condition, inspection demand, and informal warning do not have the same legal effect or review path. The second question is whether the action is final and reviewable. The third is which objections must be raised before the agency to preserve them. Comments and hearing submissions should connect objections to statutory text, evidence, alternatives, procedure, and requested relief rather than rely only on policy disagreement ([2] [3] [18] [19]).

Participation is most effective when it improves the decisional record. In rulemaking, a useful comment supplies verifiable data, identifies a statutory mismatch, tests assumptions, quantifies a burden, or proposes an administrable alternative. In adjudication, useful participation isolates contested facts and law, preserves evidentiary objections, and uses available internal review. Volume, rhetoric, or identical form comments do not substitute for relevant analysis because agencies must reason from the record rather than count preferences ([1] [4] [7]).

For Congress, administrative-law quality begins in statutory drafting. A legislature that wants an agency to set technical thresholds, grant waivers, choose among factors, impose civil penalties, or adjudicate specified claims should say so and identify constraints. It can prescribe notice, consultation, evidentiary hearings, deadlines, review courts, standards, remedies, and whether a nonbinding agency penalty determination must be proved de novo before a jury prior to collection. Clear delegations reduce the risk that courts will infer a mismatch between the claimed power and the statute, while review provisions can channel challenges without eliminating accountability. Specificity has costs because future conditions cannot all be predicted; the design problem is to state outcomes and boundaries clearly while leaving administrable discretion inside them ([12] [14] [16] [25]).

For courts, the differentiated standards preserve institutional roles. Independent judgment on law after Loper Bright does not authorize judges to select regulatory policy. Arbitrary-and-capricious review tests reasoning without substituting a preferred outcome. Substantial-evidence review examines the whole record without retrying the case. Vermont Yankee prevents courts from inventing extra procedure, while Mathews permits constitutional procedure to vary with interests and error risks. Applying the wrong standard can be as distortive as reaching the wrong answer under the right one ([7] [8] [9] [10] [11]).

The framework also clarifies connections among regulatory fields. Securities regulation depends on SEC rulemaking, disclosure interpretations, investigations, and enforcement forums; Jarkesy directly altered one part of that architecture. Environmental law depends on technically dense records and statutory authority, as State Farm's reasoned-decision model and West Virginia's clear-authorization inquiry illustrate. Health regulation combines scientific rulemaking, licensing, inspections, guidance, benefit determinations, and adjudication, making the boundary between binding rules and explanatory policy especially consequential. These sectors differ in substance but share the same administrative-law questions ([7] [13] [15] [18]).

Administrative discretion creates recurring failure modes. A vague delegation can invite an agency to claim more power than Congress granted. A rushed emergency rule can overextend good cause. A guidance document can become coercive in practice. A combined enforcement process can create actual or perceived bias. A technically strong policy can fail because the agency ignored a decisive comment, concealed a premise, or chose the wrong forum. Conversely, excessive procedure can delay protection, consume adjudicative capacity, and privilege sophisticated repeat participants. The framework's purpose is not maximal procedure but procedure proportionate to legal effect, protected interests, and error risk ([3] [5] [10] [18]).

The author's assessment is that the most reliable administrative decision can be reconstructed as a chain: Congress authorized this actor to address this subject by this instrument; the agency used the required procedure; affected persons had the legally required opportunity to participate; the decision rests on the stated record; the explanation answers the material issues; and the reviewing institution applied the correct standard. A missing link identifies a legal vulnerability more precisely than a general claim that an agency is either expert or unaccountable. Administrative law matters because it turns that chain into enforceable institutional practice ([1] [2] [7] [11]).
