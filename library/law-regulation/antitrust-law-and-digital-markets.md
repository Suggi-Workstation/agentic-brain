# Antitrust Law in Digital Markets Must Distinguish Durable Power From Lawful Scale

Digital platforms can become large because they coordinate users efficiently, yet the same network effects, defaults, data advantages, and ecosystem controls can make competitive displacement unusually difficult. Antitrust law therefore asks not whether a platform is merely large, but whether it possesses legally cognizable market power and has acquired, maintained, or extended that power through conduct that harms the competitive process; newer European Union and United Kingdom regimes add prospective duties for designated firms rather than waiting for a completed antitrust violation.[1][3][7][10]

## Background

Modern digital-market disputes sit within legal rules developed long before search engines, app stores, social networks, and programmatic advertising. In the United States, Section 2 of the Sherman Act distinguishes monopoly power from unlawful monopolization. The D.C. Circuit stated in United States v. Microsoft that monopolization requires both possession of monopoly power in a relevant market and willful acquisition or maintenance of that power, as distinct from growth caused by a superior product, business acumen, or historical accident. That formulation matters because antitrust law protects competition rather than guaranteeing that every rival survives. A platform can lawfully win a large share through quality, innovation, or lower costs; liability requires a further showing about power, conduct, and competitive harm.[1]

The Microsoft litigation also supplied an early digital-market example of how technical design and distribution contracts can reinforce one another. The case concerned a personal-computer operating-system monopoly and conduct affecting web browsers, including restrictions imposed on original-equipment manufacturers and actions that reduced the distribution opportunities of rival middleware. The D.C. Circuit treated network effects and applications barriers to entry as relevant to durable power, but it also insisted on a properly defined market and proof of barriers when plaintiffs advanced a separate attempted-monopolization theory for browsers. The decision thus established two enduring propositions: digital architecture can be exclusionary when used to preserve monopoly power, and technological language does not relax the plaintiff's burden to define the competitive arena and prove the elements of the claim.[1]

Platform economics made those legal inquiries more difficult. Rochet and Tirole formalized how a platform may need to attract two interdependent groups and allocate prices unevenly between them. A service may rationally charge one side little or nothing because participation on that side creates value for the other side. Price on only one side is therefore an incomplete measure of platform output, welfare, or power. The United States Supreme Court applied this logic in Ohio v. American Express, holding that the credit-card network at issue was a two-sided transaction platform and that evidence of a price increase on one side alone did not demonstrate an anticompetitive exercise of market power in the relevant transaction market.[2][13]

Digital markets also brought non-price dimensions to the foreground. A user may pay no money for search, social networking, mapping, or email while supplying attention, data, and opportunities for advertising. Quality, privacy, innovation, interoperability, ranking, and the terms offered to business users can therefore carry the competitive effects that a conventional price analysis would otherwise reveal. The United Kingdom's Digital Competition Expert Panel concluded that network effects, economies of scale and scope, data advantages, and restrictions on switching or multi-homing can combine to produce high concentration and winner-takes-most dynamics. It also emphasized that deliberate choices about acquisitions, standards, data access, and interoperability can strengthen those structural tendencies.[12]

The rise of ecosystems expanded the possible theories of harm. A platform may operate a core service, control access to complementary services, set rules for dependent businesses, and compete with some of those businesses. Conduct disputes accordingly include exclusive or default distribution agreements, tying and bundling, restrictions on steering customers elsewhere, discriminatory access to interfaces or data, self-preferencing in rankings or auctions, and acquisitions of actual or potential rivals. Each theory requires attention to mechanism. Vertical integration or self-supply can reduce transaction costs and improve a product, while the same control can also create an ability and incentive to disadvantage rivals. Concentration and integration are therefore evidence requiring analysis, not automatic legal conclusions.[1][3][6]

Merger law addresses risk before it becomes completed monopoly maintenance. Section 7 of the Clayton Act prohibits acquisitions whose effect may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly, and the Federal Trade Commission describes the inquiry as forward-looking. The 2023 Merger Guidelines direct the U.S. agencies to examine competition between platforms, competition on a platform, and competition to displace a platform. They also address entrenchment of dominant positions, acquisitions involving potential entrants, and the ways control of products or services used by rivals can change competitive incentives. The Guidelines state agency enforcement practice rather than replacing statutes or binding courts, but they organize the evidence agencies seek in platform transactions.[3][14]

Traditional antitrust enforcement is usually ex post and case-specific: an authority investigates identified conduct or a transaction, proves a legal violation under the governing standard, and seeks relief tied to that violation. The European Union supplemented Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union with the Digital Markets Act. The DMA explains that conventional competition provisions apply to gatekeepers but reach particular instances of market power and behavior through enforcement that occurs after the event. It therefore imposes a set of obligations and prohibitions on designated gatekeepers for listed core platform services, while preserving competition-law enforcement.[7]

The United Kingdom adopted a related but more individualized model in Part 1 of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024. The Competition and Markets Authority may designate an undertaking with strategic market status for a digital activity when statutory conditions including substantial and entrenched market power and a position of strategic significance are met. The Act permits tailored conduct requirements and pro-competition interventions. In October 2025 the CMA designated Apple and Google with strategic market status for their respective mobile platforms, while stressing that designation itself was not a finding of wrongdoing and did not create immediate requirements by itself.[10][11]

The resulting field is not a choice between old antitrust and new regulation. The author's synthesis is that the legal systems now use three complementary time horizons. Merger review tries to prevent a transaction from creating or entrenching power; monopolization or abuse-of-dominance proceedings examine whether established power was protected or extended unlawfully; and gatekeeper regimes can impose prospective rules where lawmakers concluded that repeated case-by-case adjudication was too slow or incomplete. The hard task in all three remains causal: identify the market or regulated activity, specify the mechanism that blocks rivalry, distinguish harm to a competitor from harm to competition, and choose relief that opens the competitive process without destroying the efficiencies users value.[1][3][7][10]

## Core Concepts

### The Legal Object Is the Competitive Process

Antitrust law does not make size, success, or concentration illegal by themselves. Under the Microsoft formulation, a U.S. monopolization case requires monopoly power in a relevant market and exclusionary conduct that maintains or acquires that power rather than success attributable to competition on the merits. The distinction prevents the law from punishing a firm merely for creating a better product. It also means that evidence of harmed rivals is insufficient unless the mechanism tends to impair the competitive process through reduced output, quality, innovation, choice, or another cognizable competitive dimension.[1]

This distinction is especially important in digital markets because scale often improves the service. More participants can improve matching, attract complementors, spread fixed costs, and increase compatibility. Breaking a network into smaller pieces can sacrifice some of those benefits. At the same time, scale can make exclusion more effective: a default contract can deny a rival the queries needed to improve, a closed interface can prevent complementors from multi-homing, and a discriminatory auction rule can channel transactions toward the platform's own service. The author's synthesis is that legal analysis must separate scale produced by participation from restraints that prevent others from contesting that scale.[4][6][12]

Harm to a competitor and harm to competition are not synonymous. A platform's product improvement may displace an inferior rival and benefit users. By contrast, a contract that closes a critical distribution channel can keep a rival below efficient scale without improving the product users receive. Courts therefore evaluate the challenged conduct in context, including its design, coverage, duration, business justification, effect on entry, and connection to the defendant's power. The same formal practice can have different legal significance when used by a firm without durable power and by a gatekeeper controlling a bottleneck.[1][4]

### Relevant Markets Define the Arena of Power

A relevant market identifies the products or services that constrain one another and the geographic area in which that rivalry occurs. It is not simply the defendant's preferred product category or every service competing for a user's time. The inquiry asks what substitutes customers can turn to, and in some cases direct evidence of durable power can supplement the inference drawn from shares and entry barriers. Market definition remains consequential because a narrow boundary can reveal dominance while an overly broad boundary can dilute it among products that do not discipline the challenged conduct.[1]

Zero-price services require adaptation, not abandonment, of this inquiry. If users pay with attention rather than money, analysts can examine reductions in quality, privacy, innovation, or access, along with the prices and terms imposed on advertisers or business users. A service that is free to consumers may still be one side of a revenue-generating system. The relevant competitive question is which alternatives constrain the platform's terms on each side and how a change on one side affects participation on another.[2][13]

Ohio v. American Express supplies a specific rule for two-sided transaction platforms, where the platform cannot complete the relevant transaction without simultaneous participation by both sides. The Supreme Court required evaluation of both cardholders and merchants in the transaction market and rejected reliance on merchant-side price evidence alone. The decision does not make every digital service a single two-sided market. Ad-supported media, operating systems, exchanges, and marketplaces have different transaction structures, substitution patterns, and feedback effects, so the classification must follow evidence rather than the label "platform."[2][6]

Market definition also changes over time. In the FTC's case against Meta, the district court ruled for Meta in December 2025 after finding that the agency had not proved its proposed personal-social-networking market and present monopoly power in a market that, in the court's analysis, had evolved to include intense competition from TikTok and YouTube. The FTC appealed in January 2026, and the appeal remained pending at the time of this topic's creation. The procedural history illustrates why evidence about current substitution and product convergence can defeat a theory even when earlier acquisitions remain central to the government's account.[15][16]

### Durable Market Power Depends on Barriers, Not Share Alone

Market share is evidence of power, but durability depends on barriers to entry and expansion. Microsoft described monopoly power as the ability to control prices or exclude competition and recognized the importance of a dominant share protected by entry barriers. In digital markets, the relevant barriers can include network effects, data advantages, high fixed costs, access to default distribution, ecosystem switching costs, compatibility limits, brand, and the need to build several sides of a platform at once.[1][12]

Network effects can create a feedback loop. A larger user base attracts more complementors; more complementors improve the service; the improved service attracts more users. Data can create another loop when greater usage produces information that improves matching or prediction, although the competitive significance of data depends on whether the information is exclusive, replicable, perishable, or available from other sources. Switching costs and multi-homing determine how strongly those loops protect the incumbent. If users and businesses can use several services at low cost, an incumbent's installed base may constrain rivals less than headline share suggests.[12][13]

Defaults and distribution can become barriers when they steer enough demand to deny rivals the scale needed to compete. In the Google search litigation, the district court found in August 2024 that Google maintained monopolies in U.S. general search services and general search text advertising through unlawful distribution agreements. The court's later remedy restricted specified conditioning and exclusive arrangements and imposed data-sharing measures rather than treating Google's share alone as the violation. The case connects power, conduct, and remedy through the scale created by distribution.[4][5]

Control of infrastructure can create a different barrier. In the Google advertising-technology case, the Eastern District of Virginia found in April 2025 that Google unlawfully monopolized open-web publisher ad-server and ad-exchange markets and unlawfully tied its publisher ad server to its exchange. The September 2026 remedy required interoperability and data sharing, prohibited specified discriminatory and self-preferencing conduct, and linked relief to the mechanisms through which Google's integrated tools disadvantaged rivals. The adjudication did not condemn vertical integration as such; it addressed proven conduct involving particular markets and technical relationships.[6]

### Exclusion Must Be Distinguished From Competition on the Merits

Exclusionary conduct is behavior that tends to maintain or acquire monopoly power through means other than competition on the merits. The category is functional rather than confined to a single list. Microsoft considered contractual restrictions, technical integration, and interference with distribution while assessing whether each act had an anticompetitive effect, a procompetitive justification, and a sufficient connection to maintenance of the operating-system monopoly. This effects-based approach recognizes that software design can be both innovative and exclusionary, requiring a court to identify the mechanism rather than infer liability from product integration alone.[1]

Exclusive dealing and default agreements are not automatically unlawful. Their significance depends on matters such as coverage, duration, practical alternatives, the share of efficient distribution they foreclose, and whether they generate efficiencies. A default can reduce user search costs; a portfolio agreement can simplify installation; and revenue sharing can subsidize a product. The concern arises when a firm with durable power uses such arrangements to close enough access that rivals cannot obtain scale even if users could benefit from their products. The Google search judgment is a current example of a court finding that distribution contracts crossed that line.[4][5]

Self-preferencing refers to a platform favoring its own downstream or complementary service when setting rankings, access conditions, or transaction rules. It is not a universal U.S. antitrust offense. Liability under general antitrust law still depends on a recognized claim, market power, exclusionary effects, and the governing jurisdiction's doctrine. European Union law has developed a different path. In September 2024 the Court of Justice upheld the judgment concerning Google Shopping, where the Commission had found that Google favored its own comparison-shopping service in general-search results while subjecting competing services to demotion. The court emphasized the particular characteristics and discriminatory operation at issue rather than declaring every instance of first-party preference abusive.[8]

The DMA converts some recurring concerns into ex ante obligations for designated gatekeepers. Its rules address matters including steering, combinations of data, interoperability in specified settings, access for business users, and treatment of a gatekeeper's own services. Because obligations attach through designation and the regulation's terms, enforcement need not reproduce a full abuse-of-dominance case for every practice. The European Commission's April 2025 decisions found Apple in breach of the DMA's anti-steering obligation and Meta in breach concerning the choice offered under its "consent or pay" model, imposing fines and ordering changes. Those were DMA non-compliance findings, not judgments that concentration alone violated antitrust law.[7][9]

### Mergers Can Remove Existing, Potential, or Platform Competition

Digital mergers can combine direct competitors, unite a platform with a critical input or complement, or eliminate a firm that might develop into a future constraint. Section 7 of the Clayton Act is preventive: it addresses transactions whose effect may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. The 2023 Merger Guidelines instruct the agencies to examine whether a merger can entrench a dominant position, eliminate substantial competition, limit access to products rivals use to compete, or affect competition between, on, or to displace a platform.[3][14]

Potential competition is difficult because the counterfactual concerns a path that the acquisition may stop. Evidence can include the target's capabilities, plans, growth, strategic significance, and the incumbent's internal assessment, but uncertainty remains about whether entry would have occurred and become competitively meaningful. The FTC's Meta case tests this problem after consummation. The agency alleges that buying Instagram and WhatsApp eliminated significant competitive threats; the district court rejected the agency's trial case on market definition and current power, and the FTC appealed. The case warns against reporting allegations, an acquisition approval, a trial judgment, and a pending appeal as if they were the same legal conclusion.[15][16]

Platform mergers also require more than a conventional horizontal share calculation. A transaction can affect competition among platforms, rules governing participants on a platform, or the prospects that a different technology displaces the platform. It can combine datasets, distribution, complementors, or access points in ways that alter entry conditions without immediately raising a consumer price. The Guidelines make these mechanisms part of agency analysis, but the agency must still connect a plausible theory to evidence and the statutory standard.[3]

### Ex Post Antitrust and Ex Ante Gatekeeper Rules Use Different Triggers

General antitrust law is conduct- and transaction-specific. It usually requires a defined market or another legally sufficient route to prove power, an identified restraint or acquisition, competitive effects, and a causal connection to relief. Its advantages include individualized evidence, judicial process, and the ability to distinguish efficient conduct from exclusion. Its disadvantages can include delay, expense, and remedies imposed only after market conditions have changed.[1][12]

The DMA instead designates gatekeepers for core platform services under statutory criteria and applies specified obligations. Its stated objective is contestable and fair digital-sector markets, and it supplements rather than displaces Articles 101 and 102 TFEU. The framework trades some case-specific liability analysis for clearer prospective rules, while retaining procedures concerning designation, specification, investigation, defenses available under the regulation, and proportionality of remedies.[7]

The United Kingdom model gives the CMA authority to designate firms with strategic market status in a particular digital activity and impose tailored conduct requirements. It also authorizes pro-competition interventions when a designated undertaking's activity has an adverse effect on competition. This design is neither a universal code applied identically to every large platform nor an ordinary infringement action. The designation is activity-specific, forward-looking, and separate from a finding that the firm committed wrongdoing.[10][11]

The author's synthesis is that the regimes should be compared by legal function rather than by slogans such as "strict" or "permissive." Antitrust adjudicates a charged violation and ties relief to proven harm. Gatekeeper regulation manages recurring risks associated with designated positions before each act becomes a completed infringement. Both can fail if the authority defines the bottleneck incorrectly, ignores efficiencies, writes rules that incumbents can evade, or cannot monitor technical compliance.[7][10][12]

### Remedies Must Restore Contestability Without Operating the Firm

An antitrust remedy should stop the unlawful conduct, prevent recurrence, and restore competitive conditions connected to the violation. Microsoft explains that relief must unfetter the market, terminate the illegal monopoly where warranted, deny the fruits of unlawful conduct, and ensure no practices likely to produce monopolization remain. It also warns that structural relief in a monopolization case requires a sufficient causal connection between the violation and the creation or maintenance of power.[1]

Structural remedies alter ownership or market structure, as through divestiture. Conduct remedies constrain behavior, as through prohibitions on exclusivity or discrimination. Access remedies require sharing of interfaces, data, or technical information; choice and steering remedies preserve routes through which users or business customers can reach alternatives. Digital cases often combine forms because a clean separation may still require interoperability, and a behavioral rule may be ineffective without data or interface access.[1][5][6]

The Google search remedy illustrates a conduct-and-access approach. It restricted contracts that condition licensing or revenue sharing on specified placement of Google search-related products and imposed measures intended to open search distribution and data to rivals. The Google ad-tech remedy used interoperability, data-sharing, non-discrimination, and prohibitions on specified self-preferencing. These orders show that courts can target the scale and technical mechanisms supporting exclusion without assuming that every integrated asset must be divested.[5][6]

Gatekeeper statutes add administrative remedies. The DMA permits fines and, for systematic non-compliance, proportionate behavioral or structural remedies; the UK Act permits conduct requirements, enforcement orders, commitments, final-offer mechanisms in specified circumstances, and pro-competition orders. The author's assessment is that remedy quality should be judged against five questions: does it remove the proven barrier, preserve product benefits not caused by the violation, create an administrable compliance test, resist circumvention as technology changes, and provide evidence by which an independent decision-maker can evaluate results?[7][10]

## Evidence

### Microsoft: Architecture and Contracts Can Maintain Digital Monopoly Power

United States v. Microsoft remains the doctrinal starting point because the D.C. Circuit reviewed separate acts rather than treating the defendant's integrated product as one undifferentiated offense. The court affirmed monopoly-maintenance liability based on portions of Microsoft's course of conduct, including restrictions affecting original-equipment manufacturers and other distribution channels, while reversing or remanding other theories. Its method linked each challenged act to foreclosure of middleware threats and the applications barrier protecting the operating-system monopoly. It also rejected an attempted-monopolization claim where the plaintiffs had not adequately defined the browser market or established its barriers to entry.[1]

The case provides evidence about method rather than a rule that all integration is suspect. Technical design can improve a product, and distribution agreements can provide efficiencies, but neither category receives automatic immunity when used to protect monopoly power. The opinion's act-by-act treatment, burden framework, and insistence on causation became a model for later digital cases. Its remedy discussion also explains why a court needs a connection between the conduct proved and the structure it proposes to change.[1]

### American Express: A Platform Market May Require Both Sides

Ohio v. American Express tested anti-steering provisions that prevented merchants from encouraging customers to use competing cards. The Supreme Court characterized credit-card networks as two-sided transaction platforms, defined the relevant market to include both sides, and concluded that the plaintiffs had not shown anticompetitive effects in that market. In particular, evidence of higher merchant fees alone was insufficient because the transaction jointly served cardholders and merchants, and the plaintiffs had not proved a net reduction in transactions or another market-wide suppression of competition.[2]

The decision is evidence against one-sided inference, not proof that platform restraints are lawful. It directs analysis toward the transaction structure, cross-side effects, and total competitive outcome for that market. Digital-market analysis must therefore establish whether the service is comparable to the simultaneous transaction platform in American Express or whether distinct markets and separate harms are more accurate. The ad-exchange court, for example, evaluated both sides of the exchange while recognizing that its structure was not identical to the credit-card platform.[2][6]

### Google Search: Defaults, Scale, and Data Were Joined in One Theory

In August 2024 the District Court for the District of Columbia found Google liable for maintaining monopolies in general search services and general search text advertising. The liability theory centered on distribution agreements that secured default or exclusive placement and reinforced scale. The court distinguished possession of monopoly power from unlawful maintenance: Google's quality and prior success did not immunize contracts that the court found foreclosed substantial distribution and deprived rivals of queries needed to compete effectively.[4]

After a remedies trial, the September 2025 order prohibited specified conditioning arrangements and exclusive contracts, limited certain revenue-sharing structures, and required sharing of search index and user-interaction data under defined conditions. The remedy reached emerging generative-artificial-intelligence products where necessary to prevent circumvention, while declining the government's requested Chrome divestiture. The sequence demonstrates the discipline of separate stages: market and conduct findings first, then a remedy record addressing what was necessary and administrable.[5]

### Google Advertising Technology: Vertical Integration Was Tested Through Specific Conduct

The Eastern District of Virginia's April 2025 opinion examined the chain of tools through which publishers sell open-web display advertising and advertisers buy it. The court found monopoly power in publisher ad servers and ad exchanges, unlawful monopoly maintenance in those markets, and unlawful tying between Google's publisher ad server and exchange, while rejecting some other government claims. The evidence concerned acquisitions, contractual and technical integration, auction rules, and the relation between Google's tools rather than a presumption that operating at several levels of the stack was itself unlawful.[6]

In September 2026 the court ordered behavioral and access relief including interoperability with rival products, data sharing, anti-discrimination protections, and prohibitions on specified self-preferencing bidding conduct. The remedy is significant because it treats interoperability as a means of restoring rivalry within an integrated technical system. It also creates monitoring challenges: effective compliance depends on interface performance, data quality, timing, and treatment of rival bids, not merely on removing words from a contract.[6]

### Google Shopping: EU Abuse Doctrine Reached Discriminatory Ranking

The European Commission's Google Shopping decision found that Google favored its own comparison-shopping service in general-search results while rival comparison services were subject to demotion. In September 2024 the Court of Justice dismissed Google's appeal and upheld the General Court. The judgment explained that competition on the merits may lawfully marginalize less efficient rivals, but the conduct at issue could be classified as an abuse in light of its specific characteristics, including differential treatment and the importance of traffic from general search.[8]

This case is evidence that self-preferencing analysis is contextual even in a jurisdiction willing to condemn the practice under abuse-of-dominance law. The legal conclusion depended on Google's dominant position, the features of the service and ranking conduct, and capability to restrict competition. The decision should not be reduced to the proposition that a firm must always treat its own and third-party products identically.[8]

### DMA Enforcement: Prospective Duties Change the Liability Question

The European Commission's April 2025 Apple and Meta decisions demonstrate how DMA enforcement differs from a conventional dominance case. The Commission found Apple's App Store steering terms non-compliant with the obligation allowing developers to inform users about alternative offers, and it ordered removal of technical and commercial restrictions while imposing a EUR 500 million fine. It found Meta's then-operative "consent or pay" model non-compliant with the obligation to offer users a less data-intensive alternative and imposed a EUR 200 million fine. The decisions followed investigations and preliminary findings under the DMA framework.[9]

These decisions did not require the Commission to establish a new relevant market and prove abusive dominance for each obligation. The operative questions were gatekeeper status, the applicable statutory duty, the design actually implemented, and compliance. That change can accelerate intervention, but it also shifts contestability questions into the interpretation, specification, and proportionality of statutory duties.[7][9]

### UK Strategic Market Status: Designation Is a Regulatory Trigger, Not a Verdict

The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act directs the CMA to assess substantial and entrenched market power and strategic significance for a digital activity, including a forward-looking assessment. In October 2025 the CMA confirmed strategic-market-status designations for Apple and Google in their respective mobile platforms. The CMA said both possessed substantial and entrenched power and strategic significance, but expressly stated that designation was not a finding of wrongdoing and imposed no immediate requirement by itself.[10][11]

This is evidence of an institutional separation between identifying a regulated position and finding a breach. The designation creates jurisdiction for tailored conduct requirements or pro-competition work; subsequent measures require their own procedures and reasons. The distinction guards against treating the status label as proof that every contested business practice is harmful.[10][11]

### Meta: A Failed Trial Theory Shows the Burden of Current Market Proof

The FTC's Meta case alleged that acquisitions of Instagram and WhatsApp helped maintain a personal-social-networking monopoly. After trial, the district court entered judgment for Meta in December 2025, reasoning that the FTC had not proved its proposed current market and monopoly power in light of competition that included TikTok and YouTube. The FTC filed a notice of appeal in January 2026 and continued to allege unlawful maintenance through acquisition of competitive threats.[15][16]

The opposing outcomes at different procedural stages are analytically useful. Internal documents and acquisition history can support a theory about potential competition, but the plaintiff must still satisfy the legal elements that govern the case at the time of adjudication. Product evolution can alter substitution and market boundaries during long litigation. The author's synthesis is that digital antitrust requires dated evidence: a market definition, share, or competitive threat established for one period cannot simply be carried forward without testing current behavior.[15][16]

### Comparative Record: Doctrine Is Adaptable but Remedies Are the Bottleneck

Taken together, these matters show that established doctrine can reach digital conduct. Microsoft addressed technical and contractual exclusion; American Express adapted market analysis to a transaction platform; the two Google cases applied monopoly rules to defaults, data, auctions, tying, and integrated tools; and Google Shopping applied EU abuse doctrine to discriminatory ranking. The cases also show disagreement and limits. American Express rejected the government's proof, and the district court rejected the FTC's Meta market at trial.[1][2][4][6][8][15]

The newer regimes respond less to a total absence of law than to speed, repeatability, and remedial difficulty. The Furman Review argued that concentrated platform markets combine structural tipping forces with conduct and acquisition choices, and it recommended a pro-competition regime alongside faster antitrust and merger enforcement. The DMA and UK Act institutionalized variants of that logic. The evidence therefore supports a bounded conclusion: digital features complicate market definition, power, causation, and relief, but they do not erase the distinction between lawful success and exclusionary conduct.[7][10][12]

## Implications

### For Courts and Competition Authorities

Authorities should begin with a mechanism rather than a label. "Network effects," "gatekeeper," "self-preferencing," and "ecosystem" identify possible sources of concern but do not establish a violation. A disciplined case specifies the relevant market or statutory digital activity, the users and business participants affected, the bottleneck controlled, the conduct that changes access or incentives, the counterfactual without that conduct, and the measurable competitive outcome. Microsoft, American Express, and the Meta judgment show the cost of skipping market and causal proof; the Google cases show how a coherent chain can connect distribution or infrastructure control to durable power.[1][2][4][6][15]

Evidence should be matched to the platform's architecture. Transaction platforms require cross-side analysis; ad-supported services may require separate attention to users and advertisers; innovation platforms require evidence about developers, interfaces, and complements; exchanges require evidence about participants on both sides and the rules matching them. Authorities should not assume that a single market contains every participant merely because one firm coordinates them. Nor should a zero consumer price end the inquiry when the disputed harm concerns quality, privacy, advertiser terms, business-user access, or innovation.[2][3][6][13]

Time is itself an evidentiary variable. Digital products, distribution routes, and user practices can change during litigation. Market studies and complaints should state the period measured, preserve technical evidence, and update substitution analysis before remedy. The Meta judgment demonstrates that a market theory can lose force as products converge; the search and ad-tech remedies demonstrate that relief must address emerging routes of circumvention without becoming detached from the adjudicated violation.[5][6][15]

Remedy design should include a verification model before entry of the order. An interoperability obligation needs performance standards, documentation, testing access, version-change rules, and a process for resolving degradation claims. A data-sharing obligation needs definitions of fields, latency, privacy safeguards, security, and parity. A non-discrimination rule needs a benchmark against which treatment can be compared. The author's assessment is that a remedy too vague to generate auditable evidence shifts the dispute from competition into endless interpretation and is unlikely to restore contestability.[5][6]

### For Legislatures and Digital-Market Regulators

Ex ante regulation should be used for recurring bottlenecks where delay and repeated case-specific proof are likely to let foreclosure become self-reinforcing. It should not become a substitute for defining the regulated activity and expected competitive mechanism. The DMA identifies core platform services and statutory obligations; the UK regime makes designation activity-specific and permits tailored requirements. Both structures are more defensible when duties correspond to identified dependencies such as steering, interoperability, access to data, or discriminatory platform rules.[7][10]

Designation and infringement should remain conceptually separate. The CMA's mobile-platform designations explicitly do not find wrongdoing. That separation permits prospective supervision without turning status into guilt. It also protects analytical clarity: a firm may meet a gatekeeper or strategic-market-status threshold because of durable scale, yet a specific requirement still needs a statutory basis, a reasoned connection to competition, and proportionate implementation.[10][11]

Regulators must account for trade-offs with privacy, security, intellectual property, and product integrity. Data access can lower entry barriers while increasing exposure of personal or proprietary information. Interoperability can reduce switching costs while expanding attack surfaces. Steering can increase choice while creating fraud or payment disputes. The author's synthesis is that these concerns should shape safeguards and proportionality, not operate as blanket reasons either to reject competition remedies or to ignore adjacent legal duties.[7][10]

Sunset, review, and measurement provisions are essential where rules apply to changing technologies. A duty that improves contestability today may become irrelevant or harmful after a platform shift. Conversely, a formally compliant interface may fail in practice if technical changes degrade rivals. Regulators should define indicators before intervention, including switching, multi-homing, entry, use of alternative distribution, terms offered to business users, service quality, and innovation by complementors. The legal power to intervene is not evidence that the intervention worked.[10][12]

### For Platforms, Complementors, and Transaction Counsel

A dominant or designated platform should treat product design, contracts, and technical governance as one competition-law system. Legal risk does not reside only in agreement language. Defaults, ranking parameters, interface permissions, auction logic, data access, and update practices can interact to create exclusion. Internal review should document the user benefit claimed, alternatives considered, expected effect on rivals and complementors, and whether the measure is broader or longer than needed to achieve the legitimate objective.[1][6][8]

Business justifications should be contemporaneous and testable. A claim that integration improves security or quality is stronger when the firm can show engineering evidence, consistent treatment, and a narrower design analysis. It is weaker when technical restrictions apply selectively or change after a rival becomes threatening. Microsoft and the Google matters demonstrate that courts examine conduct in its commercial and technical context rather than accepting the categories "product design" or "integration" as conclusions.[1][4][6]

Merger diligence in digital markets should examine more than present horizontal overlap. Counsel should assess whether the target is a likely entrant, supplies a critical input, controls a distribution point, enables multi-homing, or could help displace the platform. Internal strategy documents can become evidence about the target's competitive significance. The FTC's Meta litigation also shows that such evidence is not self-executing: the legal case still needs a supportable market, power, conduct, and counterfactual.[3][15][16]

Complementors should identify dependencies that can become legal as well as commercial risks. These include access to users, discoverability, APIs, data portability, payment systems, ranking, and notice of rule changes. Contract negotiation may not eliminate a gatekeeper's leverage, but documentation of discriminatory changes, technical degradation, switching costs, and lost access can distinguish a competition concern from dissatisfaction with ordinary rivalry. Firms operating across jurisdictions must also distinguish a general antitrust claim from rights created directly by the DMA or a UK conduct requirement.[7][10]

### For Investors and Industry Analysts

Concentration is a starting signal, not a legal forecast. A high share can reflect superior execution, network efficiency, exclusion, or several causes at once. An analyst should map the relevant market, substitutes, multi-homing, control points, distribution agreements, data replicability, and entry requirements before treating share as durable power. The same map should identify which parts of the moat depend on user value and which depend on contractual or technical restraints vulnerable to legal intervention.[1][12]

Regulatory risk should be modeled by remedy mechanism rather than by fine size alone. A one-time fine may have little effect on long-term cash flows, while an anti-steering rule can alter take rates, a data-access rule can reduce an information advantage, an interoperability mandate can lower switching costs, and a distribution restriction can weaken a scale loop. Structural relief can change ownership and strategy more radically, but conduct remedies can also reshape economics if they reach a central bottleneck.[5][6][7][9]

Jurisdiction matters. U.S. monopolization law generally demands proof of power and exclusionary conduct; EU competition law and the DMA provide different routes; the UK regime permits tailored requirements after strategic-market-status designation. An analyst who imports a result from one regime into another without adjusting the legal trigger will misstate risk. The author's assessment is that the correct unit is the specific service in the specific jurisdiction under the specific procedure, not the global company as an undifferentiated regulatory object.[1][7][10]

Remedy implementation creates both downside and opportunity. Incumbents may lose exclusivity or control, while independent search services, ad-tech providers, app developers, payment providers, or interoperability specialists may gain access. Yet formal access does not guarantee adoption. Investors should track technical quality, default behavior, user switching, developer participation, and enforcement disputes after an order. The competitive effect appears in changed behavior, not in the headline announcing relief.[5][6]

### For Researchers and Public Debate

Claims should preserve procedural status. A complaint alleges; a trial opinion finds; an appellate judgment may affirm, reverse, or narrow; a designation creates regulatory jurisdiction; and a non-compliance decision applies a specific statutory duty. The Google search liability finding, the Google Shopping appellate judgment, the DMA Apple and Meta decisions, the UK designations, and the pending FTC appeal against Meta are different legal events. Combining them into a generic statement that a company "violated antitrust" would erase material distinctions.[4][8][9][11][16]

Research should separate structure, conduct, and outcome. Network effects and data can explain concentration; they do not prove exclusion. A restrictive interface can burden rivals; it does not establish consumer harm without a theory and evidence. A remedy can compel access; it does not prove restoration of competition without post-remedy measurement. The author's synthesis is that digital antitrust debates improve when each claim states which link in this chain it supports and which links remain uncertain.[1][12]

Comparative analysis should use common questions while preserving doctrinal differences. For each regime ask: what triggers jurisdiction, what must the authority prove, what defenses or justifications are available, what remedy can be imposed, who reviews the decision, and how compliance is measured? This method makes the U.S., EU, and UK approaches comparable without pretending they are interchangeable.[1][7][10]

The broad policy implication is restrained but consequential. Digital scale can produce substantial consumer and business value, and antitrust law would undermine competition if it punished that value merely because rivals lost. Durable control over distribution, interfaces, data, or transaction rules can also let a platform prevent displacement and transfer the benefits of participation into protected power. The legal task is to preserve the first while proving and remedying the second. That task requires precise markets, dated evidence, mechanism-specific theories, and remedies that can be independently tested.[1][3][5][6]
