# Migration, Diaspora, and Border Regimes -- Human Mobility Remade States, Labor, and Identity, and States Answered by Monopolizing Movement

People have always moved, but between 1840 and 1940 cheap steam transport, industrial labor demand, and frontier settlement raised long-distance migration to at least 150 million voyages across three great circuits: the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asia, and North Asia [1]. States answered this mobility by turning control over movement from a private and local prerogative into a national and international monopoly built on passports, registration, quotas, and citizenship rules [5][6][8]. The twentieth century added mass displacement by war and partition, which created the refugee as an international legal category and showed that border regimes decide who belongs as much as who moves [9][10].

## Background

For much of the twentieth century the dominant account of migration history was a modernization story. The geographer Wilbur Zelinsky's 1971 "mobility transition" hypothesis held that pre-modern societies were stable and self-sufficient, that most people stayed where they were born, and that only industrialization in the nineteenth century loosened these chains and produced unprecedented movement [2]. Jan and Leo Lucassen summarize this view and its critics: from the 1970s social historians such as Charles Tilly argued that proletarianization and wage labor began in north-western Europe in the sixteenth century, while studies of Germany by Steven Hochstadt and of Britain by Colin Pooley and Jean Turnbull found high mobility long before industrialization [2]. What remained missing was a long-run quantitative test, and the Lucassens built one for Europe from 1500 to 1900 [2].

A second lineage came from economic history. Richard Easterlin's 1961 study correlated nineteenth-century European emigration with rates of natural increase twenty years earlier, and later work by Tomaske and others added lagged emigration as an explanatory variable; Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson describe this debate as "crippled by lack of adequate data" for two decades until internationally comparable real wage series became available in the early 1990s [3]. Their work reframed the European exodus as part of an evolving global labor market in which migration helped Old World wages converge on New World wages [3]. Ran Abramitzky and Leah Boustan later surveyed research on immigration to the United States, the largest receiving country, including studies that link individuals across censuses and compare newly digitized passenger manifests with census counts [4].

A third lineage widened the geography. Adam McKeown observed that most histories recount the age of mass migration as a transatlantic age and remember Asian migration, if at all, as a limited number of indentured laborers; he argued that this memory obscured migrations of comparable size from India and southern China into Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean rim, and from northern China, Korea, and Russia into Manchuria, Siberia, and Central Asia [1]. Counting port and customs records, he estimated 55 to 58 million migrants from Europe to the Americas, 48 to 52 million from India and southern China to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean rim, and the South Pacific, and 46 to 51 million from North-East Asia and Russia to Manchuria, Siberia, Central Asia, and Japan between 1840 and 1940 [1]. On this account the period was a global, not an Atlantic, age of mass migration.

A fourth lineage asked how states learned to control movement. John Torpey argued, by analogy to Marx's account of the means of production and Weber's account of the means of violence, that modern states and the international state system "expropriated" the legitimate means of movement from private actors such as landlords, masters, and slaveholders [5]. In this account passports, identity documents, and registration systems are not neutral paperwork but instruments through which states made people dependent on state authorization to move [5]. Official histories of United States policy record the turning points of that process for one major receiving country: the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, which the National Archives describes as the first significant law restricting immigration into the United States, and the nationality quotas of 1921 and 1924 [6][7][8].

A fifth lineage is the history of forced displacement. The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) notes that until the twentieth century there were no universal standards for protecting people who fled persecution or war; efforts were local and ad hoc until the League of Nations, after the First World War, treated refugees as an international problem [9]. The Second World War, decolonization, and partition then produced displacements measured in tens of millions [9]. Prashant Bharadwaj, Asim Khwaja, and Atif Mian applied census methods to the 1947 partition of British India and estimated its flows district by district [10].

The author's synthesis of these lineages is that migration history now rests on three propositions. Mobility is a normal condition of human societies rather than a modern anomaly [2]. The scale and direction of mobility responded to transport costs, labor markets, and demography [1][2][3]. And states, not migrants alone, defined the categories -- laborer, citizen, alien, refugee -- through which mobility became legal or illegal [5][6][9].

## Core Concepts

### Mobility is old; modern migration changed its scale

The Lucassens defined migration as "cross-community" movement across a cultural, often linguistic, border and counted six forms for Europe: emigration, immigration, colonization of sparsely settled land, migration to cities of over 10,000 people, seasonal labor migration, and the movement of soldiers and sailors [2]. Their migration rate -- all such moves in a 50-year period divided by the population at mid-period, a proxy for the share of people who migrated in a lifetime -- rose from 11.4 percent in 1500-1550 to 17.7 percent in 1700-1750, then to 21 percent in 1800-1850 and 35.5 percent in 1850-1900 [2]. Early modern Europe was therefore mobile: cities above 10,000 people generally could not reproduce themselves until the nineteenth century, so virtually all of their growth came from migration, and the authors count more than 80 million soldiers and sailors over four centuries [2]. They conclude that the jump after 1850 came from cheaper and faster transport and that "it was not the underlying structural causes of migration that changed, but rather its scale" [2]. The Dutch Republic shows the point: its migration rate in 1600-1650 was about twice the European average of the mass-migration era after 1850 [2].

### Three circuits of the mass migration era

McKeown's estimates place three circuits of roughly equal size side by side [1]. The Atlantic circuit sent nearly 65 percent of transatlantic migrants to the United States, while Argentina had the largest foreign-born share of population; before the 1870s more than half of European emigrants came from the British Isles, after which emigration spread south and east [1]. The Southeast Asian circuit carried more than 29 million Indians and at least 19 million Chinese, with Indians going above all to Burma, Ceylon, and Malaya [1]. The North Asian circuit grew after the Qing dynasty relaxed restrictions on settling Manchuria and Russia opened Siberia after emancipation in 1861; it moved 28 to 33 million Chinese to Manchuria and Siberia and at least 13 million Russians to Central Asia and Siberia [1]. Global migration exceeded 3 million people a year after 1900, transatlantic flows peaked above 2.1 million in 1913, and the populations of the main destination regions rose from about 10 percent of world population in 1850 to 24 percent in 1950 [1]. Earlier transport set the pace: an Atlantic crossing took about a month in the mid-eighteenth century, the steamship Great Western crossed in 16 days in 1838, and by 1870 the crossing took 8 days [4].

### A spectrum from free to coerced movement

Migration did not divide neatly into free and forced. The majority of voluntary migrants who settled in the American colonies before 1775 arrived on indentured servants' contracts, which bound mostly young men from the United Kingdom or Germany to work for a defined period, often seven years, in exchange for passage [4]. After the abolition of slavery in the Caribbean sugar islands and South America in the 1830s, indenture was used to transport workers, primarily from India and China [4]. McKeown stresses, however, that indenture was a small part of Asian migration: slightly more than 2 million Asians migrated under indenture, less than 5 percent of all migration from Asia, and fewer than 8 percent of Indian emigrants were indentured at departure [1]. Many, if not most, Chinese migrants paid their own passage or borrowed from relatives and friends, others had passage advanced by wealthy Chinese under the credit-ticket system, and the organization of Chinese migration was largely independent of European regulation [1]. A majority of Indian migration to Southeast Asia moved under some form of assistance or debt obligation through recruiters known as kangani and maistry [1]. United States officials described the same credit mechanism: merchants paid passage and workers repaid the loans from their wages [7]. At the coerced end stood slavery: Portuguese traders brought hundreds of thousands of West African slaves to Iberia and Italy between the 1440s and the 1640s, and Tatar raiders carried captives from Russia, the Baltic, Poland, and Ukraine to the Ottoman Empire from the early sixteenth century [2]. Torpey places these arrangements in one history of control over movement: before states monopolized movement, masters controlled indentured servants, landlords controlled serfs, and slaveholders controlled slaves [5].

### Chains, networks, and diasporas

Migrants rarely moved alone in a social sense. Hatton and Williamson report that prepaid tickets bought by earlier emigrants financed 50 percent of Swedish emigrants in the 1880s, 40 percent of Norwegian emigrants in the 1870s, 30 percent of Finnish emigrants in 1891-1914, and about 25 percent of Danish emigrants in 1881-1895 [3]. Emigrants were typically young, male, single, and unskilled, and those who emigrated were aided by previous emigrants through remittances, accommodation, and help finding work [3]. Return was common. McKeown estimates that 63 to 75 percent of arrivals in Southeast Asia between 1871 and 1930 eventually returned, and return rates from the United States were 13 percent in the early 1880s and more than 50 percent in the 1910s [1]. These ties produced diasporas: dispersed communities linked to a homeland by money, marriage, and return. McKeown lists merchant diasporas -- Chinese, Indian Chettiars and Parsis, Hadhramis, Hausa, and Lebanese Christians -- that flourished in the period, while the Armenian diaspora was decimated by genocide [1]. Remittances in Asia flowed into land, houses, schools, and ancestral halls in home villages [1].

### The state monopoly of legitimate movement

Torpey's central concept is that states came to monopolize the authority to permit movement and that this process took centuries, moving from local to national control as poor relief was nationalized and local controls were replaced by restrictions at outer national borders [5]. Passport controls were not always tightening: the Reichstag of the North German Confederation voted to abolish them in 1867 [5]. Torpey cites a survey of international legal opinion just before the First World War, by Bertelsmann (1914), that found no consensus for the view that states had an unequivocal right to bar foreigners from entry [5]. Passports spread and were enforced more vigorously during and after the First World War [5]. States also deputized private carriers. The 1882 United States act fined any ship master who knowingly landed a Chinese laborer up to 500 dollars per laborer and required customs collectors to record departing laborers' names, ages, occupations, and physical marks in registry books [6]. Identity documents could also be created for people their states had abandoned: during Fridtjof Nansen's tenure as the League of Nations High Commissioner (1921-1930), an international conference he organized created travel and identity documents for refugees, known as Nansen passports [9][5].

### Citizenship, race, and the national-origins border

Border regimes classified people by origin as well as by legal status. The 1882 act suspended the entry of Chinese laborers, skilled and unskilled, for ten years and barred state and federal courts from admitting Chinese to citizenship [6]. The National Archives notes that for the first time federal law proscribed entry of an ethnic working group on the premise that it endangered the good order of certain localities [6]. The Scott Act of 1888 barred re-entry even to long-term legal residents, the Geary Act of 1892 renewed the ban and required each Chinese resident to register and obtain a certificate of residence or face deportation, and the ban became permanent in 1902 [6][7]. The 1924 Immigration Act set national quotas at 2 percent of each nationality counted in the 1890 census and excluded any alien ineligible for citizenship, which in practice excluded immigrants from Asia [8]. The State Department's historians conclude that the act's "most basic purpose" was "to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity" [8]. Citizenship rules produced comparable exclusions elsewhere in the decolonized world: Burma's constitution did not include the Rohingyas among indigenous groups qualifying for citizenship, and Bangladesh's 1972 citizenship provisions left many Biharis potentially stateless [9].

### Displacement, partition, and the refugee category

War and state breakup produced the largest single movements. UNHCR records that more than 40 million people were displaced in Europe in May 1945, besides about 13 million ethnic Germans expelled from eastern Europe and 11.3 million forced laborers and displaced persons found on the territory of the former German Reich [9]. The partition of British India in 1947 displaced an estimated 14 million people [9]. International responses formalized the refugee as a category: the 1951 Convention defined a refugee by a well-founded fear of persecution, limited it to events before 1 January 1951 until the 1967 Protocol removed that limit, and established the principle of non-refoulement without recognizing a right to asylum [9]. The definition shifted refugee identification from national groups, as in the inter-war years, to individual case-by-case assessment [9].

## Evidence

### McKeown: counting global migration from port and customs records

McKeown used port and customs statistics from around the world to estimate long-distance migration from 1840 to 1940 [1]. The count mostly covers third-class and steerage passengers, people registered as immigrants, emigrants, or laborers, and members of official colonization schemes such as those from Russia to Siberia and Central Asia; it includes multiple trips by the same people and omits overland travelers, first-class passengers, and migrants who evaded inspection [1]. The finding is a total of at least 150 million long-distance voyages, divided among the three circuits described above, with Asian circuits as large as the Atlantic one [1]. Comparative rates reinforce the point. Peak emigration rates in the 1910s were 10.8 per thousand for Italy, 8.3 for Norway, and 7 for Ireland, while Guangdong reached at least 9.6 per thousand in the 1920s [1]. Between 1870 and 1930 about 35 million migrants entered Southeast Asia, an area of 4.1 million square kilometres, against 39 million entering the United States, with 9.8 million square kilometres [1]. McKeown also documents a divergence in outcomes. In the Atlantic world migration accompanied wage convergence and capital followed migrants to frontier areas; in Asia wages did not converge, capital came from Europe and Japan rather than following the migrants, and much of the profit followed raw materials back to where the capital came from [1]. Return patterns and sex ratios differed by circuit: women were rarely more than 20 percent of Indian migrants before the 1930s, and Manchurian migration in the 1920s was 80 to 90 percent male [1].

### Lucassen and Lucassen: a quantitative test of the mobility transition

The Lucassens tested Zelinsky's hypothesis by summing all cross-community migrants into, out of, and within Europe in each 50-year period from 1500 to 1900 and dividing by the population at the middle of the period [2]. Data came from urban growth estimates corrected for excess urban mortality, maritime labor force reconstructions, army strength and wastage rates, and published series on colonization and emigration [2]. They found that total migrations rose from 8.7 million in 1500-1550 to 44.5 million in 1800-1850 and 118.8 million in 1850-1900 [2]. The early modern rates were far higher than the modernization model predicted, but the post-1850 jump was real [2]. Regional contrasts were sharp. The commercial Dutch Republic reached a migration rate of 70 percent in 1600-1650, while serf-based Russia stayed somewhat below the European average until the abolition of serfdom in 1861 and then rose to 46.8 percent in 1850-1900, above the European 35.5 percent [2]. The authors also identify the role of state formation: Russian expansion drove colonization of the steppe, and the "fiscal-military state" turned taxes into wages for mercenaries who moved across the continent [2]. They describe their reconstruction as "partial and preliminary" [2].

### Hatton and Williamson: what drove the European exodus

Hatton and Williamson estimated a pooled regression of decadal gross emigration rates for eleven western European countries from 1850 to 1913, using emigration data from Ferenczi and Willcox, purchasing-power-adjusted urban unskilled real wages for sending and receiving countries, lagged natural increase, the agricultural share of the labor force, and lagged emigration [3]. More than 40 million people emigrated from Europe to the New World between 1850 and 1913, and the United States absorbed nearly two thirds [3]. Each specification reached an adjusted R-squared above 0.7 [3]. A permanent rise of one per thousand in natural increase two decades earlier raised emigration by 0.77 per thousand in the long run, a 10 percent rise in the home-to-destination wage ratio lowered it by 0.8 per thousand, and the coefficient on lagged emigration lay between 0.41 and 0.46 [3]. The time trend was never significant, which the authors read as evidence against falling steerage costs as an explanation within their sample [3]. Aligning countries on their peak emigration decade produced an inverted U: rates rose from a little above 2 per thousand in the first decade of the cycle to nearly 6 per thousand in the peak (fifth) decade, then fell to around 3 per thousand in the eighth decade and 1 per thousand in the ninth [3]. The upswing was driven chiefly by the demographic transition, and the downswing by real wage convergence averaging 4.5 percent per decade [3].

### Abramitzky and Boustan: the United States as a receiving society

Abramitzky and Boustan's survey synthesizes evidence on selection, assimilation, and policy for the United States [4]. The foreign-born share of the population rose from 10 percent in 1850 to about 14 percent from 1870 to 1920, fell to 5 percent in 1970, and returned to 14 percent by 2010 [4]. Official return statistics, first collected in 1908, suggest about 30 percent of arrivals returned, while Bandiera, Rasul, and Viarengo, comparing newly digitized passenger manifests with census counts of the foreign-born, imply return rates of 60 to 75 percent in the 1900s and 1910s, as discussed by Abramitzky and Boustan [4]. In a panel of natives and immigrants from 16 European countries linked across the 1900 to 1920 censuses, the average long-term immigrant held similarly paying occupations to natives on arrival and moved up at the same rate, neither converging nor diverging; the authors conclude that cross-sectional data overstate historical convergence [4]. Restriction came in steps: a literacy test in 1917, quotas in 1921 based on the 1910 census, tighter quotas in 1924 based on 1890 population stocks, and the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, which eliminated the country-specific quota system in favor of preferences for close family members and sponsored skills [4].

### Bharadwaj, Khwaja, and Mian: reconstructing partition from censuses

Bharadwaj, Khwaja, and Mian compared the 1931 census of British India with the 1951 censuses of India and Pakistan across 287 comparable districts, treating the 1941 census as unreliable [10]. Inflows recorded as displaced persons in India and muhajirs in Pakistan totalled 14.49 million by 1951, about 3.3 percent of the population; outflows, estimated from missing minority populations, totalled 17.9 million [10]. The gap implies 3.4 million missing people in the abstract and 3.7 million in the body of the paper [10]. The missing include people who died and people who moved to third countries; the authors judge that, to the extent their outflow estimates are accurate, the greater part likely reflects mortality during partition [10]. Along the Punjab border about 2.2 million went missing, roughly 16 percent of migrating Muslims and of migrating Hindus and Sikhs alike, close to the 2.2 to 2.9 million estimated in a separate demographic study by Hill and colleagues [10]. Muslims in Indian Punjab fell from about 3.5 million in 1931 to 0.2 million in 1951 [10]. UNHCR records that a second South Asian rupture in 1971 sent about 10 million people from East Pakistan into India, the largest single refugee displacement of the second half of the century, and that more than 9 million had returned by the end of February 1972 [9].

## Implications

### For readers of history: migration as a normal condition

The first implication is interpretive. If early modern Europeans changed communities at rates of 11 to 18 percent per half-century, and if the Dutch Republic reached 70 percent in the seventeenth century, then sedentary populations are not the historical baseline against which migration appears as disruption [2]. The author's assessment is that narratives presenting mass migration as a uniquely modern rupture overstate novelty and understate continuity; the evidence supports a change in scale driven by transport and labor markets, not a change in kind [2][1]. A second interpretive implication is geographic. Treating the Atlantic as the paradigm case hides migrations of similar scale into Southeast Asia and North Asia, and it hides the different outcomes those circuits produced: wage convergence and capital that followed migrants in the Atlantic, but wages that did not converge and profits that followed raw materials out of the region in Southeast Asia [1]. A third concerns labor regimes. On the author's reading, indenture, credit-ticket migration, and recruiter systems sit between free migration and slavery, and Torpey's framework shows why the end of private control over movement and the rise of state control belong to one history [5][1][4].

### For students of states and institutions: borders as instruments of classification

The history of border regimes shows that control over movement and control over membership developed together. The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act combined an entry ban, a documentary system of certificates and registry books, penalties on carriers, and a bar on naturalization [6]. The 1924 act made eligibility for citizenship a condition of admission and so excluded Asians as a class [8]. The author's interpretation is that border regimes functioned as instruments of national definition: quotas based on the 1890 census and on the national origins of the whole population favored British and western European ancestry and limited southern and eastern Europeans [8]. Restriction also redirected flows rather than ending them. After the United States ban, many Chinese settled in South America and the Caribbean [4], and McKeown finds that the share of southern Chinese emigration going to non-Asian destinations fell from at least 40 percent in the 1850s to about 6 percent by the late 1880s, which he attributes to exclusionary laws and the scarcity of inexpensive direct transport to Latin America after the end of indenture [1]. On the author's reading, decolonization repeated the pattern of membership defined at the border. Citizenship provisions in Burma and Bangladesh left Rohingyas and Biharis without secure nationality, and Uganda's 1972 expulsion of South Asians turned a long-settled community of about 75,000 people into refugees and resettled migrants within months [9].

### For analysts of labor markets and economic convergence

Migration changed labor markets in both sending and receiving countries. Hatton and Williamson find that real wage convergence between Old and New Worlds eventually reduced emigration from the typical European country in their sample and that demographic booms raised it two decades later [3]. Abramitzky and Boustan report Goldin's (1994) estimate from cross-city variation in the 1910s that a one percentage point rise in the foreign-born share lowered wages of unskilled laborers and artisans by around 1.5 percent, and the finding of Moser, Voena, and Waldinger (2014) that United States patenting in fields associated with Jewish scientists dismissed in Nazi Germany rose by 30 percent after 1933 [4]. The author's assessment is that these results caution against both extreme claims: immigration had measurable distributional effects on competing workers, and it also transferred skills and ideas that raised productivity. A related lesson concerns the sequencing of emigration. The inverted-U pattern implies that emigration rises during early development, when populations grow fast and migrants can finance the move, and falls as home wages catch up [3]. This is a historical regularity in eleven western European countries before 1914, not a law that predicts any present case.

### For students of partition and state formation

Partition shows that drawing a border can itself become the largest single cause of migration. Bharadwaj, Khwaja, and Mian find that flows along the western border were almost three times as large as those along the eastern border, and that migrants moved to districts closer to the border, to large cities, and above all to districts vacated by out-migrants [10]. In this "relative replacement effect", a district's share of inflows tracked its share of outflows almost one for one in Pakistan and to a lesser extent in India, which the authors read as suggestive of the reallocation of evacuee property to incoming migrants; in Delhi about 0.45 million people left and about 0.5 million arrived, roughly 28 percent of the 1951 population [10]. State policy differed by region: citing Kudaisya and Tan, the authors note that the Indian government facilitated an exchange of population in Punjab but tried to prevent one in Bengal, where the replacement effect was weaker [10]. UNHCR places the 1973-1974 exchanges between Bangladesh and Pakistan in an older tradition of treating population exchange as a way to resolve minority problems, comparable to the exchanges between Greece and Turkey and between Greece and Bulgaria in the 1920s [9]. The author's assessment is that partition-era states did not merely suffer migration; they channeled it through property allocation, repatriation agreements, and citizenship rules, and in doing so redrew the demographic map of the successor states [10][9].

### For understanding identity, family, and diaspora

Migration remade families and identities as well as economies. Young single men predominated in most flows, return rates were high, and women's share of migration from Asia rose only slowly [1][3]. McKeown describes Asian migration as a multigenerational strategy that endured and expanded among the same villages and towns for over a century [1]. The author's reading is that many families experienced migration as temporary separation sustained by remittances rather than as permanent resettlement. Diasporas built on these ties also moved capital: Parsi merchants used capital earned in the India-China trade to establish textile mills in India, and Chinese capital funded and organized gold-rush migration to California and Australia in the 1850s [1]. Refugee history adds a further identity effect. Nansen passports, the 1951 Convention's individual definition, and the plight of stateless groups show that the ability to move, to return, and to belong depends on documents issued by states [9][5]. Torpey notes that a passport serves as evidence of eligibility for re-admission, and that under the doctrine of "restricted returnability" states must admit their own citizens so that other states can expel unwanted aliens; the right to return thus flows from the exigencies of sovereignty in the state system rather than from rights inhering in the individual [5]. The author's inference is that people whom no state will re-admit, such as the stateless Biharis, fall outside this protection [5][9].

## Limits of the Evidence

Each quantitative source carries specific limits. McKeown's totals count voyages, including repeat trips, and exclude overland movement and first-class travel, so they measure flows rather than distinct people [1]. The Lucassens call their reconstruction partial and preliminary; their urban migration figures are lower bounds and their seasonal labor estimates are projected back from a single systematic survey around 1811 [2]. Hatton and Williamson's sample covers eleven western European countries with comparable wage data and omits eastern Europe, and Ireland and the Latin countries emigrated more than the model predicts [3]. The partition estimates depend on the comparability of 1931 and 1951 census districts and on expected minority populations projected from 1931; the missing-population figure combines deaths with emigration to third countries, and the authors' reading of it as mostly mortality holds only to the extent that their outflow estimates are accurate [10]. UNHCR's history is written by the agency whose work it describes and deals largely with crises in which the agency played a central role [9]. Some figures also differ by definition: Abramitzky and Boustan report about 55 million departures from Europe between 1850 and 1920, of whom the United States absorbed nearly 30 million, while Hatton and Williamson report more than 40 million between 1850 and 1913 with nearly two thirds going to the United States [4][3].
