# Colonialism and Decolonization -- Imperial Rule Reordered Societies, and Independence Did Not Erase Its Institutions

European colonialism joined settlement, dispossession, foreign sovereignty, and unequal political rule across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, but it did so through institutions that varied sharply by place and period [1]. The principal post-1945 wave of decolonization replaced most formal empires with sovereign states; it did not end every colonial relationship, produce a uniform route to independence, or erase inherited borders, administrative systems, economic specializations, and disputes over historical responsibility [6][7].

## Background

Colonialism is best treated as a family of relationships rather than a single administrative design. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes it as European settlement, violent dispossession, and political domination, while noting that scholars do not use "colonialism" and "imperialism" consistently: some reserve colonialism for settlement or direct territorial rule and use imperialism for broader political, military, or economic domination [1]. This topic therefore uses colonialism for formal foreign rule over territory and people, and imperialism for the wider project of extending power, including through protectorates, spheres of influence, and informal control. The distinction is analytical, not a claim that one form was necessarily less coercive.

The early modern Atlantic empires grew from maritime expansion, conquest, settlement, missionary projects, coerced labor, and commercial competition. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas set a north-south demarcation line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands between the Castilian and Portuguese crowns. It did not lawfully dispose of other peoples' sovereignty, nor did other European powers accept a permanent Iberian monopoly, but it shows how two monarchies attempted to allocate overseas claims without the participation of the societies concerned [2]. In the Americas, conquest and settlement combined with epidemics, warfare, forced labor, slavery, and land seizure. These mechanisms differed among empires and regions, so "first-wave colonialism" should not be reduced to coastal trade or to one economic model [1][2][24].

A second acceleration came in the late nineteenth-century Scramble for Africa. Britannica estimates that European states controlled about 10 percent of African territory in the 1870s and had incorporated about 90 percent by 1914; Ethiopia and Liberia remained outside formal European incorporation at that date [3]. Strategic rivalry, national prestige, trade routes, prospective markets, resources, missionary activity, and racial ideologies all mattered. Their relative weight varied, and the expectation of profit did not mean every colony paid for itself [3]. Steam transport, telegraphy, medicine, firearms, and metropolitan fiscal capacity altered the balance between European expeditions and African states, but conquest still met extensive negotiation, accommodation, and armed resistance [3].

The Berlin West Africa Conference of 1884-1885 is central but frequently misdescribed. Its General Act addressed commerce in the Congo basin, navigation on the Congo and Niger, suppression of the slave trade, neutrality, and notification and authority requirements for future occupations on the African coast [4]. It did not draw most of Africa's final borders in the conference room. The rules on occupation expressly concerned new coastal claims, and later bilateral treaties, wars, surveys, and local negotiations fixed most boundaries [4][5]. Berlin nevertheless mattered: it helped manage rivalry among colonial powers, recognized Leopold II's Congo project, supplied a legal vocabulary for European claims, and excluded African representatives from decisions about African sovereignty [5][32]. The accurate formulation is therefore that Berlin furnished part of the diplomatic and legal framework for an already advancing partition, not that it completed the partition by itself.

Colonial administration ranged from large settler societies to thin foreign bureaucracies governing through local intermediaries. Direct and indirect rule were ideals as much as separate systems; actual administrations mixed foreign officials, chartered companies, missionaries, settlers, soldiers, merchants, chiefs, courts, and existing institutions [1]. That variation matters because the phrase "colonial legacy" can otherwise hide who exercised power, how revenue was collected, which communities were favored, and which institutions survived. The Congo Free State illustrates an extreme personal regime: Leopold II was recognized as sovereign, concessionary extraction and the Force Publique imposed forced labor and violent rubber collection, and Belgium annexed the territory in 1908 after international exposure of abuses [31][33]. Estimates of demographic loss remain extraordinarily wide, so a precise statement that ten million people were killed cannot be presented as a measured count; the evidentiary record supports catastrophic mass death and population decline, not an exact toll [31].

Decolonization likewise had a longer chronology than the shorthand "1945-1975." Jansen and Osterhammel trace the decline of European, American, and Japanese colonial supremacy from World War I through the 1990s [6]. The largest concentration followed World War II, when anti-colonial mobilization, weakened European states, war-created military and political networks, changing international norms, and superpower competition interacted. The United Nations Charter affirmed self-determination as a principle, and General Assembly Resolution 1514 of 14 December 1960 declared that all peoples have a right to self-determination and called for a speedy and unconditional end to colonialism [7]. The United Nations still lists 17 Non-Self-Governing Territories, which confirms that decolonization was neither completed in 1975 nor reducible to the transfer of flags in one postwar generation [7].

The United States and Soviet Union cannot simply be labeled two anti-colonial liberators. The United States often endorsed self-determination and an open international economy but also protected European alliances and opposed movements it associated with communism. The Soviet Union supported anti-colonial causes within Marxist-Leninist ideology while also seeking aligned governments and strategic influence [8]. Newly independent states were agents in this system, not prizes passively sorted between blocs. The 1955 Bandung Conference developed principles of Afro-Asian cooperation; the Non-Aligned Movement was formally established at its first summit in Belgrade in 1961, not founded at Bandung itself [10].

## Core Concepts

### Sovereignty, settlement, and layered rule

Formal colonialism transferred or denied sovereignty: external authorities claimed the final power to legislate, tax, police, adjudicate, wage war, and regulate land. Settlement intensified this structure where migrants sought permanent land and political control, but many colonies had few European residents and relied on intermediaries [1]. Protectorates and princely or chiefly jurisdictions could retain visible rulers while foreign powers controlled defense, diplomacy, trade, succession, or taxation. "Indirect" therefore did not mean independent. The practical question is which decisions remained local, which were supervised, and what coercive power backed the arrangement.

This layered perspective corrects two opposite errors. One is to treat every colony as a centralized European bureaucracy that replaced all earlier institutions. Colonial governments often lacked personnel and information, so they governed through selected authorities, codified flexible customs, and adapted existing tax and land systems. The other error is to mistake retained offices for continuity without domination. Recognition, salaries, courts, weapons, and removal powers could change whom local authorities answered to and convert contested offices into administrative hierarchies. The author's synthesis is that institutional persistence under colonialism was usually selective reconstruction: old forms survived, but their jurisdiction, incentives, and political meaning changed [1][17].

### Extraction, investment, and unequal integration

Colonies were incorporated into imperial economies through taxes, labor regimes, land concessions, trade rules, transport, currencies, and commodity production. Extraction was central in many places, but the blanket claim that colonial governments built only mine-to-port railways and suppressed all local industry is too strong. Infrastructure could serve military control and exports while also lowering transport costs, supporting new towns, or enabling African producers. A Ghana railway study, for example, describes lines built to connect the coast with mines and Kumasi while also finding large and persistent local development effects [34]. The correct analytical task is to identify who selected a project, who paid, who received access, and which counterfactual network was not built.

India's textile history shows why monocausal language fails. Clingingsmith and Williamson find that India moved from a major textile exporter in the early eighteenth century to losing export and much domestic market share by the mid-nineteenth century [15]. Their explanation evaluates several interacting forces: the decline of Mughal political order and agricultural productivity, global market integration, falling transport costs, rapid British manufacturing productivity, relative-price changes, and transfers to Britain [15]. Colonial policy shaped those conditions, but the evidence does not support the simpler claim that Britain mechanically "dismantled" a previously unchanged industry by one policy.

The link from Atlantic slavery to British industrialization is similarly a live empirical debate. Profits from slave trading alone were small relative to British output in conventional estimates, while a newer NBER study broadens the mechanism to slaveholding wealth and finds that places more exposed to that wealth shifted toward manufacturing, cotton mills, and steam power [16]. Its authors conclude that slavery wealth accelerated industrialization, while recognizing a literature that disputes the magnitude and causal channel [16]. A review should therefore report the current finding and the contested identification, not state that triangular-trade profits simply financed the Industrial Revolution as settled fact.

### Ideology, classification, and knowledge

Colonial power required more than arms. Christian mission, commercial improvement, a "civilizing" duty, social evolution, and racial hierarchy provided changing languages of justification. The Scramble for Africa was accompanied by widely held European claims of racial superiority [3]. Administrative practice also turned categories into governance through tools such as censuses, maps, law codes, pass systems, and identity documents; Rwanda's colonial ethnic identity cards are a documented instance [27]. The author's synthesis is that such classifications could harden distinctions that had previously been contextual or politically negotiable.

The distinction between belief and excuse is important. Officials and missionaries could sincerely believe that they were advancing education or welfare while participating in institutions premised on unequal sovereignty. Sincerity does not establish accuracy or remove coercion. Conversely, colonized people appropriated schools, print, law, religion, military service, and international language for their own projects. Anti-colonial nationalism did not arise outside colonial institutions; it often used and transformed them. This dual use explains why the same school, army, legal code, or newspaper can appear in the history of imperial consolidation and in the history of resistance [1][6].

Postcolonial analysis extends this point to representation. Edward Said's concept of Orientalism treated the production of authoritative knowledge about an imagined "Orient" as connected to institutions and power, while Frantz Fanon's work linked colonial domination to racialized experience, violence, political liberation, culture, and the dangers facing postcolonial statecraft [35][36]. These are interpretive frameworks, not substitutes for archival or quantitative evidence. Their value is to ask who defined the categories and whose voice was excluded; their claims still require testing against the specific colony, period, and institution under study.

### Borders, states, and institutional persistence

Colonial boundaries are often described as arbitrary, but two propositions must be separated. First, some boundaries divided political communities and ethnic homelands, and those partitions can have measurable effects. Michalopoulos and Papaioannou find higher incidence, severity, and duration of political violence in the homelands of African ethnic groups split by colonial borders, along with greater discrimination and economic disadvantage [22]. Second, not every border was a ruler-straight line unrelated to African conditions. Paine, Qiu, and Ricart-Huguet find that historical political frontiers directly affected 62 percent of bilateral African borders and that major water bodies were more common primary features than straight lines [23]. Both results can be true: border formation incorporated strategic European bargaining and some local realities while still partitioning many communities.

The post-independence durability of these borders also requires explanation. In 1964, Organization of African Unity member states pledged to respect the borders existing when they achieved national independence, describing border disputes as a grave source of dissension and the inherited borders as a tangible reality [39]. That collective choice helped turn inherited territorial units into institutions without proving that every boundary was locally legitimate or eliminating later disputes. The author's synthesis is that inherited boundaries are neither irrelevant lines nor sufficient causes of conflict: their effects depend on citizenship, political inclusion, cross-border intervention, economic geography, state capacity, and decisions made after independence [22][23].

Institutional persistence is likewise probabilistic rather than automatic. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson propose a chain from European settler mortality to settlement patterns, colonial institutions, present institutions, and income [17]. Their instrumental-variable estimates imply large institutional effects, but the data and instrument became the subject of a direct scholarly dispute. Albouy argues that many mortality observations were assigned across countries or combined from incomparable populations and that corrected estimates lose robustness; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson reply that restrictions and outlier treatment preserve or strengthen their result [18][19]. The durable lesson is not a universal binary of "extractive" versus "inclusive" colonies. It is that institutions may persist, but identification, measurement, indigenous exclusion, later reforms, and alternative channels must remain visible.

### Decolonization as transfer, struggle, and construction

Decolonization included at least three distinct processes: ending external sovereignty, constructing or remaking a state, and contesting the economic and cultural relationships left behind. Political independence could arrive through negotiated constitutional transfer, revolutionary war, collapse of an imperial center, settler withdrawal, partition, federation, or international trusteeship [6][7]. These routes shaped armed forces, party systems, administrative continuity, property settlements, and citizenship. They also explain why the final legal transfer may be peaceful even when the preceding mobilization and repression were violent.

The principal postwar wave accelerated through multiple causes rather than one superpower decision. Between 1945 and 1960, three dozen Asian and African states achieved autonomy or independence, according to the U.S. State Department's historical summary [8]. European fiscal and military weakness mattered, but so did organized nationalist movements, strikes, insurgencies, veterans, colonial constitutional reforms, international publicity, and the example of earlier successes. The UN's growing membership changed the normative environment: it began with 51 original members in 1945, reached 99 in 1960, and has 193 members after South Sudan's admission in 2011 [9].

Some scholars place the 1989 collapse of Soviet domination in Eastern Europe and the 1991 dissolution of the Soviet Union within a wider history of imperial endings and self-determination. That comparison can illuminate center-periphery relations, but it must not merge the departure of Central European states from the Soviet bloc, the restoration of Baltic independence, and the varied trajectories of other Soviet republics. "Decolonization" is therefore a useful but contested comparative frame, not a settled synonym for every post-Soviet transition [37].

## Evidence

### Demographic violence and the limits of precise totals

Colonial demographic history demonstrates both the scale of disruption and the danger of false precision. Koch and colleagues synthesized 119 regional population estimates for the Americas and estimated a decline from about 60 million people in 1492 to about 6 million by 1600, caused by epidemics, warfare, famine, and slavery [24]. Their continent-wide result supports an approximately 90 percent decline, but it is a reconstruction with uncertainty across regions, not a body count or a claim that disease alone explains every death. Its method also linked abandoned land and vegetation recovery to atmospheric carbon change, showing how demographic and environmental evidence can be combined [24].

The Congo Free State presents a different evidentiary problem. Documentary evidence establishes forced labor, hostage-taking, mutilation, village destruction, hunger, disease, and reduced reproduction under Leopold II's regime [33]. Population baselines and later counts, however, are too uncertain to turn the familiar ten-million figure into an exact death toll. A 2024 Journal of African History discussion notes estimates ranging from one to fifteen million and an unresolved debate over the number [31]. The defensible conclusion is catastrophic demographic loss caused by a violent extractive system; the uncertainty should be stated rather than hidden.

For German South West Africa, evidence supports both genocidal intent and mass mortality. Germany's federal government states that General Lothar von Trotha ordered the annihilation of the Herero in October 1904 and of the Nama in April 1905, and it identifies violence, thirst, and camps as causes of death [25]. The government recognized the events as genocide in 2021 and reported in May 2025 that further talks over a joint declaration with Namibia were continuing [25]. Amnesty International reports the frequently cited estimates that about 80 percent of the Ovaherero and 50 percent of the Nama were killed, while documenting affected communities' challenge to the proposed reconciliation process [26]. These sources agree on genocide but differ in institutional position and legal framing, which should remain explicit.

### Economic and institutional studies

Long-run quantitative studies provide evidence of persistence, not a license to infer one mechanism from correlation. Nunn reconstructs slave exports from shipping records and records of enslaved people's ethnic origins and finds a robust negative relationship between export intensity and present economic performance [20]. He uses historical selection evidence and instruments to argue for an adverse causal effect, while the design still depends on reconstructed exports, geographic assignment, and exclusion assumptions [20]. Nunn and Wantchekon then combine ethnicity-level slave-export estimates with contemporary survey data and find that people whose ancestors were more heavily raided report lower trust; their identification tests attribute most of the estimated effect to internalized norms and beliefs, with institutional channels also relevant [21]. The second study supports a trust mechanism that the first study alone did not establish.

The settler-mortality literature illustrates productive dispute. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson use mortality rates faced by Europeans as an instrument for present institutions and estimate large effects of institutions on income [17]. Albouy's published critique argues that 36 of 64 sample countries received mortality values from other countries and that combining soldiers, laborers, bishops, and campaign observations made the first stage unreliable after correction [18]. The original authors accept concern about high-mortality outliers but argue that capping or excluding them strengthens the substantive relationship and that Albouy's deletions and campaign coding are historically unjustified [19]. A complete review must present the claim, critique, and reply. Treating the original estimate as an uncontested law would overstate the evidence.

Economic change within particular colonies further resists simple verdicts. Clingingsmith and Williamson document India's large fall in world industrial output share and textile competitiveness while modeling domestic political disruption, agricultural productivity, British industrial advance, global price changes, and transfers together [15]. Heblich, Redding, and Voth use geographic exposure to British slaveholding wealth and compensation records to argue that slavery wealth promoted local manufacturing, cotton-mill diffusion, steam adoption, and capital accumulation [16]. These studies support material links between empire, slavery, and industrial change, but neither validates a universal formula in which every colonial export caused metropolitan development or every colonial investment harmed local development.

### Border formation and conflict

The best evidence on African borders corrects both denial and determinism. Michalopoulos and Papaioannou compare partitioned and nonpartitioned ethnic homelands using historical ethnolinguistic mapping and geocoded conflict data. They find that split homelands experienced higher conflict incidence, severity, and duration, more interventions from neighboring countries, more government discrimination, and worse surveyed economic outcomes [22]. The result concerns ethnic partitioning and defined outcomes; it does not show that ethnic diversity by itself causes war or that all African conflicts originate in colonial cartography.

Paine, Qiu, and Ricart-Huguet reconstruct the border-formation process with spatial data, treaties, and diplomatic histories. Their finding that precolonial political frontiers directly affected 62 percent of bilateral borders revises the claim that Europeans generally drew lines without examining conditions on the ground [23]. It does not rehabilitate colonial sovereignty or deny arbitrary segments. Instead, it shows that strategic bargaining, African treaties, historical states, rivers, lakes, and straight-line convenience all entered the final map. Together, the two studies support a narrower causal statement: specific partitions imposed durable costs, even though the continent's border system was not uniformly random [22][23].

### Contrasting paths to independence

India's 1947 partition shows that a negotiated transfer can coexist with mass violence. Stanford historian Priya Satia reports that about 15 million people moved or were forced to move and that estimates of deaths range from roughly 500,000 to 2 million [13]. This range is more defensible than presenting one to two million as settled. British administrative haste, provincial partition, organized communal politics, collapsing order, and local decisions interacted; separate electorates were part of the colonial institutional background, but they do not alone explain the violence [13].

Algeria demonstrates why politically produced casualty totals must be labeled. The 1954-1962 war joined FLN guerrilla struggle, French counterinsurgency, settler politics, torture, diplomatic pressure, and violence within communities. Britannica reports French-source estimates of 300,000 to 500,000 Algerian casualties and an Algerian claim as high as 1.5 million [14]. Those figures are not endpoints of one statistical confidence interval. They arise from different records, definitions, and commemorative traditions, so the review should say that the total is disputed rather than merging scholarly and official claims.

Vietnam combined decolonization, civil war, revolutionary state-building, and Cold War intervention. The First Indochina War began in 1946 and ended after the Viet Minh defeated France at Dien Bien Phu in 1954; the Geneva settlement temporarily divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel and called for reunification elections [11]. The United States had already supported France financially and militarily before 1954 and then backed Ngo Dinh Diem's government in South Vietnam [11]. Calling the later conflict simply a U.S. "neocolonial" war presents one interpretation as a complete description. The evidence requires the several overlapping dimensions to remain visible.

British African transfers also mixed negotiation with coercion. Ghana became independent in 1957, Nigeria in 1960, Tanganyika in 1961, and Kenya in 1963; Tanganyika and Zanzibar formed the United Republic of Tanzania in 1964, so "Tanzania in 1961" is incorrect [9]. Constitutional transfers do not make the whole process peaceful. The British government later recognized that Kenyans were tortured and otherwise ill-treated during the Emergency and settled claims from 5,228 people [12]. Kenya therefore belongs in a category of negotiated final transfer after insurgency, detention, torture, and counterinsurgency, not in a simple list of peaceful decolonizations.

## Implications

### Historical explanation without colonial determinism

Colonial history is indispensable to explaining contemporary institutions, but it is not a complete theory of current outcomes. A border can structure mobilization without mechanically causing war; an extractive tax system can persist without making reform impossible; a commodity economy can create vulnerability without producing the same policy in every successor state. The studies reviewed above identify conditional effects and disputed causal channels [17][18][19][22][23]. The practical implication is to specify the inherited mechanism and the later decisions that sustained, redirected, or dismantled it.

Rwanda illustrates this discipline. Belgian colonial authorities favored Tutsis and required ethnic classification on identity cards, hardening political distinctions and giving them administrative force [27]. Those policies are necessary context for later exclusion and violence, but the 1994 genocide also required post-independence regimes, civil war, extremist organization, propaganda, arms, and deliberate state action. Colonial racialization is therefore a causal component, not an explanation that removes responsibility from later actors [27]. The same causal discipline applies wherever imperial amalgamation, partition, or mandate rule is invoked.

### Development policy and institutional diagnosis

For development analysis, "colonial legacy" should be converted into testable institutional questions. Who owns land and minerals? How is revenue raised? Which regions connect to ports, markets, schools, and courts? Does public authority depend on taxation or on externally controlled rents? Can citizens remove officials, enforce contracts, and obtain services? These questions distinguish mechanisms that the broad label otherwise conflates. They also make reform targets visible: land records, fiscal transparency, local accountability, transport integration, equal citizenship, education, and judicial capacity.

Resource wealth is a useful example. Ross's review finds robust evidence that petroleum wealth can make authoritarian regimes more durable, increase some forms of corruption, and trigger conflict in low- and middle-income states under certain conditions [30]. That is narrower than saying natural resources are inherently a curse or that colonial extraction alone determines later governance. Colonial concession systems and export corridors can shape the starting conditions, while post-independence tax policy, revenue sharing, political competition, and administrative competence influence whether rents reinforce or weaken accountability [30]. Comparisons among resource-rich states should therefore test institutional mechanisms rather than assume one outcome from the resource itself.

### International order after empire

Decolonization remade international institutions as well as domestic governments. UN membership grew from 51 original states to 99 by 1960 and 193 by 2011, shifting the General Assembly's composition and giving newly independent states a venue to press self-determination, racial equality, economic sovereignty, and development claims [9]. Resolution 1514 both reflected and strengthened this new majority, while the UN trusteeship and decolonization machinery supplied procedures and international scrutiny [7]. The remaining Non-Self-Governing Territories show that the legal and political agenda did not vanish when the largest empires dissolved [7].

Nonalignment was one strategy for agency within the Cold War rather than passive neutrality. Bandung in 1955 connected peace, development, and anti-colonial cooperation; 25 states formally established the Non-Aligned Movement at Belgrade in 1961 [10]. The movement did not erase differences among its members or keep all of them equidistant from the superpowers. Its historical significance lies in collective agenda-setting by states that rejected the premise that decolonization merely transferred them into one of two blocs [8][10].

### Evidence, interpretation, and public memory

Colonial history is especially vulnerable to compressed numbers and inherited slogans. "Berlin drew Africa," "ten million were killed in the Congo," "Britain destroyed Indian textiles," and "all colonial railways ran only from mines to ports" each contains a real historical problem but overstates a causal or quantitative claim [5][15][31][34]. Better practice separates the documented event, the estimate, the method, and the interpretation. Where sources disagree, the disagreement belongs in the account.

The same rule applies to intellectual frameworks. Said and Fanon help identify representation, racialization, violence, and the unfinished work of political liberation [35][36]. Quantitative studies test persistence in income, trust, institutions, or conflict [17][20][21][22]. Diplomatic and legal records reconstruct sovereignty and border formation [4][5][23]. None of these methods is sufficient alone. The author's synthesis is that reliable colonial history requires triangulation among archives, local histories, demographic reconstruction, political economy, legal records, oral testimony, and explicit uncertainty.

### Redress, apology, restitution, and continuing disputes

Historical responsibility is now contested through apologies, reparations, restitution, archives, memorials, and curriculum. The wording matters. Belgium's King Philippe expressed "deepest regrets" for exploitation, racism, and violence in Congo but stopped short of a formal apology in 2022 [28]. Germany recognizes the Herero and Nama genocide and its moral, historical, and political responsibility, yet its federal government reported in May 2025 that talks over the 2021 joint declaration were still continuing [25]. Amnesty International argues that the proposed EUR 1.1 billion in reconstruction and development programs is not reparations and that affected Ovaherero and Nama representatives were not meaningfully included; in September 2026 it again called for full reparations rather than development aid alone [26][38]. A review must report these positions separately rather than say that Belgium apologized or Germany paid reparations.

Restitution can produce a more concrete transfer while leaving wider claims unresolved. France transferred ownership of 26 objects looted from the royal palaces of Abomey in 1892 to Benin in November 2021 after legislation made the return possible [29]. That act established neither a general settlement for all objects in French collections nor agreement about the legal principles governing every case. It demonstrates instead that redress is institution-specific: returning an object, apologizing, financing development, compensating victims, opening archives, and acknowledging legal liability are different actions with different beneficiaries and consequences [25][26][28][29].

For historians, policymakers, and citizens, the central implication is methodological and ethical. Independence ended foreign sovereignty for most colonies, but it did not automatically dissolve institutions, categories, property relations, borders, or memories produced under imperial rule [6][7]. Understanding those continuities requires evidence precise enough to distinguish persistence from inevitability and responsibility from inherited disadvantage. That distinction permits a more accurate history and a more useful question: not whether colonialism still "explains everything," but which mechanisms remain active, for whom, and through which present institutions.
