# Library Topic Template (v4) -- How We Write Library Topic Files

A library topic is a researched, structured knowledge artifact that
lives in a domain folder under `library/<domain>/`. It is written by
the Writer process, reviewed by the Auditor process, and proposed by
the Discoverer process. Each topic is a self-contained unit of
knowledge: it explains a subject, cites sources, and cross-references
related topics.

## Global Formatting Rules

The entire GitHub org is plain 7-bit ASCII, lowercase, hyphen-delimited.
These rules are non-negotiable. CI enforces them.

- **ASCII-only:** Every character in every file is 7-bit ASCII (U+0000
  through U+007F). No emoji, no smart quotes, no Unicode dashes or
  arrows, no accented letters. The `ascii-guard.yml` CI gate fails the
  build on any violation.
- **Lowercase only:** All filenames, slugs, tags, domains, and folder
  names use lowercase exclusively. No CamelCase, no UPPERCASE, no
  mixed case.
- **Hyphens, not underscores:** Use hyphens (`-`) to separate words in
  filenames, slugs, and tags. Never use underscores (`_`).
  Correct: `loss-aversion.md`. Wrong: `loss_aversion.md`.

## Frontmatter Rules

- `name` is a lowercase kebab-case slug, unique within the domain.
  Derive it from the topic title. Example: `loss-aversion`,
  `discounted-cash-flow`, `plate-tectonics`.
- `id` is ISO 8601 UTC (`YYYYMMDDTHHMMSSZ`). Never reuse. Never change
  after publishing. MUST generate with: `date -u +'%Y%m%dT%H%M%SZ'`
  at creation. Estimating or rounding = GATE FAILURE.
- `tier` is always `library-topic`.
- `domain` is the kebab-case domain slug matching the folder name.
  Must match one of the 28 domain anchors exactly. Example:
  `value-investing`, `psychology-behavior`, `history`.
- `author` is who wrote the topic. Capitalized agent name. Examples:
  Ava, Link, Researcher-1, Researcher-2.
- `tags` use lowercase, hyphens for spaces. Domain-specific tags that
  help the brain-index search tool find this topic. Prefer precise
  tags over generic ones.
- `links` are paths relative to the agentic-brain root. Use relative
  paths (e.g. `library/psychology-behavior/cognitive-biases.md`,
  `reflections/2026-07-19_ava_verification-bottleneck.md`). Do not use
  the `brain:` prefix. Do not use absolute paths or `file://` URIs.
- `audited` is added by the Auditor ONLY on APPROVE verdict. Omit
  from the initial write. Value: `true` when audited and approved.
- `audit-score` is added by the Auditor ONLY on APPROVE verdict.
  Value: the weighted audit score (0.0-10.0). Omit from the initial
  write.

## Naming Convention

Files are named: `<topic-slug>.md`

- `topic-slug` -- kebab-case, max 80 chars, unique within the domain.
  Derived from the topic title. Keep it descriptive and searchable.
  Example: `loss-aversion.md`, `discounted-cash-flow.md`.

Location: `library/<domain>/<topic-slug>.md`

- `domain` -- the domain folder name. Must match the `domain` frontmatter
  field exactly.
- Example full path: `library/psychology-behavior/loss-aversion.md`

## Body Structure

The topic body MUST include the following sections in this order.
Each section has a defined purpose. They are mandatory structural
elements -- not suggestions and not "at writer's discretion."

### Title (level-1 heading)

The title makes a claim about the topic. It is something someone can
agree or disagree with. Not a label -- a statement.

**Good:** `# Loss Aversion -- Why Losses Hurt Twice as Much as Gains`
**Bad:** `# Loss Aversion` (label, no claim)
**Bad:** `# All About Loss Aversion` (vague, no claim)

### Opening Paragraph

Summarize the topic in 2-3 sentences. A reader with no domain knowledge
should understand what this topic is about and why it matters. State
the core claim or finding up front. Do not bury the lead.

### Section Order

The following sections MUST appear in this sequence. Domain-specific
titles are noted where permitted; the presence of each section is
non-negotiable.

```
1.  ## Background
    Historical or intellectual context. How did this knowledge develop?
    MUST appear immediately after the opening paragraph.

2.  ## Core Concepts
    The essential ideas of the topic. What are the building blocks?
    Title MAY vary by domain: ## Core Biases by Category,
    ## Core Principles, ## Core Mechanisms. MUST appear.

3.  (domain expansion sections)
    Additional ## sections expanding on specific aspects of the Core
    Concepts. MAY appear between Core Concepts and Evidence.
    Examples: ## Kuhn and Paradigm Shifts, ## Practical Moat Analysis
    Framework. These are deeper dives into subtopics introduced in
    Core Concepts -- NOT replacements for mandatory sections.

4.  ## Evidence
    Empirical support, research findings, case studies. What data or
    observations back the claims? Title MAY vary: ## Evidence and
    Research Foundation, ## Empirical Support. MUST appear.

5.  ## Implications
    Why this topic matters. Practical applications, consequences,
    connections to other domains. MUST appear.

6.  (pitfalls / criticisms / frameworks)
    ## Common Pitfalls, ## Criticisms, ## Practical Frameworks.
    MAY appear after Implications. These are meta-commentary on the
    topic, not core content.

7.  ## Writer Scoring
    4-dimension scores with justifications, weighted formula, and
    topic similarity assessment. MUST appear after the last body
    section and before Sources.

8.  ## Sources
    Cited references with authority ratings. MUST appear after
    Writer Scoring and before See Also.

9.  ## See Also
    Cross-references to related topics and brain artifacts. MUST
    be the final section. No content may follow.
```

Sections 7-9 (Writer Scoring, Sources, See Also) MUST appear in this
exact order. No content may follow `## See Also`.

Use `###` level-3 headings for sub-sections within body content sections.
Avoid going deeper than level-3.

Every factual claim should be traceable to a source in the Sources
section. If a claim is the author's synthesis or interpretation, label
it as such: "The author's assessment is..." or "This suggests that...".

### Why Mandatory Sections

The standard pattern (`## Background`, `## Core Concepts`,
`## Evidence`, `## Implications`) emerged from the first four topics
written across four domains. Every topic that followed this pattern
produced a coherent, scannable, cross-referenceable artifact. The one
topic that omitted `## Core Concepts` and `## Evidence` by embedding
them inside domain-specific body sections produced an artifact that was
harder to scan, audit, and cross-reference.

The sections are now MUST (not "standard pattern at writer's
discretion"). Domain-specific titles are permitted but the presence of
each named section is non-negotiable.

### Sources

A `## Sources` section at the end of the file listing all references.
Each source entry includes:

1. The source title or description.
2. The URL (if accessible).
3. An authority rating: `[high]`, `[medium]`, or `[low]`.

Format:

```markdown
## Sources

1. Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of
   Decision under Risk." Econometrica, 47(2), 263-291.
   https://www.jstor.org/stable/1914185 [high]

2. Thaler, R. (1980). "Toward a positive theory of consumer choice."
   Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 1(1), 39-60. [high]

3. Clear, J. "Loss Aversion: Why We Hate Losing More Than We Love Winning."
   https://jamesclear.com/loss-aversion [medium]
```

Authority ratings:
- `[high]` -- academic papers, official data, primary sources,
  reputable publications with editorial standards.
- `[medium]` -- reputable blogs, industry publications, secondary
  sources with clear attribution.
- `[low]` -- personal blogs, forums, unattributed content, sources
  with no identifiable author or institution.

### Writer Scoring

A `## Writer Scoring` section recording the scored dimensions with
justifications. MUST appear after the last body content section and
before `## Sources`. Format:

```markdown
## Writer Scoring

- **Core match:** X.X/10.0 -- <1-2 sentence justification>
- **Scope fit:** X.X/10.0 -- <1-2 sentence justification>
- **Knowledge value:** X.X/10.0 -- <1-2 sentence justification>
- **Source authority:** X.X/10.0 -- <1-2 sentence justification>

**Weighted score:** (core * 0.35) + (scope * 0.35) + (value * 0.20)
+ (authority * 0.10) = **X.X/10.0**

**Topic similarity:** <X% overlap with existing topics. Proceeded /
Flagged / Skipped.>
```

The format is `**Dimension:** X.X/10.0 -- <justification>` -- colon after
the dimension name, score with `/10.0` suffix, double dash, then
justification. Do NOT use the parenthetical format
`**Dimension (X.X/10.0):` -- that is the old format that caused the
format drift documented in ENT-029.
Candidate queue ID MUST be referenced in the justification or the
similarity line.

### Cross-references

A `## See Also` section at the end (after Sources) linking to related
topics within the library and related brain artifacts. At least one
cross-reference is required. Format:

```markdown
## See Also

- `library/psychology-behavior/cognitive-biases.md` -- broader category
  this topic fits within.
- `library/value-investing/mr-market.md` -- how loss aversion manifests
  in investing behavior.
- `reflections/2026-07-19_ava_verification-bottleneck.md` -- related
  insight on decorrelated review.
```

## Quality Gates

Every library topic passes these checks before the Writer commits:

- **G1 -- Title Makes a Claim:** The level-1 heading is something
  someone can agree or disagree with. Not a label like "Loss Aversion"
  -- a statement like "Loss Aversion -- Why Losses Hurt Twice as Much
  as Gains."
- **G2 -- Opening Paragraph Is Self-Contained:** A reader with no
  domain knowledge understands what this topic is about and why it
  matters from the opening 2-3 sentences alone.
- **G3 -- Every Claim Is Sourced:** Every factual claim traces to a
  source in the Sources section. Synthesized claims are labeled as
  such. No orphan facts.
- **G4 -- Sources Have Authority Ratings:** Every source entry includes
  a `[high]`, `[medium]`, or `[low]` rating. At least 2 of the 3+
  sources must be `[high]` or `[medium]`. A topic sourced entirely
  from `[low]` authority sources fails this gate.
- **G5 -- Cross-references Exist:** At least one link to a related
  library topic or brain artifact. Zero links = isolated knowledge
  that does not compound.
- **G6 -- Domain Anchor Compliant:** The topic stays within the domain
  anchor's In scope and avoids Out scope. Verified by reading the
  domain's `anchor-<domain>.md` file before writing.
- **G7 -- Topic Similarity Gate:** Before writing, the candidate topic
  was checked against existing topics in the domain. If >= 80% overlap,
  the topic was skipped. The overlap estimate is recorded in Writer
  Scoring.
- **G8 -- Frontmatter Complete:** All 7 required fields present (name,
  id, tier, domain, author, tags, links). `id` was generated by
  `date -u +'%Y%m%dT%H%M%SZ'` and is not human-rounded.
- **G9 -- Formatting Rules:** ASCII-only (zero non-ASCII characters),
  lowercase slugs and tags, hyphens not underscores. CI enforces
  ASCII via `ascii-guard.yml`.
- **G10 -- Output Destination Correct:** File written ONLY to
  `library/<domain>/<topic-slug>.md`. Never written to the workspace.
- **G11 -- Section Order:** All mandatory sections present (Background,
  Core Concepts, Evidence, Implications) in correct sequence. Body
  sections -> Writer Scoring -> Sources -> See Also. No content may
  follow `## See Also`.

## Library-Specific Gates (L1-L4)

These gates verify pipeline integration -- they confirm the topic was
produced by the full library pipeline, not written in isolation.

- **L1 -- Pipeline Integration:** The candidate queue ID (from
  `library/candidate-queue.md`) is referenced in the Writer Scoring
  section. This creates an auditable chain: discoverer -> candidate ->
  writer -> topic. A topic without a candidate ID was written outside
  the pipeline and is not traceable.
- **L2 -- Anchor Freshness:** The domain anchor file was read during
  THIS writing cycle. The anchor paragraph was not summarized from
  memory. The scope (In/Out) was actively checked against the topic
  content. A stale anchor check (from a previous session) fails this
  gate because the anchor may have been updated.
- **L3 -- Candidate Queue Updated:** The processed candidate was
  removed from `library/candidate-queue.md`. A leftover candidate
  entry after a successful write causes double-processing by the
  next writer cycle.
- **L4 -- Library Log Written:** An entry was appended to
  `logbook/library.log` with the ENT counter, topic title, weighted
  score, dimension breakdown, source count with authority ratings,
  similarity overlap, and cross-reference count. This entry is the
  only record the auditor uses to select topics for review. Missing
  or incomplete log entries cause audit gaps.

## Custom Library Gates

### Pipeline Traceability (L1 detail)

The candidate queue is the single entry point for all library topics.
Every topic MUST be traceable to a discoverer-proposed candidate. The
traceability chain is:

```
discoverer proposes candidate -> candidate-queue.md (candidate ID assigned)
  -> writer picks candidate -> topic written (candidate ID in Writer Scoring)
  -> auditor reviews topic -> audited: true + audit-score added
```

A topic that appears in a domain folder without a candidate ID in its
Writer Scoring section bypassed the discovery process and cannot be
audited for gap coverage. The auditor skips topics missing candidate ID
references.

### Section Positioning Rules

Domain-specific body sections fall into two categories with different
positioning rules:

| Category | Position | Examples |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Expansion sections | Between Core Concepts and Evidence | `## Kuhn and Paradigm Shifts`, `## Practical Moat Analysis Framework` |
| Meta-commentary sections | After Implications, before Writer Scoring | `## Common Pitfalls`, `## Criticisms`, `## Practical Frameworks` |

Expansion sections deepen Core Concepts. Meta-commentary sections
reflect ON the topic. Mixing these categories produces topics where
pitfalls appear before the evidence that would justify them.

### Format Consistency

The Writer Scoring format is standardized across all topics to enable
automated parsing by the auditor and index generator:

- Correct: `**Core match:** 9.0/10.0 -- Loss aversion is central...`
- Wrong: `**Core match (9.0/10.0):** Loss aversion is central...`
- Wrong: `- Core match: 9.0` (missing `/10.0` suffix)
- Wrong: `**Core match:** 9.0/10.0` with no justification

The colon-after-dimension, score-with-slash, double-dash-separator
format is machine-parseable. The parenthetical format is not. This
was the format drift documented in ENT-029: template-library v3
prescribed `**Dimension (X.X/10.0):` but every correct topic used
`**Dimension:** X.X/10.0 --`. The topics were right; the template
was wrong. v4 aligns the template with production reality.

# Loss Aversion -- Why Losses Hurt Twice as Much as Gains

Loss aversion is the psychological phenomenon where losses are felt
approximately twice as intensely as equivalent gains. Identified by
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky as a core component of Prospect Theory
(1979), it explains why people hold losing investments too long, reject
fair bets, and make risk-averse choices even when the expected value is
positive. Loss aversion is not a flaw -- it is an evolved survival
mechanism that becomes maladaptive in modern decision environments.

## Background

Before Kahneman and Tversky, the dominant model of decision-making was
Expected Utility Theory -- the idea that people make rational choices
by weighing outcomes by their probabilities. Kahneman and Tversky
demonstrated that real human behavior systematically violates this
model. People do not evaluate outcomes in absolute terms; they evaluate
them relative to a reference point, and they weight losses more heavily
than gains.

The key insight came from a series of experiments in the 1970s where
participants consistently rejected gambles with positive expected
value if there was any chance of loss. The "reflection effect" showed
that people are risk-averse for gains but risk-seeking for losses --
the opposite of what utility theory predicts.

## Core Concepts

### The Value Function

Prospect Theory replaces the utility function with a value function
that has three key properties:

1. **Reference dependence:** Outcomes are evaluated as gains or losses
   relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms.
2. **Loss aversion:** The value function is steeper for losses than for
   gains. A loss of $100 hurts roughly twice as much as a gain of $100
   pleases.
3. **Diminishing sensitivity:** Both gains and losses show diminishing
   marginal impact. The difference between $0 and $100 feels larger
   than the difference between $1,000 and $1,100.

### The Endowment Effect

A direct consequence of loss aversion: people value items they own
more than identical items they do not own. In Thaler's (1980) classic
experiment, participants given a coffee mug demanded roughly twice as
much to sell it as participants without a mug were willing to pay.
Owning the mug created a new reference point, and giving it up felt
like a loss.

### Status Quo Bias

Loss aversion produces inertia. The potential loss from changing away
from the current state looms larger than the potential gain from
changing. This explains why people stick with default options, stay in
suboptimal jobs, and hold losing investments.

## Evidence

Kahneman and Tversky (1979) demonstrated loss aversion across multiple
experimental paradigms. In one, participants choosing between a sure
$500 gain and a 50% chance of $1,000 overwhelmingly chose the sure
gain (risk aversion in the gain domain). But when choosing between a
sure $500 loss and a 50% chance of $1,000 loss, they chose the gamble
(risk-seeking in the loss domain). The only difference was the framing
as gain or loss.

Thaler (1980) extended this to real-world behavior, showing that
consumers violate economic rationality in predictable loss-averse
patterns. Benartzi and Thaler (1995) applied loss aversion to explain
the equity premium puzzle -- why stocks historically return far more
than bonds when rational models predict a smaller gap. Their answer:
investors check their portfolios too frequently (myopic loss aversion)
and the short-term pain of losses drives them to demand a large premium.

## Implications

For investors: loss aversion explains why investors sell winners too
early (locking in gains) and hold losers too long (avoiding the pain
of realizing a loss). The disposition effect is a direct consequence.
Value investors like Buffett and Munger explicitly train themselves to
overcome this bias -- "be greedy when others are fearful" is an
anti-loss-aversion heuristic.

For policy: default options in retirement plans and organ donation
leverage status quo bias. Making enrollment the default dramatically
increases participation because opting out feels like a loss.

For everyday decisions: loss aversion affects negotiation (concessions
feel like losses), pricing (discounts are framed as "saving" a loss),
and commitment (sunk cost fallacy is partly loss aversion -- walking
away means accepting the loss).
